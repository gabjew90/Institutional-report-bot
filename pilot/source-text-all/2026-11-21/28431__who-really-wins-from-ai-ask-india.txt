November 19, 2026 
If you really want to understand the true potential for AI, look beyond Silicon 
Valley to a market that has flown under the radar of AI investors until now: 
India.  
As markets agonise over who will win the AI race, India is quietly testing whether 
it even matters. With little homegrown AI technology but a vast, young, tech-
savvy population, it may show that creating new technology counts less than 
adopting it at scale. 
This report sets out the bull and bear cases for India and AI. It also draws out 
some unexpected lessons for other markets that want the benefits of AI without 
letting jobs and industries get steamrollered in the process. 
And it looks at the short-term case for India as a diversifier from AI volatility. 
India’s low correlation with other global indices is suddenly looking like a 
strength, helping to drive record inflows of foreign investment into Indian 
equities.

The US and China have the models, the chips and hundreds of billions of dollars 
in data centre investments, but rapidly developing India has almost 1.5 billion 
potential users, digital rails that are already built, an enormous technology 
workforce and some of the world’s largest unmet needs in finance, healthcare 
and agriculture. 
India recently became ChatGPT’s largest market by weekly users and, thanks to 
its pervasive digital infrastructure, even a street vendor selling a single piece of 
fruit will typically hold up a QR code on their phone for instant payment. 
“Smartphone scrolling has taken over from cricket as India’s No. 1 pastime,” a 
banker told me on a recent visit to Mumbai, India’s financial capital, where 
swarms of battered three-wheeled black and yellow auto rickshaws weave 
between fleets of freshly buffed Mercedes sedans. 
The real test for AI will not be in the frontier labs of San Francisco but on 
streets like these in Mumbai and thousands of cities like it.

What would winning AI actually mean?  
Investors in the US, China and Korea have so far largely focused on AI capabilities, 
picking the winners in the AI trade in a volatile daily referendum on potential gains 
years in the future.  
By contrast, India can adopt an infrastructure-lite approach that sidesteps the 
whole debate, embracing the benefits without incurring the costs of trying to stay 
on the bleeding edge. 
It can plug off-the-shelf software into well-developed national digital systems, 
tapping into the world’s largest population, with a median age of 29, and 
deploying its considerable buying power as two blocs compete to set the global 
standard. 
Success does not need to mean building the most powerful foundation model. 
Instead, it may be deploying it for: 
1. Higher productivity 
2. Faster income growth 
3. Better financial, educational and healthcare access 
4. More productive employment rather than mass displacement 
5. Higher-value IT and service exports 
6. Capturing enough of the AI value chain domestically 
While India has fundamental economic and infrastructure challenges, it ranks No. 
3 in the world for AI vibrancy, albeit far behind the leaders US and China, 
according to the Stanford HAI Global AI Vibrancy Tool. That is thanks to its strong 
position in Research & Development and in talent.

Six reasons India could be an AI winner 
1. 1.5 billion potential adopters 
Demographics + development + unmet demand 
India has demographics on its side.  
It overtook China’s population of over 1.4 billion in 2023 and is set to hit 1.5 billion 
people in 2028 before peaking at 1.7 billion in about 2060, according to the 
United Nations’ base scenario. By that time, China’s population will have likely 
dropped to little more than 1 billion. The populations of Europe and the US will 
not be hugely different from today. 
Moreover, India is developing fast. Between 2011 and 2023, almost 500 million 
people were lifted out of poverty, with extreme poverty falling from more than a 
quarter of the population to 2.6 percent, the World Bank said last month. The only 
equivalent has been China, which lifted 800 million people out of poverty over 
four decades.

Almost half of its workers are still employed in agriculture, representing a 
significant pent-up supply of labour and consumer demand. The economic centre 
of the world is likely to continue to shift back towards its historical centre of 
gravity. As late as 1820, China accounted for about a third of global GDP and India 
for about 17 percent, while Western Europe was about a quarter and the US a 
mere 2 percent. 
The country is expected to grow 7 percent this year and next, the IMF forecast 
last month, outpacing the advanced economies at 2.1 percent this year and 1.8 
percent next year, and China, the growth driver for the early part of this century, 
at 4.5 percent and 4 percent.

2. The digital rails are already built 
Smartphones + low-cost data + JAM 
AI adoption does not depend on building new rails. It can run on the infrastructure 
that India has spent more than a decade creating.  
For starters, the cheapness of internet and mobile connectivity puts most other 
economies to shame. Internet access has shot up from 15 percent to 70 percent 
over the past 10 years, close to the global average and far above low-income 
countries.

Source: Our World in Data, citing ITU via World Bank, Deutsche Bank Research 
 
That expansion has been driven largely by mobile phones running off brand-new 
infrastructure, allowing India to leapfrog the PC-led stage of digitalisation that 
higher-income countries had to trudge through first. 
It has also laid the foundations for a Digital Public Infrastructure in stages since 
2009, creating common digital systems for enterprises and government 
authorities to build on. These give organisations a stable platform to deploy AI at 
low cost.

India’s “JAM” digital trinity is built on Jan Dhan, universal access to formal banking 
with zero-balance bank accounts; Aadhaar, a unique biometric digital 
identification that allows instant electronic know-your-customer (eKYC) 
verification; and Mobile, ubiquitous smartphone connectivity. 
On top of that is the Unified Payments Interface (UPI), the world’s largest real-
time fast payments system across different accounts and mobile apps. 
Consequently bank account ownership has jumped from just over half of the 
population in 2014 to almost 90 percent today. 
Amid that transformation, AI will enable financial institutions to offer targeted 
micro credit to hundreds of millions of people for whom it would have been too 
expensive to score, grant and monitor in the past. AI will also enable them to 
personalise their services in a country with 22 official languages, more than 120 
major languages and around 1,600 others. 
Personalised AI loan collections 
For example, one bank which requested anonymity said it is trialling an AI-
automated collection system to chase customers who are late in paying off 
personal loans. It predicts who is likely to fall behind and when they will be most 
responsive, for instance, contacting office workers with daytime text messages 
rather than calls. 
The system speaks to customers in their own language and escalates 
automatically by getting more assertive if they still don’t pay. It is continuously 
learning and adjusting the timing, channel and tone of reminders. 
As a result, it is getting significantly better collection ratios and therefore able to 
issue more loans. The size of the collections team has not changed. This example 
is not unique to India but the potential economies of scale are. 
“Incredible builder energy,” Altman says, versus “negative” Americans 
OpenAI has taken a big bet on India. CEO Sam Altman said on a recent Indian 
podcast that it has overtaken the US in number of ChatGPT users. The number of 
users has doubled since the beginning of the year, buoyed by advertising and 
affordable pricing. 
The company charges many customers less than $5 a month and has offered free 
12-month subscriptions for others. Since late August it has shown relevant ads to 
adults on budget plans, while companies can advertise for as little as about $8 a 
day. 
“There is incredible builder energy of people using it,” Altman said, adding that 
Indians are treating the chatbot as less of a novelty and more of a working tool. 
By comparison, the US, “at least on expressed preference, is unusually negative 
on AI relative to the rest of the world”, he said.

3. India has unusually large challenges that AI is unusually suited to solve 
Healthcare + education + credit + agriculture 
India’s disadvantages may paradoxically create its greatest opportunities.  
The current narrow focus on office productivity overlooks the vastly more 
important issue of access. India has large unmet needs where the marginal cost 
of conventional provision is high but the marginal cost of AI is potentially tiny. 
The capabilities of AI matter more, not less, in a country with thousands of 
languages, educational inconsistency, insufficient rural healthcare, low formal 
credit penetration and fragmented agriculture. The operating leverage from even 
small improvements is significant. 
AI-powered diagnostics and telemedicine can bring specialist-level healthcare to 
the two thirds of the population who currently lack it. That could be in the form 
of an AI model trained on local disease patterns diagnosing tuberculosis or 
diabetic retinopathy via a smartphone camera. 
In education, AI tutors delivering personalised instruction in regional languages 
could leapfrog the chronic shortage of qualified teachers, much as mobile 
payments leapfrogged branch banking. 
Credit where it’s due 
Two areas would be perhaps most transformative for growth. 
Firstly, AI-driven alternative credit scoring — using utility payments, mobile data, 
and behavioural signals — could finally unlock formal credit for the hundreds of 
millions with no credit history, boosting consumption and entrepreneurship at the 
base of the pyramid. 
Harvesting the benefits 
Secondly, AI in agriculture. India is roughly neck and neck with the US as the 
country with the most arable land, accounting for more than half of its land area, 
but crop yields are significantly below global standards. The average yield per 
hectare is about half of that in the US, with fragmented landholdings, low 
irrigation coverage and pests and diseases destroying an estimated 30 percent of 
crops every year. 
AI-enabled precision agriculture is beginning to enable farmers to better forecast 
yields; mechanise processes; and detect and eradicate pests, diseases and weeds. 
In one example, Swiss seed and pesticide company Syngenta signed up in June 
to work with Annam.AI, India’s first open-data agricultural ecosystem, to develop 
crop-health, pest-forecasting and heat-stress models. The initiative is set to bring 
free, real-time, personalised intelligence to India’s 150 million mostly 
smallholding farmers. Backers include the Indian government, the Indian Institute 
of Technology Ropar and Google.

4. An enormous technology workforce can implement AI 
Graduates + US immigration restrictions + engineers + services companies 
India has abundant AI talent and has been a net beneficiary of the clampdown in 
the US on foreign talent.  
With scale on its side, although only about 30 percent of its university-age 
population is enrolled in higher education, it still produces roughly 11 million 
graduates a year, equivalent to three entire US high-school graduating classes. 
Indian-born entrepreneurs have founded or co-founded 96 American unicorns, 
more than immigrants from any other country and more than twice as many as 
founders from China. Two of the four most valuable companies – Microsoft and 
Google’s parent Alphabet – are led by men who grew up and studied in India.  
Back home, India may have only one quarter of the number of top AI authors and 
inventors as the US but that still puts it above every other nation in a list compiled 
by the Stanford HAI Index (it does not include China).  
The pattern of outflow of those authors and inventors from India is almost the 
mirror image of the inflow to the US, with talent outflow peaking in 2022 but since 
falling dramatically as the inflow to the US has tapered off.

The trend looks likely to continue. Last month, the US announced that it would 
suspend eight tech companies, including Microsoft, Infosys and TCS, from a green 
card programme dominated by Indian employees that allows skilled foreign 
workers to gain permanent residency through their employment. 
5. Government is actively lowering barriers 
India AI + local models + infrastructure + regulatory approach 
Prime Minister Narendra Modi has become a vocal advocate for AI. He hosted the 
India AI Impact Summit in New Delhi in February attended by over 20 heads of 
state and a delegation of major global tech leaders. 
He joked with Bill Gates in an interview in 2024 that “in our country, we call our 
mother ‘Ai’. Now I say that when a child is born, he says ‘Ai’ as well as AI as children 
have become so advanced.” Since then, he has made appearances with 
schoolchildren encouraging them to use AI to hone their skills.

IndiaAI to build capacity and adoption 
His government launched the IndiaAI mission in 2024 to build the domestic AI 
ecosystem and drive adoption. It allocated more than 100bn rupees ($1.1bn) over 
five years on seven initiatives, including building out India’s compute capacity 
with more than 10,000 GPUs; strengthening datasets; boosting skills; and 
increasing access to finance.  
Earlier this month, it released three locally developed, lightweight foundation 
models funded under the initiative, which are tailored for Indian languages and 
applications, and can function on local devices like smartphones without an 
internet connection.  
They could point the way to India owning pockets of the value chain where local 
imperatives, such as linguistic diversity and cost and energy efficiency, drive it to 
build models that work domestically and have broader appeal and a competitive 
advantage in similar markets elsewhere. 
Meanwhile it has made strides to slash red tape, shifting from an emphasis on 
compliance to facilitation. It placed in Group A in the World Bank’s GovTech 
Maturity Index, assessing public sector digital adoption, last year. 
The big prize for India in the AI value chain would be to become the world’s 
alternative data centre hub to the US, currently home to about half of the total. 
It is also a strategic imperative to avoid becoming hostage to external providers. 
“Thriving AI ecosystem” hosting “the whole world’s data” 
“We invite the whole world’s data to reside in India,” Modi said in an interview in 
February. “India is laying the foundation for a thriving AI ecosystem by expanding 
computing power and data centre infra capacity.” 
It won’t happen overnight. India accounts for less than half of one percent of 
global GPU cluster performance, according to Epoch data. The US contains three 
quarters, China 14 percent and Europe 10 percent.  
India only had 153 data centres as of last year, according to Cloudscene data. 
That put it in 14th place globally, far behind not just the US but also lagging 
behind countries like Russia, Brazil and Mexico.

No room at the data centre 
As AI demand ramps up, colocation capacity is becoming scarce. Hyperscalers 
and neoclouds are increasingly reserving and partly funding capacity from 
specialist landlords before it opens.  
Vacancy rates have fallen from 13 percent in the first half of 2021 to a record low 
of just 2.8 percent in the first half of this year, even as supply has increased by 60 
percent in the same period, according to data from Jones Lang LaSalle (JLL). 
Nonetheless, government support is helping to hook in broader investment from 
national champions and global technology companies. India’s data centre 
capacity is set to triple from 1.6 GW to 6 GW by 2029, which would make it Asia’s 
fastest growing digital infrastructure market, according to JLL. 
Reliance Industries unveiled plans at the India AI Impact Summit in February to 
invest the equivalent of $110bn over seven years on AI-focused infrastructure, 
including gigawatt-scale data centres and a national edge-computing network. 
Adani Group said it will invest $100bn in renewable-powered, AI-ready data 
centres by 2035. The Tata Group and OpenAI agreed to develop 100 MW of AI 
infrastructure, with an option to scale capacity to 1GW. 
Putting the AI in Mumbai 
Growth will be nurtured by tax holidays, initiatives like Digital India, and 
commitments from the global hyperscalers and neoclouds, which will build about 
1.3 GW, or 30 percent of the 4.4 GW in new capacity, themselves. The remaining 
70 percent will be colocation. About a third of the buildout will be around Mumbai 
and a quarter in Hyderabad, JLL data show. 
Foreign technology companies announced $67.5bn of broad investment 
commitments in India in the run up to the AI Impact Summit, comprising $35bn 
from Amazon, $17.5bn from Microsoft and $15bn from Google, the latter to set 
up a 1 GW data centre in Andhra Pradesh, its largest AI hub outside the US. 
“Immense” opportunity, Google’s Pichai says 
“The AI opportunity in India is immense,” Google CEO Sundar Pichai, who grew up 
in Chennai, India, said last year. (Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella grew up 12 hours’ 
drive north in Hyderabad.) 
The sums pale by comparison with the historic $800bn that the US hyperscalers 
are investing in data centres this year, mainly at home, rising to over $1trn next 
year, but it’s a start. 
It may also be no bad thing to be a fast follower in a market with so many 
uncertainties, not least over the practical and economical life of the Nvidia GPU 
chips that account for more than half of the capital costs.

6. India can buy rather than invent in a competitive global market 
US/China competition + strategic autonomy + cheaper models + open source 
The US and China are the source of nearly all of the AI frontier models and chips, 
leaving the rest of the world as customers. That is a double-edged sword. 
On the one hand, it poses a significant challenge for countries like India. At best, 
it is economic. At worst, it is a threat to national security. 
The US, for example, has shown that it can effectively ban the use of a model 
without warning, highlighting the dangers of depending too much on one model 
– or one country.  
Others have been faster to react to the potential risks to sovereign autonomy. 
East Asia and Pacific countries passed 77 data localisation measures between 
2000 and 2024 and European and Central Asian ones passed 66, according to 
data from the Stanford HAI AI Index. South Asia was a comparative laggard at 24 
and North America barely there at three.

Bugs and crashes 
There is also the more banal risk of concentrating good old-fashioned bugs or 
crashes in a single point of failure, as the Microsoft Outlook outage demonstrated 
last week when it gave millions of office workers around the world an 
unexpectedly early start to the weekend. 
However, concentration of suppliers also presents an opportunity.  
Both the US and China have been ramping up their capabilities to compete in 
what US President Donald Trump has positioned as existential, saying “Whoever 
wins AI, wins”. National security concerns are used to help justify the historic 
expenditure in AI as a latter-day Apollo space programme but with no end in sight.

Best of both worlds 
Rising tensions may force other countries to pick a side and potentially suffer the 
consequences if the US were to cut off chip supplies or China were to stop 
shipping rare earth metals. 
Yet none will be in as strong a position as non-aligned India to extract the best 
terms from both. With an open hand to each side and a market too big to ignore, 
it may become ever more important as an AI customer to the top two nations and 
provider to the rest. 
And if a race to reach (still unproven) superintelligence forces both sides to build 
rapidly depreciating infrastructure overcapacity at great expense, all the better 
for India.  
Moreover, every dollar that the leading model developers in the US and China 
spend driving down the cost of intelligence potentially transfers a consumer 
surplus to India. The freedom to choose from an expanding pool of cheap, 
commoditised models is likely to benefit India more than most. 
Four ways it could go wrong 
Even with these tailwinds, success in AI is easier said than done.  
India will need to overcome significant hurdles to become a leading AI adopter, 
let alone a producer. 
1. AI attacks India’s biggest competitive advantage in services 
India spent 30 years creating a services economy around relatively inexpensive 
educated labour. It is the world’s biggest outsourcing hub by far, with estimates 
putting its share of global business-process sourcing at around 40 percent. 
Yet it is that form of labour that generative AI appears today to be most likely to 
automate, whether in customer support, junior coding, routine analytics, testing 
or back-office Global Capability Centre (GCC) work. The worst-case scenario is 
that AI does to Indian white-collar outsourcing what Chinese manufacturing did 
to Western factory employment. 
Service fears look overdone 
Our base case though is that these fears look overdone. 
Despite its high profile internationally, the technology industry only actually 
employs about 5.8 million people, less than 1 percent of the country’s workforce. 
Roughly 2.4 million of those people are employed by the more than 2,100 GCCs 
set up by multinational companies in India, according to Nasscom data.  
The industry does punch significantly above its weight economically. 
Telecommunications, computer and information services exports increased by 
about 13 percent to more than $200 billion, or about 5 percent of India’s $4trn 
economy, in fiscal 2026, government figures show. Business services swelled 16 
percent to about $124bn. More than half of India’s software services are destined 
for the US and most of the rest to the UK and Europe.

Hirings up, not down 
Critics say that AI will slow IT and GCC export growth. Even if it does not 
significantly disrupt the sector, existing jobs will need to be repurposed and 
entry-level hiring will dry up. 
But that’s not what the data say for now: active technology openings have 
reached a record 130,000 this month, up 27 percent year-on-year, according to 
data from staffing firm Xpheno. 
India’s apparent vulnerability may actually be an advantage. Its huge base of 
engineers, software developers and technology-services firms are already 
accustomed to adapting global technologies for corporate customers. 
They provide increasingly sophisticated services such as software development 
and analytics. Those are hard to automate and look likely to be enhanced not 
replaced by AI.  
Thanks to this background, it ranks at the top globally in both relative AI skill 
penetration and organisational support, according to data from the Stanford HAI 
AI Index 2026.

The bullish case from India’s tech trade association Nasscom, as argued in a new 
report, is that as enterprises embed AI into their operations, that will increase the 
demand for outsourced technology and business process management (BPM) 
services such as integration, orchestration and governance. There will, however, 
be a 12-18 month transition phase of muted growth as providers shift towards AI-
enabled, higher value offerings. 
Resilient labour market structure? 
In other words, the composition of the Indian labour force may make it ideally 
suited to be more resilient than most going into a period of profound disruption. 
The unemployment downside is limited, as almost 80 percent of jobs have low 
exposure, fewer than most other sizable economies, and just 8 percent have high 
exposure and low complementarity, according to World Bank calculations. 
Meanwhile there is healthy potential upside, with a relatively high 14 percent of 
roles showing high exposure and high complementarity.

“No evidence to support apocalyptic predictions” 
The evidence is supportive for now. A survey of about 650 IT firms by the Indian 
Council for Research on International Economic Relations found “no evidence to 
support apocalyptic predictions of large-scale losses following AI adoption. 
Instead, the results point to a fairly modest though broad-based moderation in 
hiring, concentrated primarily at the entry level.” 
Employment in Nifty IT Index companies has flatlined since early 2023, not long 
after the launch of ChatGPT at the end of November 2022, but that was after a 
post-Covid spike that tech companies around the world are still absorbing. 
Figure 12: Nifty IT Index aggregate employment (millions) 
Source: Bloomberg Finance LP, Deutsche Bank Research 
 
Indeed, occupations with the greatest exposure to AI, notably technical and 
analytically intensive roles, were experiencing the greatest increase in demand, 
and software divisions were seeing the least moderation in employment 
compared with other business functions, it found.  
Unless the scope of AI expands dramatically, “rising global demand for AI-
enabled goods and services should support significant net job creation in India’s 
IT sector,” the Council found. 
Furthermore, these skills may enable India to adopt AI faster than most 
economies and adapt it to local circumstances. Few other lower-middle-income 
countries can match its globally integrated technology industry, IT-skilled 
workforce and blossoming R&D ecosystem, let alone the scale of its domestic 
market. 
2. High usage may not translate into productivity 
Although India has a world-class tail in tech talent, its distribution and capacity 
for adoption are still uneven and concentrated in a few urban areas.  
In addition, scale does not automatically translate into job readiness, and usage 
is not the same thing as productivity.  
AI could either help solve India’s human capital challenges and inequalities – or 
magnify them.

Glass half-empty or half-full? 
The market is anything but saturated, with only 23 percent of surveyed formal 
firms using AI when taking sophistication into account, versus 43 percent in the 
US. Indeed, fewer than 40 percent of its unincorporated establishments even use 
the internet, World Bank data show. Population growth is greatest in poorer states 
with less access. 
Meanwhile the headline number of graduates overstates the pool of readily 
deployable skills. Measures vary but Mercer’s Graduate Skill Index 2025 found 
that only 43 percent of the more than one million students assessed were 
immediately job ready and 46 percent for AI and machine-learning roles. 
Of course, the glass is half-empty or half-full, depending how you look at it. The 
lower the adoption today, the greater the potential uplift from adoption 
tomorrow. 
But history shows that general purpose technologies only deliver when there is 
complementary organisational change.  
Less chat, more action 
Chatbot usage alone – in India or anywhere else – will not accelerate growth and 
development if firms do not upgrade to AI agents and redesign workflows; 
students use it for shortcuts rather than learning; workers improve output without 
wages rising; companies lack clean data; or institutions remain inefficient. 
It will take time to build the skills, systems and safeguards needed to make the 
most of it. With India being a particularly price-sensitive market, the business case 
will need to be crystal clear to justify shelling out on new systems and AI tokens 
instead of simply adding low-cost headcount.  
There is also the slow-burn risk that the pipeline of tomorrow’s senior engineers 
dries up if firms stop hiring juniors today. 
3. Power, compute and capital become binding constraints 
India’s physical infrastructure is already straining under the demands of AI.  
Rapid AI expansion is putting pressure on a web of local, coal-dependent power 
grids.  
While data centres need continuous, uninterrupted power, transmission line 
expansion is behind target, increasing the risk of regional supply shortages and 
local voltage drops. AI computing workloads create sudden, massive power 
swings, which put particular strain on equipment. 
The Ministry of Power recently nearly doubled its projected additional electricity 
load from AI data centres to more than 26 GW by 2031-2032, about 7 percent of 
total peak power demand. 
Critical global bottleneck 
However, India is also making progress here, even as US and European 
policymakers increasingly fret about how they will overcome this critical 
bottleneck.

India has rapidly grown overall capacity over the past three decades to generate 
2,000 TWh in 2024, a slower pace than China but far outpacing the US and 
European Union. 
Figure 13: Electricity net generation (billion kWh, 1980-2024)

It has become the world’s second-fastest growing renewable energy market and 
the second-largest manufacturer of solar photovoltaic (PV) modules, according 
to a new report from the International Energy Agency. 
Boosted by state incentives to use green energy, its renewable power capacity 
has almost quadrupled over the past decade to exceed 300 GW this year, helping 
to reduce its heavy dependence on imported fossil fuels. Even so, data centers 
need vast amounts of uninterrupted electricity, meaning intermittent renewable 
energy cannot fill the gap entirely.  
Investments depend on imports 
The country also reportedly plans to invest almost $100bn in electricity capex 
through 2032 to help prevent compute bottlenecks. Overall its return on invested 
capital for AI compute and grid technology is a top-tier 21 percent, according to 
Bloomberg estimates. 
Having said this, Bloomberg estimates that India’s AI infrastructure investment 
next year will only be about 0.5 percent of GDP and the domestic benefits will be 
constrained by the fact that it largely depends on imports.  
The country imports the vast majority of printed circuit boards (PCBs) as well as 
AI-server racks, power-distribution units, cooling systems and cables.  
Risk of costs, delays and overcapacity 
Like other AI buildouts worldwide, costs could spiral well before any payout, with 
larger projects taking three to five years before becoming operational, not to 
mention potential delays from global shortages of components such as 
transformers, switchgear and cooling equipment.  
Conversely, there is the risk of overcapacity by the end of the decade, where 
building boom suddenly leaves cloud service providers having to slash prices in a 
buyers’ market.

Capital challenges 
India still lacks some of the financial heft to be able to build out its own AI 
capacity at anything like the rates of the US, China or Europe. 
Private investment in AI in India tripled to slightly more than $4bn last year, 
according to Quid. That put it in the middle of a pack of smaller, wealthy countries 
but far behind China at $12bn, albeit much funding there comes from 
government guidance funds, let alone the US at more than $285bn.

4. Barriers mount, from regulation to public opposition 
Wider economic questions and structural reforms go beyond the scope of this 
report. 
However, in short, India’s AI ambitions will depend on it making further progress 
in cutting red tape and improving administrative efficiency. Last year, it ranked 
64th out of more than 200 economies for government effectiveness, according to 
the World Bank’s Worldwide Governance Indicators. 
The world’s biggest democracy is also not immune from the AI protests that have 
put the brakes on data centre developments around the world, from Virginia and 
Texas to Dublin and Amsterdam.  
Protests limited for now 
There have been protests in cities including Mumbai and Visakhapatnam against 
data centres planned by Amazon and Google over concerns including water, 
electricity use and environmental damage. 
Even so, these protests are still comparatively limited compared with the 
snowballing of opposition elsewhere, meaning India remains a potentially more 
favourable site for future development as other locations step back.  
The Indian government meanwhile has begun to pay attention. It sought to 
reassure consumers last month that it will introduce a national policy for data 
centres so that they do not end up having to pay more for electricity.  
Currently policy is fragmented among states and, critics argue, is not predictable 
or sustainable enough. 
Rolling out the green carpet 
Despite, or perhaps because of, this lack of a single policy, India is already one of 
the most welcoming countries if you want to build a data centre quickly with 
green energy. Media reports suggest that buildouts can take less than two years, 
compared with three to six years in the US. 
India stands out as one of the countries most positive about AI, with two thirds of 
respondents to an Ipsos survey last year saying they are excited.  
Yet they were also among the most concerned, with two thirds also admitting to 
being nervous, second only to Australians. Other excited countries, including 
China and South Korea, are relaxed by comparison. 
Protection over innovation 
And when it comes to the role of regulation, Indians are top of the table in calling 
for a defensive approach. Some 54 percent said in the Ipsos poll that the priority 
of AI regulation should be protecting industries, while 46 percent favoured 
advancing science, medicine and other fields. The global average was 41 percent 
for protection and 58 percent for advancement.

Investment snapshot: short-term AI diversifier and long-term 
beneficiary 
Short-term diversifier 
India’s equity market has minimal direct AI exposure, being dominated by banks, 
healthcare and IT services, rather than hardware or chips. That has cost it dearly 
this year, when it has been characterised as the “anti-AI trade”.  
Yet that weakness now looks like a strength in recent days as Indian stocks have 
bucked the global selloff. 
International investors disposed of a net $26bn of Indian equities in the first nine 
months of this year, according to Bloomberg data, in part because of the market 
mix, elevated valuations and high oil prices.  
India’s benchmark Nifty 50 Index tumbled 14 percent from the start of the year 
until the tech market peak on October 7, and the Nifty IT Index slumped 27 
percent, while their international counterparts surged. The S&P 500 Index gained 
14 percent, the Nasdaq was up 18 percent and the tech-heavy Korean KOSPI shot 
up 58 percent. 
The story has reversed dramatically over the past five weeks, with fears about the 
sustainability of the AI trade punishing AI darlings and the markets that they have 
come to dominate. 
At the same time, Indian stocks have risen on the rotation, as well as optimism 
about credit growth, robust earnings and healthy consumption.

Foreign investors have bought a record $9.5bn of Indian stocks this month. The 
Nifty 50 Index rose about 12 percent between October 7 and November 18, while 
the Nifty IT Index jumped 13 percent. By contrast, the S&P 500 was down 6.1 
percent, the Nasdaq slipped 11 percent and the Kospi gave up a quarter of its 
value. 
Near-zero correlation 
In fact, with differing domestic market drivers, currency movements and earnings 
cycles, the one-year correlation between the Nifty IT Index and the Nasdaq 
Composite Index, Kospi and S&P 500 was close to zero as of November 17, 
according to Bloomberg data. 
The one-month correlation with those indices is roughly -0.4 to -0.5 as they have 
moved inversely in recent weeks. The Nifty 50 shows modestly positive one-year 
correlation of +0.1 to +0.3 to the other indices, suggesting greater sensitivity to 
global risk appetite. 
Lower P/E ratios 
Despite the recent shift in valuations, the Nifty IT Index has much lower price-to-
earnings ratio of 19 times than the Nasdaq’s nearly 40 times earnings. (The 
difference is smaller for price to forward earnings.) 
However, overall multiples on Indian stocks are still relatively high, meaning that 
further gains would likely require an increase in earnings. The Nifty 50 trades at 
22 times earnings, albeit far below its peak of 34 five years ago, and S&P 500 at 
23. 
Risks to the “anti-AI” hedge 
And the trade comes with some risks. Weaker IT exports and dollar earnings could 
collide with a capex cycle requiring more imported goods, which could put 
pressure on the current account.

The “anti-AI” hedge may run out if sentiment around AI becomes consistently 
positive once more. Concerns about AI regulation in the US and about the 
consequences of geopolitical tensions between the great powers could subside, 
reducing the relative appeal of India as an alternative. 
Long-term beneficiary 
While India may be a hedge against AI overexposure in the short-term, this report 
makes the case for why it may benefit from just that exposure in the longer term. 
As the World Bank points out, it took about 80 years for steam power to reach 
lower-income countries, 40 years for electricity and 20 years for the internet. By 
contrast, it took a mere six months for ChatGPT to be used extensively. 
Large parts of AI can be built on existing digital infrastructure rather than having 
to wait on the tedious work of laying rails, putting up pylons and hooking up 
computers that slowed down previous rollouts.

You don’t need the latest AI model to enable millions of people and enterprises

don’t even need to invent it. 
It won’t be easy, but it is not impossible – and nowhere else has AI operating

Appendix: India’s love affair with tech: from Big B to Big AI 
India has had a love affair with technology for centuries. There is evidence that 
Indian mathematicians formulated the binary number system, calculated pi and 
defined zero as a real number.  
Nowadays, technology features prominently in popular culture, with legendary 
actor Amitabh Bachchan sporting an early pocket-sized computer in the 
Bollywood hit Kabhi Khushi Kabhie Gham back in 2001 and the dramatic 
denouement taking place in front of a mobile phone shop. 
Big B, as he is popularly known, has since been heavily involved in AI across digital 
avatars, voice cloning and film production, while his co-star Shah Rukh Khan, a 
screen icon in his own right, has made numerous AI investments and is currently
