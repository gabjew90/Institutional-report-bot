Over the past year, investment in the infrastructure  
needed to support AI's growing ambitions has accelerated 
sharply. Hyperscalers, chipmakers, and AI developers 
have committed unprecedented amounts of capital 
to expand computing capacity. Based on announced 
plans across the AI ecosystem, Apollo estimates that 
cumulative capital expenditures could reach ~$3.5 trillion 
through 2028.1
So far, the industry dynamics have been encouraging. 
Demand for compute capacity continues to exceed 
available supply, GPU rental prices are rising, and some 
infrastructure providers are reporting capital payback 
periods of less than a year.2 Yet the enormous scale of 
investment raises an important question: How much 
revenue will AI ultimately need to generate to justify 
all this spending?
Our analysis suggests the answer is approximately 
$1.5 trillion to $2 trillion in annualized AI revenue by 
the end of 2028, roughly 5-7 times today's level. By 
2030, that figure could reach $3 trillion to $5 trillion, 
depending on how much additional infrastructure  
is deployed.3 
The exact amount of AI revenue needed depends 
on how much AI compute actually gets deployed 
and the margins required within each layer of the AI 
ecosystem. Still, our analysis suggests that AI revenue 
will need to reach the trillions of dollars in the 
coming years, and reaching those levels will require 
AI to penetrate much larger markets and deliver 
measurable economic value across industries.

Ultimately, this revenue is anticipated to largely come 
from end users at the application layer: businesses and 
consumers paying for AI-powered products and services, 
including consumer subscriptions and enterprise AI 
applications. Today, end-user AI revenue is already growing 
at an extraordinary pace. Annualized AI application revenue 
reached approximately $200 billion in the second quarter 
of 2026, up from just $11 billion in early 2024. By September, 
that figure had climbed to approximately $276 billion, 
representing roughly 3.75-fold annual growth since 2023.8
That growth is impressive by almost any historical 
standard. Yet it also underscores how much further the 
industry needs to go. To reach our estimated $1.5 trillion 
to $2 trillion revenue requirement by the end of 2028, 
AI revenue would need to grow approximately 2.3 to 
2.6 times annually over the next two years (Exhibit 2). 
Recent growth rates suggest that trajectory is achievable, 
although maintaining rapid growth becomes increasingly 
difficult as the revenue base expands.

What Does It Take to Make the Math Work?
The economics behind AI infrastructure are relatively 
straightforward. Building and operating computing capacity 
is expensive, and every participant in the AI ecosystem 
eventually needs to generate sufficient revenue to cover 
those costs and earn an acceptable return.
Consider a single gigawatt (GW) of AI computing capacity.4 
Based on Apollo's estimates, the annual cost of operating 
that capacity, including depreciation and operating 
expenses, is approximately $7 billion.5 But the economics 
extend beyond the physical infrastructure. Hyperscalers 
providing compute capacity, companies developing 
foundation models, and businesses delivering AI-powered 
applications each need to earn a margin on their activities, 
at least in the long run.6 
Assuming margins across these layers, we estimate each GW 
of deployed AI capacity ultimately requires approximately 
$20 billion in annual end-user revenue to support the 
economics of the ecosystem (Exhibit 1). With total deployed 
AI capacity potentially reaching roughly 100 GW by the end 
of 2028, the revenue required to support the buildout could 
reach approximately $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion annually.7

Of course, this calculus assumes that projected 
infrastructure spending materializes on schedule. Power 
constraints, chip shortages, construction delays, and 
restrictive policies in the aftermath of the midterms could 
slow deployment. Importantly, capital markets can provide 
another natural check on overinvestment. If AI revenues 
disappoint, lower valuations and higher financing costs 
could slow the pace of infrastructure spending, potentially 
reducing the revenue ultimately needed to justify the 
buildout. This feedback mechanism could help prevent 
excess capacity and mitigate the risk of an AI investment 
bubble. Anthropic’s S-19 highlights a related risk, warning 
that slower revenue growth could constrain investment 
in compute and model development, weakening its 
competitive position and putting further pressure on future 
growth. If these pressures persist, they could weigh on 
capital spending plans for the second half of 2027 and 2028.
Companies also retain some flexibility to stage investment 
as demand develops. Anthropic, for example, describes 
securing data center capacity early while “deferring larger 
accelerator investments until our specific compute needs are 
more certain.” A slower buildout would, all else equal, lower 
the near-term revenue requirement, although contractual 
obligations on capacity already committed would remain. 
Finally, our model stacks a margin at each of the three layers, 
so greater vertical integration or more ecosystem subsidies 
would lower the revenue needed to justify the buildout. 
Where the Opportunity Lies
The scale of the opportunity becomes clearer when viewed 
against the markets AI could potentially address. The global 
cloud computing market generated approximately $419 
billion in revenue in 2025, while the online advertising and 
semiconductor markets were approximately $715 billion 
and $795 billion10, respectively. Even the global enterprise 
software market, estimated at nearly $1.2 trillion,11 is smaller 
than the $1.5-$2 trillion in annual AI revenue our model 
suggests could be needed by the end of 2028 (Exhibit 3).
AI’s potential, however, extends well beyond traditional 
technology spending. Its economic value likely lies in 
helping people work more productively and enabling 
businesses to expand what they can deliver. With global 
labor income estimated at approximately $58 trillion 
annually,12 even modest productivity improvements could 
generate substantial additional economic value.

Exhibit 3: Putting AI’s Revenue Ambitions in Perspective
AI could also make new products and services economically 
viable, expanding demand and creating new categories 
of work over time. For AI providers, earning a share of the 
incremental value their technology helps create could 
support a path toward the annual revenue our model 
suggests may be needed. 
Beyond labor productivity, several other sources of demand 
could help support AI's revenue ambitions. Enterprise IT 
budgets offer significant room for growth: According to a 
May 2026 McKinsey client survey,13 the median enterprise 
allocates approximately 7% of its IT budget to AI, compared 
with roughly 23% among AI-native organizations. Consumer 
spending presents another opportunity, as AI-powered 
applications compete for a share of the hundreds of 
billions of dollars spent annually on digital advertising 
and subscriptions. AI could also expand existing markets, 
including pharmaceuticals, cybersecurity, and defense 
technology, by enabling new products and services. 
No single market is likely to generate trillions of dollars 
in AI revenue on its own, but together they represent a 
substantial opportunity for growth.
The math is demanding. But AI has something few 
other technology cycles have had: the potential to 
capture spending from markets that extend far beyond 
technology itself.
In our view, the question is not whether AI can translate 
those opportunities into sustainable revenue at scale. We 
believe AI revenue will likely continue to increase rapidly 
over the next few years as it penetrates more use cases and 
models continue to improve.
The question instead is whether AI revenue can increase 
quickly enough to support the infrastructure buildout. The 
nature of the buildout could help mitigate the downside 
risk: Capacity is built in stages, and the majority of the 
cost sits in accelerators and IT equipment that can be 
deferred, allowing spending to adjust if necessary.
The industry has demonstrated its ability to attract 
capital and build infrastructure. The next phase will be 
the translation of that investment into a broadening 
revenue base.
