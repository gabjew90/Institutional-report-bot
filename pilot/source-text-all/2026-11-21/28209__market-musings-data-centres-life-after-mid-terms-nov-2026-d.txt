Market Musings 11/26
Data centres:  
life after mid-terms
Data centres and affordability were in the spotlight at the US mid-term elections, with many 
governors, senators, and House candidates making the two issues focal points of their campaigns. 
This focus has a direct impact on new data centre build-out, and an impact on the listed US  
utility companies that are positioned for the step up in electricity demand growth. After the  
Democratic sweep, we don’t think the data centre build-out is over. Instead it gets more  
nuanced – which helps active investors. 
Affordability & data centres
The central question raised during the election is who will pay for this data centre build-out, as households are already facing 
higher bills. According to EIA data1, average US residential electricity prices are expected to rise 10.5% between 2024 and 2026, 
almost double the 5.8% increase in consumer prices. As bills climb, some residents have become increasingly unhappy about data 
centres being built near their neighbourhoods. Welcome to NIMBY – not in my backyard.

Texas: a case for  
sensible regulation
Texas illustrates how sensible regulation could 
look. Congratulations to Governor-elect Gina 
Hinojosa. One of her campaign promises was  
a moratorium on data centres. We see three 
categories of data centres:
              Already operational 
              Under construction 
              Planned 
The moratorium applies mostly to the third 
category. This map shows their locations: 
How to sweeten the deal? Money talks.
Utility companies need to keep their residential customers happy – these are the voters that can change governors, that appoint 
regulatory commissioners. One tool utilities can use is to have a pricing framework for charging one price to residential customers, 
and another to large load customers (data centres, industrial and commercial clients). Some utilities under our coverage go so far 
as to use the data centre customers to subsidise the electricity cost for residential customers. This additional charge for larger 
customers is designed to pay for additional infrastructure and electricity needed to serve them rather than shifting those costs  
to residential consumers.
In our stock selection we focus on listed utility companies that already have this framework in place, either through self-designed 
tariffs for data centre customers or tariffs mandated by their state. In the map above, those areas with the most pushback 
(the red dots), are the areas where it is more material to look for this pricing framework. As an extra step, we then overlay our 
proprietary affordability analysis – which considers how material electricity bills are as a percentage of income in each service 
area, as well as the rate of increase3. This helps assess whether the pricing framework goes far enough. Together these inputs 
inform our valuation work. 
The moratorium doesn’t necessarily mean a full stop. In order for these data centres to move beyond the planning phase,  
they need to disclose how much electricity and water they will use, and from which supplier. This seems like a sensible regulatory 
requirement to us, and we believe this helps electric utility companies, whose name needs to be added to the planning request, 
get more control over tariffs and prices paid by different customers. Without the named utility, the planned data centre doesn’t 
make it off the PowerPoint slide.

Investment implications
We don’t believe this is the moment where politics ended the AI theme. We do believe it marks the start of more sensible 
regulation and a further shift in power from data centres towards utilities. At the same time, a more nuanced build-out 
creates opportunities for bottom-up stock pickers to find alpha.
