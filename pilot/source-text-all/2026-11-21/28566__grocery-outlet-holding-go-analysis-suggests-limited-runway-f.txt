Given GO recently lowered its unit growth rate again and remains 
focused on existing markets while pulling back from new markets, 
we analyze the potential new store opportunity set and evaluate 
implications for long-term growth. All in, we see a limited runway for 
new store growth for GO, while costs could continue to increase, 
pressuring unit economics. As such, we continue to view GO as 
overvalued relative to peers.  
Key Takeaways 
 
 
 
Amid soft comp trends (+0.5% last quarter), the closure of 36 
underperforming stores earlier this year (largely in the East Coast), 
and the strategic review underway for its Southeastern assets (UGO, 
which it acquired in 2024), GO’s new store openings are focused on 
infill opportunities in the near-term. Thus, we partnered with GS 
Data Works to evaluate the potential new store runway for GO’s 
existing markets, which are predominately across the West Coast 
(~85%). Assuming 10 stores per million people as the base case 
(similar to some of its more mature markets), we see an opportunity 
for an additional 162 new stores (vs its 561 store base as of 3Q26), 
noting its initial target of 4.8k locations across the US at the time of 
its IPO in 2019. The majority of these locations sit within areas with 
an average household income of $100-110k, vs the company’s 
national median of ~89k. Additionally, we note the majority of the 
opportunity sits within large, well-established markets, which likely 
carry higher upfront and operating costs, while cannibalization 
could trend up over time with densification, which could weigh on 
unit economics over time.

We also see risks to unit economics given tight supply across retail real estate, which has 
been detailed in this note by GS REIT analyst Caitlin Burrows, highlighting limited new 
retail supply and strip center starts tracking below historical levels. With 3Q results, GO 
lowered its new store growth to +3.5% per year, from +6% (which previously was a 
reduction from +10% in 2025), likely underscoring the increasing challenge in finding the 
right locations.  
Assuming GO maintains its new unit growth of +3.5% per year, we see a runway of ~7 
years based on our analysis, with increased risk of cannibalization should the company 
decide to expand past that. Even assuming further comp improvement towards a more 
normalized +LSD range, we would need to see evidence that the brand can gain traction 
in new markets before underwriting a larger store base opportunity or potential 
acceleration to the new store growth rate.  
At a +LSD unit growth rate with slightly positive comp, a bull case multiple for GO could 
track closer to dollar stores (NTM P/E for 16.1x for DG and 17.8x for DLTR), but we still 
think a discount makes sense given GO’s higher variable cost structure which limits 
flow-through from better comps, plus the dollar stores have more upside potential 
through each’s discretionary mix (basket and margins), along with a stronger track 
record of execution, including better comp performance recently. On the flip side, we 
see pure play grocers as a floor valuation (NTM P/E of 12.5x for KR and 11.4x for SFM), 
and view a 1-2x turn higher as reasonable given the +LSD store growth and recent comp 
improvement. As such, we remain comfortable with our valuation framework reflecting 
bear/base/bull cases of 12x/14x/16x, and view GO’s current NTM P/E multiple of 18.1x 
as overvalued, especially in light of our analysis suggesting limited runway for unit 
growth.

We are Sell-rated on GO with a 12-month price target of $8 based on our risk-reward

better-than-expected trade down benefits; incremental sales due to strength in 
consumer spending; regional macroeconomic strength on the West Coast (~60% of 
stores are in CA); more closeout opportunities supporting margin expansion as well as a 
better customer experience; fewer price investments; faster than expected new store 
growth; faster sales ramp of newly opened stores

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring
