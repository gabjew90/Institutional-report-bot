 
Treasury Sells First 9-Day Bill Since July 2008 As 
Month-End Cash Gets Tight 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, NOV 19, 2026 - 08:55 PM 
For most of the past two decades, the US Treasury could plan its cash needs more than a week 
and a half in advance. Today it couldn't. 
At 1pm ET, Treasury sold $15 billion of 9-day cash management bills (CMBs) at a high rate of 
3.910% and a 3.97x bid-to-cover, issuing on Nov 24 and maturing on Dec 3. That's the first 
9-day bill since July 21, 2008, when Treasury sold $9 billion at 1.565%. That was ten days after 
IndyMac was seized, and Lehman had just two months left to live! 
In the entire TreasuryDirect auction record back to 1980, there have been just 11 such auctions.

Today's bill wasn't a one-off either. Four weeks ago, on Oct 22, Treasury sold $25 billion of 
10-day CMBs to bridge a few days when, per John Comiskey of Reverse Engineering Finance, 
the TGA was about to dip below the minimum set by Treasury's cash balance policy (even with 
nearly $1 trillion in the account). Today's bill does the same job across month-end, settling the 
Tuesday before Thanksgiving and maturing just after the Dec 1 outflows. Treasury flagged it in 
 

 
the Nov 4 refunding statement, where it said it "anticipates potentially issuing a short-dated 
CMB" around the end of November. 
Two in a month may not sound like much, but ultra-short CMBs have become rare: six of the 
past ten years had none at all. 
 
Demand was fine, at least. Indirects took 45.8% of today's bill (vs. 18.3% in October), and 
dealers only 52.0% (vs. 77.6%). So the issue is why Treasury keeps needing these, and as we 
laid out just before this post, the answer runs through the Fed. Goldman's rates team expected 
reserve management purchases to restart this month at $10 billion. Instead, the New York Fed 
scheduled no reserve purchases between Nov 16 and Dec 11, extending a pause that began in 
mid-August. 
 

 
 
So for the next few weeks, the Fed isn't adding reserves, Treasury is refilling the TGA ahead of 
its projected late-November peak of about $1.025 trillion, and the gap at month-end gets 
plugged with a bill that lives for nine days. Nothing says "ample reserves" like borrowing over 
Thanksgiving to get through December 1. 
Nine Days At A Time 
And this is the easy part. The government was $685.1 billion below the $41.1 trillion debt limit 
as of Nov 13 (a gap that is shrinking very fast), and both Goldman and JPMorgan expect it to 
bind around February. After that, the TGA drains and later has to be rebuilt in a hurry, which in 
2019 and 2025 is exactly when funding markets wobbled. Goldman's warning is that "Reserves 
are currently at more of a cusp on the demand curve." 
Back in January 2025, we were already a little skeptical of the reassurances: 
Almost two years later it's still mostly easy, until the cash runs short for a week and Treasury 
reaches for a tool it last needed in the summer of 2008.  
Maybe that's all today was. More likely, it's a preview of a bumpier 2027, one with more of these 
stopgaps and a Fed that, as we argued earlier, ends up rediscovering bill purchases the hard 
 

 
way. When the world's biggest borrower starts planning its cash nine days at a time, the "ample" 
in ample reserves is doing a lot of work.
