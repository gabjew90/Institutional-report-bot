As we head into 2027, AI unsurprisingly remains the dominant 
investment story. But the debate has shifted markedly in the 
months since our midyear global outlook.
Investors are asking less about the scale of the opportunity and 
more about its sustainability, as tech groups turn to cash to 
fund the build-out of data centres on a vast scale.
So far, the economic backdrop remains supportive. Global 
growth has proved resilient, supported by these multi-trillion-
dollar capex plans, while labour markets remain 
relatively steady.
Mounting questions
Yet there is a growing sense that markets are on shaky ground. 
Investors must navigate the collision of higher interest rates 
with simmering geopolitical tensions, particularly in the Middle 
East, and turbulent domestic politics.
Add mounting questions over fiscal sustainability, brought into 
sharp relief recently by France, and the potential for a dramatic 
readjustment in the investment landscape is clear.
Within this outlook, we assess how these issues might play 
out and affect our clients’ assets. Key takeaways include:
•	
Policy responses and energy constraints may dominate 
AI’s next phase, especially in light of the US midterms
•	
Higher-for-longer rates are becoming a truly global story
•	
Decarbonisation holds the key to energy security, 
in our view
We also discuss the risks to global growth, how retail 
investors are reshaping markets, the next chapters for defined 
contribution and defined benefit pensions, and five possible 
investment surprises in 2027.
Are we in a bubble?
One of the most frequent questions I receive from clients is 
whether we are in an AI-driven bubble.
Readers of our blog will know I have long viewed bubbles 
through frameworks developed by Kindleberger and Minsky. 
Our own Bubble Index builds on that thinking, looking across 
five areas: credit expansion, macroeconomic conditions and 
disruptive change, speculative behaviour, valuations, and 
patterns in asset-price performance.
The first two flash warning signs today. But the other three 
suggest to me that we are not yet in bubble territory.
Sentiment indicators do not suggest widespread investor 
euphoria, most hyperscalers have de-rated – in some cases 
significantly – and non-profitable tech companies have 
recently underperformed.
AI and the liberation of the investor
Another, related question I hear frequently is: how are we 
using AI within L&G’s Asset Management business?
In short, the technology is transforming how we invest – 
freeing us from gathering and processing information, allowing 
us to spend more time on testing, challenging and applying 
judgement.
To make the most of this opportunity, and manage the inherent 
risks, we have rolled out a Global Toolkit for Investments 
(GTI)*, which comprises three interconnected “factories”.
•	
Data: to make information easier to find and use
•	
Software: to build tools around real investment problems
•	
Knowledge: to connect our research and insights
Interestingly, we are seeing a blurring of traditional roles: 
investment professionals build technology around their own 
expertise, while software engineers working alongside them 
acquire a deeper understanding of markets.
These capabilities are ultimately aimed at helping us make 
better-informed decisions on behalf of our clients – and to 
chart a course through the complex market environment we 
explore over the coming pages.

Still standing 
Global growth has shrugged off an energy shock. 
Higher rates, fiscal strains and questions over the AI 
boom are likely to test its resilience.
Considering the surge in energy prices, global growth has 
proven remarkably resilient in 2026.
This reflects in part AI investment spending, from which the US 
is benefiting the most and remains a relative winner – with real 
GDP growth expected to be just above 2%.
Indeed, consensus growth forecasts for 2027 have changed 
little in recent months and anticipate a similar pace of growth 
to continue even as inflation and interest rates have 
ratcheted higher.
Europe is lagging, with real GDP unlikely to exceed much 
more than 1%. This is primarily due to its exposure to surging 
natural gas prices and lack of large-scale fiscal support to 
cushion the real income squeeze from rising utility bills.
China’s growth is on track to hit its target, helped by 
government support and despite property weakness. 
Unemployment rates across the world generally appear 
steady, at close to full employment, with very limited signs of 
AI disruption at this stage.
What could go wrong?
Growth risks are relatively balanced. Further rises in energy 
prices could contribute to a broader inflation shock. The French 
presidential election, whose first round is in April 2027, adds 
to sovereign debt concerns. These could be amplified by an 
energy price shock and spillover into the European periphery.

phase of AI
Artificial intelligence has spent the past several years 
primarily as a technology and investment story. In 2027 
it may be defined more by energy constraints, policy 
responses and geopolitics.

As the AI boom reaches its fifth year, investors continue to 
wrestle with whether AI warrants cloud providers’ colossal 
capital spending and whether current valuations reflect 
realistic revenue projections or excessive optimism. Even 
five years on, the pace of AI-related improvement shows no 
signs of slowing. As AI breaks out from niche appeal to wider 
distribution, its implications are being felt more broadly. This 
means the next phase of the AI cycle may be determined 
increasingly by forces outside the technology itself. AI’s 
capabilities may continue to advance rapidly, but its trajectory 
will increasingly depend on whether the industry can secure 
sufficient energy, retain political support, navigate geopolitical 
competition and finance its expanding physical footprint.

Much of the boom has played out in infrastructure. Chipmakers 
and power providers have seen substantial increases in 
volumes and prices as firms race to deploy servers that sustain 
AI models. Epoch.AI estimates that AI-driven computation 
volume has expanded roughly five-million-fold since 2022. 
AI-assisted programming is becoming the norm, and agents 
capable of multi-step digital tasks are proliferating. 
Cloud demand, as opposed to AI demand, still underpins 
infrastructure demand, making up almost two-thirds of 
demand and growing at a 23% compound annual growth rate 
that jumps to 40% + when AI-related demand is included, 
according to Epoch.AI. Sceptics have expressed fears of 
overbuilding, but usage has surged, and constrained supply 
cannot keep up with cloud, let alone AI, demand. Physical 
buildout of infrastructure takes time: typically five-10 years 
to bring new infrastructure online. As a result, infrastructure 
vacancy rates are at record lows.

Hitting limits
AI deployment is rubbing up against other constraints as 
well. Power, both supply and transmission, is proving to be a 
large bottleneck. As data centres expand in size, the volume 
of power they need has swelled, straining the grid’s ability 
to supply them. Financial conditions are also tightening 
worldwide. Higher interest rates are forcing companies into 
more elaborate financing structures to sustain their capital 
spending. 
The buildout is generating political and geopolitical frictions 
too, as public anxiety about AI’s consequences rises. 
Many localities within the US are opposed to the ongoing 
deployment and are seeking to halt construction where they 
can and make approval for new infrastructure more difficult, 
raising project costs and timelines. Democrat victories in 
key gubernatorial midterm contests raise the prospect of 
political brakes being applied to at least some data centre 
development. However, at the same time, support for the 
AI infrastructure buildout crosses party lines. The previous 
Democratic administration also sought to accelerate 
construction, while Democrat-led states have actively attracted 
hyperscaler investment. The political debate is more about 
how to build than whether to build. Both political parties 
in the US want their country to lead China in the AI race, 
creating pressure to accelerate chip production, data-centre 
construction, permitting and access to energy, though they 
can’t ignore the domestic costs of that race. 
At the same time, AI capabilities now extend to levels 
previously only available to national governments. Frontier AI 
models can now be used to orchestrate mass cyber-attacks 
and could be used in the future to design novel pathogens or 
weapon systems. The key question is where and when these 
frictions and anxieties begin to constrain the AI investment 
cycle, alter the supply outlook and change the compensation 
required for AI-linked risk. As constraints mount, pressure for 
AI to produce substantial financial and societal returns could 
impede its rapid growth.

Nothing lasts forever?
Ultimately, the future of the AI buildout becomes a financing 
question. It increasingly depends on public bonds, securitised 
debt, private capital and hybrid structures. This year has shown 
that with risk-free yields elevated and AI deals receiving a 
choppier reception, capital is not unlimited. Equity and IPO 
funding can strengthen the cushion beneath creditors, but 
safety, political and return-on-investment concerns could 
undermine valuations. 
These constraints underlie the risk of a wider unwind in 
both market sentiment and financing, similar to the end of 
the dot-com bubble. Indeed, research from the Bank for 
International Settlements finds the current boom could 
produce overcapacity, as happened during the server and fibre 
optic cable boom in the early 2000s. However, back then, 
new infrastructure was built speculatively. Today, most new 
infrastructure is being built to order, with demand exceeding 
supply and data centre development yields and capitalisation 
rates remaining strong and stable over the last several years 
despite cost inflation, according to Green Street and 
Citi Research. 
Such cycles can run for years before correcting, and an end 
would require that supply starts outstripping demand. For 
now, supply constraints look likely to only further exacerbate 
the strength of infrastructure demand. In our view, ultimately 
there will be winners and losers within the AI ecosystem and 
a correction in spending or valuations would not invalidate the 
technology. From our perspective, AI success does not depend 
on any single company or application.

Portfolio impacts
For portfolios, this backdrop argues against treating AI as 
a binary bet. The technology may continue to diffuse even 
as returns migrate between model developers, chipmakers, 
cloud providers, power companies and users. The next phase 
of the buildout is likely to bring more differentiation among 
firms, particularly around exposure to political or infrastructure 
constraints. 
Against this backdrop, we believe it’s important to distinguish 
exposure to adoption from exposure to today’s valuations, to 
favour firms with strong balance sheets and demonstrable 
revenue, and to diversify across both infrastructure providers 
and businesses using AI to raise productivity. The boom 
may have further to run, but the eventual winners will not 
necessarily be those spending the most today.

Higher for longer is taking on a new meaning. While investors 
debate how far the US Federal Reserve’s renewed hiking 
cycle could extend, rising rates and global bond yields are 
also increasing pressure elsewhere, particularly where debt-
servicing costs and fiscal dynamics are already challenging.
From the path of US monetary policy and sovereign debt stress 
in France to growing differentiation across emerging markets, 
the question is increasingly where higher yields could expose 
vulnerabilities, and what that means for global fixed 
income investors.
Is the Fed pricing too much, too soon?
Coming into the year, the market was pricing around 50bps of 
cumulative easing for 2026. The Middle East conflict, the scale 
of AI capex and stalled progress towards the 2% inflation 
target led the market to price in almost 100bps of hikes over 
the next 12 months.

From monetary policy to sovereign stress
But central bank policy is only one part of the higher-for-
longer debate. The rise in global bond yields is also putting 
pressure on sovereigns, particularly where fiscal challenges 
were already evident.
France is a case in point. Its long-term fiscal challenges are 
well known, but the latest widening in French government 
bond spreads was not triggered by a new political shock. 
Rather, rising global bond yields and higher debt-servicing 
costs have made an existing fiscal challenge harder to resolve. 
At current yields and across a wide range of plausible growth 
outcomes, the fiscal tightening required merely to stabilise 
debt-to-GDP is exceptionally large.
The question is increasingly whether France remains a chronic 
fiscal challenge that markets can absorb or evolves into a 
more acute concern. Higher rates, weaker growth or renewed 
energy price pressures could accelerate the move from a future 
problem to a present one.
And this is not simply a French issue. Other sovereign spreads 
have also started to rise, while, as the second-largest economy 
in Europe, France is too big to ignore. Recent European 
Central Bank (ECB) communications have taken a more dovish 
tone, suggesting it sees the balance shifting from a pure 
inflation shock towards increasing growth risks. This is likely 
to temper European yield rises relative to others should the 
move continue, although it does not guarantee lower yields. 
Sovereign stress could also make incremental ECB rate hikes 
increasingly difficult, reinforcing the potential for divergence 
between European and US rates.
For our active global aggregate portfolios, this environment 
highlights the potential benefits of bringing rates and credit 
decisions together within an integrated investment process. 
Our approach draws on multiple sources of alpha across 
rates and credit, with allocation, duration and individual 
opportunities considered together alongside their correlations 
at the portfolio level.
The asymmetric risks around France have also led us to 
introduce French curve flattener trades against Germany, 
reflecting our view that France itself may become a constraint 
on how far European yields can rise. These positions may 
change and are not recommendations for individual investors.
Emerging markets: stronger foundations, greater 
differentiation
Higher global yields are also creating a more challenging 
backdrop for emerging markets (EM), but the recent repricing 
looks more like a global rotation towards quality than an EM-
specific deterioration.
Higher and more volatile US rates, a stronger dollar, elevated 
oil prices and weaker risk sentiment have driven EM spreads 
wider. Yet market access remains broadly intact, while foreign-
exchange reserves remain close to record levels and sovereign 
rating momentum continues to be positive. At the same time, 
the capacity to absorb shocks varies considerably across 
countries as higher financing and energy costs test fiscal and 
external positions.
Importantly, quality does not simply mean investment grade. 
In EM, we think about quality in fundamental terms: credible 
policymaking, improving fiscal and external balances, adequate 
reserves and a sustainable path for debt. That can include 
high-yield sovereigns where reforms are strengthening 
creditworthiness and creating positive ratings momentum.
For our EMD portfolios, stronger fundamentals therefore do not 
mean buying EM indiscriminately. In our view, the opportunity 
increasingly falls into three themes: reform credibility, stronger 
external buffers and looking beyond sovereign debt. 
Ultimately, the opportunity in EM is increasingly about 
relative value rather than broad beta. As markets mature, 
the investment decision is shifting from simply which country 
to own towards which combination of issuer, sector, capital 
structure and maturity offers the most attractive risk-adjusted 
expression of that view.
Drivers of rising US 10-year Treasury yields since 
end-February
Yet while inflationary pressures remain stronger than 
policymakers would like, we see evidence that core inflation 
has probably peaked. And while inflation expectations have 
moved modestly higher since the Iran war began, real yields 
have accounted for more than 90% of the increase in 10-year 
yields (see overleaf chart).
The impact of higher rates and energy prices on the economy 
is also still to filter through. This time, the consumer does not 
have tax cuts or as high a saving rate to fall back on to sustain 
consumption growth.
Following September’s 25bps hike, the market is pricing in 
around a further 80bps of tightening over the next 12 months. 
We believe risks are skewed towards the Fed delivering less 
than this, with the tightening cycle likely to be limited to 
reversing the insurance cuts of 2025. Consequently, we have 
turned more constructive on the short and intermediate parts 
of the US yield curve.

reducing dependence on imported fossil fuels. However, 
outcomes will depend on infrastructure investment, 
system flexibility, technology availability and supply-chain 
resilience.
Energy security

Infrastructure Research Manager

Head of Climate Solutions 
Geopolitical shocks have brought energy security back to the 
forefront of government and investor attention. 
Traditionally, energy security has meant securing access 
to globally traded hydrocarbons. But disruption to oil 
and liquefied natural gas (LNG) markets has exposed the 
vulnerabilities inherent in that model, particularly for countries 
reliant on imported fuels.
Regional conflicts and logistical bottlenecks can rapidly 
feed through into higher prices, electricity bills and pressure 
on energy-intensive industries. Replacing one overseas 
hydrocarbon supplier with another may diversify the source 
of supply, but it does not remove exposure to volatile 
international markets. 
In an increasingly fragmented global economy, energy security 
therefore requires a broader consideration of how energy is 
produced, transported and consumed. 
The limitations of gas 
Natural gas has played an important role in this debate. Over 
the past decade, it has often been characterised as a “bridge 
fuel”, offering a dependable source of power as economies 
build lower-carbon energy systems. 
Yet recent geopolitical events have also demonstrated 
the exposure of international gas markets to severe price 
movements. As a result, we’ve seen that countries not 
dependent on natural gas (Spain, for example) have shown 
greater energy resilience than those countries exposed to 
gas price swings. Gas may remain important in balancing 
power systems during the transition, but continued reliance on 
internationally traded hydrocarbons leaves economies exposed 
to many of the geopolitical risks they are seeking to manage.
Decarbonisation potentially offers a structural response. 
Renewable energy resources can be developed domestically 
and, once the infrastructure is in place, do not require a 
continuous supply of imported fuel. Energy security and 
decarbonisation can therefore increasingly reinforce 
one another. 
Electrification changes the demand side
The transition is also taking place on the demand side of the 
energy system. Electric vehicle sales across Europe and Asia 
are growing strongly,1 contributing to a longer-term shift away 
from liquid fuels in transport. More broadly, electrification 
offers economies a way to reduce hydrocarbon dependence 
across areas including transport, heating and industry. 
The scale of that ambition is significant. The European 
Commission’s Electrification Action Plan sets an indicative 
ambition to increase electricity’s share of final energy 
consumption from 23% today to 46% by 2040.2 That 
potentially expands the investment opportunity beyond 
clean-energy generation to the technologies and infrastructure 
required to accommodate growing electricity demand. 
AI and data centres add another dimension. The growth of 
AI could strengthen the link between electricity use and GDP 
because AI infrastructure relies heavily on electricity, which 
can be generated from a diverse mix of domestic, renewable 
and nuclear sources, reducing economies’ reliance on imported 
oil and gas and potentially improving energy security. At 
the same time, AI may improve efficiency across grids, 
manufacturing and logistics. Growing computing requirements 
also strengthen the need for reliable power systems and the 
infrastructure needed to support them.

Investing across the transition
For investors, this argues for considering energy security as a 
system rather than looking for a single technological winner. 
The opportunity spans renewable generation such as wind 
and solar, equipment manufacturers, low-carbon hydrogen, 
electricity networks and smart-grid technologies, alongside 
battery storage and the wider battery value chain. 
The precise opportunities differ across public and private 
markets. Within infrastructure, for example, the benefits of 
changing power prices depend on factors including location, 
technology, contracting arrangements and generation profile. 
Public-market strategies can potentially provide diversified 
exposure across different parts of the clean-energy and 
electrification value chains. 
Ultimately, the investment case extends beyond any individual 
geopolitical crisis or commodity-price cycle. Electrification is 
increasing the importance of electricity to transport, industry 
and the digital economy, while renewable penetration is 
creating additional requirements for storage and networks. 
For investors seeking exposure to energy security, a diversified 
approach across these interconnected areas may therefore 
offer a way to participate in the longer-term reshaping of the 
global energy system.

From generating power to building the system around it
As electricity becomes more important to economies, the 
investment requirements of the transition are evolving. 
Renewable generation has represented an early response 
to both decarbonisation and energy-security concerns. As 
renewable penetration grows, however, variability in when 
electricity is generated can contribute to power-price volatility.
That creates a subsequent requirement for batteries, networks 
and other technologies capable of balancing supply and 
demand. Storage can move electricity between periods of 
abundance and scarcity, while grid investment can help 
manage congestion and connect new sources of generation 
and demand. Digitalised grids, automation, advanced sensors 
and demand-response technologies can provide further 
flexibility. 
This progression from generation towards storage, networks 
and flexibility broadens the investable opportunity. It also 
reflects the ultimate objective of energy security: a power 
system that can meet growing electricity demand reliably 
while reducing dependence on imported fossil fuels.
There are new dependencies to consider. Clean-energy supply 
chains are themselves concentrated, with much of the world 
dependent on China for solar panels, batteries and power-
electronics components. Diversifying manufacturing and supply 
chains is therefore becoming another element of 
energy security.

If accumulation has defined the development of Defined 
Contribution (DC) to date, retirement income could define its 
next chapter. 
In 2027, we expect greater focus on how policymakers, 
pension providers and the investment industry can help 
members turn a lifetime of saving into a sustainable 
retirement income.
Retirement is not simply accumulation in reverse. Once 
withdrawals begin, the sequencing of investment returns 
becomes critical. Market falls early in retirement, combined 
with withdrawals (especially if they are large), may have a 
lasting impact on the value of a member’s savings and the 
income they can sustain. Inflation and longevity add further 
uncertainty, making the investment challenge, and indeed 
member understanding, increasingly complex.
During accumulation, DC savers can generally tolerate 
more short-term volatility in pursuit of long-term growth. In 
decumulation, the balance shifts: investments must continue 
generating returns while supporting withdrawals and retaining 
sufficient liquidity. 
The challenge is to develop solutions that help members turn 
their pension savings into an income in a way that is easier 
to understand and manage. For some, the priority may be 
generating a sustainable income over the longer term while 
keeping part of their pension invested. For others, it may be 
accessing their savings more flexibly over a shorter period – 
but not cashing out in one go potentially due to tax reasons – 
to bridge the gap until their State Pension or a defined benefit 
pension begins. 
These needs call for different approaches. Someone seeking 
an income over several decades may require a different 
investment and withdrawal framework from someone funding 
a shorter transition into retirement. Solutions should therefore 
provide flexibility where it is needed, while helping members 
understand the implications of their choices and how their 
income may need to adapt over time. 
International DC markets illustrate the challenge of designing 
for retirement. In the US, people predominantly withdraw from 
their account or leave it invested, although we are starting to 
see some in-plan retirement-income solutions emerging. In 
Australia, the Retirement Income Covenant came into force in 
July 2022 to encourage trustees to provide better solutions 
for retirees; while there has been some innovation, it is widely 
accepted that evidencing tangible improvements for retirees 
has been slow.
While there are interesting things to glean from other markets, 
we have to recognise the regulation, tax regime and indeed 
member behaviours and aspirations are not necessarily the 
same.
In the UK, the direction of travel is towards a broader 
retirement-income ecosystem, where drawdown, annuities and 
newer approaches offer different combinations of flexibility, 
certainty and investment exposure.
The accumulation era demonstrated the power of defaults, 
diversification and scale. The next phase of DC investment 
must retain those strengths while connecting fund design more 
closely to the income members ultimately need.

US Treasury market reckoning
Since the middle of 2026, the market has started to fret about 
the French public debt trajectory. Despite very similar debt 
dynamics, the US government has escaped similar attention. 
At prevailing interest rates, the size of the fiscal adjustment

Quiet fragmentation returns to credit markets
Tight headline spreads are masking an increasingly 
differentiated credit landscape, a development that may 
catch markets off guard in 2027. Issuer dispersion has risen 
meaningfully since the start of 2026, even as aggregate 
spreads remain historically tight. Over the last 25 years, 
within-sector issuer dispersion has accounted for almost one-

value without requiring a directional call on overall spreads.

AI, it would be easy to assume AI is also driving a rise in 
dispersion. Yet, even excluding US hyperscalers, relative issuer 
dispersion remains elevated. Indeed, dispersion now sits in 
the top quintile across Communications, Autos, Retailers and 
Financials ex-Banks, suggesting that the real surprise for 2027 
may be how much hidden complexity lies beneath the surface 
of credit markets.

Complexity, not credit risk, commands the premium 
The surprise in investment-grade private credit may be that 
some of its potentially more attractive opportunities come not 
from taking more credit risk, but from being paid to navigate 
complexity and move quickly. We believe a financing with 
bespoke collateral, uncertain funding dates or an accelerated 
timetable can offer compelling relative value because few 
investors have the sourcing reach, underwriting resources and 
governance needed to execute it.
This opportunity is not confined to unfamiliar borrowers or 
thinly held names. Mars* financed its Kellanova* acquisition 
with a $26 billion Rule 144A/Regulation S bond alongside 
a $1 billion private placement, selecting different markets 
for different parts of the same capital need.3 Chip financing 
provides a more complex example. Equipment may be 
delivered through multiple draws over a shifting schedule, 
potentially making upfront public issuance inefficient and 
creating negative carry before the assets begin generating 
revenue. Private investors can commit around those delivery 
dates; once funded and standardised, the notes may become 
DTC-eligible and resalable to qualified institutional buyers 
under Rule 144A.4
The counterintuitive point is that investors may not need to 
move further down the credit spectrum to potentially find 
attractive relative value. Instead, the premium may come 
from accepting complexity that others are unable or unwilling 
to navigate. Capturing that premium requires the ability 
to compare relative value across financing channels and 
determine whether additional spread adequately compensates 
for structural and execution risk.

In real estate, income resilience defies rate volatility 
Real estate investors can perhaps spend too long trying to 
forecast market interest rates and hence property yields. These 
are important drivers of required and realised returns, but 
cannot be controlled. 
What can be controlled, and is fairly unique to real estate, is 
income creation. Despite persistent rate pressure, it’s possible 
to capture income by choosing markets where supply is tight 
and demand is predictable in addition to the sectors most 
likely to be in demand across cycles. Income creation can be 
driven by asset-level risk and operation. For instance, providing 
excellent occupier experience while preserving operational 
income is important; but improvements in technology, skills 
and experience will differentiate asset performance. With 
such income factors controlled, as far as is possible, we can 
have conviction in relative performance leaving room for 
‘uncontrollable upside’ from any positive surprises on rates.

Leverage and market dynamics
Retail investors routinely express short-term views through 
single-stock leveraged ETFs and one-day options, which can 
magnify movements in the underlying asset. In the first half of 
2026, assets in leveraged ETFs hit more than 4.5 times their 
2020 level, reaching a record $218 billion.10 
High volumes in derivatives can also interact with trading in 
the underlying shares. South Korean semiconductor company 
SK Hynix* has become the poster child of how these flows 
may contribute to sharper price movements.11 
Whether speculative retail activity makes boom-and-bust 
cycles more likely remains open to debate. The use of 
leveraged products and one-day options attracts attention, 
but leverage is not new. More broadly, debt financing for AI 
infrastructure may be supporting parts of the current market 
upswing, while incentives within financial markets can 
encourage the build-up of leverage. 
These factors do not establish that retail activity is driving the 
cycle, but they add to the channels through which moves can 
be amplified.
What’s behind rising retail participation? 
One force behind this is the growing financialisation of 
household wealth. Equity holdings recently overtook housing 
as a share of household wealth in the US for the first time in 
15 years.7 
This change isn’t confined to the wealthy: since 2010, equity 
and mutual fund ownership among the bottom 50% of US 
households has increased by over 570%, a bigger rise than 
across every other wealth cohort.8 
Technology has also lowered barriers to participation. 
Alongside zero-commission trading, platforms such as 
Robinhood* offer intuitive interfaces that make it easier to buy 
and sell investments.
Speculative interest has also migrated, with weaker crypto 
asset performance redirecting attention towards equities. 
Meanwhile, social-media communities such as WallStreetBets 
have accelerated the spread of investment narratives around 
meme stocks.9

High rates and 
endgame optionality
Stronger funding positions are giving UK and US defined 
benefit plans greater freedom to choose their destination 
– and more reason to build portfolios that keep multiple 
paths open.
DB
Christopher Wroblewski 
Head of Solutions Strategy, L&G - Asset 
Management, America
Robert Pace 
Head of Delegated Portfolio Design
Higher government bond yields have improved funding levels 
globally and expanded the choices available to defined benefit 
(DB) plans. In the UK, gilt yields have continued to rise, while 
in the US, higher Treasury yields and market movements 
have also helped many plans move into surplus. Across both 
markets, this improved position is widening endgame options 
and bringing the management of liabilities and surplus into 
sharper focus.
Keeping multiple paths open
Some plans are moving towards buyout or pension risk 
transfer; others are running on for now, preserving flexibility 
while weighing their options. Longer-term run-on is being 
considered by many UK plans, particularly larger ones, where 
scale can support the investment capabilities and governance 
needed. In the US, hibernation similarly involves retaining 
the plan and managing liabilities as they run off, while 
considering how surplus assets might support growth, benefit 
enhancements or other strategic objectives.
These choices need not require an immediate commitment to 
a single destination. For plans balancing continued operation 
with a potential future transfer, today’s rate environment 
may offer an opportunity to strengthen liability management 
against a more conservative valuation basis and move closer to 
the cost of eventual risk transfer.
Building a portfolio around benefit payments
Across these paths, cashflow-driven investing (CDI) is 
becoming more important. Meeting benefit payments from 
investment income and maturing assets can reduce reliance on 
asset sales and provide greater certainty over future cashflows.
Yet higher government bond yields do not necessarily 
make corporate bonds attractive: tight spreads leave 
less compensation for taking credit risk, complicating 
decisions about where to invest. Many plans are therefore 
supplementing traditional sovereign and corporate bond 
portfolios with a wider range of cashflow-generating assets, 
such as securitised credit and private credit, as well as other 
alternatives. These can broaden the opportunity set, but their 
cashflows need careful assessment alongside credit quality, 
liquidity and structural complexity.
Building an effective CDI strategy is not only about deploying 
new capital; it also means deciding what role existing illiquid 
holdings should play in endgame portfolios. Plans can fold 
expected distributions into their cashflow management plans, 
or decide whether, when and how to sell, weighing secondary-
market pricing against ongoing cashflow requirements and 
evolving endgame objectives.
Managing liabilities and surplus together
For plans considering longer-term run-on in the UK or 
hibernation in the US, the broad balance-sheet framework 
used by life insurers potentially offers a useful point of 
reference. It distinguishes between a portfolio focused on 
liability coverage and benefit payments, and a surplus portfolio 
invested according to the sponsor’s objectives and risk 
tolerance.
The liability-focused portfolio can combine a broad range of 
assets designed to match cashflows, help deliver an attractive 
yield, and seek to protect against interest-rate and inflation 
risk. The surplus portfolio can then provide a buffer focused on 
preserving capital and maintaining resilience, while seeking to 
generate growth over time.
Crucially, these are complementary parts of one coherent, 
liability-aware portfolio, drawing on the full spectrum of 
investment opportunities available. Investments need to 
work together to meet benefit payments, protect hard-won 
surpluses, withstand market setbacks and maintain agility. For 
UK and US plans alike, the appropriate balance will depend on 
whether a plan is preparing for buyout or pension risk transfer, 
keeping its options open, or investing for long-term run-on in 
the UK or hibernation in the US.
