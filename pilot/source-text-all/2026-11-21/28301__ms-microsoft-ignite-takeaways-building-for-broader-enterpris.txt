Full-Stack AI Opportunity Reinforced by Management Discussions. 
Weparticipated in a Q&A with CEO Satya Nadella and hosted a conversation with 
CFO Amy Hood at Ignite in San Francisco. Across both discussions, and echoed in 
commentary from keynote sessions, management emphasized the importance of 
building a diversified AI business spanning infrastructure, platforms, and 
applications, with capacity allocation reflecting that broader opportunity. 
Importantly, Microsoft continues to deliberately preserve flexibility to serve first-
party products, Foundry, and enterprise customers rather than maximizing long-
term GPUaaS commitments. Product announcements further supported our 
Enterprise AI Flywheel thesis, connecting enterprise context, model orchestration, 
and application distribution more tightly across the stack. While consumption 
outside coding remains early, we see multiple opportunities to broaden 
monetization alongside continued improvements in infrastructure and model 
efficiency. Microsoft can be the trusted layer around whichever models an 
enterprise uses: it brings the data and context, keeps corporate intelligence in the 
customer’s control, governs security, access and spend across every agent, and 
routes each task to the best-priced model, increasingly its own task-specific MAI 
models. We come away from Ignite incrementally more confident in Microsoft as 
one of the few enterprise AI companies already generating returns on its AI 
investments. Remain OW.

• Neocloud Strategy Is Unchanged – Owned Capacity Remains Preferred. 
While recent headlines around 3P capacity have drawn some attention, 
management was clear that Microsoft’s approach to neoclouds has not 
changed. The preference remains to build its own facilities, followed by long-
term leases where Microsoft controls the fit-out, networking, and 
equipment. These arrangements provide greater control over efficiency, 
deployment, and the timing of equipment investment, which represents the 
majority of data-center spending. Management also attributed some deal 
cancellations to suppliers falling behind on service-level commitments, 
rather than a broader change in sourcing strategy. We therefore interpret the 
commentary as continued selectivity around execution and economics, rather 
than evidence of a change in underlying capacity requirements / strategy. We 
also note that we also don't see this as a comment on any change in supply / 
demand in the market.
• Efficiency Gains Extend Beyond GPUs and Apply to New Capacity As Well. 
Management highlighted CPU utilization and storage as important sources of 
the capacity improvements Microsoft has achieved across its fleet. While the 
external debate often centers on GPU availability, serving AI workloads 
requires efficiency across the broader infrastructure stack. Importantly, new 
deployments also incorporate these improved efficiency assumptions, 
extending the benefits beyond the existing fleet. Management sees further 
opportunities ahead, although these gains should not be treated as an 
entirely separate source of upside to capacity plans. We continue to see 
infrastructure and software efficiency as supporting both additional revenue 
capacity and a lower cost of delivery.
• Product-Level Margin Improvement Remains the Priority, With Cloud Mix 
Still a Headwind. Management distinguished between improving the 
underlying individual gross margins of Azure and M365 (the target) and 
managing the reported Microsoft Cloud gross-margin percentage (an 
outcome, not the focus). The objective is to improve economics within each 
product, while allowing businesses with different margin profiles to grow 
according to demand. Consequently, faster Azure growth can continue to 
dilute the blended Cloud margin even as product-level margins improve. This 
remains an important distinction for investors assessing the AI investment 
cycle: reported mix pressure does not necessarily imply deterioration in the 
economics of individual services. We continue to focus on utilization, 
infrastructure efficiency, model costs, and higher-value platform 
consumption as the principal drivers of improvement.
• Multi-Model Differentiation Is About Outcomes and Cost, Not Choice 
Alone. Management emphasized the system surrounding the model – 
context, harness, memory, and trust, stressing selecting the most effective 
model at the lowest appropriate cost. Microsoft sees a continuing role for 
frontier models to address frontier problems, complemented by specialized 
MAI and open-weight models where those provide better economics. This 
supports our view that greater model availability increases the importance of 
the platform connecting models with enterprise data and workflows.

• Copilot Consumption Expands the Opportunity, but Adoption Outside 
Coding Remains Early. Management described subscriptions and usage-
based billing as complementary components of the business model, 
providing a recurring base alongside incremental spending as customers 
undertake more work. At the CEO session, management spoke to one large 
customer whose Cowork spending exceeded its spending on Microsoft’s 
other products combined, though CFO meetings cautioned that this example 
is not representative of the broader customer base and that the products 
and sales motion remain too new to establish a reliable adoption timeline. 
Coding workflows continue to demonstrate strong adoption, while broader 
knowledge-work consumption is developing. We see the customer example 
as illustrating the potential spending opportunity, with the pace of broader 
adoption still an important variable.
• Cost Control and Visibility Are Important to Broader Enterprise Adoption. 
Management drew parallels with the early Azure transition, when customers 
similarly sought greater control and predictability over spending. Alongside 
security, customers need to understand the value delivered and retain the 
ability to manage consumption. Agent 365 Business Analytics and Cost 
Management address these requirements by connecting spending with 
adoption and outcomes, alongside budgeting policies and alerts. We view 
these capabilities as supporting broader deployment by helping customers 
manage variable usage, although the revenue benefit will depend on how 
quickly customers expand production workloads.
• Custom Silicon Complements a Diversified Fleet. Management positioned 
Maia within a broader sourcing strategy, drawing parallels with Microsoft’s 
evolution from Intel toward AMD and Cobalt. The objective is to capture 
innovation across suppliers while developing first-party capabilities where 
Microsoft can improve performance and economics. This supports our view 
of custom silicon as an additional efficiency lever rather than an assumption 
that workloads migrate entirely onto Microsoft hardware. The Cobalt 200 
announcement provides a further example of this approach, with Microsoft 
citing up to 50% better performance vs. Cobalt 100 for its agentic AI 
workflows.
