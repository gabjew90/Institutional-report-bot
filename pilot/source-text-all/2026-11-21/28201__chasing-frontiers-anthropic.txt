Wait, What Does Anthropic Do, Again? 
Anthropic is in the business of building and selling intelligence. As such, the firm’s core strategy can be 
distilled into two equally important and reinforcing parts.  
 
First, the firm devotes considerable resources to building, or training, large language models. Then, 
once these models are trained, Anthropic sells access to them using a combination of its application 
programming interface platform and first-party applications such as Claude Code and Cowork. 
 
The mutual reinforcement of both training and subsequent monetization happens elegantly. By building 
new models that unlock new capabilities and workflows, Anthropic is able to drive adoption and 
monetization of these models. The dollar inflows from monetization are then channeled back into 
training the next series of models.

While we understand investor concerns around potential commodification of LLMs that could make 
Anthropic’s business and financial outlook bleak, we view the future differently. We have a positive 
outlook on Anthropic’s ability to remain at the frontier of artificial intelligence model research, 
demonstrate capability leadership in key domains, and monetize these capabilities by selling primarily 
to enterprise customers. 
 
Three key factors underpin our view that Anthropic will continue to gain wallet share among enterprise 
customers: 
 
First, we believe AI adoption across enterprises remains in early stages, with enterprises increasingly 
looking to adopt AI to improve efficiencies and drive revenue. In our view, this low-penetration, high-
appetite dynamic creates a secular tailwind that will boost Anthropic’s top line. 
 
Second, we believe enterprises are willing to pay a premium for frontier-level intelligence. As model 
capabilities unlock new workflows, they expand Anthropic’s total addressable market, with each new 
capability driving more dollars to frontier intelligence. This preference for “best in class” is not isolated 
to AI models; we have more than two decades of enterprises demonstrating this behavior across a 
variety of software domains. 
 
Third, we see Anthropic increasingly creating industry applications in key domains, thereby attracting 
sticky software spending onto its platform. We have seen early evidence of this in software engineering 
with Claude Code, and we expect the firm to develop similar first-party applications in other highly 
monetized domains such as legal research, financial research, healthcare, cybersecurity, and design.

Has Anthropic Built an Economic Moat? 
We assign Anthropic a Morningstar Economic Moat Rating of narrow, primarily owing to the firm’s 
intangible assets and cost advantage. We also see emerging customer switching costs and an indirect 
network effect further bolstering the firm’s competitive advantage. 
 
While building large language models is compute and resource-intensive, the firm’s research velocity, 
enterprise pricing power as well as penetration, and signed multiyear compute deals give us confidence 
that Anthropic will be able to generate excess returns on invested capital over the next 10 years. 
 
Intangible Assets 
We view Anthropic’s intangible assets—frontier research know-how, recursive model development, and 
safety alignment training—as its primary moat source. On the firm’s AI model research, we have seen 
Anthropic sustain capability leadership in key domains, especially in agentic coding and cybersecurity. 
This leadership has translated directly into pricing power, with Anthropic’s models priced at a premium 
to other frontier labs, while rapidly scaling the number of enterprise customers. 
 
The firm’s edge in model research is also aided by recursive model development, a process in which 
training and testing of new models is aided by Claude, with Claude assisting in running experiments, 
generating and processing training data, and creating evaluations and safety audits for the upcoming 
models. 
 
Finally, while the firm has been investing aggressively in model development, it has made safety and 
alignment a key attribute of its model development strategy. This messaging resonates well with 
companies in a heavily regulated industry such as financial services and healthcare. The firm’s model 
capability, as well as its focus on safety, has allowed it to gain adoption within these regulated 
industries, with the firm’s customers including nine of the 10 largest pharmaceutical companies, the 10 
largest banks, nine of the 10, and eight of the 10 largest healthcare companies.

Cost Advantage 
We believe that Anthropic holds a structural cost advantage relative to other model providers as well as 
enterprises seeking to self-host open-weight models. We view this cost advantage as being driven by 
four mechanisms. 
 
First, training costs increase materially with each generation. As a result, a challenger entering today 
must not merely match the leaders’ current spending but also have some novel method to “catch up” on 
all the accumulated learnings these model companies have made by iterating through various model 
architectures and methodologies. We see these high training costs and the growing gulf between 
existing AI labs and new entrants as contributing factors to the whittling down of the model field—from 
many dozens of model companies claiming to be leading-edge back in 2023 to just a handful at the 
frontier in 2026. 
 
Second, Anthropic’s multi-chip hardware portfolio allows it to dynamically route workloads to the 
lowest-cost silicon. This orchestration layer, which Anthropic has built in-house, is incredibly hard to 
replicate, with Anthropic as the only frontier AI lab that has a fungible compute fleet across four 
hardware configurations (AWS Trainium, Google TPUs, Nvidia GPUs, and AMD GPUs).

Third, the firm’s emphasis on algorithmic and methodological improvements across training and 
inference has allowed it to grow its effective output of a given amount of compute. These “compute 
multipliers,” which are increasingly being discovered with the help of Claude, enable the firm to both 
train more capable models and also be able to serve existing models more efficiently. 
 
Fourth, due to Anthropic’s scale, it can procure compute from cloud infrastructure and hardware 
providers at attractive prices, not available to smaller peers. Anthropic can commit to multiyear terms 
and offer to buy gigawatt-scale capacity from infrastructure providers, so it can capture better unit 
economics than smaller peers that can only commit to smaller deals.

Switching Costs 
While we do not see switching costs as a fully formed moat source for Anthropic as of yet, we are seeing 
increased evidence that Anthropic is entrenching itself in enterprise workflows that are hard to replace. 
We see two key ways in which Anthropic is building switching costs for its model ecosystem. One, the 
firm is pushing for multi-product adoption, with more than half of its large customers—defined as those 
spending more than $100,000 annually—using both the Claude platform and a first-party app such as 
Claude Code or Cowork. Across our software coverage, we see multiproduct adoption as stickier than 
adoption of a single product. 
 
Two, as enterprises increasingly shift to agentic workflows, we will see a rise in long-running, persistent 
agents. When deploying these long-running agents, enterprises need to set up intricate system prompts, 
tool-use definitions, and error-handling processes, all of which allow the agent to work autonomously 
for extended periods of time. This additional tooling is calibrated to specific models, with Anthropic’s 
forward-deployed engineers also playing a key role in building this tooling around Claude. Swapping a 
model out requires building the tooling again, which is both time- and cost-intensive. 
 
Despite the above points, we do not attribute a switching cost moat source to Anthropic as more than 
80% of the firm’s revenue is consumption-based, with customers able to reduce consumption in 
relatively short order. Also, for customers not leveraging more than a single product and building long-
running agents, switching away from an existing model provider is relatively simple. 
 
Network Effect 
Finally, we see early signs of a network effect forming for Anthropic in two distinct ways. One, when 
prosumers/free users use products such as Claude Code, they generate multistep agentic workflows 
that are tracked and monitored by Anthropic. This includes tool calls, which code edits they accept, 
failure modes, as well as user feedback. All this data can then enable Anthropic to improve/fine-tune its 
upcoming models. 
 
Two, by looking at usage patterns and telemetry data, Anthropic can gauge which workflows end-users 
are building on top of its existing models. This data can allow Anthropic to build first-party products in 
key areas, with Claude Code and Claude Design as two good early examples. As Anthropic expands the 
roster of pre-built tools, with product discovery being aided by usage of its models, it can expand its 
target market by unlocking new personas, thereby attracting more users to its platforms. 
 
What Are the Key Risks and Uncertainties Facing Anthropic? 
We assign Anthropic a Morningstar Uncertainty Rating of Very High. This rating reflects our view on the 
wide range of financial outcomes for a business investing heavily in large language models, a nascent 
technology. We see three clear drivers of financial uncertainty for Anthropic. 
 
One, we assume that training larger, more-complex models continues to expand their respective 
capabilities, especially in domains that are monetizable. While this assumption makes sense to many,

including us, there is uncertainty worth unpacking here. For the better part of the past five years, model 
improvements have operated under a framework known as “scaling laws.” Put simply, the framework 
posits that an AI model’s intelligence scales predictably as you increase the volume of its training data, 
its post-training alignment, and the time it spends pondering over a specific problem. 
 
We have seen these scaling laws work elegantly over the past few years, enabling models to get better 
and more economically useful as AI labs, such as Anthropic, worked to improve each scaling axis. We’d 
note, however, that the scaling laws framework is an observation, not an actual physical law, that could 
stop holding true at any point in the future. If scaling laws do stop “working,” model improvements may 
become incremental, not transformational, thereby potentially shrinking Anthropic’s total addressable 
market and giving competitors the opportunity to catch up with Anthropic. 
 
Two, there are myriad ways in which enterprise adoption curves could inflect in the coming years. A 
good historical analogy is the difference in adoption of radios, swift and widespread, versus washing 
machines, slow and gradual.

The gulf between the two states of this future world—one where AI adoption is fast and one where it is 
slow—is substantial. According to our own estimates, the 2031 revenue differential between the two

Three, as part of its IPO documentation, Anthropic has made the developing capabilities of its AI models 
to improve themselves, a process known as recursive self-improvement, a clear part of the investment

We are seeing clear progress on this front for the firm. First, Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, now writes 
more than 90% of contributions to the firm’s codebase. Second, Claude now leads around a quarter of 
its model research tasks, up from around 1% in March of this year. Third, Anthropic’s amended 
prospectus claims that the firm’s internal estimates indicate that the firm may soon meet its own 
threshold for accelerated, AI-driven progress. We have some corroboration of this acceleration, with 
METR, a safety-focused third-party group that reviewed Anthropic’s internal data, estimating this month 
that Anthropic’s pace of AI progress has roughly doubled over the last year, with METR attributing 
loosely 80% of this acceleration to AI itself. 
 
While investors may dismiss these claims as dubious or overblown, we are seeing outside AI 
researchers take them seriously, and we do believe that they merit further attention. Outside AI 
researchers now see an increasing likelihood that AI can begin outperforming Anthropic’s best human 
researchers sometime in 2027, which would dramatically accelerate the firm’s product capability unlock 
and budget capture among enterprises. 
 
We flag this “fast takeoff” scenario, as it further widens the range of outcomes in both directions. On the 
upside, if the claims made by outside researchers as well as METR prove accurate and model 
improvements generalize beyond certain domains including coding, then our base case will likely prove 
far too conservative. We could see an argument that even our bull case, which values the firm’s equity at 
$3.4 trillion, would be too conservative under a true RSI loop scenario. 
 
On the downside, however, a misaligned “super AI researcher” may justify an industrywide pause, 
leading to slowdowns in both model capability improvements as well as enterprise AI adoption while 
raising the odds of regulation aimed at slowing frontier AI progress. 
 
How Is Anthropic’s Financial Condition? 
We view Anthropic’s financial position as healthy. The firm has more than $130 billion in cash and 
marketable securities, having burned through less than $10 billion in cash since 2023. 
 
While the business does have more than $850 billion in compute commitments, we’d remind investors 
that these commitments are structured over more than 10 years. With the firm’s existing cash balance, 
along with around $100 billion in expected IPO proceeds, it can cover its noncancelable compute

This balance sheet strength is a key reason why Anthropic, instead of throwing more compute toward 
revenue-generating inference demand, has dedicated the majority of its compute toward training better 
models. 
 
Longer term, we see these frontier AI labs building robust, free cash flow-generating businesses, with

Bulls Say 
Anthropic is at the forefront of technological innovation arguably as big as the Industrial Revolution, 
with knowledge work accounting for more than $20 trillion annually. This is orders of magnitude greater 
than the markets software firms operate in, with revenue set to ramp dramatically in 2027 and beyond. 
 
The firm’s focus on key domains including software engineering, cybersecurity, and natural sciences 
allows it to embed itself in high-volume, price-insensitive workflows. Growing token volumes have been 
paired with price increases, demonstrating legitimate pricing power for frontier models at each launch. 
 
As we highlighted above, bulls will also point to recursive self-improvement as a potential accelerant. 
With Claude already writing the bulk of Anthropic’s code and taking on a growing share of its model 
research, bulls see a path to AI-driven progress compounding on itself, which could unlock capabilities 
and enterprise budget capture well ahead of our base-case forecast. 
 
As AI becomes embedded across enterprise workflows, it may soon compete with labor budgets in 
addition to enterprise software and IT services budgets. Given the size of global labor budgets, even a 
modest shift of entry-level work toward AI could create a massive market opportunity for Anthropic.

Bears Say 
Frontier labs may not capture the bulk share of the AI market opportunity if non-frontier intelligence 
models become “good enough” for many enterprise customers, who may switch to cheaper open-weight 
and in-house model alternatives.  
 
While cloud providers, or CSPs, serve as key Anthropic suppliers, they are also competitors, with every 
interest in commodifying the intelligence layer to capture value in the cloud infrastructure layer. In the 
past, Anthropic has sought more compute capacity than its CSPs were willing to provide, and further 
constraints, or planned capacity that fails to come online on schedule, could limit Anthropic’s ability to 
scale and maintain its position at the frontier.  
 
While the vast majority of Anthropic’s revenue is based on consumption, which can be variable, the 
firm’s costs are mostly fixed. Put another way, if variable revenue doesn’t scale to cover fixed costs, the 
company could face financial distress. 
 
Is the Firm a Good Steward of Capital? 
We assign Anthropic an Exemplary Capital Allocation Rating based on its sound balance sheet, 
exceptional investments, and appropriate shareholder distributions. 
 
We view Anthropic’s financial position as healthy. The firm carries more than $130 billion in cash and 
marketable securities on its balance sheet, with the upcoming IPO likely adding another $100 billion to 
the firm’s cash pile.

While the firm does carry off-balance-sheet liabilities in the form of compute commitments, we see its 
cash pile, along with strong revenue generation, as more than strong enough to pay for the growing 
compute bills. 
 
The firm’s investment strategy has been simple. Nearly all of the firm’s reinvestment over the past few 
years has been dedicated to compute. The return on this investment has been not only capability

top line that is driven by enterprises leveraging its models. 
 
We expect the firm’s investments to be geared mostly toward compute, as the firm continues to train 
larger, more complex models that unlock more economically useful tasks, thereby driving further 
adoption and enterprise penetration. 
 
Finally, on shareholder distributions, the firm has not returned capital to shareholders through share 
buybacks or dividends. We won’t hold our breath for either capital return method to begin anytime soon, 
with Anthropic seeking to leverage every incremental dollar toward either building newer models or 
securing compute to serve them to customers. We see the firm’s focus on growth as appropriate given 
its stage in the company lifecycle. 
 
How Much Is Anthropic Worth? 
Our fair value estimate of $92 per share for Anthropic values its equity at $1.7 trillion, implying a 2027 
price/sales multiple of around 13 times. 
 
We believe Anthropic is well positioned to increase revenue rapidly, with our base-case forecast calling 
for a top-line compound annual growth rate of 47% over the next five years. We see three key drivers of 
this revenue growth. 
 
One, we expect the firm to drive deeper into existing accounts. The firm has almost 12,000 customers 
spending more than $100,000 a year, more than 1,900 customers spending $1 million annually, and 
more than 250 accounts spending more than $10 million each year. We see Anthropic expanding deeper 
into all of these accounts by moving enterprises from using simple, low-volume applications like Claude 
chat to integrating Claude into agentic infrastructure that all large enterprises are trying to build. As 
Claude takes over more automated, long-running tasks, we see a natural inflection upward in total 
spending on Claude by enterprise customers. 
 
To give some perspective on penetration, we’d note that out of Fortune 500 customers spending more 
than $100,000 on Anthropic’s models, their spending on Claude as a percent of their total expenditures 
was less than 0.1%. In other words, for every $1,000 a Fortune 500 client spends, only $1 currently goes 
to Anthropic. We see Anthropic being able to materially expand this number over time. 
 
Two, while the firm has impressive enterprise penetration, we’d note that new logo additions are far 
from over. The firm has more than 50% of the Fortune 500 as active customers. Put another way, there is

still almost 50% of the Fortune 500 that are not spending much, if at all, on Anthropic’s products. We’d 
imagine the penetration for Fortune 1000 and so forth is likely even lower. We view these customers,

Three, instead of trying to build an enterprise sales team directly, the firm’s strategic distribution

While we feel fairly confident in Anthropic’s ability to execute across these three verticals and drive 
sales growth, we think it is appropriate to also consider both bull and bear cases for the firm.

Our bull case forecast is driven primarily by rapid adoption of AI in large enterprises, as well as 
Anthropic gaining significant market share against other frontier labs, namely OpenAI and Google, as 
well as open-weight model providers. 
 
Our bull case captures rapid adoption, with Anthropic’s sales capturing 14% of total software and IT 
services budgets in five years. Layered on top of this rapid adoption is also an increase in Anthropic’s

Research Methodology for Valuing Companies 
 
Overview 
At the heart of our valuation system is a detailed projection of a company’s future cash flows, resulting from our analysts’ 
research. Analysts create custom industry and company assumptions to feed income statement, balance sheet, and capital 
investment assumptions into our globally standardized, proprietary discounted cash flow, or DCF, modeling templates. We use 
scenario analysis, in-depth competitive advantage analysis, and a variety of other analytical tools to augment this process. We 
think analyzing valuation through discounted cash flows presents a better lens for viewing cyclical companies, high-growth firms, 
businesses with finite lives (mines, for example), or companies expected to generate negative earnings over the next few years. 
That said, we don’t dismiss multiples altogether but rather use them as supporting cross-checks for our DCF-based fair value 
estimates. We also acknowledge that DCF models offer their own challenges (including a potential proliferation of estimated 
inputs and the possibility that the method may miss short-term market-price movements), but we believe these negatives are 
mitigated by deep analysis and our long-term approach.  
 
Morningstar’s Equity Research Group (“we,” “our”) believes that a company’s intrinsic worth results from the future cash flows it 
can generate. The Morningstar Rating for stocks identifies stocks trading at a discount or premium to their intrinsic worth—or fair 
value estimate in Morningstar terminology. Five-star stocks sell for the biggest risk-adjusted discount to their fair values, whereas 
1-star stocks trade at premiums to their intrinsic worth.  
 
Four key components drive the Morningstar rating:  
▸ our assessment of the firm’s economic moat.  
▸ our estimate of the stock’s fair value.  
▸ our uncertainty around that fair value estimate.  
▸ the current market price.  
 
This process ultimately culminates in our single-point star rating.  
 
Economic Moat 
The Morningstar Economic Moat Rating is a structural feature that Morningstar believes positions a firm to earn durable excess 
profits over a long period of time, with excess profits defined as returns on invested capital above our estimate of a firm’s cost of 
capital. The economic moat rating is not an indicator of the investment performance of the investment highlighted in this report. 
Narrow-moat companies are those that Morningstar believes are more likely than not to achieve normalized excess returns for at 
least the next 10 years. Wide-moat companies are those that Morningstar believes will earn excess returns for 10 years, with 
excess returns more likely than not to remain for at least 20 years. Firms without a moat, including those that have a substantial 
threat of value destruction-related risks related to environmental, social, and governance; industry disruption; financial health; or 
other idiosyncratic issues, are more susceptible to competition. Morningstar has identified five sources of economic moats: 
intangible assets, switching costs, network effect, cost advantage, and efficient scale. 
 
Fair Value Estimate 
Each stock’s fair value is estimated by using a proprietary discounted cash flow model, which assumes that the stock’s value is 
equal to the total of the free cash flows of the company is expected to generate in the future, discounted back to the present at the 
rate commensurate with the riskiness of the cash flows. As with any DCF model, the ending value is highly sensitive to 
Morningstar’s projections of future growth. 
 
Fair Value Uncertainty 
The Morningstar Uncertainty Rating represents the analysts’ ability to bound the estimated value of the shares in a company 
around the fair value estimate, based on the characteristics of the business underlying the stock, including operating and 
financial leverage, sales sensitivity to the overall economy, product concentration, pricing power, exposure to material ESG risks, 
and other company-specific factors. Based on these factors, analysts classify the stock into one of several uncertainty levels: Low, 
Medium, High, Very High, or Extreme. Our recommended margin of safety—the discount to fair value demanded before we’d 
recommend buying or selling the stock—widens as our uncertainty of the estimated value of the equity increases.

Morningstar Rating for Stocks 
The Morningstar Rating for Stocks is a forward-looking, analyst-driven measure of a stock’s current price relative to the analyst’s 
estimate of what the shares are worth. Stock star ratings indicate whether a stock, in the equity analyst’s educated opinion, is 
cheap, expensive, or fairly priced. To rate a stock, analysts estimate what they think it is worth (its “fair value”), using a detailed, 
long-term cash flow forecast for the company. A stock’s star rating depends on whether its current market price is above or below 
the fair value estimate. Those stocks trading at large discounts to their fair values receive the highest ratings (4 or 5 stars). Stocks 
trading at large premiums to their fair values receive lower ratings (1 or 2 stars). A 3-star rating means the current stock price is 
close to the analyst’s fair value estimate.
