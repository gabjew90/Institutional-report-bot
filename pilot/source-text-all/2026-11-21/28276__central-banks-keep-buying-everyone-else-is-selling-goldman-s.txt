 
Central Banks Keep Buying, Everyone Else Is 
Selling: Goldman's 2027 Gold Playbook 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 18, 2026 - 02:55 PM 
Gold's year-end blues got a little bluer this week. 
Spot bullion touched $3,830 in early trading, its lowest level since October 2025, as the dollar 
kept marching higher and traders braced for another Warsh rate hike next month (per Reuters). 
That's roughly $300 below where it traded just one month ago. 
And, right on cue, Goldman blinked again. Eleven days after the bank's commodity team led by 
Lina Thomas cut its end-2027 forecast to $4,800 from $5,400, Goldman's freshly minted 2027 
precious metals outlook, titled "It Is 2022 Again"(available to pro subs), trims it once more to 
$4,650/toz, while introducing an end-2028 target of $5,600. As we noted last week, Goldman's 
gold targets have a curious habit of "mysteriously" tracking the price of gold almost one for one, 
and the latest move did nothing to break the streak. 
Here is the thesis in one paragraph: 
For precious metals, 2027 increasingly looks like 2022: a rising Fed policy rate weighs on 
private investor demand, but structural central bank buying keeps a firm bid under gold. We 
therefore see near-term downside risk and elevated volatility as the Fed continues to hike, but 
still see gold prices grinding higher to $4,650/toz by end-2027 on continued central bank 
purchases. Once the Fed starts cutting, we expect Western ETF demand to recover, supporting 
a stronger gold rally to $5,600/toz by end-2028, with significant upside risk to our base case if 
macro-policy hedge demand were to return. 
In other words, the hedge funds are out, the central banks are in, and nobody is coming to 
rescue the price until the Fed blinks. 
Why 2027 Looks Like 2022 
The road map is pure monetary policy. Goldman's economists expect a second 25bp hike in 
December, followed by a third in March 2027, which will keep weighing on rate-sensitive 
 

 
Western ETF demand. Relief only comes with four 25bp cuts between November 2027 and 
September 2028, taking the funds rate down to a terminal 3.25-3.5%. And don't expect ETF 
buyers to front-run it: one 25bp cut is worth only about 60 tonnes of ETF demand, spread over 
six months. 
That still leaves gold 19% higher than spot by end-2027, with almost all of the lift coming from 
central banks. 
 
Back in 2022, the Fed's hiking cycle crushed rate-sensitive investors, but the freezing of 
Russia's reserves unleashed the official-sector buying spree that kept gold afloat. Goldman's 
take on the replay: 
We think that the 2027 setup resembles 2022: central banks provide a strong structural bid, but 
a higher federal funds rate weigh on investor positioning, driving a more modest pace of gold 
price increases on net. 
 

 
 
The "debasement" crowd is expected to fold too, with GLD call demand drifting back to its 
historical average. Rate-insensitive maybe... but not stupid. 
How Low Can It Go? $4,265 And A 300-Tonne ETF Puke 
Goldman lays out two downside paths. In the first, the Fed hikes four times with a higher 
terminal rate, which takes the end-2027 forecast down to $4,585. The second is uglier: 
Gold ETF holdings have risen substantially over the past three years, reflecting lower rates, a 
normal upward trend as investor wealth grows..., and potentially a boost from the recent 
debasement trade. We expect two additional Fed rate hikes to unwind the latter, but cannot 
cleanly separate it from the structural wealth trend. As a conservative lower bound, we therefore 
assume all ETF holdings above the level implied by rates alone to unwind, implying another 300 
tonnes of selling and gold at $4265/toz by end-2027. 
 

 
 
A quick bit of napkin math: with spot at roughly $3,850 this afternoon, Goldman's "extreme" bear 
case still has gold about 11% higher by end-2027. Which says either something about how 
cheap gold has gotten, or about how bullish Goldman's bear case is. 
 
The tactical risks are a different story, and here Goldman echoes what it told clients two weeks 
ago when it explained why "gold has become an inverse oil trade." Spec positioning on COMEX 
is already low (45th percentile), but higher oil prices could trigger more systematic CTA selling, 
 

 
and a sharp equity drawdown "for instance related to uncertainty about the AI cycle" could 
initially drag gold lower as margin calls force investors to raise cash. With Nasdaq futures down 
another 1.2% this morning as tech and AI "take another leg lower" (per Goldman's TMT desk), 
that's hardly a hypothetical. 
 
Our friends at The Market Ear flagged last Thursday that $3,900 was the "line in the sand" for 
gold, with the next meaningful support near $3,400 (although they also noted systematic shorts 
were already stretched). That line has now been crossed. Heraeus' metals team adds that a 
25bp December hike is now fully priced, with core inflation still running at 0.3% a month. 
Why Gold Flunked The Hormuz Test 
If inflation spiked in 2026, why didn't the inflation hedge rally? Because "the source of inflation 
matters," says Goldman, and there are two very different flavors of stagflation: 
●​ Institutional credibility shocks, where markets doubt the central bank will contain inflation 
(the 1970s), and "gold surged as investors sought value outside the system." 
●​ Supply shocks, like Russia cutting gas to Europe in 2022, where the disrupted 
commodity outperforms, while gold "typically underperforms initially" as tighter policy and 
higher yields hit ETF demand and equity drawdowns trigger margin-related liquidations. 
"The 2026 Hormuz shock fits this second regime," Goldman writes. Put differently, gold wins 
when the Fed loses credibility, and loses when the Fed has to prove it. Regular readers will 
recall that Goldman's futures desk warned back in March that gold "may not be the safest 
haven," just as the oil shock was getting going. 
 

 
Hence the split recommendation. For CTAs and macro funds, "the next 3-6 months may remain 
challenging," with a rising funds rate, uncertain energy prices and tight drawdown limits making 
it "difficult to maintain long positions." For longer-horizon money, though, "the current correction 
in gold prices may offer a more attractive entry point." 
China Needs Three More Years Of Buying (At Least) 
The structural bull case rests on one assumption: central banks keep buying 60 tonnes a month 
through 2026-28, versus a pre-2022 average of just 17 tonnes. The latest nowcast was soft (31 
tonnes in September, taking the three-month pace down to 51 tonnes from 85), but Goldman 
calls it the usual pause after a violent price move, and points to something more interesting in 
the footnotes: 
Central bank gold holdings in custody at the Bank of England rose sharply in September (+ 82 
tonnes) by more than we can explain through implied UK net monetary gold imports (17 tonnes) 
or NY Fed custodial (0 tonnes), pointing to additional London OTC purchases being stored in 
custody at the Bank of England that are not captured by our nowcast of central bank buying. 
Translation: the official buying is probably bigger than the official data show, something we have 
been pounding the table on for a while, most recently when China was caught "still buying more 
than it admits" last month. 
And speaking of China, Goldman did the math on how long Beijing's shopping spree could run. 
The PBoC holds an estimated 11.8% of its reserves in gold, versus roughly 80% for the US, 
Germany, France and Italy. The global average is above 20%, which Goldman views as a 
plausible medium-term target for large EM central banks (Russia went from 8% to 20% between 
2014 and 2020, then repatriated its gold ahead of sanctions). 
 
 

 
At China's current pace of about 20 tonnes a month (in line with its 12-month average, and the 
largest identifiable buyer in September), Goldman estimates it would take approximately three 
years to reach a 20% gold share, even after accounting for the fact that the buying itself pushes 
up the value of existing holdings. 
 
And 20% may be the floor. Goldman argues that larger gold reserves help with the final, hardest 
step of RMB internationalization, getting the currency into other countries' reserves, the same 
way gold helped the dollar dethrone sterling after WWII as a currency "as good as gold." The 
bank's conclusion: China's desired gold allocation "could ultimately exceed 20%." 
Turkey Is The Exception, Not The Rule 
The flip side of the central bank bid is the fear of central bank selling, which we discussed at 
length when Goldman spotted who dumped $19.5 billion in gold earlier this month. Turkey's 
129-tonne reserve drop at the start of the Middle East conflict spooked plenty of people, but 
Goldman thinks gold swaps (which show up as declines even when nothing is sold) explain part 
of it, and reserves have since rebounded. 
More importantly, Turkey is an outlier, with gold at more than 60% of FX reserves, making it a 
natural source of FX liquidity. Only a handful of countries (Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, 
Russia, Venezuela) share that profile. As for the Gulf, gold is just 4% of reserves in the UAE, 
8% in Saudi Arabia, 5% in Oman and around 24% in Qatar, and since they run dollar pegs, "FX 
intervention is more likely to occur through US Treasury liquidation rather than gold sales." 
 

 
 
Good news for gold bugs. The Treasury market might see it differently. 
What about the old DM hoards? Goldman says the debate has moved "from whether to own 
gold to where to store it," noting that France and the Netherlands have reportedly moved gold 
away from New York. We have been tracking this repatriation trend since the Dutch started 
moving billions in gold out of the US in September, and as of last week it has a new destination: 
Goldman's read is that the focus on reserve security reinforces "the case for retaining rather 
than selling gold." Nobody worries this much about where to store something they plan to sell. 
Silver: The Squeeze Is Over, And The Fed Is Still Hiking 
Silver's run to a record above $120/toz in January was, in Goldman's telling, a perfect storm: 
tariff speculation pulled metal into the US through 2025, draining London, and then Fed cuts and 
the debasement trade poured investor money into what was left. We flagged it in real time: 
The numbers are striking: 1,000 tonnes of weekly net silver demand typically lifts prices about 
2%, but Goldman estimates that sensitivity jumped to roughly 7% during the squeeze. When 
investor demand faded, the price fell just as hard. 
 

 
 
Goldman doesn't see a repeat in 2027: even if tariff fears pull silver into the US again, Fed hikes 
should suppress ETF demand, "removing the second leg of the 2025/26 squeeze." That also 
kills the popular "buy silver, the gold/silver ratio is too high" trade. Private investment flows into 
both metals move in tandem, which kept the ratio in a 45-80 range for decades, until central 
bank buying decoupled gold in 2022. Silver has no central bank bid, so Goldman expects gold 
to "outglitter" silver again in 2027, at least until the Fed returns to easing. 
 

 
 
That said, we noted last week that silver specs have hardly ever been this short, and positioning 
that lopsided tends to end in a violent squeeze, whatever the fundamentals say. 
As for platinum and palladium, Goldman sees outright Section 232 tariffs as unlikely, but the 
threat alone keeps pulling inventories into the US and draining liquidity elsewhere, a 
national-security win that costs Washington nothing. The result: more volatility, but not 
necessarily higher prices, with European BEV sales up 66% y/y in August hitting diesel-heavy 
platinum, while US hybrids (up 27% y/y in Q3) offer palladium some cover. 
 

 
 
Waiting For Warsh To Blink 
To be fair to Goldman, nothing in the outlook is bearish on gold's long-term story. The upside 
case is explicit: once falling rates "remove the cap on the gold rally," fiscal worries across the 
West, "potentially reinforced by higher military spending and rising debt burdens in 
energy-importing countries," could drive "substantial upside beyond our base case of $5600/toz 
by end-2028." 
Our issue is with the timing. Goldman has the Fed hiking into March and not cutting until 
November 2027, a full year of patience from a central bank presiding over a Nasdaq in 
correction, mounting AI losses and a fiscal outlook that isn't getting any tighter. We think the Fed 
blinks well before that, which pulls forward the part of the outlook where ETF buyers return to 
compete with the PBoC, and probably not at $4,650. 
Then again, if Goldman's targets keep tracking the price, the best buy signal may well be the 
day Lina Thomas raises hers again.
