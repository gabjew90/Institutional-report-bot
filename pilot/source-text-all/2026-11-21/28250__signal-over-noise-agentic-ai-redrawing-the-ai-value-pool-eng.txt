From the ROI debate to business-model disruption
As the AI cycle approaches its fourth anniversary following 
ChatGPT’s launch in November 2022, the return on 
AI investment has dominated investor debate over the 
past year. Markets have repeatedly shifted as investors 
try to price the dynamics of AI monetization and have 
often overreacted, disproportionately rewarding potential 
beneficiaries while effectively assigning zero terminal value 
to businesses perceived to be vulnerable to disruption.
Software has been particularly exposed, as concerns that 
agentic tools could displace traditional applications have 
called the durability of SaaS revenues into question, often 
triggering broad-based selling across the sector.
Similarly, in cybersecurity, the emergence of models with 
more advanced cyber capabilities triggered sharp declines in 
shares as investors questioned the value of existing products. 
Yet the broad-based selloff quickly reversed as investors 
recognized that the same capabilities would strengthen 
demand for cybersecurity services by expanding the range 
and sophistication of potential attacks.
These episodes highlight the market’s rush to separate 
leaders from laggards, with sentiment shifting rapidly 
as investors try to anticipate the evolution of AI-first 
technology. In the digital economy, we believe this transition 
will depend heavily on AI agent adoption. The share of

When the customer’s interface becomes an agent
Historically, the internet has largely been designed around 
human interaction. Websites, checkout flows, and software 
dashboards assume that users are actively browsing, 
clicking, and making decisions on a screen. As a result, many 
internet business models rely on a two-step process: first 
capturing human attention, then converting that attention 
into revenue.
The emergence of the agentic internet challenges this 
model. Autonomous AI agents are becoming an intelligent 
layer between users and the digital world, executing 
multi-step tasks, navigating workflows, and completing 
transactions on their behalf. If agents become the primary 
means of accessing the web, users may increasingly 
bypass the interfaces designed to attract and retain their 
attention. This could weaken the link between attention 
and monetization, putting pressure on businesses whose 
revenues depend on direct human engagement.

Our central thesis was that the transition from chatbots 
to agents would not only fundamentally reshape inference 
demand but also become a key driver of a markedly different 
AI business landscape, extending well beyond the use cases 
evident at the time. That outlook now appears increasingly 
plausible, with token consumption tracking broadly in line 
with our earlier projections.
Recent usage data support the direction of this dynamic. 
OpenRouter reported 790 trillion tokens in October 2026, 
up 37% from September and almost 25 times January’s 
volume. Based on the platform’s classification, agentic API 
keys accounted for 76% of tokens versus 8% a year earlier. 
October agentic token volume was approximately 7.2 times 
human-classified volume (Figure 2). OpenRouter’s role as an 
API router serving software-development workflows means 
its traffic is naturally tilted toward automation and multi-
agent pipelines. Nevertheless, the evidence strengthens the 
argument that AI usage is moving beyond isolated questions 
toward persistent execution and agentic workloads.

What agents mean for digital businesses
This transition creates risks for two of technology’s largest 
business models if they fail to evolve: digital advertising 
and enterprise software. Both are built around human-
facing interfaces but serve different purposes. Advertising 
platforms organize content to capture consumer attention 
and convert it into purchases, while enterprise software 
structures data and workflows to help employees complete 
tasks. As agents increasingly discover products and execute 
workflows directly, value could shift from the interface to 
the outcome.
For digital advertising, the challenge is that delegated 
purchasing can bypass the touchpoints through which 
platforms monetize human attention. Agents could 
compare specifications, prices, and availability before 
completing a purchase, without users visiting a search 
page or browsing a retailer’s website. This could change 
who captures the value of customer discovery by reducing 
opportunities for conventional advertising and increasing 
the importance of agentic discovery. When an agent 
intermediates the transaction, accurate, machine-readable 
product information, real-time inventory, and integrations 
with purchasing systems become more important.
Enterprise software faces a parallel challenge. Traditional 
software-as-a-service models often combine graphical 
interfaces with per-seat pricing, linking revenue growth 
to the number of employees using the software. Agents 
that reconcile invoices, route support tickets, or interact 
directly with underlying systems could reduce the need 
for additional human-operated seats and weaken the 
dashboard’s role as the primary interface.
However, removing the interface does not remove the need 
for software. The opportunity for software providers centers 
on remaining essential to the workflow by supplying trusted 
data, business logic, permissions, and execution capabilities 
while adapting pricing toward usage or outcomes.
Incumbents have assets, but also revenues to defend
Across both markets, the central risk is that the 
customer relationship moves upstream to the agent, leaving 
incumbent platforms with less control over discovery, 
interaction, and monetization. That points to disruption 
for incumbents whose main revenue streams and business 
models were built around the internet economy.
The transition is not uniformly negative for large technology 
companies. Their infrastructure, distribution, data, and 
customer relationships provide important advantages. But 
they must build new revenue streams while protecting 
existing ones.
That debate came into sharper focus during the 3Q26 
earnings season, with Amazon and, in particular, Alphabet 
illustrating the tension between AI-driven growth and 
pressure on existing businesses. Both companies beat cloud 
revenue expectations, with AWS growing 39% year over 
year and Google Cloud expanding 103%, while advertising 
revenues fell short of forecasts. Amazon’s advertising 
slowdown was largely driven by specific event timing and 
comparison effects. Alphabet’s results, however, raised a 
more structural question. Although search-query growth

reached a five-year high and Search revenue grew 12% year 
over year, revenue growth decelerated. While other factors 
contributed to the slowdown, strong growth in AI Mode

searches, also weighed meaningfully on revenue growth. 
This highlights the potential for cannibalization risk as AI-
mediated search, and eventually agentic interfaces, shift 
activity toward interactions that generate less advertising 
revenue, even when overall usage rises.
Our intention is not to take an outright negative view of 
Alphabet, but rather to highlight it as the clearest illustration 
of the tension between a strong asset base and AI-driven 
growth engines on one side and pressure on established 
advertising economics on the other. Its dependence on 
search makes the transition a potential overhang, yet 
Google’s vertical integration spans the AI value chain, from 
TPUs and cloud infrastructure to proprietary models and 
applications such as Waymo.
That breadth provides multiple routes to capture emerging 
revenue pools and underscores why Google’s competitive 
position should not be assessed through search alone. 
Paid assistant subscriptions and enterprise AI services 
already offer additional monetization channels. Scaling 
them efficiently could offset pressure on existing economics 
and reinforce the company’s competitive position by 
leveraging its integrated offering across the value chain. 
Nevertheless, a successful transition requires continuous 
innovation, disciplined execution, and leadership willing to 
prioritize the largest emerging revenue pools, even where 
they challenge established business models.
What this means for investors: Diversify the 
beneficiaries
Putting it all together, we believe the speed of AI disruption 
remains underappreciated by markets. Nevertheless, our 
conclusion is not to abandon large-cap technology, but 
rather to prepare portfolios for an AI transition likely 
to combine market expansion with the redistribution of 
revenue.
AI is not a zero-sum game. Productivity gains should 
expand economic output and create new revenue pools, 
while enterprise AI spending can also generate cost 
savings and efficiency improvements. However, we do 
not expect aggregate economic growth alone to generate 
all the incremental revenues required to justify the scale 
of AI investment. Some of those revenues will likely be 
redistributed from existing profit pools, creating leaders and 
laggards even as the overall market expands.
The greatest pressure could fall on digital businesses that 
prospered under the internet model, particularly where 
economics depend on human attention, interfaces, or 
per-user software consumption. Given the concentration 
of these business models among the largest technology 
companies, this also creates portfolio-level concentration 
risk. Businesses anchored more heavily in the physical 
economy should be comparatively insulated from direct 
interface disruption, while compute, power infrastructure, 
security, and differentiated AI applications provide 
alternative ways to participate in the transition.
From a portfolio perspective, this argues for broader 
participation and greater diversification. Equal-weighted 
equity exposure can reduce mega-cap concentration, 
increase exposure to smaller companies and potential new 
entrants, and create more room to capture tomorrow’s 
market leaders.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
