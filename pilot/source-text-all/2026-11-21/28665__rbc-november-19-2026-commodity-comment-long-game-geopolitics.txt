Geopolitics: Postcard from DC — Split Screen War 
 
helima.croft@rbccm.com 
The nine-month Iran conflict has increasingly become a split 
screen war. One set of market observers seems singularly 
focused on exports moving through Hormuz, assuming the bulk 
of the disruption is in the rear-view mirror. Meanwhile, an 
increasing number of security experts in Washington and the 
region are alarmed by Iran’s willingness to pursue an escalation 
strategy. The White House "over soon" messaging has also 
contributed to the short-duration collective conclusion. Yet, 
the history of armed conflicts in the post-WWII period shows 
that short wars are actually something of an anomaly, not the 
norm, with the average interstate conflict lasting 2.1 years. As 
we have previously noted, a recurrent theme in our 
conversations in Washington has been that the rise in transits 
has come at enormous cost not only to the US Treasury but also 
to US force readiness. Moreover, a clear consensus from these 
discussions is that Iran shows no signs of dropping its 
maximalist demands for full enforcement of the June MoU…  
…despite the blockade’s mounting economic cost. Even the 
leading proponents of the “let-the-blockade-do-the-work” 
strategy in Washington and the region concede that it will 
require a significant amount of time to achieve its stated 
objectives (with estimates ranging from 8-9 additional months
to as far out as 2 years). All the while, Tehran is expected to 
continue its steady escalation of strikes on Hormuz tankers and 
US military assets, with Kuwait and Bahrain bearing the brunt 
of recent Iranian fire. We question whether elevated exports 
could be maintained in a hasty American withdrawal scenario, 
especially if there is a concurrent removal of the US military 
assets aiding transits and no maritime management 
agreement amongst key regional players. Neither the current 
unstable stalemate nor an escalation-to-exit scenario seems to 
provide a beneficial backdrop for elevated exports or 
production over the next 6-9 months. 
Oil: Losing Steam 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
Middle East crude loadings and Strait of Hormuz transits have 
both receded in recent weeks, suggesting to us that exports 
from the region are not invulnerable to the uptick in strikes, 
despite a sentiment shift in oil markets. Over the last four 
weeks, crude loadings in the region have averaged around 4.9 
mb/d below pre-war averages (Jan-Feb). While ex-Hormuz 
loadings over this period sit around 1 mb/d higher than pre-
war, given the much-discussed re-routing efforts (e.g., via East-
West), Mideast Gulf crude loadings inside the Strait of Hormuz 
are down around 5.9 mb/d. Though these weekly figures can 
be quite volatile, the trajectory seemingly continues to point 
lower, with initial loadings this week even lower (both inside 
and outside of Hormuz). Similarly, 7DMA commodity vessel 
transits in the Strait are down over 50% over the last 30 days. 
This reinforces our conviction in our team’s fundamental view.

Natural Gas: Winter Balance 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
Prices rose midweek as a shifting weather outlook introduced 
colder temperatures through late November, boosting HDD 
forecasts despite broader expectations for a mild winter. Next 
week’s cold snap is expected to be short-lived, with medium-
term forecasts through December still projecting HDDs 4% 
below seasonal norms, reinforcing expectations for a mild El 
Niño winter. Nonetheless, near-record LNG feedgas demand 
has sustained throughout November, providing a firm source of 
structural demand and limiting the market’s ability to absorb 
weather-driven consumption increases. While the inventory 
surplus remains intact, sustained export demand and 
intermittent cold spells could gradually erode this buffer as the 
withdrawal season progresses. As such, signs of sustained 
inventory erosion will be telling in the coming weeks, 
particularly if colder weather forecasts materialize alongside
elevated LNG demand. While the broader outlook remains 
bearish for rescomm, tightening balances could provide 
intermittent upside support to prices as winter progresses. 
Gold: Floor Search 
christopher.louney@rbccm.com 
With gold still on its back foot, where do we go from here? With 
gold trading into the low $3,700s, we again highlight our low 
scenario ($3,661/oz in 2027) as where we think gold should find 
some fundamental support. Near-term headwinds are clearly 
mounting, mostly due to hawkish commentary from Fed 
officials. Yesterday, Governor Waller said inflation is still going 
in the wrong direction, urging a more aggressive rate hike. 
While yields above 5% are a hurdle for gold regardless, we think 
a lot of the bad news is already priced in. Yields are high and the 
dollar is strong, but that's not all that matters. Central bank 
appetite and investor allocations remain strong, equity market 
volatility persists, uncertainty is a key factor, and inflation — 
while currently pushing gold lower — is a fickle driver that can 
move gold in either direction. Our latest call, narrowing our 
2027 view and rolling out 2028, still has our conviction. 
 
Clean Energy: Solar Slowdown 
allison.enck@rbccm.com 
The first three quarters of 2026 saw a 12% y/y increase in total 
installed US power capacity, with wind experiencing the largest 
year-on-year gains even as federal policy seeks to stymie 
development. Onshore wind installed 6.4 GW of capacity 
through September, an 84% increase from last year, which in 
large part can be attributed to a 3.5 GW project coming online 
in New Mexico in May. While renewables experienced another 
period of capacity gains overall, growth was down during this 
period from the two previous years (8% in 2026 compared to 
19% in 2025 and 62% in 2024). This is due to new solar capacity 
contracting, down 10% y/y through September, following a 
trend set in 2025 where annual solar capacity additions were 
down 11% y/y. This trend is unsurprising as trade, tariff, policy,
and finance headwinds simultaneously hit the solar sector —
headwinds that could increase if data center demand for clean 
energy declines.
