Global Equity Market Arithmetic 
Quantifying the speculative factor driving equity markets 
 
Equity markets are highly polarised, with a significant number of negative pairwise stock 
correlations, driven by divergent AI and energy narratives. This has seen the correlation 
between strong and weak price-momentum portfolios collapse (turning negative in the US), 
despite both holding long positions in stocks and therefore usually being positively correlated. 
Pinning down the precise drivers is difficult, not least because momentum fuelled by AI and oil 
prices has many faces, from the Mag 7 and hyperscalers to semiconductors. As an alternative, 
we instead use principal component analysis (PCA) to identify the key statistical drivers of 
excess stock returns within each market. The top right chart is revealing: PC1 explains 27% of 
the variance in Japanese stock returns and 17% in the US but has been relatively subdued in 
Europe. From our perspective, PC1 increasingly looks like speculative stock momentum. Why? 
A portfolio comprising the top 5% of ACWI World stocks by prior 12-month price performance, 
(120 of 2,400 global stocks), tracks this first principal component remarkably closely. Yet its 
composition is strikingly different from the index: 24% of its stocks are from the US, 21% from 
China, 23% from Taiwan, 9% from Korea, 6% from Japan and 10% from elsewhere. Despite 
representing just 7% of total market capitalisation, these stocks have recently accounted for 
more than 25% of trading volume. This is a different kind of concentration risk.

The average sector correlation with PC1 looks very much like the AI thematic, with Capital Goods, 
Materials, Semiconductors and Tech Hardware all positively correlated. This confirms what we 
already suspected: AI speculation has been a major driver of cross-sectional returns, its impact 
has been highly polarising, and that speculative momentum now appears to be unwinding. 
To be clear, the AI theme is struggling, but it is the speculation surrounding it that is coming under 
the most pressure. Given that this is where the greatest excess was in the first place, this is 
probably not a surprise. 
GICS industry group correlation to PC1 
 
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/ Equity Quant 
The next principal component (PC2) explains 7% of the variation in European stock returns, 8% 
in the US and 4% in Japan. While significantly less important than PC1, PC2 is nonetheless 
interesting, appearing to capture an energy/inflation dynamic that emerged alongside the rise in 
oil prices in Q1 2026. 
Performance of the second principal component (PC2)

PC2 is correlated with the usual inflation-sensitive suspects, notably Energy and Materials, but 
also, rather intriguingly, Utilities. Normally seen as defensive and counter-cyclical, Utilities have 
acquired a rather different complexion as investors focus on the potentially enormous power 
demands of AI.

The factor correlations make the distinction even clearer. PC1 is essentially high-volatility 
momentum with an anti-value bias. PC2 also has a momentum tilt, but this time alongside lower 
risk, lower volatility and quality income. So, we appear to have two rather different versions of 
momentum operating in the same market. Can they really coexist? The contrasting performance 
of the Nasdaq 100 and our defensive Quality Income strategy (SGQINTRD Index) suggests so!
