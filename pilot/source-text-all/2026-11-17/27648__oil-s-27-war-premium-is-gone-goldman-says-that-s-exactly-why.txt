 
Oil's $27 War Premium Is Gone: Goldman Says 
That's Exactly Why You Should Own Commodities In 
2027 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 15, 2026 - 07:30 PM 
Two weeks ago, oil was priced for war. 
On the Friday before the midterms, Goldman estimated that $27 of every barrel of Brent was 
pure geopolitical fear. Then came a Democratic sweep, some Qatari phone diplomacy and the 
steepest weekly drop in Brent since the COVID crash, followed by another leg lower on 
Monday. Now, according to Goldman's commodity team, that premium is down to only a few 
dollars. 
Most strategists would take that as a cue to turn bearish. Goldman's commodity team, led by 
Daan Struyven, uses its just-published 2027 outlook, "Adapting to Rough Waters" (available to 
pro subs), to argue the opposite: 
We believe that shifts in market sentiment and in near-term Mideast escalation risks, rather than 
in physical fundamentals, have driven the November selloff in energy and copper prices. We 
estimate that the risk premium in Brent spot prices has dropped from $27 on the Friday before 
the midterms to only a few dollars over the last few days. In our view, this reduction in risk 
premia increases the hedging and diversification value of commodities by offering a more 
attractive entry point. 
In other words, the insurance just got a lot cheaper, while the house is still sitting next to the 
same fire. 
 

 
 
That said, Goldman is not exactly pounding the table on returns. Its base case of further energy 
price moderation produces near-zero total returns for the BCOM index through end-2027 (-1%), 
after a 26% gain this year that was driven almost entirely by energy (+110% YTD). This is a 
hedging pitch through and through, and the bank lays out its full menu of 2027 hedges and 
trades below. 
 
The Gulf "Adapted"... At A Price 
Regular readers know the story of 2026: energy prices ran far above pre-war expectations, but 
given how long the Iran war has lasted, they rose less than almost anyone expected. Goldman 
 

 
credits a "remarkable adaptation" of both demand (destocking and fuel switching, especially in 
China, which got the world through the March-May supply crunch) and supply. 
On the supply side, Persian Gulf crude exports, including the "dark" barrels Goldman started 
counting in September, recovered to near 2025 levels by September, and the global oil market 
likely flipped back into surplus in October. Goldman expects ship-to-ship transfers and new 
pipelines to lift Mideast oil output to an average of 1.1mb/d above 2025 levels in 2027. 
But here's the catch: what worked for crude has not worked for everything else. 
Refined products and LNG are more flammable than crude and much harder to shuffle from ship 
to ship, while refineries, aluminium smelters and liquefaction trains hit in strikes are still not fully 
repaired. As a result, Gulf exports of products, LNG, aluminium feedstock and fertilizer remain 
well below pre-war levels: 
 
And the adaptation that did happen came with a hefty bill. Goldman estimates that the tanker 
market's workarounds account for 3/4 of the ~$20/bbl year-on-year jump in its global average 
crude freight rate, reflecting a war risk premium to cross Hormuz and a smaller effective fleet, as 
tankers shuttle oil from inside the Gulf to east of Hormuz for ship-to-ship transfers, while the US 
blockade sidelines Iran's fleet. Translation: freight stays expensive for as long as the shuttle 
runs and the blockade holds. 
Naturally, we were right on top of it, having tracked the tanker-rate crisis since February, all the 
way through "The $1 Million Tanker Was Just The Start" (Oct 6). Goldman adds that flexible 
 

 
tankers that used to carry products have switched to crude, dragging product freight higher too, 
and that those costs largely pass through to the pump. Hence the (parabolic) chart on the right: 
 
Three Reasons To Hedge Now, Starting With The One Everyone 
Just Sold 
Tactically, Goldman sees three factors favoring long commodities as a portfolio hedge: i) upside 
risk to energy prices, ii) the unusually high sensitivity of stocks and bonds to energy prices, and 
iii) upside risk to agricultural prices. 
The first is the obvious one. Despite the diplomatic headlines, Goldman flags "the recent pickup 
in tanker attacks near Hormuz" as a downside risk to its Gulf supply base case, a risk it expects 
to rise in the coming quarters. As recently as Tuesday, Iran's IRGC was still claiming mine 
strikes on tankers, which is what passes for de-escalation these days: 
Goldman's escalation math is stark: 
●​ Brent might rally over $130 in early 2027 and average around $120 if Gulf output stays 
2.5mb/d below its 2025 average (versus 1.1mb/d above in the base case). US wholesale 
diesel would hit new highs around $230 if renewed escalation knocks out another 3mb/d 
of refining. 
●​ TTF might rally near 150 EUR/MWh in Q1 if Gulf LNG exports average just 25-30% of 
pre-war levels this winter and Europe's winter is one standard deviation (0.7°C) colder 
than normal. 
 

 
 
For context, when Goldman hiked its oil forecasts on Nov 1, the upside scenario had Brent at 
$140 and averaging $130 in 2027, with the Gulf 3.5mb/d short. So the tail got a little thinner in 
11 days, but the base case for 2027 Brent stayed at $88. What collapsed was the premium, 
while the fundamentals barely moved. 
European gas is where the asymmetry really shows. Goldman's 2027 TTF average of 45 
EUR/MWh sits 34% below the forward curve, yet its Q1 scenario grid ranges from 41 to 147 
against forwards at 85: 
 

 
 
Note the footnote: Goldman now assigns a 30% probability to the low winter supply scenario, 
down from 45% thanks to a sharp month-to-date jump in Mideast LNG exports. Still, with 
European inventories low, a cold snap and an LNG hiccup at the same time would send prices 
sharply higher "especially following the recent risk premium liquidation." 
Stocks And Bonds Have Become An Oil Trade 
The second factor is the one that should worry the 60/40 crowd. Since the Iran war began, 
equities, and especially bonds, have become unusually sensitive to oil. The 6-month rolling 
correlation of daily changes between Brent and 10-year Treasury yields hit roughly 0.5, while 
Brent's correlation with the S&P 500 sank to around -0.45 (approximate, read off the chart): 
 

 
 
Goldman concedes those correlations have softened lately, with the 1-month correlation 
between Brent and yields negativesince late September as "AI/risk-asset volatility has become 
the bigger driver." That is a polite way of saying the Nasdaq found a way to sell off all by itself. 
But the bank expects the oil link to come right back in a renewed supply shock: 
This heightened sensitivity suggests that an energy supply shock from potential renewed 
escalation in 2027 might be associated with lower bond prices, as in 2026, and lower equities, 
as in 2022—especially given our base case of slower earnings growth and downside risks to 
global GDP and employment. 
Stocks and bonds falling together is exactly when a portfolio needs a third leg, and here 
Goldman has history on its side: in every 12-month period over the past six decades when both 
equities and bonds posted negative real returns, either gold or broader commodities delivered 
positive real returns. Diversification at its finest: 
 

 
 
The third factor is food. BCOM Agriculture is already up 10% YTD per Goldman, and still faces 
upside risk from Hormuz-linked fertilizer trade (typically 1/3 of global fertilizer exports), Black 
Sea grain (15-20% of global grain trade), biofuel diversion and an El Niño forecast to be the 
strongest on record. West African cocoa (~65% of global exports) and Vietnamese robusta are 
first in the firing line, while drought-starved European rivers are already choking Danube grain 
exports and Rhine barge traffic. 
The Two Hedges Goldman Likes: Gasoline And A Cold Winter 
So how does Goldman want clients to hedge? It skips outright crude and goes for two 
"asymmetric" energy trades: 
●​ Long June 2027 European gasoline (EBOB). Refiners have been switching output from 
gasoline to higher-margin diesel and jet, leaving gasoline tighter than it looks, and 
relatively low gasoline margins leave room for sharp upside if refining gets hit again. 
After the selloff, Goldman sees a 20% expected return in its base case and 62% under 
renewed escalation, versus a 4% loss in its most bearish scenario. Not a bad 
risk/reward, at least on paper. 
●​ 27Q1 TTF call spreads, a cheap way to hedge a tight European winter after the risk 
premium liquidation. 
Longer term, Goldman likes the other side of European gas: puts on TTF Summer 2029, as the 
2025-26 wave of LNG FIDs floods the market after 2027. Because in commodities, as in 
everything else, nothing cures high prices like high prices. 
 

 
Copper Up, Aluminium Down, Gold... Cut Again 
The structural case rests on two pillars. First, commodities are insurance against a world where 
supply is concentrated and governments are increasingly willing to weaponize it: the 2022 
Russian gas shock, last year's Chinese rare earths shock and this year's Hormuz 
cross-commodity shock are, in Goldman's words, "symptoms of the same shift." Second, the 
US-China AI and power race is colliding with tight supply, especially in copper. 
Goldman sees LME copper at $15,600/t by Dec27, 13% above spot (and up from just under 
$10,000 last year), as US tariff ambiguity keeps metal flowing into US stockpiles and China's 
scrap-invoicing crackdown forces fabricators onto refined cathode, draining ex-US stocks to 
seasonal lows. Aluminium is the mirror image: abundant bauxite and a wave of China-backed 
smelters in Indonesia (rising to ~4% of global output by end-2027 from 1% at end-2025) should 
push the market into surplus and the price down to $2,600. Hence the pair trade: long Dec27 
copper, short Dec27 aluminium, which Goldman notes should also be "more immune" to "a turn 
in AI cycle, more Fed hikes" than an outright long. 
 
And then there is gold, where Goldman's forecast continues its now familiar trajectory. Just a 
week after cutting its Dec27 target to $4,800 from $5,400, the bank is "nudging down" again, to 
$4,650/toz, because its economists now expect a third 25bp Warsh hike in March 2027 on top of 
December's. If $4,650 sounds familiar, it should: it is almost exactly the $4,660 "three-hike" 
downside scenario Goldman laid out last Thursday, which has now been promoted to the base 
case in seven days.

Cheap Insurance In A Neighborhood That Keeps Catching Fire 
Not everyone on the Street sees the November puke as sentiment alone. JPMorgan estimated 
a global oil surplus of about 1.4mb/d in October, while Morgan Stanley flagged that Dated Brent 
 

 
has slipped below the nearby futures contract, a sign of a soft physical market, according to 
Reuters. If they're right, the "few dollars" of premium left in Brent is the least of oil's problems. 
Goldman's answer is that a surplus is precisely what the base case assumes, which is why it 
forecasts $88 Brent and near-zero index returns. The hedge argument is about the tails, and its 
summary for the coming year is straightforward: 
Our base case puts 2027 average Brent and European TTF natural gas prices at $88/bbl and 
45EUR/MWh, respectively, as Mideast supply adapts further to rough waters. Yet even as 
supply adaptation has spread, it has worked less well for other commodities than for crude oil 
and has sharply raised shipping costs. 
We'd go a step further. The market just repriced the Gulf as if the war were over, while the IRGC 
is still mining tankers, refineries and LNG trains are still broken, and the "lasting diplomatic 
breakthrough" remains as elusive as it has been all year. Every previous "peace is at hand" 
moment in this conflict ended with a tanker on fire, and with a December Fed hike still on the 
table, a renewed oil spike would hit stocks and bonds at the same time. The hedge is cheap 
now because it's unloved, and that is usually when it pays. 
Then again, as Goldman's ever-falling gold target shows, the forecasts tend to follow the price.
