2026 has delivered a double major surprise to oil markets.  
First, the effective closure of Hormuz—previously seen as an unlikely tail scenario—did 
not only materialize but also lasted much longer than expected at the start of the war 
(Exhibit 1, left), leading the market to upgrade 2026 oil price expectations (Exhibit 1, 
right).  
Second, given the actual length of the shipping disruptions, oil prices have risen less 
sharply than expected. The key reason is that global inventory declines in 
March-October (Exhibit 19) were limited to ~600mb (~5% of global estimated stocks, 
2.4mb/d) because of 1) the 2025 pre-war surplus (worth ~300mb of builds in 
March-October) and 2) remarkable oil market adaptation, particularly the: 
Recovery in Persian Gulf oil output despite ongoing shipping risks given pipeline 
n
redirections, dark exports, US Navy escorts, and ship-to-ship transfers (which 
contained the March-October 2026 loss in Gulf oil output vs. its 2025 average to 
~2050mb) 
Decline in oil demand in response to higher prices, especially in Q2 and in China, 
n
which switched to power and coal (reducing net global stock draws by ~775mb) 
Strength in oil output outside the Gulf, especially Brazil (reducing draws by ~375mb)

Reason 2: Persian Gulf Supply Is Likely to Rise Further 
Although the recovery of Gulf output is likely to be less smooth than our stylized base 
case (Exhibit 4), we expect it to trend higher as continued adaptation and added 
transportation resources—especially a larger tanker fleet and pipelines—support 
further production growth. In our base case, Gulf liquids output rises nearly 4mb/d from 
its October 2026 level in 2027. Our base case that 2027 Gulf output exceeds its 2025 
average by 1.4mb/d largely reflects overshoots in Saudi Arabia and the UAE, helping 
global stocks to recover from current low levels.3 
As Gulf producers are investing in pipelines that bypass the Strait of Hormuz and 
transport oil directly to the Gulf of Oman, Red Sea, or Mediterranean Sea, we expect an 
additional 3.8mb/d of pipeline capacity to come online over the next year. The three 
additions are the 1) completion of the new West-East pipeline in the UAE, 2) repurposing 
of a gas pipeline to transport crude oil in Saudi Arabia, and 3) the expansion of the Saudi 
port of Yanbu (Exhibit 20). This should allow Gulf countries to export over 14mb/d, or 
around 60% of their 2025 average export volumes, via pipelines bypassing Hormuz 
alone by end-2027 (Exhibit 6).

Reason 3: China Oil Demand Is Likely to Remain Below its Pre-War Trend  
We assume that global oil demand, which remained down a sizable 3mb/d 
year-over-year in 2026Q3, recovers to its pre-war trend outside of China. This forecast 
of a full recovery outside China is consistent with the rebound in global demand from the 
2011 and 2022 negative oil supply shocks once the availability and affordability of oil 
products improved (Exhibit 7).  
We expect persistent weakness in Chinese road fuel oil demand to drive all the 0.4mb/d 
of “scarring” from the Hormuz shock to global oil demand by 2027Q4. We assume that 
just over half of the 0.7mb/d YoY September decline in China gasoline and diesel 
demand (Exhibit 8) persists as the surge in usage of electric passenger cars and trucks 
likely accelerated China’s energy transition.4 In contrast, we see the earlier weakness in 
China’s petrochemical oil demand as largely driven by destocking and thus 
unsustainable, consistent with the rebound in China’s naphtha, LPG, and ethane demand 
in September (Exhibit 8). Taken together, we estimate that China’s total oil demand 
peaked in 2025.

renewed demand weakness in the fall as prices rose again support our view that part of the weakness in China 
road fuel oil demand is price-driven, and therefore reversible.

Oil Products 2027 Base Case: Prices Ease But Remain Above Average 
While we expect crude prices to normalize, we expect product prices to ease 
sequentially but remain well above seasonal averages given structurally tighter refining 
and a double whammy from reduced refinery runs in the Mideast and Russia. 
We expect US wholesale margins vs. Brent for diesel and gasoline to average $59/bbl 
and $28/bbl in 2027, respectively, well above 2025 averages (Exhibit 9). Our diesel 
forecast is below forwards as we expect most of the current near-record-high risk 
premium to unwind by end-2027 as Mideast refining capacity returns. By contrast, we 
see gasoline margins above forwards next year as market pricing is much lower for 
gasoline5 and as refinery yield switching toward diesel and jet fuel and robust OECD 
gasoline demand will likely keep gasoline stocks low. 
A moderation in crude prices and refined product margins should also reduce US retail 
prices by end-2027 for gasoline to $3.7/gal (from $4.0/gal now) and for diesel $4.6/gal 
($6.1/gal now).  
 
Driver #1: Structural Refining Tightness 
Limited refinery net additions in 2020-2025 amid a large wave of OECD refinery closures 
and strikes on Mideast and Russia refineries are forcing the unconstrained refineries to 
run very hard (Exhibit 10). While we assume a full Mideast refining capacity recovery by 
mid-2027, limited net growth in refining capacity of 0.4mb/d in 2027 (assuming new 
Mideast capacity comes online) and 0.3mb/d in 2028 is unlikely to offset ongoing 
Russian product output losses if demand largely recovers.

Driver #2: Adaptation to Mideast Shock at a Cost 
Supply adaptation is more challenging and costlier for refined products than for crude. 
We estimate that Persian Gulf product exports remain 60% below their 2025 average 
given 1) damage to refineries, 2) high risks of transporting of flammable products, and 3) 
challenges transferring products ship-to-ship. Given crude production resilience in the 
Mideast and strength in the Americas, global crude exports are now 12% above their 
2025 average, while refined product exports remain 6% below (Exhibit 11). If tanker 
attacks continue, Mideast product exports are likely to remain below normal, and 
support product margins.

The Mideast conflict has raised refining costs, especially the costs of shipping and global 
natural gas, which ultimately lifts wholesale and retail product prices via cost 
pass-through and lower refinery runs.  
Global crude and product freight rates surged to all-time highs in October (Exhibit 18). 
While the freight rally started in crude, it spilled over into refined products as flexible 
tankers switched to shipping crude. Our base case is that crude and product freight 
rates remain well above historical norms, but ease to $9/bbl by end-2027 from $15/bbl 
today (with upside risks) as extra tankers reach the market and as our probabilistic 
forecast assumes a gradual reduction in the boost to shipping costs from the US 
blockade of the Iranian fleet.  
Deferred refinery maintenance imposes implicit costs on refiners via higher future 
maintenance and higher risks of unplanned outages. Planned refinery maintenance has 
been 2mb/d below average this fall as many Western refineries have postponed 
maintenance, with expected planned maintenance for April 2027 already exceeding 
seasonal norms by 1mb/d (Exhibit 21). High maintenance is likely to weigh on refined 
product supply in 2027H1, when Mideast refineries may still run below capacity.  
Driver #3: Russian Product Output Remains Constrained 
Russian seaborne exports of refined products remained 1mb/d below historical seasonal 
norms in October, and we assume that high refinery outages in Russia will continue to 
weigh on refinery runs and product exports in 2027.

(Exhibit 12).6 While we assume Mideast refinery runs recover by mid-2027, persistent 
outages in Russia and high global planned maintenance are likely to keep refinery runs 
1.3mb/d below trend next year, with downside risks.

Price Risks 
We still see significant risks to crude oil and product prices with upside risk to market 
prices in the near-term from geopolitical escalation and from a recovery in the risk 
premium. We, however, also see medium-term price downside from stronger supply, 
more demand scarring, or a faster erosion in the risk premium than our base case 
assumes. We illustrate the risks to our price forecasts with two scenarios (Exhibit 13 for 
crude, Exhibit 14 for products). 
Upside price scenario:  
Brent might reach $130/bbl and average around $120/bbl in 2027 if average 2027 
n
Gulf oil output remains 2.7mb/d below its 2025 average (vs. 1.4mb/d above in our 
base case). 
The most likely price upside trigger is renewed Mideast escalation that 
o
involves attacks on energy infrastructure, including export terminals and 
refineries. 
US wholesale product prices might reach $230/bbl for diesel and $170/bbl for 
n
gasoline if renewed escalation reduces 2027 Gulf oil output by 2.7mb/d below its

Downside price scenario:  
Brent might decline into the $60s in 2027 if average 2027 Gulf oil output rises 
n
2.6mb/d above 2025 levels and if the disruption assumed in our global supply 
allowance does not occur.

declines in the timespreads risk premium and the supply security incentive

Recommendations 
We conclude with recommendations.  
Investors 
To investors seeking to hedge geopolitical escalation and price upside risk, we 
n
continue to recommend long summer 2027 European gasoline positions. The recent 
gasoline selloff provides asymmetric opportunities (Exhibit 14, right panel). 
Fundamentally, these positions could benefit from weaker gasoline output due to 
further refinery strikes, heavy planned spring 2027 maintenance, and refinery yield 
shifting output from gasoline to diesel and jet fuel.  
To clients trading oil markets at a higher frequency, we recommend staying nimble 
n
and leaning against large swings in our crude risk premium estimate (Exhibit 2) as 
long as there is neither a definitive sustained diplomatic breakthrough nor a 
re-escalation with major long-term damage to oil infrastructure. This implies a 
preference for long positions when the risk premium is small or negative, but for 
short positions when the risk premium is very high. Given the large swings in implied 
volatility and call-put skew ratios observed in the conflict so far, we also recommend 
being nimble in terms of expressing views via futures or options markets.  
Oil Producers 
To producers seeking protection against the downside risk to crude prices over the 
n
medium and long-run from higher Mideast supply, greater damage to long-run oil 
demand, and downside risks to global GDP, we recommend buying put options 
because put skew remains low.  
To cheapen the cost of price downside protection, producers might also sell call 
n
options (i.e. buy collars) because call vol remains quite high. Alternatively, producers 
wanting to retain full exposure to price upside from geopolitical escalation might 
cheapen protection against moderate price downside with put spreads, i.e. by also 
selling far out-of-the-money put options as price-sensitive strategic restocking 
demand (e.g. from China) is likely to limit extreme price downside.  
Oil Refiners  
To oil refiners we recommend locking in deferred diesel margins from late 2027 
n
because a still very high market risk premium leaves deferred diesel margins well 
above our base case and especially above our downside case for diesel margins 
(Exhibit 9, left panel).
