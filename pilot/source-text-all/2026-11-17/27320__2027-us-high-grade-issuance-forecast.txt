Another year of record gross issuance: We forecast $2.1tr of US HG gross supply 
for 2027, up 5% YoY.
Elevated maturities and coupon income make record gross more digestible: We 
estimate $1tr of maturities, about flat YoY which leads to net issuance up 13% to 
$1.06tr. This equates to +11.2% of the current size of the market versus a long-term 
average of 11.0%. In other words, gross issuance looks higher than it is from a net 
absorption standpoint. Coupon income will rise to $556bn (+12% YoY). Thus, ‘net 
net’ supply should climb to $508bn (+14% YoY vs +202% this past year) which is 
modestly below the 2020 record. We also estimate about $22bn in calls and a material 
step up in tenders in 2027, both further sources of reinvestment cash.  
In Tech we trust: We forecast Tech and Media to issue $612bn (+23% YoY) together, 
of which $225bn is from hyperscalers (-3% YoY) and $225bn from data centers and 
GPU financing deals. This means Technology and Media together account for 
$532bn, or 50%, of market-wide net issuance. Under our forecasts, Tech goes from 
12.1% of the index currently to 14.5% by YE 2027 and overall TMT goes from 19.6% 
to 22.6% of the index. This would take the HG Tech sector alone to $1.5tr or the same 
size as the entire HY index currently! On the flip side, Energy supply falls 34% YoY 
and Basic Industries, Capital Goods and Consumer fall ~26%. 
Thematic issuance drivers: To the upside, stronger ‘animal spirits’ could drive M&A 
financing above our expectations (we forecast only $144bn in M&A supply, down 
52% YoY after a record 2026). Additionally, commercial paper usage is very high 
which could translate into incremental front-end supply if term funding picks up. To 
the downside, the key risk is that AI capex plans are cut leading to less Tech supply. 
Also, Non-USD issuance hit a record $253bn, up 31% YoY and 13.3% of USD 
issuance, the highest share post-GFC. We forecast $275bn of non-USD supply in 
2027, with some risk to the upside as issuers add new currencies to their funding mix 
vs. to the downside as non-USD Tech exposures have ramped up significantly already.
Shorter duration of supply: Issuers shortened by 0.3yr as yields rose YTD. In 2027, 
issuance may skew shorter as GPU deals, which have shorter tenors, take center stage 
in the next leg of AI capex and as M&A, which typically skews long-duration, slows.

Another record gross supply year with $2.1tr forecast for 
2027, with a heavier net issuance technical
Gross issuance: We forecast a record $2.1tr of HG gross issuance in 2027, up 5% YoY, 
marking another step higher in a market where $2tr+ annual supply may increasingly 
become the norm as the market continues to grow (JULI par value just hit $9.5tr). 
Several of the forces that drove record issuance in 2026 should persist into next year, 
including continued AI-related financing and elevated refinancing needs, although the 
composition of supply is changing: Technology issuance is expected to increase another 
28% to $495bn, while Media/Entertainment rises 7% to $117bn. Combined, the two 
sectors should account for nearly 30% of total HG supply in 2027. This is partially 
offset by a normalization in other sectors, most notably Energy, where issuance falls 
34% to $62bn, and M&A-related financing, which we expect to decline materially after 
a banner 2026. Thus, supply will remain unusually concentrated in sectors funding the 
AI infrastructure buildout which increases the sensitivity of our aggregate forecast to the 
AI capex cycle.
There are two-sided risks to our forecast: On the upside, stronger ‘animal spirits’ could 
drive M&A financing above our expectations, while the current elevated stock of 
Commercial Paper could provide incremental front-end supply if issuers increasingly 
term out short-term funding amidst fears of more Fed rate hikes. Conversely, we expect 
Technology and Media/Entertainment issuance to remain exceptionally high, creating 
downside risk if AI capex plans are revised lower or the next wave of data-center and 
associated GPU financings is delayed. Our credit analysts believe most 2027 data-center 
projects are already funded, limiting the immediate impact from the Blue Wave, but see 
greater uncertainty around the timing and financing of 2028+ projects as permitting and 
development become more difficult in several key states (link). Non-USD issuance 
represents another downside risk as the hyperscalers have grown the menu of funding 
currencies significantly over the past year, beyond just the more liquid markets such as 
EUR and GBP.
Maturities and net issuance: We expect $1.04tr of maturities in 2027, about flat YoY, 
while gross issuance increases $100bn. As a result, net issuance rises 13% to a record 
$1.06tr. This equates to +11.2% of the current size of the market versus a LT average of 
11.0%, so well shy of a record high. The increase is also highly concentrated as 
Technology and Media/Entertainment together account for $532bn, or 50% of market-
wide net issuance. Under our forecasts and certain assumptions on index eligibility, 
Tech goes from 12.1% of the index currently to 14.5% by YE 2027 and overall 
TMT goes from 19.6% to 22.6% of the index. This would take the HG Tech sector 
alone to $1.5tr or the same size as the entire HY index currently! Note as well that 
2028 maturities are 10% higher than 2027, which is a factor in determining the amount 
of cash available to many index tracking funds that sell bonds once they reach 12m to 
maturity.
Coupon income and ‘net net’ issuance: We estimate $556bn of coupon income in 
2027, up 12% YoY, which offsets over half of net issuance and leaves $508bn of ‘net 
net’ supply, up 14% YoY but still just shy of the $512bn record in 2020. Thus, roughly 
$1.6tr of cash is being returned to investors through maturities and coupons alone, with 
upside risk to this figure from calls and tenders which we don’t factor in given their 
unpredictability but discuss in further detail below.

Coupon income provides an additional and growing source of cash returned to investors. 
We estimate $556bn of coupon income in 2027, up $60bn or 12% from $496bn in 
2026. We estimate this based on the existing debt stock and then add an estimate for 
1H27 issuance that will pay its first coupon in 2H27. Combined with maturities, this 
takes total cash returned to investors to $1.59tr in 2027, up from $1.56tr in 2026. For 
2028, we project $476bn of coupons from the existing universe and estimate another 
roughly $95bn from bonds issued in 2027 and 1H28. Thus the trajectory remains 
higher into 2028, with maturities and coupons stepping up to $1.72tr in aggregate. 
Our coupon estimates assume new bonds are issued at approximately the current 6.12% 
JULI yield and continue to pay coupons semi-annually (we do this in order for the 
2027/28 estimated coupon to be comparable to the 2026 actual coupon, which counts 
bonds issued in 1H26 that paid a coupon in 2H26).

M&A debt: Animal spirits likely to cool after a banner 2026
M&A-related issuance was a meaningful driver of the increase in HG supply in 2026, 
with $295bn funded YTD and another $7bn pending, taking expected M&A-related 
issuance to $302bn, or 15% of total supply, the highest share since 2018. Importantly, 
the return of M&A “animal spirits” has been relatively benign for credit despite the 
heavier volume, with not a lot of mega-deals: Average M&A bond deal size declined to 
$4.9bn from $7.4bn in 2016 and $6.4bn in 2018. We expect these animal spirits to 
cool in 2027, forecasting $144bn of M&A-related issuance, down 52% YoY and 
equivalent to 7% of our $2.1tr gross issuance forecast. If realized, this share of M&A 
issuance will be the lowest since 2020. Of this, $87bn is associated with transactions 
already announced and expected to fund next year, while we assume another $57bn 
from transactions yet to be announced. Sector-wise, Healthcare/Pharma should remain 
the largest source of M&A financing at $50bn, or 35% of our 2027 forecast, followed by 
Consumer at $20bn, Capital Goods at $18bn and Transportation at $15bn.
Our lower forecast reflects an expectation that 2026’s active pace of acquisition 
financing moderates, with the post-election policy backdrop providing an additional 
potential constraint on deal activity. Thus, while stronger-than-expected animal spirits is 
an upside risk to our $144bn forecast, our base case is for M&A to shift from a major 
supply driver in 2026 to a more modest contributor in 2027.

Floater issuance likely to maintain its 2026 pace
Floating-rate issuance remained elevated at $103bn in 2026 YTD, or 5.4% of total 
supply, albeit down from its 2025 peak of $118bn, or 7.2%. Historically, lower policy 
rates have coincided with a greater use of floating-rate debt. Since 2010, floaters have 
averaged 10.2% of total issuance when the Fed funds rate was below 4.0%, versus just 
6.2% when it was at or above 4%. While floater issuance tended to rise alongside Fed 
funds earlier in the cycle, that relationship has disappeared more recently, with 
essentially no correlation between Fed funds and floater issuance share since 2022. This 
makes intuitive sense from an issuer perspective as lower or declining rates increase the 
appeal of floating-rate debt as coupons reset lower, while higher or rising rates increase 
that coupon burden and the incentive to lock in fixed-rate funding. With Fed funds 
currently at 4% and markets pricing roughly 60bp of hikes in 2027, the rate backdrop 
therefore argues against a meaningful increase in floater issuance next year.
However, spread economics have moved in the other direction and should provide some 
offset. The spread premium on matched floater versus fixed-rate bonds widened from 
just 5bp in 2022 to 21.5bp in 2025, before narrowing to 15.8bp in 2026. The 6bp 
improvement this year makes floaters relatively less expensive for issuers on a spread 
basis, even if the prospect of higher reference rates makes the all-in coupon less 
attractive. Indeed, since 2023 the floater-fixed spread differential has shown a stronger 
relationship with the share of floater issuance (+52% correlation) than with the Fed 
funds rate (-33%), suggesting relative pricing and supply-demand dynamics may be 
more relevant to floater issuance than the outright level of policy rates.
Thus looking to 2027, a shallow Fed hiking cycle should limit issuers’ appetite to 
increase floating-rate exposure, while the improvement in relative spread economics is 
an offset. On balance, we expect floater issuance to remain around the same pace as in 
2026.

Elevated commercial paper could provide an incremental 
tailwind to front-end issuance
Non-financial commercial paper (CP) outstanding remains elevated at $375bn, 
having risen $77bn over the past three months and $40bn over the past year. The 
3m increase ranks in the 99th percentile since 2001, with balances now again 
approaching the $390bn peak reached in March. The increase has also proven more 
persistent than expected. Our Short-Term Fixed Income strategists noted in 1H that the 
2026 increase in CP appeared to reflect a combination of corporates bolstering liquidity 
amid elevated macro uncertainty and using CP as a temporary funding bridge while the 
relative cost of longer-term financing remained less attractive. At the time, they 
expected balances to moderate with typical 2H seasonality. Instead, CP outstanding 
declined only briefly through July before rising sharply again and is now approaching 
its March peak.
Historically, periods of rapid CP accumulation have tended to be followed by 
stronger front-end bond issuance, suggesting at least some of these balances are 
eventually termed out. Since 2001, there have been 76 months when the 6m increase in 
CP outstanding ranked in the top quartile; over the subsequent 6m, 1-3yr non-financial 
issuance increased 10.4% YoY on average and rose in 56 of the 76 observations, or 
74%. The relationship strengthens when the relative economics of term funding 
improve. Among the 38 observations where a top-quartile CP increase was subsequently 
accompanied by a narrowing in the 3yr corporate bond-to-CP yield differential, front-
end issuance increased 14.4% YoY on average and rose in 31 cases, or 82%. This 
suggests the current elevated stock of CP could provide an incremental tailwind to short-
dated HG issuance in 2027, particularly if the 3yr corporate bond-to-CP yield 
differential narrows from current levels.
For now, however, the economics still favor CP. The 3yr JULI yield is 5.30% versus a 
4.02% Tier 1 non-financial CP rate, leaving a 128bp term-funding premium that has 
widened 89bp YTD. While the current differential is not particularly extreme over the 
full history, its sharp widening this year helps explain why issuers have had little 
incentive to term out CP balances thus far. We therefore would not assume that the 
entire increase in CP translates into bond issuance. Rather, a narrowing in the 3yr bond-
to-CP yield differential would strengthen the case for some of today’s elevated CP 
balances to migrate into the bond market, providing upside risk to front-end non-
financial supply in 2027.

Corporate hybrids are becoming a more meaningful part of US

Corporate hybrid issuance has undergone a structural step-change since Moody's revised 
its methodology in February 2024 to allow qualifying hybrids to receive 50% equity 
credit with a minimum initial life of 30 years. Prior to the change, annual USD 
corporate hybrid issuance averaged less than $10bn; issuance increased to an average

on credit metrics.

temporary burst in issuance following the methodology change. The USD corporate 
hybrid market has grown to approximately $135bn, but remains just 1.4% of the broader 
US HG bond market. Meanwhile, the product itself has become more standardized as 
92% of USD hybrid deals issued since 2024 have had 30-year maturities, consistent 
with Moody's revised equity-credit framework, while roughly 73% of 2025-26 USD 
hybrid volume included coupon floors. In our view, the combination of a larger, more 
established investor base, a more standardized structure and continued elevated funding 
needs among ratings-sensitive issuers should keep hybrids an increasingly relevant 
component of US IG supply in 2027. We published a detailed piece on this market here.

Duration of supply is likely to decline as rates remain high and 
GPU supply takes center stage
The tenor of HG issuance shortened in 2026 to 9.6y from 9.9y in 2025 as borrowing 
costs moved higher. Issuers appear to be particularly sensitive to changes in long-end 
borrowing costs rather than the absolute level of yields. Over the past year, the 
correlation between the deviation in the monthly long-end issuance share versus its 
trailing 12-month average and the change in 30yr JULI yields versus the prior three-
month average has been -71%. Periods of rapidly rising yields have therefore generally 
coincided with issuers shortening their funding, while more gradual changes in yields 
have had a smaller impact on tenor decisions. This remains relevant heading into 2027 
with the JULI yield currently at 6.12% and the cost of refinancing legacy debt also 
rising sharply: the average coupon on new issuance was 283bp above the coupon on 
maturing debt in October, the widest differential since November 2023. Issuers are 
therefore not only refinancing at higher outright yields, but locking in a substantially 
higher coupon than on the debt being replaced. If rates remain elevated, we expect this 
to continue to encourage issuers to limit the duration over which they lock in today’s 
higher funding costs.
The changing composition of AI-related financing should add to this trend. Hyperscalers 
and data centers have been important sources of long-duration supply, but both moved 
shorter in 2026. The weighted-average maturity of hyperscaler issuance declined from 
18.6 years in 2025 to 15.1 years in 2026, while data-center issuance declined from 23.6 
years to 17.2 years. Issuance excluding hyperscalers and data centers was already 
considerably shorter at 9.1 years in 2026, versus 9.6 years in 2025. Meanwhile, the next 
leg of AI financing is shifting toward GPU-related debt, which typically carries shorter 
maturities of ~5-7 years, reflecting rapid hardware obsolescence, uncertain residual 
values and the need to align repayment with contracted customer cash flows. If GPU 
financings continue to skew shorter than hyperscaler and data-center debt, the changing 
mix of AI supply will provide an additional drag on aggregate issuance duration. The 
other contributing factor to long-end supply is M&A which we expect to decline 
materially in 2027 as discussed above. Taken together, alongside elevated borrowing 
costs, a shift towards GPU financing deals within the AI complex and low M&A-related 
issuance, we expect the average tenor of HG supply to move lower again in 2027.

center borrowers, and Banks together accounted for $314bn, or 82%, of the YoY 
increase in gross supply. At the other end of the market, issuer breadth has narrowed 
sharply. Five sectors outside Banks and TMT, which we classify as the “physical 
economy” (Property/Real Estate, Consumer Cyclicals, Consumer Non-Cyclicals, 
Energy, Basic Industries, Transportation), have a relatively high share of 2025 issuers 
that have not returned to the market in 2026. These sectors now account for only 25% of 
issuers and 17% of YTD supply, both the lowest in our data and down from 33% and 
26% in 2025, respectively. This does not simply reflect lower refinancing needs, as 
maturities for these sectors are only 6% lower YoY. Some of the decline reflects a shift 
toward EUR or other currency markets, but there is no obvious explanation for much of 
the retrenchment. The combination of record borrowing by the largest issuers and 
declining participation across a significant portion of the remaining market is therefore 
making the IG primary market considerably more concentrated.

US Banks (Kabir Caprihan): We expect issuance to be $235bn, down 4% from 2026. 
For U.S. GSIBs, we expect total USD issuance to decrease slightly to $182bn, 
consisting of $150bn in senior holdco supply (6% decline from YTD 2026), $21bn in 
bank-level senior notes (3% decline from YTD 2026), and $11bn in subordinated holdco 
debt (15% decline from YTD 2026). Consistent with our prior supply outlooks, the 
senior holdco forecast of $150bn for 2027 is expected to come from the eight U.S. 
GSIBs identified by the Federal Reserve. We estimate $169.5bn of relevant maturities 
for U.S. GSIBs at the holding company level across all currencies. Regarding non-USD 
senior holdco issuance, we project $22bn to occur in 2027. Since 2015, non-USD as a 
percentage of total senior holdco supply has fluctuated but averaged ~22%, with YTD 
2026 coming in at 22% and 2025 at 18%. We expect lower non-USD funding and see 
the allocation for non-USD issuance at about 15% of overall senior holdco debt. If the 
basis between dollars and non-USD currencies decreases, we expect increased USD 
issuance, while an increase in the basis would incentivize U.S. GSIBs to issue a larger 
portion of their senior holdco debt in other currencies. Subordinated issuance is 
generally a function of Tier 2 capital amortization for the large U.S. GSIBs as well as 
capital requirements. We are assuming $11bn of T2 supply comes to market in 2027 
among U.S. GSIBs compared to $13bn that occurred in 2026 YTD.
Turning to US Regional Banks, we expect total supply to increase 4% from YTD levels 
to about $53bn. Within the sector, we think senior holdco supply will be about $35bn,

and meaningfully greater than the historical average. We do expect bank-level issuance

historical average. Lastly, we see holdco subordinated issuance to decline from YTD

our issuance estimate, Ally Financial, Charles Schwab, and Synchrony Financial are

Explanation of Credit Research Valuation Methodology, Ratings and Risk to Ratings:
 J.P. Morgan uses a bond-level rating system that incorporates valuations (relative value) and our fundamental view on the security. Our 
fundamental credit view of an issuer is based on the company's underlying credit trends, overall creditworthiness and our opinion on whether the 
issuer will be able to service its debt obligations when they become due and payable. We analyze, among other things, the company's cash flow 
capacity and trends and standard credit ratios, such as gross and net leverage, interest coverage and liquidity ratios. We also analyze profitability, 
capitalization and asset quality, among other variables, when assessing financials. Analysts also rate the issuer, based on the rating of the 
benchmark or representative security. Unless we specify a different recommendation for the company’s individual securities, an issuer 
recommendation applies to all of the bonds at the same level of the issuer’s capital structure. We may also rate certain loans and preferred 
securities, as applicable. This report also sets out within it the material underlying assumptions used. We use the following ratings for bonds 
(issues), issuers, loans, and preferred securities: Overweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to 
outperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark); Neutral (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to perform 
in line with the relevant index, sector, or benchmark); and Underweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected 
to underperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark). For CDS, we use the following rating system: Long Risk (over the next three months, 
the credit return on the recommended position is expected to exceed the relevant index, sector or benchmark); Neutral (over the next three 
months, the credit return on the recommended position is expected to match the relevant index, sector or benchmark); and Short Risk (over the 
next three months, the credit return on the recommended position is expected to underperform the relevant index, sector or benchmark).
