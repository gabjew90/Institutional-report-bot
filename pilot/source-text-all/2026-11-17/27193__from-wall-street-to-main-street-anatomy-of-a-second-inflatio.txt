In Summary 
• At the rig, energy has eased on de-escalation hopes but stays elevated. At the pump, 
refining is the bottleneck. After the US midterms and President Trump’s signal that further 
strikes on Iran are not needed, Brent is trading around USD90/bbl, down more than 20% 
from its late-October peak. Crude flows have almost returned to pre-war levels, but a 
geopolitical premium, higher freight costs and stock rebuilding is keeping prices elevated. 
We expect Brent to average USD100/bbl in Q4, lifted by the October peak, and fall close 
to pre-war levels, to USD73/bbl by end-2027. A Hormuz deal (guaranteed navigation, 
insurance and escorts, phased US sanctions relief for Iranian nuclear limits) would 
compress the premium further. European gas is averaging EUR85/MWh in Q4 and should 
fall to EUR45/MWh by end-2027, still 40% above pre-war levels. With about 3.5mn b/d of 
refining capacity offline and crack spreads at USD45-75/bbl, wholesale diesel is up 100-
115% ytd (US pump +70%, Europe +35%) and should stay high until Q2 2027. 
• The shock reaches the shopping basket with a lag and compounds through food. 
Producer prices are up 6.3% y/y in the US and 11.1% in the eurozone, and China exports 
inflation (export prices +15% y/y) despite weak domestic demand. Food follows: eurozone 
producer prices peak around Q1 2027 and consumer food inflation later in Q2-Q3, with the 
temperature effects associated with a strong El Niño potentially adding up to +0.5pp to EU 
food inflation. Headline inflation therefore rises even as energy falls: the US goes from 4.0% 
to a peak of 4.3% in early 2027 (3.3% for the year), the eurozone from 4.0% to 4.4% in Q1 
2027 (3.0%) and Japan from 2.1% to 2.2% (1.7%), against 2022 peaks of 9.0% in the US, and 
10.6% in the eurozone. Emerging markets with weak currencies, and dependent on energy 
and food imports, are the hotspots (India, Philippines, Egypt, Nigeria, Türkiye). In a 
downside scenario with Brent staying above USD100/bbl, 2027 inflation could average 
4.5% in the US and 4.8% in the eurozone. 
• Containment requires more tightening from central banks, but we don’t see AI 
triggering second-round effects. High near-term inflation expectations could feed into 
wage demands, but a wage-price spiral looks unlikely: US wage growth is 3.1% against a 
2022 peak of 5.6%, eurozone labor markets are back to pre-Covid tightness, fiscal support 
is far smaller and real rates are at decade highs. We expect the Fed to hike three more 
times (Dec, Jan, Mar) to 4.50-4.75% before cutting to 3.75% by end-2028. The ECB goes to 
3.00% (Dec, Mar) and then 2.25%, the BoE to 4.5% (Dec, Mar) and then 3.25%, and the BoJ 
to 1.75% (Dec, Mar) before holding. Emerging markets moved first, frontloading hikes that 
peaked in Q2-Q3 2026. South Africa, India, Philippines, Colombia and Czechia still plan 
more. Regarding whether AI capex and wealth effects build pent-up demand, as in 2022, 
we do not see it. We estimate AI adds only +0.3pp to US inflation in 2026-27 through 
investment, wealth effects and energy demand, before turning disinflationary as labor 
demand shrinks, wages are capped and productivity rises, though the timing is highly 
uncertain. 
• On Wall Street, bonds finally got the inflation memo while risky assets continue to shrug 
off the inflation scare. After the Treasury sell-off and repricing of Fed hikes this week, 
markets have caught up with our scenario. Our forecasts for long-term yields stay elevated: 
the US 10-year ends 2026 at 5.00% and 2027 at 4.75%, the Bund at 3.30% and 3.50%.

On Main Street, wages do not compensate, and the shock is spurring protests. Real 
wages are falling: US -0.9% y/y and -1.2% in the eurozone in Q4, so households lose 
purchasing power. Policymakers have cushioned the blow with fuel tax cuts and pump 
price discounts (Spain, Germany), energy bill caps (Italy), subsidies (Japan) and minimum 
wage hikes (Germany, France, Spain) but fiscal space is far smaller than in 2022. 75% of 
the Allianz Pulse survey respondents expect living costs to rise by more than 2% next year, 
and fears for job losses because of AI have surged, especially in Europe. Affordability 
protests have spread across France, Italy, Spain, Belgium and other countries, many of 
them in the “middle-resilience trap” of our Social Resilience Index, where prosperity 
coexists with polarization and weak reform capacity. 
 
Inflation under pressure from commodity prices 
 
Inflation is accelerating on both sides of the Atlantic. Eurozone HICP inflation rose to +4.0% y/y in October (from 
+3.8% in September), with energy inflation accelerating to +20.8% y/y (from +18.9%), while services inflation rose to 
+3.3% y/y and food inflation to +1.6% y/y. Importantly, core inflation remained steady at 2.5% y/y. In the US, CPI 
inflation accelerated to +4.0% y/y in October1, while core inflation rose to +2.9% from +2.6% in September. Prices 
accelerated across the board. Energy remains the main driver, accelerating again, to +21.2% y/y, while services 
inflation edged up to +3.6% (+0.4pp); food inflation also accelerated (to +2.8%, +0.4pp) as well as goods inflation 
(+0.9%, +0.2pp). This suggests that the pass-through from higher input prices into core consumer prices is starting to 
be increasingly visible.  
 
Inflationary pressures remain uneven, with energy prices still elevated and pass-through ongoing, but far milder 
than in 2022-23. Our inflation transmission scorecard (see Table 1) points to a clear divergence between persistent 
upstream pressures and relatively contained indirect and second-round effects. Direct shocks remain elevated, but 
the pass-through to broader inflation is still uneven. Refining capacity disruptions and fuel inventory pressures have 
already reached or exceeded their 2022 crisis thresholds (100–114%). Meanwhile, oil and gas prices remain at 
around 70% of their previous stress levels, although wholesale electricity prices in Germany have reached 82%. The 
transmission to producer and consumer prices remains more contained: most indirect indicators stand at 6–60% of 
their 2022 peaks, although food commodity prices (85%) and Chinese export prices (96%) suggest that upstream 
inflationary pressures remain significant.

Producer prices (PPI) are accelerating, which raises the risk of CPI pass-through over the next four to six months. 
In the US, PPI rose by 6.3% y/y in September, about 55% of its 2022 peak. With around 50% of the CPI basket exposed 
to this transmission, our analysis suggests at least another +0.3pp of upside to CPI in the coming months, from 
products where retail prices have so far failed to fully offset the rise in producer prices. In the eurozone, PPI grew by 
+11.1% y/y in September, the highest level since 2023 but still well below the 2022 peak of +40% y/y (see Table 1). 
Eurozone firms’ selling price expectations point the same way: the net balance of firms expecting to raise prices in 
the coming three months rose to around 21 points on average across sectors in October (from 18 in September), 
against an average of more than 40 points in 2022. Expectations are highest in metals, travel, electronics and 
warehousing, followed by land transport, food and retail, with the sharpest rise since February in food products (+23 
points in the eurozone, +43 points in Germany) – see Figure 1.  
 
The survey is closely tied to producer prices, with manufacturing PPI at a one-month lag in the eurozone, and leads 
headline HICP inflation by around four months. The lead is longer at the sector level: six months for services, eight 
for processed food and nine for manufactured goods. Transmission runs first through producer prices (after four to 
five months for food and five to seven months for manufactured goods across the eurozone, Germany and France) 
and then to consumer prices (a further three to five months for food and one to two for manufactured goods). Around 
half of food producer price increases reach consumers in the eurozone and Germany, about 40% in France and 30-
40% for manufactured goods. On this basis, we estimate that the survey adds around +0.6pp to eurozone headline 
inflation by June 2027, across the 86% of the basket that excludes energy and unprocessed food (services 47%, 
manufactured goods 25%, processed food 14%). Processed food accounts for most of it (+0.36pp to overall inflation), 
followed by manufactured goods (+0.22pp), while services add only +0.03pp.

Beyond crude oil: refining bottlenecks to sustain inflationary pressures until at 
least mid-2027 
 
Crude oil markets: fundamentals are improving but the geopolitical premium remains historically elevated. In 
October, Middle Eastern crude exports have peaked at 16mn b/d, back to pre-war levels, with flows through Hormuz 
at 80% of January level. The recovery is supported by alternative export routes and increasing military-protected 
shipments. Yet Brent prices remain above USD90/bbl, still reflecting a substantial geopolitical risk premium and 
potential future demand from countries looking to replenish their stocks as the conflict drags on and to benefit from 
higher refining margins. Crude oil imports in China have increased by 9.4% m/m in October, reaching almost 10 
mb/d, the highest level recorded since March, as the country is showing early signs of inventory rebuilding. Markets 
are not only pricing barrels currently available but also the risk that renewed attacks could interrupt supply again. 
However, against the backdrop of improving supply fundamentals, our longer-term oil price assumption still takes 
into account some price consolidation but starting from a higher level - leaving our annual average forecast at 
USD80/bbl for 2027 (+5% compared to the previous forecast). Although upside risks would increase if prices 
remained above USD100/bbl beyond mid-November as there could be a short-lived military escalation after the US 
midterms and before a deal is reached. A Hormuz deal is the main downside risk to prices. It could combine 
guaranteed freedom of navigation, insurance and escort arrangements, and phased US sanctions relief in exchange 
for Iranian nuclear limits. This would compress both the risk premium and freight costs. Until then, upside risks would 
rise if prices stayed above USD100/bbl beyond mid-November, as a short-lived military escalation is possible after 
the US midterms and before a deal is reached. Our 2027 forecast assumes some price consolidation, but from a 
higher starting level. 
 
Additionally, shipping costs are another reason why recovering crude supply has not translated into lower 
delivered prices. Vessel rates between the Middle East and China remain around USD1.4mn per day, more than 12 
times pre-war levels, with insurance and shipping rates at historical levels. Crude transportation costs have risen 
from around USD3/bbl before the conflict to roughly USD20-25/bbl, despite a recent fall after the US mid-term 
elections. On top of this, the recovery in exports has itself increased demand for tankers. Ship-to-ship transfers, 
protected convoys and longer shipping routes require more vessels to transport the same volumes of crude and keep 
freight rates elevated. This has led to increasing regional price divergence, with crude delivered to European and 
Asian refineries carrying a high premium while surplus regions like North America now face large discounts. Brent is 
also benefiting from the proximity of North Sea supply to European refineries, making it a less representative

More importantly for inflation, refined products have increasingly decoupled from crude oil, meaning lower 
Brent prices may not translate into an equivalent reduction in pump prices. A combination of refinery outages in 
the Middle East and Russia, as well as sustained export restrictions throughout this year have increasingly tightened 
refined product markets. Disruptions to energy infrastructure in the Middle East and Russia have left around 3.5mn 
b/d of refining capacity offline, while depleted inventories and export restrictions have pushed diesel crack spreads 
to historically high levels. In November, despite diesel markets remaining structurally tight, crack spreads have 
eased to USD45/bbl in the US and USD75/bbl in Europe, driven by increased releases from strategic reserves and 
the resumption of Russian exports. On top of this, high refining margins do not necessarily translate into higher 
refiners’ profitability  as rising freight costs are compressing their margins  particularly in Asia  limiting the incenti e 
to increase output. As a result, wholesale diesel prices have surged by +100-115% ytd, while at the pump diesel prices 
have increased by 70% ytd in the US and by 35% ytd in Europe, reflecting the diluting effect of taxes, distribution 
costs and lag in retail price adjustment. We expect wholesale prices to remain elevated until Q2 2027 before 
gradually easing, even as prices at the pump remain high. In our baseline scenario, the final retail price increase 
relative to January 2026 should moderate from 20-40% across major economies in Q4 2026 to 12-27% by Q2 2027, 
before largely stabilizing by year-end. Governments could cushion the impact through fuel-tax adjustments, 
particularly in Europe, where taxes account for around 35-50% of pump prices, compared with around 10% in the 
US. However, as direct fuel inflation fades, power and food prices could take over as the main channels of 
inflationary pressure in 2027. While higher pump prices are transmitted relatively quickly to fuel-intensive industries, 
the pass-through to gas, electricity and food prices can take one to three quarters, with broader spillovers to other 
sectors extending beyond two quarters. Our inflation transmission scorecard confirms that direct energy-price 
pressures have already reached or exceeded their 2022 reference levels for several indicators (see Table 1), while 
most indirect effects on food prices, producer prices and margins remain well below previous peaks. We continue to 
expect a delayed pass-through, likely to sustain consumer-price pressures into H2 2027.

Climate risk could reinforce this delayed transmission, just as the direct energy contribution begins to decline. 
The current energy crisis is happening at the same time as a super El Nino phenomenon, which has already produced 
extreme weather conditions in some regions, with an effect on 2026 and 2027 harvest outputs. The temperature 
effects associated with a strong El Nino could add +0.5pp to EU food inflation and +0.29pp to US food inflation in 
20272. This means that even if diesel and fertilizer prices normalize, production losses could add a second layer of 
food inflation on top of the energy shock.  
 
Beyond food, the energy shock is spreading across intermediate goods, but the full pass-through has yet to reach 
consumer products. In the eurozone, the PPI for intermediate goods rose by around +7% y/y in September, and those 
for non-durable consumer goods were still decreasing by 0.8%. This suggests that a large share of the pass-through 
remains ahead. This has a fast, direct impact on transport and therefore energy-intensive industries such as freight, 
mining and construction, which in turn transmits higher costs to the manufacturers purchasing their outputs, 
including chemicals, plastics and metals. As contracts are renewed and inventories depleted, this gradually reaches 
vehicle prices, household equipment and packaged goods. Transport-intensive services, including logistics, 
deliveries and passenger transport, face additional direct fuel-cost pressures. This helps explain why intermediate-
goods PPI is already rising strongly while consumer-goods prices have been slower to respond. While direct energy 
inflation is expected to decline sharply throughout 2027, delayed cost transmission should partly offset this 
disinflation.  
 
China is set to become a source of inflation for the rest of the world as inflationary pressures behind its export 
prices become broad-based and the CNY has strongly appreciated this year. This is in contrast with    na’s 
domestic situation, where consumer inflation is likely to remain limited for longer in the context of soft consumer 
spending. China's PPI returned to positive territory in March for the first time since September 2022, driven by the 
surge in energy prices following the closure of Hormuz. Export prices quickly followed, turning positive in April for 
the first time in three years, and accelerated sharply over the summer, reaching +15.1% y/y in August, only marginally 
below the June 2022 peak of +15.7%. On top of this, the Chinese yuan has appreciated 4.6% ytd against the USD and 
9% against the euro, further increasing prices of imported Chinese goods in Europe or the US. The composition of 
Chinese exported inflation has fundamentally shifted compared to the 2022 episode. For the first time since the late 
2000s, a clear bifurcation has emerged within the export price basket. HS 84 and 85 goods categories, which include 
machinery and electronics, including AI-related goods, have surged on the back of the AI boom and persistent supply 
tightness, reaching +27.6% y/y, while the rest of Chinese exported goods remained in deflationary territory until July, 
when they crossed back into positive ground at +0.3% y/y. This divergence carries important implications: 
Inflationary pressures emanating from China are only beginning to spill over to the rest of the world. With PPI 
continuing to climb, reaching +5.7% y/y in October, the bulk of the pass-through from producer to export prices is

Second-round effects remain contained for now, but forward-looking indicators point to rising inflation risks in 
2027. Labor markets are not fueling an inflation spiral: US wage growth stands at 3.1% y/y in November, and should 
accelerate only modestly to 3.4% and 3.5% in 2027-28 respectively. In the eurozone, the vacancy rate has returned 
to its pre-Covid level of 2.1% as labor shortages recede. Unemployment rates of 4.1% in the US and 6.5% in the 
eurozone also point to less labor-market tightness than in 2022, with wage pressures reflecting catch-up effects 
rather than a self-reinforcing spiral (Figure 11). We expect wage growth of +2.8% and +2.9% in 2027-28, respectively, 
with +3.0% and +3.3% in Germany and +2.4% and +2.3% in France. Unlike in 2022, the private sector lacks the 
demand cushion since purchasing power should continue to contract until early 2027 while firms will also need to 
continue absorbing part of the external cost shock through their margins. Overall, fiscal support is much more limited 
than in 2022, when European governments deployed substantial packages, above EUR180bn in the big 4 eurozone 
countries against EUR20bn since February 2026.

In our latest Allianz Pulse survey3, we asked respondents across eight countries which issue concerns them most 
right now – and the leading public concern is inflation. It is the single most-named issue, cited by 33% of 
respondents overall and rising to 46% in the UK, 41% in the US, 37% in France and 30% in Germany (Table 2). Grouped 
more broadly, economic issues, comprising inflation, jobs and housing, account for half of all top-of-mind issues 
across countries, climbing to 63% in the US and 57% in the UK, and dwarfing the next tier of healthcare (12%), 
immigration (8%), geopolitics (7%) and climate (5%). Notable is both the level of concern and its uniformity: Inflation 
tops the ranking of concerns for every generation and gender, and in every country except Poland, where healthcare 
narrowly leads. Cost-of-living pressure is therefore the key public concern that unites young and old, rich and poor, 
north and south. That breadth matters for the inflation outlook, because a public this attuned to prices is one whose 
expectations, wage demands and spending behavior can themselves feed the inflation drivers. At the same time, 
job worries are mounting: 28% of all respondents across expect AI to replace their job entirely (7%) or in significant 
part (21%) within the next five years. Pessimism about the impact of AI on jobs is deepest in Europe, led by Austria, 
France and Germany, while US respondents are least pessimistic. Greater job insecurity could temper wage 
demands even as price concerns intensify, one channel through which AI may weaken wage pressures.

US: Low fuel taxes and AI-fueled demand amplify the pass-through, forcing three more Fed hikes by Q1 2027 
 
We raised our US CPI inflation forecast for 2027 by +0.7pp to 3.3%, a bigger revision than in Europe. Peak inflation 
should be reached in early 2027, at around +4.3%, but core inflation will accelerate at above +3% by early 2027 and 
peak at around +3.7% in June-July. After 3.5% annual average in 2026 (revised up +0.1pp from our September 
economic outlook), we now expect sequential headline CPI to hover around +4.1-4.3% y/y between November 2026 
and February 2027, before gradually easing to 2.5% y/y by December 2027, thanks to favorable energy base effects. 
This means we now expect headline CPI inflation at annual average +3.3% (+0.7pp). We now see core inflation much 
firmer and stickier. Three factors explain why the US gets the bigger revision. First, taxes make up only around 10% 
of US pump prices, against around half in Germany, France and Italy, so the refined-product shock hits US consumers 
about twice as hard. Second, demand is a bit stronger than anticipated, which will enable firms to pass costs on 
more easily. Business investment, powered by AI and ICT-related capex, is growing above +6% y/y, creating 
bottlenecks as demand outstrips supply. Third, leading indicators point to strong inflationary pressures persisting in 
the next few months. For instance, though ISM and PMI business surveys generally eased off a bit in October they 
remained broadly consistent with inflation significantly above 4% by Q1 2027. Input prices are rising faster than 
output prices and the PPI-CPI gap points to another +30bps of inflation.

In Japan, we expect inflation to average 1.8% this year, before easing to 1.7% in 2027, revised down as the

Underlying pressures should nonetheless keep building: we expect higher energy prices to continue feeding into

EM central banks front-loaded their response, and the hiking cycle is behind us, with risks to the baseline from 
geopolitical escalation in the Middle East and elsewhere. Tightening was concentrated in H1 2026. Colombia 
raised rates by 100bps each in Q1 and Q2 and is expected to reach a peak of 12.5% in December, hovering at around 
6pps in real terms. Indonesia delivered cumulative hikes of 100bps in Q2, with risks titled to the upside for additional 
hikes. South Africa, which added 50bps over Q2–Q3 and the Philippines, +125bp since April, are both expected to 
deliver a +25bp hike in their November and December meeting respectively, with risks tilted to the upside for further 
hikes in South Africa. Czechia started tightening in June with a 25bps hike and is expected to deliver another 25bps 
at its November meeting. South Korea (+50bps) and India (+25bps) started their tightening cycle after and we 
expect both to deliver a final +25bps hike by the end of the year. By contrast, Poland kept policy rates unchanged 
since its last cut in March, and we expect it to remain on hold as underlying inflation stays relatively contained 
despite higher energy prices. Romania has also remained on hold, but faces a more challenging trade-off between 
persistent inflation and weakening economic activity, as ongoing fiscal consolidation weighs on domestic demand 
while currency vulnerabilities limit the scope for monetary easing. Countries with high real interest rates have been 
able to look through the shock and keep cutting: Nigeria (around 10pps above the US in real terms), Brazil (9pps),

Exposure to energy imports especially from the Middle East is the first determinant of the current inflation shock: 
energy importers in Asia have taken the biggest hit. Comparing annual average inflation in 2025 with our 2026 
forecasts, the largest increases are in the Philippines (from 1.7% to 5.9%), India (from 2.2% to 4.7%), Thailand (from -

interest rate is around 1pp below the US. Inflation dynamics across CEE have been more heterogeneous, with the 
energy shock exposing differences in underlying price pressures. Poland and Hungary have seen relatively 
contained core inflation, supported by moderating wage growth and weakening consumer demand in Poland, and 
subdued underlying economic activity and currency strength in Hungary. Czechia, by contrast, continues to face 
stickier underlying inflation, particularly from wage growth and housing costs, while Romania remains the regional 
outlier, with elevated inflation, persistent services price pressures and additional risks from currency weakness, 
despite subdued domestic demand amid fiscal consolidation. 
 
The shock seems temporary for now, but food pressures, the second main pass-through channel after energy, 
could make it more persistent. In most countries, inflation will peak in 2026 or 2027 and fall back by 2028. In India, 
Indonesia, Malaysia and Colombia, the peak comes in 2027, which suggests a delayed knock-on through food, 
transport and wages. Only Morocco, with very low inflation, sees the peak in 2028. Food takes up a very large share 
of household spending in Nigeria (59%), Egypt and the Philippines (37%) and Vietnam and Morocco (35%), so higher 
fuel, fertilizer and freight costs pass through to consumer prices, and into social pressure, faster than in developed 
markets. Central Europe has seen favorable food disinflation dynamics so far, but risks are now shifting to the upside,

Looking ahead, we still expect several central banks to hike in this cycle including India, South Africa, Colombia 
and Czech Republic. Our baseline sees the shock fading in 2027–28, even though an entrench-for-longer 
geopolitical environment could bring further measures especially to those countries exposed on food imports, 
currency vulnerability and fiscal buffers. First, food pass-through could prove stickier than energy in countries 
where food takes up a large share of household spending (Nigeria, Egypt, the Philippines, Vietnam), leading to 
social pressure. Second, currencies remain vulnerable in countries with twin deficits, notably Egypt, the Philippines, 
Romania and Türkiye. Further depreciation could trigger a second round of hikes just as easing cycles resume. Third, 
fiscal buffers are uneven: Indonesia and Malaysia are absorbing part of the shock through subsidies (around 2% of 
GDP each), which explains why Malaysia's inflation peaks at only 2.2%. However, government support measures 
can also delay inflationary pressures, as the eventual withdrawal of subsidies, price controls or tax relief could lead 
to renewed price increases in 2027. This is particularly relevant in Czechia, where regulated household energy tariffs 
could rise, and Hungary, where the potential removal of food margin caps and higher excise duties could add to 
inflation. Moreover, several sovereigns run deficits of 6% of GDP or more (Brazil 8%, China 7.6%, Hungary 7.5%, Egypt 
and Poland 7%, Romania 6.4%), which leaves little room to cushion households if the shock lasts. 
 
Egypt, Türkiye and the Philippines are the weakest sovereigns in the EM asset class, given high inflation, 
depreciated currencies and limited fiscal space. Egypt’s fiscal deficit is among the highest in E s  with high inflation 
after several consecutive shocks since the Covid-19 pandemic. It also faces high interest payments (11% of GDP), a 
large share of which are in foreign currency, while also having a large trade balance deficit due to its dependence 
on imports, with the exception of energy imports. The combination of a controlled currency depreciation and 
prolonged inflation in Türkiye, on the other hand, risks weighing much longer than expected on corporate 
competitiveness, particularly for vertically integrated and energy-intensive sectors (manufacturing, metallurgy, 
agribusiness), as well as on purchasing power and inclusive growth in an already politically-charged context.

Markets: Higher-for-longer rates, a weaker euro and mounting downside risks 
 
We have revised up our 10y yield forecasts on higher inflation and a more hawkish Fed. Compared to our 
September outlook,4 higher inflation and, in the US, a temporarily higher policy rate have made us raise our US 10y 
rates forecast by +30bps to 5.00% at end-2026 and 4.75% (+25bps) at end-2027. Yields then ease further to 4.25% in 
2028 (-5bps) as markets price in the Fed's rate normalization. For 10y German Bunds, we keep our end-2026 forecast 
at 3.30% but now expect 3.50% at end-2027 (+30bps) and 3.25% at end-2028 (+25bps). Higher-for-longer inflation 
in Germany with less disinflation compared to the US keep Bund yields higher for longer. Temporary geopolitical 
escalation before year-end could see rates shortly spike to 5.4% (US) and 3.8% (Germany). With the Fed now hiking 
more than the ECB, the USD should stay strong. We cut our EURUSD forecast to 1.13 at end-2026 (from 1.16 earlier) 
and lows of 1.10, before the euro gradually recovers to 1.15 in 2027 and 2028. Our equity (14% and 8% for 2026 and 
2027 in the US, 15% and 9% in Europe) and credit outlook (80 to 90bps trading rage for investment grade in 2026 
and 2027 both in US and Europe) is broadly unchanged as the AI-fueled fundamentals theme will continue to 
dominate our forecasts leading to a temporary detachment from traditional negative pass-through effect from 
higher rates to equity performance, especially for the technology sector. However, in the path to our end-of-year 
forecasts we can have up to 6% correction in equity markets and 20bps widening in investment grade corporate 
spreads as short-term inflation and rates driven volatility temporarily passes through to risky assets.

With this in mind and just focusing on equities, earnings are doing the heavy lifting. Equities have taken the back-
up in yields largely in their stride. The S&P 500 has de-rated from 22x to 19x forward earnings this year but still trades 
within 5% of its record high, as AI-related profit growth has more than made up for the multiple compression caused 
by higher rates. Relative to bonds, stocks are no more expensive than they were two years ago, with the earnings 
yield still around 270bps above real 10y Treasury yields. Balance sheets are not the weak link either: large caps 
locked in long-dated, fixed-rate funding, and interest coverage is close to 20-year highs. If anything, higher capital 
costs are pushing management teams to invest harder in AI to protect growth, which shall keeps the earnings engine 
running. 
 
The biggest concern is a second leg of rate shocks rather than the current level of yields. Equities can live with 
rising yields, however they tend to struggle with is a disorderly move. Historically, S&P 500 returns have stayed 
positive while yields rose, unless the 1-month increase exceeded two standard deviations, roughly 40-50bps today. 
Beyond that point, returns turn negative, and more sharply when real yields lead the move (Figure 18). In our 
downside scenario, where second round effects lock in higher inflation and 10y yields push towards 5.8%, multiples 
and earnings would come under pressure at the same time, which is why we see S&P 500 returns of only +4% this 
year, against +14% in the baseline implying a double-digit correction from current levels. Long duration growth 
names, including the more speculative end of the AI trade, and small caps with heavier floating rate debt would 
bear the brunt.

While markets are shrugging off the shock, Main Street is not: the most direct impact is on affordability, and 
protests against it are spilling into Euro e’s streets. Since mid-September, cost-of-living protests have spread 
across at least ten European countries, with inflation, energy and housing as the common triggers. France, Italy, 
Spain and Belgium are most affected so far: on 17 October alone, 550,000–800,000 people marched in France, and 
insured losses from the riots of 17–18 October are estimated at up to EUR1.2bn. Our geopolitical tracker shows the 
most intense protest wave since its data begin in 2015, surpassing even the gilets jaunes peak. This is consistent with 
the Pulse results above, where inflation is the top public concern in almost every country surveyed. Most of the 
countries where people are taking to the streets sit in or around the “middle-resilience trap,” identified in our Social 
Resilience Index 2025.5 This trap describes advanced economies stuck at scores of around 65–70 out of 100, where 
prosperity coexists with polarization and weak reform capacity. Belgium (69.1), Spain (66.9) and Italy (66.8) sit inside 
the band, and France (74.7) slightly above it. Markets have historically looked through social unrest, but this time 
the protests coincide with concerns about fiscal sustainability. Eurozone sovereign stress has risen from 40bps to 
67bps since August and we estimate that around 15bps of the French OAT spread could be attributed to the protests.
