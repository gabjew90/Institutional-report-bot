COMMENTARY
Who Loses in Creative Destruction?
How Avoiding Disruption Can Enhance Returns
Market Insight
Everyone Wants to Find the Next Big Disrupter
Technological revolutions can create extraordinary wealth. Investors naturally focus 
on seeking to identify the next Amazon, Google, or Nvidia—the companies that emerge 
as dominant beneficiaries of transformative change. Entire industries have been built 
around predicting these leaders. Yet there is another side of innovation that often 
receives less initial attention: companies that will be disrupted. Both winners and losers 
are a result of what economist Joseph Schumpeter termed “creative destruction,” 
which describes the economy’s ability to grow even as it dismantles industries.
Identifying the beneficiaries of a technological revolution requires company-specific 
judgments about which innovators will execute successfully and capture an emerging 
opportunity. Vulnerable business models, by contrast, can often be identified at 
a broader industry level by understanding the nature of the innovation itself. As 
technology disrupts industries, changes in customer behavior, cost structures, and 
competitive dynamics can provide early warning signs of disruption. The businesses 
most at risk from these changes are often hiding in plain sight.
Passive investors may be more exposed to these future losers than they realize. 
Market-cap-weighted indices can be heavily invested in yesterday’s winners and may 
only gradually gain exposure to tomorrow’s champions. As a result, those who invest 
in strategies that closely follow benchmarks may enter periods of rapid innovation with 
substantial exposure to businesses susceptible to disruption.  
Technological change is about destruction as well as creation. As a result, we believe 
that investors should devote as much attention to determining which businesses are 
most vulnerable to innovation as they do to identifying its ultimate beneficiaries.
The Easier Question: Who Will Lose?
The investment community often focuses the discussion of rapid technological change 
on which companies will be the next great beneficiaries and gain large amounts of 
market share. That is, of course, a very important question. In our view, determining 
who will be the winners of innovation requires bottom-up, fundamental research and a 
specialized skill set. Diligent proprietary research including discussions with suppliers, 
customers, competitors, and other industry participants can illuminate the competitive 
positioning and economics of new products and services as well as how they could 
impact financial results.

Identifying losers might be easier because unlike 
winners, which typically must be determined one 
company at a time, many losers can be identified 
through a common business model that becomes 
structurally disadvantaged by technological change. A 
good example is retailing (see Figure 1). In our view, it 
became increasingly apparent that many department 
stores and specialty retailers would face mounting 
pressure as consumer spending migrated online. As 
e-commerce adoption accelerated, traditional retailers 
were burdened by high fixed costs, large real estate 
footprints, and labor-intensive operating models. Online 
retail offered generally higher returns on capital and 
greater convenience for consumers. While predicting the 
emerging leaders required company-specific analysis, 
the direction of disruption was more immediately clear 
and its trend was long-lasting. Amazon ultimately 
proved more successful in e-commerce than eBay, 
while Walmart eventually adapted more effectively to 
online retail than Target, but it was the broader trend 
of the decline in department store retail sales and the 
significant increase in e-commerce sales that had 
broader investment implications.  
The same pattern emerged in media (see Figure 2). 
Company-specific research may have helped investors 
assess whether Google, Yahoo, AOL, or another internet 
company would become the dominant digital advertising 
platform. However, the vulnerability of newspapers, 
classified advertising, and television became increasingly 
visible as consumers spent a larger share of their 
time online, driven by the explosion in the volume and 
variety of digital content. Digital advertising also offered 
more precise audience targeting, measurable returns 
on investment, and real-time campaign optimization, 
further reinforcing the migration of advertising spending 
online. The structural disadvantage that led to the long-
term decline for traditional media businesses was clear.
Across both retail and media, the same pattern 
emerged: technological disruption created 
opportunities for new leaders while revealing vulnerable 
incumbent business models. In our view, discussions of 
disruption frequently emphasize the former despite the 
importance of the latter.
Passive Investing Problems
Disruption can hit passive investors or those that closely 
track benchmarks particularly hard. The problem is 
the result of market-cap-weighted indices allocating 
capital based on current size rather than future 
opportunity. The market size of a company is often 
based on recent results because shorthand valuation 
metrics, such as price-to-earnings multiples, typically 
figure prominently in the broad market assessment 
of equities. Consequently, incumbents usually begin 
disruption cycles as large index weights while disruptors 
often begin as small weights, leaving passive investors 
with substantially greater exposure to legacy business 
models than to emerging alternatives. Many future 
winners may still even be private companies.

Empirical evidence of this imbalance over the past two 
decades supports this view (see Figure 3). In 2005, 
physical retail-related companies, including department 
stores, apparel retailers, electronics retailers, and mall 
real estate owners, accounted for roughly nine times 
the S&P 500 weight of e-commerce companies. By

The Value Trap
The risk of over-allocating to companies that will be 
disrupted is even higher in value style indices, in our 
opinion. Traditional style benchmarks, such as the 
Russell indices, use metrics that cause more innovative 
companies to be classified as growth companies and 
many mature or slower-growing companies to be 
classified as value companies. For example, the price-to-
book ratio figures prominently in Russell’s classification 
methodology. Companies with lower market value to 
book, or net asset value, are more likely to be included 
in the value index. These companies have less of 
their market capitalization attributable to intangible 
assets that do not show up on the balance sheet such 
as patents, trade secrets, or copyrights. Because 
innovation is often missing from financial statements, 
and particularly the balance sheet, traditional metrics 
based on those accounting conventions skew toward 
companies whose value is derived more from existing 
assets like property, plant, and equipment rather than 
research & development, which can potentially generate 
more dynamic growth.

Why Visible Losers Stay Mispriced
If value destruction is predictable as a result of new 
technology, why doesn’t the equity market quickly price 
it in? We believe that there are two reasons that eventual 
losers of innovation stay overvalued for extended 
periods of time: 
1.	
The Growth Projection Bias. The market tends 
to assume that companies will continue growing, 
even when technological disruption begins to hurt 
their competitive position. Analysts often follow 
management guidance closely and executives rarely 
forecast their own decline. Instead, they typically 
emphasize new initiatives, product launches, or cost 
reductions that are expected to restore earnings 
growth. Analysts often want to believe these 
narratives and may be reluctant to model severe 
deterioration until there is overwhelming evidence 
that it is occurring. As a result, earnings estimates 
can remain too high for too long. This is particularly 
problematic during periods of disruptive innovation, 
when new technologies can rapidly change consumer 
behavior and industry economics. Despite margin 
compression, declining revenue, and weakening 
competitive advantage, consensus forecasts may 
continue to anticipate stabilization and an eventual 
return to growth. By the time expectations fully 
reflect the new reality, a substantial amount of value 
destruction may have already occurred.
2.	 The Value Trap Phenomenon. Investors are often 
attracted to stocks that look inexpensive relative 
to their own history or the broader market even 
when their fundamentals are under pressure and 
at risk of further erosion. A stock trading at low-
double-digit or high-single-digit price-to-earnings 
multiple screens well for many investors and can 
look like a bargain. Yet in many disrupted industries, 
the fundamentals decline faster than expected and 
the valuation multiple remains low such that the 
stocks continue to decline. For example, S&P 500 
department stores traded at a modest P/E of 12.1x 
at the end of 1999, a massive 50% discount to the 
S&P 500. Despite the attractive valuation which 
persisted over the next two decades, department 
store stocks fell 4% through 2019 while the S&P

Disruption due to Artificial Intelligence 
Most current discussions of artificial intelligence (AI) 
focus on the infrastructure beneficiaries, including 
semiconductor designers and manufacturers, cooling 
and power providers, networking companies, cloud 
platforms, power generation, and large language model 
providers. These may indeed become enormous winners 
as consumers and businesses reap the productivity 
benefits of this technology.
However, history suggests investors should devote equal 
attention to understanding the other side of this potential 
productivity boom: which industries will see their ability 
to create value ultimately commoditized by AI? The 
explosion in output that AI will generate is likely to reduce 
the competitive advantages of many businesses and 
therefore destroy a huge amount of shareholder value.
Potentially vulnerable business models may include 
those built around routine information gathering, 
administrative complexity, and basic content creation. 
Just as e-commerce challenged physical retail and digital 
media disrupted traditional publishing, AI may challenge 
industries whose economics depend on information 
scarcity or labor-intensive white-collar processes. 
We believe that AI will drive a dramatic decline in 
the cost of creation. Tasks that once required large 
teams, specialized expertise, and months or years of 
development can increasingly be completed by far 
fewer people in a fraction of the time. As the cost and 
time required to create products and services declines, 
barriers to entry may fall as well. When creation 
becomes cheaper, competition tends to increase, placing 
pressure on incumbent profit pools and established 
business models, in our view. 
Seen through this lens, industries built around 
delivering knowledge work may be especially at risk. 
Advertising agencies, IT consulting firms, enterprise 
software, business process outsourcing providers, 
insurance brokers, and certain information services 
businesses have historically benefited from the cost, 
expertise, and time required to produce content, code, 
analysis, research, and administrative output. As AI 
reduces those requirements, these industries may face 
increasing competition and pressure on their profits 
and stock prices.
Finding Opportunities, Avoiding Disruption
Every technological revolution generates wealth creation 
as well as value destruction. While investors devote 
most of their time and resources to identifying the 
beneficiaries of innovation, we believe equal attention 
should be paid to understanding which business models 
may be vulnerable to it.
Emerging companies with new competitive advantages, 
lower cost structures, and more enticing products or 
services attract investor funds. Investment leaves those 
industries and corporations that cannot keep up with the 
changing business landscape. While the overall economy 
may grow, many companies will lose and, in the process, 
they will either destroy shareholder value or generate 
inadequate returns for years.
This dynamic has important implications in an era 
increasingly dominated by passive investing. Because 
market-cap-weighted indices allocate capital based on 
current size and scale, investors may have meaningful 
exposure to businesses facing long-term disruption. 
The key question is whether technological change is 
beginning to undermine the foundations on which an 
industry’s profits depend. Historically, those foundations 
included store networks and physical distribution in 
retail, as well as static advertising inventory in media. 
In the era of AI, pressure may fall on industries whose 
economics depend on labor-intensive processes, 
administrative complexity, or high creation costs.
We believe understanding where value is likely to be 
destroyed or gradually eroded can be as important as 
understanding where value is likely to be created. To 
avoid disruption and enhance returns, investors may 
want to seek out active managers who understand 
innovation cycles. These cycles can significantly change 
company fundamentals, decimating incumbents, and 
anointing new leaders. Investors who recognize both 
sides of creative destruction may be best positioned to 
navigate the coming technological revolution.
