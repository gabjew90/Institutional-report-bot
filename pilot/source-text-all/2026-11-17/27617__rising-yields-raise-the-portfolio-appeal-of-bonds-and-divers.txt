Thought of the day
Higher global bond yields on recalibrated interest rate expectations and 
a rebound in appetite for stocks have hurt investors with fixed income 
positions. The policy-sensitive German 2-year yield ended 12bps higher 
last week, marking its sharpest weekly rise in two months. Japanese 10-
year yields climbed to their highest levels since 22 October, in tandem with 
higher US government yields on news that US President Donald Trump was 
not seeking a peace deal with Iran and as investors weighed comments 
from Federal Reserve President Christopher Waller around the possibility of 
a 50bps rate hike at the Fed's December meeting.
But elevated starting yields have restored rather than diminished the 
appeal of the traditional balanced (60% stock, 40% bond) portfolio. While 
equities can provide long-term growth, high-quality bonds once again offer 
meaningful income, potential capital appreciation if growth slows, and a 
cushion against some equity market declines.
Bonds have returned to the core The reset in yields has materially 
improved the role bonds can play in portfolios. After the global financial 
crisis, low interest rates left fixed income offering limited income and 
relatively little protection against an equity market drawdown, while 
exposing investors to the risk of rising yields. Current higher starting 
yields matter in several ways. They increase the income available from 
bonds and provide a greater cushion against negative returns in case rates 
increase further. They also create scope for capital appreciation if inflation 
moderates, economic growth weakens, or central banks eventually reduce 
interest rates. The portfolio role of bonds extends beyond expected returns. 
High-quality bonds can help preserve capital and may diversify equity 
exposure when economic growth disappoints. Their senior position in the 
capital structure also generally makes them less risky than equities issued by 
the same company.
60/40 is stronger, but not invulnerable. The revival of bond yields 
improves the case for a 60/40 equity-bond portfolio. But it is not 
guaranteed that bonds will offset every equity decline. The diversification

bonds have historically been more effective equity diversifiers during 
periods of low or stable inflation. If economic growth weakens and 
inflation moderates, bond prices may rise as equities come under pressure. 
If inflation remains elevated and interest rates rise, stocks and bonds can 
decline together. That distinction is important at present, as investors face 
several plausible paths for growth, inflation, fiscal policy, energy prices, and 
geopolitical risk into the year ahead.
Completion assets can address different risks. Uncertainty and the 
need to think in terms of market scenarios make the case for completion 
assets stronger. Completion assets can earn their place by doing a specific 
job that the core may not perform consistently. That might include 
generating differentiated returns, providing income, reducing sensitivity to 
equity markets, or helping preserve purchasing power during periods of 
elevated inflation.
Hedge funds may provide alternative sources of return when equities 
and bonds face pressure at the same time. Real assets can address a 
different set of risks. Commodities have historically displayed relatively 
low correlation with traditional asset classes and may benefit when 
physical supply is constrained or inflation remains elevated. And private 
infrastructure may offer relatively predictable and potentially inflation-linked 
income, while providing exposure to long-term investment in electricity 
networks, data centers, electrification, energy security, and more resilient 
supply chains.
So, the return of meaningful bond yields has made the traditional balanced 
portfolio relevant again. A diversified mix of equities and high-quality bonds 
can provide a more credible combination of growth, income, and capital 
preservation than it could during the low-yield era.
We currently rate high grade and investment grade bonds as Attractive. 
We favor exposure to short- to medium-duration high-quality bonds, while 
remaining more cautious on the longest maturities given fiscal concerns 
and elevated issuance. We also rate high yield and emerging market bonds 
as Attractive, supported by elevated yields and, in aggregate, resilient 
fundamentals.
However, 60/40 is a foundation, not a complete answer. We believe 
investors should also look at commodities, real assets, and select structured 
strategies as ways to enhance returns, improve portfolio resilience, and best 
match specific goals with portfolios, subject to careful risk management. 
Private investments in particular can involve illiquidity, limited disclosure, 
uncertain cash flows, leverage, capital calls, and blind-pool risk.
Finally, portfolio discipline matters as much as portfolio design. A 60/40 US 
equity-bond portfolio invested in 2020 would have drifted to about 80/20 
if left untouched, reflecting the stronger performance of equities. Regular 
rebalancing helps restore the intended risk profile, reduce unintended 
concentrations, and prevent recent outperformers from determining the 
portfolio’s future behavior.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
