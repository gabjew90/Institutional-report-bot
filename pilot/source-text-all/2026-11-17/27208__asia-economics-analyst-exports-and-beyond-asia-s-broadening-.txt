Exports and Beyond—Asia’s Broadening AI Dividend 
 
 
Investment in the artificial intelligence buildout continues to grow, with a large fraction 
of spend (roughly 2/3 the value of a data center) ultimately going to equipment 
producers in Asia. Earlier this year, we estimated the impact of surging tech exports on 
GDP growth across a range of Asian economies. In this week’s Asia Economics Analyst, we 
update those estimates and also look at spillover effects on domestic demand, including 
investment (in manufacturing export capacity as well as local data centers), 
consumption (via estimated equity wealth effects), and government spending (on the 
back of large increases in exporter profits and thus corporate tax revenues). 
Exports – A growing AI capex impulse 
The global AI spending backdrop remains supportive for Asian tech exporters. 
Bloomberg consensus estimates for US hyperscaler capex continue to rise (Exhibit 1), 
and are now at $800bn in 2026 and $1.1tn in 2027. Stronger AI-related investment 
underpins our upward revisions to growth forecasts across Asian economies (Exhibit 2); 
most have reported stronger-than-expected Q2 GDP growth numbers. In August, 
officials in Korea, Taiwan and Singapore all revised up their 2026 growth forecasts, with 
stronger-than-expected AI demand cited among the supportive factors. 
 
Over the past year, high-bandwidth memory became a key supply constraint for AI 
servers, driving a sharp increase in pricing. As noted by our global technology team, 
LLMs require a huge amount of memory capacity to store trillions of parameters. As 
Samsung and SK Hynix together hold over 60% of the global DRAM market and just 
below half of NAND1, Korean nominal export growth has accelerated rapidly in 2026 
(Exhibit 3). In real terms, we estimate tech exports from Korea and Taiwan still expanded 
at mid-20% yoy in Q2, whereas real growth in tech exports from key ASEAN producers 
(Singapore, Malaysia, Vietnam and Thailand) was even more robust, at around 40%-50% 
yoy in Q2.

Incorporating the latest export figures, our estimates for the tech export contribution to 
GDP growth across several Asian economies rise further. Following the same approach 
as in our previous report, we compute the real contribution of tech exports to GDP 
growth2 as gross tech exports deflated to volumes, multiplied by the domestic 
value-added share of gross exports from OECD inter-country input-output tables3. The 
upward revisions reflect a combination of stronger-than-expected Q2 export outturns 
and the upward revisions to hyperscaler capex guidance described above. 
A few cautions apply to reading the exhibit. First, the contribution is gross of import 
leakage on the investment or consumption sides, hence the estimated contribution is 
inherently larger than the net-export contribution normally reported in national 
accounts. Second, we calculate the contribution from tech exports, which is 
conceptually broader than the purely AI-related export contribution, for reasons 
discussed in Box 1 below. Third, transshipment or processing trade might materially 
affect our value-added calculations, most notably for China. A Bank of Korea study in 
September pointed out that China serves as a major hub for semiconductor processing 
trade and a substantial number of wafers that complete front-end fabrication in 
economies including Korea, Taiwan, Japan and elsewhere undergo back-end processing 
in China. The same report also notes Korean-affiliated companies in China accounted for 
nearly 70% of the increase in China’s semiconductor exports in H1 2026. These trends 
suggest potentially abrupt changes in China’s value-added share in tech exports that 
may not be captured by lagged input-output tables.

Box 1: Methodological caveats – AI-related exports vs. Tech exports 
In this report we estimate the growth contribution from tech exports—including consumer electronics for 
example—as opposed to solely AI-related exports. The reason for this choice is that the two inputs 
required by our method – deflators and domestic value-added ratios – are generally not available at the 
level of granularity needed to estimate real (volume) contributions for the most detailed goods categories. 
For example, the finest electronics category in OECD inter-country input-output tables is C26 Computer, 
Electronic and Optical Products, whereas export price indices are generally published at product-group level. 
With that caveat noted, the majority of recent tech export growth appears AI-related. Exhibit 6 shows how 
much of each economy’s tech exports are AI hardware. We defined broad tech exports (the noisier series) 
at the HS2/HS4 level that matches the sectoral granularity of price indices and inter-country input-output 
tables, and AI hardware (the signal) at HS6 using definitions adopted in IMF’s World Economic Outlook 
Update. This share is high for Korea and Taiwan, and lower for Vietnam and Thailand, where broader 
non-AI electronics dilute the aggregate. It conceptually explains why tech contribution might be a better 
proxy for AI contribution in the former than in the latter. While one might be tempted to scale tech 
contributions by the AI-hardware share to obtain an “AI-related export contribution”, that inherently 
assumes the same relative growth, inflation and value-added ratios between AI and non-AI tech products, 
which is unlikely to be the case.

Investment – Manufacturing capacity expansion 
The local capex response to the AI boom has arrived in Asia. Major manufacturers are 
poised to ramp up investment to expand production capacity in anticipation of 
sustained AI demand. Samsung, SK Hynix and TSMC have all announced various 
investment projects to meet accelerating demand for high-end chips, and consensus 
forecasts imply capex for each of these firms will rise sharply into 2026 and beyond. We 
assess this trend via bottom-up aggregation of capital expenditures of listed Korean and 
Taiwanese tech companies. As a share of GDP, Korea tech capex is expected to rise by 
1.1pp and Taiwan by 2.9pp in 2026 (Exhibit 7). 
 
However, headline capex should be heavily discounted when assessing its domestic 
growth contribution for two reasons. 
First, capex plans are generally global and do not necessarily contain a clear split 
between domestic and overseas investments. Company-level evidence from Samsung 
Electronics, SK Hynix and TSMC4 suggests a substantial domestic bias, potentially 
reflecting efficiency gains from existing local semiconductor clusters. Parent-company 
PP&E purchases accounted for around 80% of Samsung Electronics’ total capex and over 
90% of SK Hynix’s in H1 2026. The equivalent ratio for TSMC was also around 80% in 
20255. They provide a geographic proxy because these companies’ principal domestic 
fabs generally sit within their parent entities, while major overseas manufacturing 
facilities operate through subsidiaries. 
More importantly, semiconductor manufacturing expansion contains heavy import 
leakage. Key production equipment for such investments, including lithography, 
deposition and etch equipment, is often sourced from the Netherlands or Japan. The 
Bank of Korea estimated that 60% of Korea’s investment in semiconductor 
manufacturing equipment is imported, compared to a 35% import share across other 
types of manufacturing. Taiwan also reported in 2022 that, of Taiwan’s TWD 1 trillion 
semiconductor wafer fab investment at the time, around TWD 600 billion was used for 
procurement of foreign equipment, implying a similar import share.

Investment – Data center construction 
AI-related investment includes not only export capacity, but also local data center 
facilities. ASEAN is emerging as a hotspot for such activity, as described in our TMT 
team’s ASEAN Data Center Primer: “As tier-1 markets hit power and grid constraints, 
hyperscalers are redirecting incremental builds to ASEAN, where power, land, and regulatory 
friction are generally lower” and global players can “run large-scale, early-stage LLM training 
leveraging ASEAN’s lower-cost environment”. Another driver is export-control arbitrage, as 
Chinese players can use ASEAN as a platform for deploying US chips in model training for 
non-China markets.7 Live capacity in ASEAN remains below primary markets including 
Tokyo, Sydney and Mumbai according to Cushman and Wakefield’s data (Exhibit 8), but 
is larger relative to the size of local economies. Our TMT team expects ASEAN capacity 
to grow 4x in the next 5 years and Johor (Malaysia) to be the largest hub in ASEAN by 
2030, as it stands to benefit from most of the cloud and AI-inference spillover from 
Singapore.

While construction activities lift domestic investment, data centers can also contribute 
to growth via service exports once in operation. To examine this, we compare tech 
service trade balance trends across APAC. Exhibit 11 shows that Malaysia ICT service 
balance improved from a deficit of 20bp of GDP as of Q2 2025 to a surplus of 30bp of 
GDP as of Q2 2026 on a rolling-four-quarter basis, as data center capacity expanded. 
Last year was also the first year since 2011 that Malaysia posted an overall services 
surplus. Johor’s data center expansion, once completed, could translate into 
cross-border ICT service exports back to Singapore given its proximity and low latency.11 
By contrast, data centers in Thailand or Indonesia are more likely to be anchored on 
domestic activity12 and hence completions in these countries might to a larger extent 
flow into intermediate business services and be less visible in the balance of payments. 
Beyond these two channels, we note that data center investments can also generate 
second-order local spillovers through upward pressure on wages for related 
construction jobs13, though we assess the effect to be limited at the macro level. 
Construction typically requires thousands of workers, which generate a front-loaded 
employment impulse anecdotally based on industry research and hyperscaler 
announcements, but permanent employment is generally in the low hundreds as 
operations are labor-light.14

Consumption – Equity wealth effect 
Regional equity indices have risen sharply in recent years, boosting household wealth. 
Post recent corrections, MSCI Korea and Taiwan are still up 85% and 65% year-to-date 
respectively, with tech producers driving the gains (Exhibit 14). We leveraged our 
portfolio strategy team’s AI hardware baskets to decompose market capitalization gains 
attributable to AI-related stocks, and applied equity wealth coefficients from our earlier 
analysis. While the coefficient is small in Korea and Taiwan, due to factors including 
elevated existing leverage and concentrated equity ownership, the absolute scale of the 
equity boom still implies an estimated 15bp and 30bp GDP boost to Korea and Taiwan 
respectively.17 For Japan, we previously estimated that a 10% rise in equity prices could 
boost consumption growth by around 30bp, which would also imply a 15bp GDP growth 
boost in 2026 from AI-related equity gains. 
At the same time, we caveat that Asian households might also have substantial exposure 
to global/US equities that are AI-driven. This could potentially magnify our estimates 
above, though such transmission channels would again mostly apply to high-NIIP North 
Asia. We also expect labor-market transmission (wage effect) to be fairly muted, due to 
the capital-intensive, labor-light nature of tech manufacturing. We note that tech 
employment only constitutes around 10% of employment in Taiwan and 2% in Korea in 
2025.

Broadening AI dividend, though exports remain dominant  
We present the aggregate results of our estimates by economy in Exhibit 15. AI remains 
the principal growth driver for Korea and Taiwan in 2026, reflecting their central 
positions in the value chain. Among ASEAN economies, Malaysia appears best placed to 
benefit, with meaningful exposure across semiconductor assembly and testing as well as 
data centers. The rest of Asia is also positioned to gain, though to differing degrees and 
through different channels. Taken together, however, tech exports remain the dominant 
transmission channel through which the AI investment cycle supports regional activity. 
Most other spillovers typically only amount to a few tenths of a percentage point of GDP. 
This is economically intuitive, as they are downstream effects of export strength, with 
export-generated profits ultimately funding additional investment, fiscal revenue and 
consumption. 
This positioning is inherently double-edged. While the region is highly leveraged to the 
AI supply chain and has so far delivered robust growth thanks to the investment boom, 
such concentration leaves the region vulnerable to any reversal in AI investments. A 
moderation in hyperscaler capex, whether driven by higher financing costs, greater 
investor scrutiny of ROI or slower-than-expected progress in AI adoption, might 
generate a meaningful growth headwind for the region. However for now, signs of a 
broad-based retrenchment remain limited.18 
Another risk is potential crowding out. Sustained demand for AI-related infrastructure 
could potentially divert productive resources – land, power capacity, and skilled 
technical labor – away from other sectors, leading to input cost inflation that squeezes 
non-AI industries. Evidence to-date, however, suggests that crowding-out has remained 
limited outside localized segments where demand-supply imbalances are most acute. 
Memory crowding-out is clearly visible, as memory suppliers prioritize HBM over
