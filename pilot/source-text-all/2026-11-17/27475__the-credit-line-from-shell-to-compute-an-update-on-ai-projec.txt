From Shell to Compute: An Update on AI Project Finance 
 
 
Earlier this year, we published a deep dive on the “project finance” structures used to 
fund AI datacenters. At the time, we focused on a smaller $76 billion market of 
datacenter bonds, which were both novel in structure and required, in our view, a 
different approach to underwriting focusing on project characteristics versus traditional 
unsecured and secured corporate bonds. Subsequent work tracking the datacenter 
market saw no visible timeline delays at the time but argued that tight pricing and low 
dispersion were likely not sustainable as project specific factors—such as construction 
timelines or refinancing profiles—grew in importance. 
Since then, the market has grown faster than even we had projected and a combination 
of deal-specific dispersion and softening sentiment has taken project finance bonds 
wider. Year-to-date issuance now totals $80 billion across IG and HY datacenter bonds, 
with an additional $22 billion in non-syndicated datacenter construction and an 
additional $48 billion in chip, or compute, financing deals. 
This has raised a number of questions from investors over a few areas including 
anticipated market size, structural evolutions and considerations, key risks, and current 
investor sentiment.

Risks to the forecast: Two-sided and non-trivial 
Upside risk: Financing needs. The largest driver of upside risks, we think, comes from 
the fundraising needs for the AI buildout and the costs associated with both building 
datacenters and securing compute. Capex estimates have continued to surprise to the 
upside and off-balance sheet expenses for the Hyperscalers have grown greatly. 
Saturation constraints are limiting how much higher-rated issuers can place in corporate 
credit, while market access has become more discerning for lower-rated borrowers. As a 
result, we think a growing share of capex will be funded through structured financings at 
wider spreads. 
Downside risk: Frictions. Construction delays, tighter financing economics, and 
potential political action against new builds could all slow the buildout. This has already 
been visible in 4Q2026, as tighter economics have played a part in slowing issuance but 
have grown the backlog. Yet, a more receptive primary market would not help much if 
moratoria or construction delays, perceived or actual, weigh on new projects, a risk 
recently outlined by our equity research colleagues. Our economists view the moratoria 
as conditional, focused on new builds, and likely to fade over time. However, more 
sustained or aggressive curtailment of construction could weigh on financing in 2027.   
Q: How are the chip deals structured? Do we anticipate any evolution? 
Like datacenter bonds, chip financings are contracted cash flow structures. We have 
thus far noted two high level groups of financing structures: equipment leases similar to 
the datacenter shell triple net lease structures and compute service contracts. A 
bankruptcy-remote special purpose vehicle (SPV) is central to both. In the former, this 
SPV has a right to an agreed upon cash flow structure, typically spanning the lease terms 
and any conditional cash flows (e.g., residual value guarantees). In compute service 
contracts, the SPV generally owns the revenue-generating assets, and a contracted 
counterparty pays into the structure. Lenders hold either a priority lien on most assets, 
typically alongside a pledge of SPV equity (where it exists) and, often, the project 
contracts. As a result, investors in these deals underwrite a combination of contract, 
project and counterparty risk rather than pure corporate credit risk. The small sample of 
deals makes it difficult to draw firm conclusions, but four dimensions stand out.

First is the contract structure. Datacenter bonds have all been backed by triple net 
leases. Their service commitments span construction through ready-for-service delivery 
and, in some cases, include maintenance service agreements after construction is 
complete. Chip financing has been more heterogeneous, especially amongst the 
compute service contract cohorts. For these, the key question is the scope of the master 
services agreement (MSA), and specifically what the service level agreement (SLA) 
includes and excludes. There is no direct construction risk, but payment is tied to a 
tenant outcome: the ability to run training and inference workloads on the compute 
infrastructure provided. From a credit perspective, contracts that sidestep SLAs 
altogether, like chip equipment leases, and service contracts with narrower SLAs are the 
most favorable. Contracts with a wider SLA perimeter, where service deficiencies haircut 
revenue, are the least favorable. 
Second, obsolescence protection. The debate over the useful life of GPUs has led 
credit investors to seek protection against obsolescence risk. In practice, this has meant 
two things. Debt amortizes, so investors are not left with refinancing risk at maturity, 
and lease terms typically match or exceed the tenor of the debt. While some recent 
literature has suggested that residual values on chips might be higher and their useful 
lives longer, we believe credit investors should prefer to avoid assuming obsolescence 
risk when possible. Traditional first-lien financing is usually not equipped to underwrite 
fast depreciating, and potentially obsolete assets in this size. 
Third, the funding structure. Financing has often been arranged before energized 
shells are ready for GPU deployment. This creates a trade-off between capital flexibility 
and income reliability. As a result, many of the loan deals that have come to market have 
been structured as delayed-draw term loans (Exhibit 3). We expect delayed-draw term 
loans to remain central to the market, but we see two ways in which funding structures 
could evolve. 
Larger deals will likely require participation across markets, or access to the

much deeper IG market. These could include structured solutions for hyperscalers 
with less balance sheet capacity. They could also include large deals for frontier labs 
that carry some form of guarantee from an IG-rated counterparty. 
More shells coming online could mean more bond and regular term loan

issuance. Stretched delivery timelines and installation frictions mean some delayed 
and sequential draws will likely be built into all deals. As timelines tighten, however, 
draw periods should shorten. That could tilt issuers away from paying ticking fees 
(e.g., the cost of having available, but still undrawn financing) and toward more 
traditional bond structures.

Fourth, credit support. Credit support has scaled with the quality of the counterparty, 
and structural mechanisms have been added to strengthen the credit profile. Syndicated 
deals have largely consisted of the senior tranche of the capital structure, thus far. In the 
loan market, deals with lower-rated counterparties generally carry lower loan-to-cost 
(LTC) ratios and can use customer prepayments to reduce the cashflow risk. 
Q: What are the implications of residual value guarantees or chip supplier 
financing? 
To strengthen the credit profile of many chip financings, suppliers have guaranteed 
some or all of the debt through residual value guarantees. The mechanics vary by deal. In 
general, the guarantor must pay the balance implied by the guarantee, which is typically 
close to the outstanding principal and remaining interest on the covered debt. 
We see two implications. 
The first relates to chipmaker credit ratings. High profit margins, relatively low 
leverage, and strong market positioning help explain the high IG ratings that hardware 
firms carry. Strong demand for compute has allowed chipmakers to require up-front 
payment from most customers and to extend flexible payment terms only to 
higher-quality buyers. This has supported strong cash conversion cycles. 
By extending residual value support, however, chipmakers are underwriting a potential 
left-tail scenario. In that scenario, contracted payments fail to arrive and the fair value of 
the chips falls below the guaranteed amount. This outcome is, by definition, not 
probable and does not imply balance sheet leverage today. Even so, rating agencies have 
alluded to the compounding of left-tail exposure across deals in recent assessments, 
especially as the time horizon of the guarantee extends for years (and well beyond the 
revenue recognition period). We think this risk is worth monitoring as guarantees and 
support mechanisms scale. 
The second implication is in the CDS market. Chipmakers have extended credit 
support without commensurate cash bond issuance, which has pushed the premium of 
their CDS over cash bonds steadily wider (Exhibit 4). As we have noted before, CDS 
referencing AI-related credits now trades at a premium (e.g., wider spreads) to 
representative cash bonds, reflecting the hedging needs of a wide range of market

Despite the source, higher rates have been a drag on issuance given tighter funding 
economics. This mainly flows through rental pricing and the deal economics built around 
these predictable revenue streams. Rental pricing methods are not uniform across 
datacenter bonds. Rents are typically set using a tiered version of yield-on-cost or 
limited cost-plus pricing, under which rents rise, to an extent, with the cost of the 
buildout. 
For our purposes, it allows for a simple model in which we can unite a project DSCR, debt 
coupon and offer rental revenue (a function of yield-on-cost) that can cure more 
stringent covenants and higher rates (Exhibit 10). That said, tenants might likely push 
back on their rents hedging higher rate exposure, through caps or by lowering rental rate 
thresholds as construction costs rise. 
 
This leaves the market at a tension, in our view. For projects to remain viable, cap rates 
must be struck at accretive levels. When coupons are set higher, rents must therefore 
adjust upward if borrowers want to maintain a similar share of debt funding. Where 
tenants resist, the adjustment is likely to come through lower leverage, such as more 
equity, lower LTCs, or tighter structures. We anticipate a mix of these solutions will be 
needed as rates remain higher or other frictions mean investors require more 
conservative structures.

Appendix—outlining the forecast methodology 
Using datacenter construction data from 451 Research we measure the IT power 
expected to be online in a given quarter. We exclude Chinese datacenters given the 
different financing profile makes this wattage less relevant to our calculations. Taking 
data on datacenter type into hyperscaler owned and non-hyperscaler owned projects, 
with the view that hyperscalers will be less likely to finance compute, especially. Given 
the ambiguity over end clients, we assume that 10% of future net adds for hyperscaler 
datacenters are for non-IG end-users whereas 20% of non-IG additions are for 
Hyperscalers. In 2027 we assume 45% and 50% of shell wattage will be financed for 
hyperscalers and non-hyperscale projects, respectively. For compute the share is 30% 
and 45% across hyperscalers and non-hyperscalers.  
We assume shell costs are $15mn/MW in the US and $12mn/MW for the world ex-US 
builds and compute pricing is ~$25mn/MW across GPU and ASIC chips, with a 10% 
markup to non-IG end users. Given financing leads delivery, we use the 4 quarter ahead 
net additions for shell financing and 2 quarter ahead schedule for compute.
