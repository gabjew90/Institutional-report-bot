Chipping in: how chips have become a huge 
driver of the dollar 
What do chips have to do with a new era of dollar dominance? As we discuss in 
this paper – a great deal. Chips are not only critical to the future of AI but have 
recently emerged as a huge driver of global trade and capital flows.  
It is well known that the US is dominant in upstream chip technology and 
downstream compute. This note delves deeper into the workings of the middle of 
the chain where flows have been reinforcing the dollar’s global role. The 
manufacturing of chips largely takes place in Asia where chip-related trade 
surpluses have ballooned. As we show, these surpluses are finding their way back 
to the US via investments in US capital markets.  
Asian chip surpluses are not being recycled into US Treasuries like petrodollar 
surpluses were, but into US equities. Foreign inflows into US equities have crossed 
USD1tn a year and become the mainstay of US deficit funding. The biggest 
source of increase over the past year has come from Asia.  
Equity flows have not just been supporting the US dollar but have provided 
financing to the companies driving the AI buildout and chips boom itself. This 
introduces forms of circularity and potential instability to global flows. Moreover, 
equities are a much riskier asset class than bonds – US stocks are three times as 
volatile as Treasuries, with tech stocks up to ten times as much. Growing dollar 
dependence on inherently riskier capital should spill into higher US dollar 
volatility over time.  
The rise of chips as a driver of dollar dominance can be seen as a symptom of 
change in the ultimate source of US power - from geopolitical to technological 
leadership. This power is increasingly concentrated in the private sector with 
dollar supportive flows intermediated via private balance sheets.  
Understanding this new framework and its risks is even more important given 
rapid shifts in news flow around AI, from the US political climate, high-stakes 
capital market raisings, potential tightening in export controls, and the highly 
consequential technology race with China.  
We will be releasing updated medium term FX forecasts to reflect some of these 
dynamics later this week.

The rise of the chip dollar 
For the first time in modern history, global trade in semiconductors now 
exceeds trade in oil and gas (Figure 1) based on the four quarters of global 
exports till Q2-2026. More relevant from a currency perspective, is that chip 
surpluses in Asia ex-China have caught up to petroleum-related surpluses in the 
Gulf (Figure 2).  Why does this matter and what does this reflect?

We argue that the petrodollar is – rather quickly - being superseded by the chip-
dollar. This is both literal in terms of global surplus accumulation, but also 
archetypal. In other words, the petrodollar and chip-dollar are archetypes for two 
different states of the world, and for alternative foundations of dollar dominance.  
The petrodollar regime can be understood as one where US geopolitical 
leadership – over global security, international rules, and free trade – motivated 
global savings to be reinvested in the US sovereign via Treasury holdings. This 
cheapened funding for the US government which in turn supported the financing 
of US public goods provision.  
The emerging chip-dollar regime is one where US technology leadership is 
motivating global savings to be reinvested in US companies – providing funding 
for the US corporate sector to make enormous investments in new technology 
such as AI.  
The transition from the petrodollar to the chip-dollar is therefore a 
manifestation of change in the US’ leadership style - from geopolitical to 
technology leadership. This is not only changing the nature of global imbalances 
and capital flow recycling but is reshaping the foundations of dollar dominance.  
Revisiting the petrodollar regime  
After the 1971 Nixon shock, when the dollar’s convertibility to gold was 
terminated, we moved into a fully fiat currency world. The US dollar since then 
has been backed only by the fiscal credibility of the US government and the 
willingness of the world to finance US deficits by buying US assets.

The 1974 petrodollar deal provided a crucial pillar of support for the dollar in its 
fiat infancy and sowed the seeds for a post-Bretton Woods regime in which US 
geopolitical leadership supported dollar dominance.  In 1974, in exchange for US 
security and military guarantees, Saudi Arabia agreed to price and invoice oil sales 
in USD. These USD denominated oil surpluses were then reinvested in US 
Treasuries. This was soon replicated across the Gulf. The 1990s and 2000s saw 
huge growth in commodity and manufacturing surpluses around the world. These 
were arguably supported by the US’s global backstop for trade and security – with 
surpluses saved in USD and recycled into US Treasuries via public balance sheets, 
in what can be considered a petrodollar-plus regime.  
We wrote earlier in the year how the Iran war could undermine the critical 
foundations of the petrodollar system by challenging the US security umbrella 
and shifting perceptions around US geopolitical leadership. We maintain that 
these changes are underway. But what has become apparent since then is that 
something else has been rising to take the petrodollar’s place in support of the 
dollar.  
The chip dollar is working differently  
While petrodollar surpluses were recycled into US Treasuries, chips surpluses 
are being recycled very differently.  
We focus on the chip surpluses being built in Asia ex-China. We note that China 
runs a very large semiconductor deficit, despite having the world’s largest overall 
trade surplus - something we will return to later. Asia’s chip surpluses are thus 
primarily hosted in Korea and Taiwan which are large semiconductor 
manufacturers, to a lesser extent in Japan which produces key materials, and 
ASEAN-10 which plays a role in assembly, packaging and testing.  
To explore how chip dollar surpluses are being recycled, we zoom in on South 
Korea – the epitome of the boom in chips trade.  
Korea’s semiconductor exports have grown over 200% YoY this year, with Korean 
chipmakers dominating in the manufacture of memory chips critical to AI 
acceleration. As Figure 3 shows, Korea’s semiconductor trade surplus has 
ballooned to close to USD250bn with 100% of Korea’s overall trade surplus now 
on account of chips alone.  
What is striking is that Korea’s chip surplus is being recycled abroad entirely via 
equities. Foreign sales of Korean equities have accounted for half the capital 
outflows in the past year, while Korean purchases of overseas equities account 
for the other half (Figure 3). Korean investments in overseas equities have been 
five times more than Korean purchases of overseas bonds in the last two years. In 
short, chip surpluses are being saved in global equities, not debt. 
The primary destination of Korea’s overseas equity purchases has been the US. 
While the US accounts for around 50% of global equity market capitalization, we 
note it is 70% of Korea’s overseas equity holdings (IMF CPIS). The US bias is even 
more exaggerated for Korean retail investors – a group empowered by access to 
financial technology. Korean households have 90% of their equity exposure in the 
US (Figure 4). It appears that North Asian capital is flowing back to the very US 
technology companies that are driving the chip surpluses themselves.

The reach for US equities by Asian retail is not just happening in Korea. In Japan, 
there have been significant allocations to US focused ETFs, encouraged by the 
NISA scheme. And as we have argued in our earlier paper, the US’s embrace of 
asset tokenization and the upcoming shift by US exchanges to 23/5 trading could 
further embolden and enable retail investors to access US equities. We could thus 
just be at the beginning of this new trend. Countries in North Asia like Korea and 
Japan not only have large savings pools, but also open capital accounts.  
This shift has shown up dramatically on the US capital account. Foreign 
purchases of US equities have exceeded USD1tn over the past year (Figure 5). 
Interestingly, most of the increase in purchases has come from Asia (Figure 6). 
Notably, foreign purchases of US equities are now more than double US Treasury 
allocations, which appear in relative decline. Even foreign purchases of US 
corporate bonds now exceed US Treasuries. This is notable considering the ramp 
up in hyper scaler issuance.

Chips are the oil of the new economy  
The power of having oil priced and paid for in dollars was not just the recycling 
of petrodollar surpluses, but the value chain impact of having a key upstream 
resource dollarized. Oil is a critical input to almost every industrial process via 
petrochemicals – it is found in everything from plastic to paints, tires to 
cosmetics. It also plays a role in moving traded goods around the world as the 
transport fuel for ships. We would argue that having oil priced in dollars played a 
role in dollarizing global trade as companies looked to match the currency of 
downstream receivables to the upstream costs of oil. This is consistent with the 
Dominant Currency Paradigm (Gopinath et al, 2020).  
Chips arguably play a similar role in the digital and consumer economy as oil has 
played in the industrial economy. Chips are inputs to everything from consumer 
electronics, to automobiles, computers, communication devices, and most 
recently the bedrock of modern AI systems.  
Indeed, chips make up the lion’s share of spending in the AI buildout. Analysis by 
Epoch AI found that servers represented about 60% of the annualized cost of an 
indicative 1GW data centre in the US (Figure 7). According to the Semiconductor 
Industry Association (SIA), chips represent 95% of an AI server rack's value. In 
simple terms therefore, we can say chips make up over 50% of total capital 
expenditures for an AI data centre. Bloomberg Intelligence estimates hyper-
scaler capital expenditure commitments of close to USD8tn out to 2031 (Figure 
8), which would suggest a further USD4tn of chips demand from AI alone.

the US control over the pricing and trade in chips? 
US control over chips: technology, economic rents, and supply chains   
The integrated circuit was invented in the US in 1958 with the microchip industry 
flourishing in California to become the well-known Silicon Valley. It is hard to 
overstate the continued dominance of the US in chips since then. Work by Sean 
Kenji Starrs of Kings College London maps the present-day dominance in 
compelling terms1. 
While most chip manufacturing happens in Asia – which is where the trade 
surpluses discussed above are being accumulated – most of the economic rents 
to chip-making accrues to US companies. Around 50% of the value in the 
semiconductor supply chain is in the design process where US companies control 
87% of the revenue (Starrs). The US has a near monopoly on the electronic design 
automation (EDA) software necessary for design, and controls around 60% of the 
revenue in semiconductor manufacturing equipment without which chips cannot 
be made. Materials, manufacturing, packaging and testing of chips happen 
outside the US, but these are worth only around 30% of the estimated value of 
the industry. Indeed, Starrs finds that 70% of the profits from the semiconductor 
industry flow to US firms (Figure 9).  
Furthermore, we note that all key countries involved in the supply chain of 
advanced chips remain US aligned. Japan plays an important role in materials, 
while Taiwan and Korea dominate in production (foundries). Importantly each of 
these North Asian players leans geopolitically towards the US over China. The US 
has arguably been less vocal about withdrawing economic support for security in 
North Asia than with NATO. One can thus posit the existence of a US-Asia chip-
dollar bloc. We note that the Netherlands has a monopoly on EUV lithography 
machines required to make the most advanced chips, but production of these 
machines in turn have a dependency on a US optical equipment firm helping 
grant the US crucial leverage even here.  
The potent combination of US control over key upstream technologies and 
intellectual property, alongside a supply chain located in US allies has given the 
US enormous control over chips. In recent years, the US has actively exercised 
this power via export controls to limit China’s access to US advanced chips and 
technologies. This has been the subject of books from Chip War by Chris Miller to 
Chokepoints by Edward Fishman. The importance of ensuring the integrity of US 
export controls was made apparent in recent comments by US Trade 
Representative Greer who noted that the US wants the “crown jewels” of the 
semiconductor industry to remain in the hands of the US or its allies. The US

Where does China fit in with all this?  
This note has looked at key changes in the industrial drivers of global imbalances 
(from fossil fuels to chips) and capital flows to the US (from debt to equities). 
Where does China – with the world’s biggest trade surplus – fit in?  
China now runs an over USD2 trillion manufactured goods surplus per year but 
continues to run a persistent semiconductor deficit (Figure 10). While US export 
controls limit access to the most advanced computing chips, China continues to 
import large volumes of memory and logic chips. Restrictions on access to 
advanced manufacturing equipment, particularly EUV lithography, have 
constrained China’s advances in domestic chip-making at the technological 
frontier, although greater self-sufficiency remains a major strategic priority. We 
note that the other drag on China’s trade position is commodities, mostly fossil 
fuels. Notably therefore, the remaining holes in China’s trade surplus are 
dominated by chip-dollar and petrodollar imports. In a forthcoming paper, we will 
look at China’s gains in renewable energy, new energy exports, and electrification, 
examining how these could emerge as long-term sources of power in both the AI 
race, and in increasing China’s independence from the dollar.  
The recycling of China’s manufactured goods surplus remains the topic of 
considerable debate. China’s surplus is in many ways the mirror of the US deficit, 
albeit with growing contribution from Europe, and increasingly intermediated 
through the Global South. There remains a school of thought that these Chinese 
savings find their way to the US through state banks and custodian balance 
sheets. But we note important points of difference with the chip dollar recycling 
we have discussed.  Nearly 50% of the net international assets held by Korea,

Bringing it together 
In the age of AI, it is well known that the US is dominant in both upstream chip 
technology as well as downstream computing. The US designs and controls 
supply chains for the world’s most advanced chips and is home to 10 times as 
many data centers as any other country (Stanford HAI). Both are sources of US 
power. This note has delved deeper into the workings of the middle of the chain 
where new flows are reinforcing the dollar’s global role in trade and capital flows. 
The manufacturing of high-end chips takes place in Asia ex-China and generates 
large trade surpluses there. This money is finding its way back to the US via 
Asian investments in US equities and corporate bonds. These capital flows are 
both funding the US trade deficit and providing financing to US companies that 
are driving the AI boom. Chips are thus ensuring the continued dominance of 
the dollar in global trade, payments, capital markets, and private sector savings, 
as well as supporting the US AI buildout seen as crucial to the future.  
There is an element of circularity to be conscious of: equity flows and financing 
for US AI related stocks are being funded out of Asian chip-related surpluses 
which are themselves being boosted by demand from US AI companies. This 
could introduce instability in global flows should demand be reassessed. For 
instance, if consumer demand for compute does not keep up with costly 
investments in supply, this would not just reduce earnings estimates for AI 
companies, but ultimately chip demand. One can see how this would challenge 
foreign equity financing for the US from both a push and pull perspective.  
Separately, as we noted in our last paper, proliferation of Chinese open-source 
models could also undermine the profitability of US companies and the 
attractiveness of US markets to global capital.  
The fact that the US dollar has become more dependent on foreign equity 
inflows than US Treasuries should make it inherently more volatile and exposed, 
as we discussed here. Equities by nature are a riskier asset class than bonds. 
Recent realized volatility in S&P500 has been 3 times as much as in a US 10Y 
Treasuries. NASDAQ volatility is double that of the S&P and the Philly 
Semiconductor Index more than triple.  That US deficit financing is now 
dependent on capital flows into a more volatile asset class should spill over into 
currency volatility in time.  
In sum, US dollar dominance is now dependent on US technology leadership 
driven by private companies, and the flows underpinning the dollar are 
increasingly being intermediated on private sector balance sheets. A dollar driven 
by chips, AI and private capital connections should be inherently riskier.
