Global Style Counselling 
Multi-Factor strategies offer resilience and opportunity in a polarised market 
 
◼ Heightened dispersion across equity markets has driven extreme factor performance in recent 
months. Momentum strategies have suffered sharp unwinds, alongside a pronounced rotation 
towards defensive factors more recently. It is fair to say that there has been no shortage of 
excitement in the factor world this year! 
◼ What is particularly striking about these factor moves is the extent to which they have been 
driven by both the long and short legs. In many cases, the correlation between the two sides of a 
factor has collapsed to unprecedented lows and, in some cases, it has even turned negative. This 
is highly unusual given that the long and short legs are, in isolation, effectively long-only 
portfolios, which would typically exhibit a high positive correlation.  
◼ This highlights the exceptional dispersion in stock returns and the existence of many negatively 
correlated stocks within the market. While this dynamic suppresses volatility at the index level, it 
can often amplify the volatility of long/short strategies, particularly those with high exposures to 
the underlying themes driving the market polarisation.  
◼ Our multi-factor strategies mitigate much of this risk through factor diversification and 
portfolio risk controls. We highlighted after the summer momentum unwind that performance 
had remained remarkably resilient. The same has been true during the most recent momentum 
rout. While performance has come back somewhat in recent weeks, both strategies have retained 
the majority of the gains generated earlier in the year. 
◼ They also remain well-placed to benefit from the current market environment. High dispersion 
increases the opportunity set for generating alpha by creating greater scope for market 
inefficiencies to emerge, which systematic stock-picking strategies are often well positioned to 
exploit. It is therefore not surprising that our multi-factor strategies have historically delivered 
some of their strongest performance during similar periods of low correlations and elevated 
dispersion.

Momentum reversal erases YTD gains as Low Vol rebounds 
Following the two momentum performance pullbacks, first during the summer and then again 
over the last month, momentum has surrendered virtually all its earlier outperformance this year 
in both the US and Japan. European price momentum performance has been somewhat less 
volatile and, while it has also experienced a significant drawdown, it has managed to retain part 
of its prior gains. 
Low- versus high-volatility performance has broadly mirrored the recent reversal in momentum, 
showing a strong recovery over the past few weeks. Despite this rebound, the factor remains well 
below the peaks reached in early 2025. The recovery has been most pronounced in the US, Japan 
and Emerging Markets, whilst performance in Europe has been again considerably more muted.

Residual price momentum and earnings momentum were more 
resilient 
Given that momentum and low- versus high-volatility performance have moved in opposite 
directions, it is not surprising that the two have been closely linked. Much of the momentum 
reversal has been driven by a sharp reversal in high-vol/beta stocks, which had previously surged 
alongside the broader market.  
Our preferred momentum measure, residual (or beta-adjusted) momentum, removes the 
component of performance attributable to market exposure by adjusting returns for each stock's 
beta to the market. Isolating stock-specific momentum, tends to reduce drawdowns and proved 
more resilient than traditional price momentum during last week's sell-off, particularly in Japan. 
We also favour a combined momentum signal that incorporates both residual price momentum 
and earnings momentum, which further mitigates momentum drawdown risk.

Negative stock correlations can amplify long/short volatility 
At the market level, the presence of negative correlations helps dampen overall index volatility. 
The implications for long-short equity strategies are more nuanced, however, and depend on 
where these negatively correlated stock pairs sit within the portfolio. When one stock is held long 
and the other short, negative correlation can amplify portfolio risk rather than offset it. 
Momentum and low volatility strategies have been particularly exposed to this effect. One way to 
illustrate this is by examining the correlation between the returns of their long and short legs. As 
discussed earlier, these are both long-only portfolios and would typically exhibit a high positive 
correlation. In recent months, however, that correlation has collapsed to levels seen only during 
the COVID period, when the divergence between work-from-home beneficiaries and reopening 
beneficiaries led to a similarly extreme market polarisation.

remarkably resilient. The same has been true during the most recent momentum rout. Both our

extreme market dispersion well. While performance has come back somewhat in recent weeks,

momentum, which remains one of the core alpha signals within the models. At the same time, 
neither strategy explicitly targets the low volatility factor. Instead, both target a combination of 
good value, momentum and fundamental quality characteristics, whilst the ML version also 
incorporates the ML monthly and ML weekly signals.

In last Monday’s Global Market Equity Arithmetic, we also used principal component analysis 
(PCA) to identify the key statistical drivers of excess stock returns within each market. This is 
particularly relevant in the current environment, as relying solely on traditional sector and 
industry classifications to explain market dispersion can be misleading. In practice, themes 
fuelled by AI and oil-price sensitivity manifest themselves across a broad range of companies and 
industries, meaning their influence is not always adequately captured by standard sector 
definitions.  
PCA-based sensitivity analysis can therefore be highly complementary on top of measuring sector 
exposures. In our analysis of daily stock returns over the past year, we found that the first 
principal component (PC1) explains 27% of the variance in Japanese stock returns and 17% in 
the US but has been relatively subdued in Europe. PC1 is closely associated with the AI theme, 
while PC2 appears to capture an energy and inflation dynamic that emerged alongside the rise in 
oil prices during the first quarter of 2026. 
Multi-factor performance exhibits some positive correlation with the first two principal 
components, although their influence is substantially weaker compared to individual factors.

Negative stock correlations are both an opportunity and a challenge 
High return dispersion and low correlations can present challenges for long/short investors by 
amplifying portfolio risk and increasing the likelihood of being caught on the wrong side of the 
themes driving market polarisation. As we saw earlier, our multi-factor strategies mitigate much 
of this risk through factor diversification and portfolio risk controls. 
At the same time, such an environment can be highly supportive for the performance of 
long/short investing. High dispersion increases the opportunity set for generating alpha by 
creating greater scope for market inefficiencies to emerge, which systematic stock-picking 
strategies are often well positioned to exploit. It is therefore not surprising that our multi-factor 
strategies have historically delivered some of their strongest performance during similar periods 
of low correlations and elevated dispersion.
