Issued: November 2026
THE EMERGING VIEW
EM equity and AI: cheap exposure today, 
broader impact tomorrow
By Gustavo Medeiros and Ben Underhill
Investors are increasingly concerned about overexposure to AI. While the 
MSCI Emerging Markets (EM) Index is heavily weighted towards technology 
stocks, those stocks trade at a very significant discount to their US 
counterparts. Moreover, 60% of the index is not tech. This majority of 
companies are not growing their earnings at the rate of the tech sector, 
but are still growing them at an accelerating double-digit clip, comparable 
with developed markets (DM), but at roughly half the valuation.
EM’s exposure to AI today comes mostly through Korea, Taiwan and China, but AI as an economic force 
will undoubtedly become more important for EM growth in the coming years, through tech companies and 
more broadly. In this month’s Emerging View, we present a quantitative framework to assess EM countries’ 
exposure to both positive and negative AI risk. In our view, AI’s growing influence in EM is also likely to 
become another source of alpha, at both the country and the industry level, because it will affect EM 
economies to varying degrees and through different channels.

1. EM AI exposure is large, but offers good value 
The EM equity index has always had a changing face. In 2007, it was an index dominated 
by energy, materials and financials. By 2020, China’s weighting in the index was 40%, led by 
internet stocks, before dropping to around 20% today. IT was about 13% of MSCI EM in 
2019 and is about 41% now, similar to the S&P 500 and above that of the MSCI World (29%). 
Roughly three-quarters of EM tech is made by three world-class companies. Taiwan’s TSMC 
is close to 15% of the index and Korea’s Samsung and SK Hynix are together another c. 15%.

A valuation-driven drawdown in Korea and Taiwan since June has pulled MSCI EM lower, 
but earnings continue to rise across the board. Index forward earnings are about 20% 
higher than in June and imply growth of around 60% over the next year. These dynamics 
have left MSCI EM at 8.6x forward earnings against 17.6x for MSCI World, a relative multiple 
of 0.49 against an average of 0.74 since 2008, and the lowest in that period. EM tech 
stocks trade at an even steeper discount. 
Earnings have outrun prices
EM IT forward earnings are about seven times their end-2022 level (+620%) against a total 
return of 306%. For developed-market IT (MSCI World IT) the two have moved together: 
earnings +224%, total return +219%. EM IT now trades at 7.1x forward earnings, the lowest 
since our data begin in 2005, against an average of 14.1 over that period. MSCI World IT 
currently trades at 19.5x, close to its own average of 19.9 (Fig 2A).

Generally, tech should trade at a valuation premium. The sector has seen, and will likely 
continue to see, more innovation and stronger growth long term than other industries. 
AI is supercharging this further. But the discount on EM stocks is arguably not pricing 
this. On a price/earnings/growth (PEG) basis, which divides forward P/E by consensus 
earnings per share (EPS) growth across the next two years, MSCI EM is at 0.24 against

the gap is similar: 0.10 against 0.41. Both are the cheapest on this measure since at

The two-year figure is flattered by this year’s memory-driven jump in earnings, which 
is unlikely to be repeated due to the rapid run-up in memory prices being behind us. 
Spot DDR5 prices have risen around 5x in the last year. Prices are still going up, but

So, as a check we also use year-2 growth alone, which strips most of this effect out. 
Expected growth is then slightly higher in EM than in DM (15.7% against 13.6%), 
and EM’s PEG is still well under half MSCI World’s (0.55 against 1.29). And ultimately, 
we think the risk to earnings growth estimates two years out is heavily skewed to 
the upside. 
The takeaway is that EM is now more of a tech index, but each dollar of AI earnings 
costs less than half as much in EM as in DM. Ex-technology EM is also far cheaper 
than the rest of developed markets: 0.58 for EM ex-IT against 1.76 for MSCI World 
ex-IT (0.88 against 1.97 on year-2 growth). 
Why it is cheap, and why that may be too cheap 
The low multiple is partially a reflection of mega-earnings, but it also is the market 
pricing a chip cycle peak. Korean tech is dominated by memory, and memory earnings 
have been the most cyclical in global equities historically, usually trading on low 
multiples near the top. However, it is quite possible, in our view, that the AI build-out 
will materially reduce the cyclicality of chip demand. The marginal buyer is no longer a 
consumer upgrading an iPhone or a business renewing its laptops but a hyperscaler 
racing to add capacity responding to exponential demand from agentic AI and robotics. 
We would not go as far as saying the industry is no longer cyclical, but the shape of 
the cycle has likely shifted, and comparisons with the pre-AI era may not be a useful 
mental model. Because chip prices, particularly memory prices, have risen so much, 
the risk that aggressive supply expansion leads to oversupply is still real, and high 
prices may solve high prices in the end. But industry guidance on demand against 
chipmakers’ expansion plans suggests that is highly unlikely in 2027 or 2028. 
Beyond that, demand is harder to forecast and Chinese supply, namely from 
ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT), may become a bigger variable, although 
we expect capacity to be orientated to the domestic market. Higher memory prices, 
especially high-bandwidth memory (HBM), are also leading companies to look for 
ways to optimise the architecture of servers to slow down demand and use cheaper 
forms of memory. It is also leading to innovation, for example high-bandwidth flash 
and accelerators based on static random-access memory (SRAM). However, it is too 
early in their design processes to relieve HBM demand, and in fact should they scale, 
this would likely shift the supply bottleneck, to NAND for example, rather than relieve 
it. SK Hynix indicated in Q3 earnings that it has more than half of its capacity taken 
for the next two years and no risk of supply surplus until at least 2030. If its guidance 
is accurate, there is much upside as the company trades at slightly over 3x earnings. 
After all, the company delivered USD 62bn operating profit in the quarter, which 
was close to 10% of its market cap. 
At about 7x earnings, EM IT already discounts a sharp fall in chip earnings growth that 
may not materialise. Since the EM IT peak in June, forward earnings estimates are up 
50%, while the index is 30% lower! Bouts of volatility will remain, but the starting 
valuation gives investors a margin of safety that DM tech does not.
Financial conditions matter too. Should conditions continue to tighten, as they have 
done in 2026, capital for new projects may dry up and data centre demand would 
slow. However, the important nuance is that less data centre supply would also 
keep compute in deficit and likely prolong the overall cycle.

2. EM ex-tech earnings holding up – valuations 
remain attractive 
What about the other 60% of the index? Outside tech, EM forward EPS is up about 15% this 
year, in line with MSCI World ex-IT (+16%). But EM, again, is much cheaper. EM ex-IT trades 
at 9.9x, below its own average of 10.7x, while MSCI World ex-IT trades above its own 
(16.8x against 15.4x). The 41% discount is the widest since at least 2010.
The discount is not only a matter of mix. EM outside tech is heavy in banks, energy and 
materials, which trade on low multiples everywhere, but EM is also cheaper than DM sector 
by sector: in nine of the ten sectors outside tech, and by more than its average since 2010 
in eight of them. EM industrials trade at 8.9x forward earnings against 21.0x for MSCI World 
industrials, consumer discretionary at 11.1x against 20.3x, and financials, a fifth of the EM 
index, at 8.8x against 13.4x.
Earnings growth outside tech is broad as well. Since end-June, EM ex-IT forward earnings 
estimates have risen 6%, in line with MSCI World ex-IT. This year, industrials (+79%), energy 
(+43%) and materials (+30%) have led, consistent with the AI build-out reaching beyond 
chips into power equipment and metals, which we return to in Section 4.
The breadth shows up in country returns. Ten of the 24 markets in MSCI EM have 
outperformed MSCI World this year (+13%), and eight of them are outside Korea and 
Taiwan: Colombia (+56%), Poland (+40%), Peru and Hungary (+33%), Thailand (+28%), 
Greece (+24%), Türkiye (+22%) and Egypt (+16%). Twelve rose more than DM ex-US 
(+10%), and an equal-weighted average of the 24 markets has kept pace with 
MSCI World (+13%) despite steep falls in China, India and Indonesia, as per Fig 5.

3. AI will reach EM across sectors 
The impact of AI on EM economies has been most obvious this year in the huge export 
revenue gains of chip exporters. These gains have been largest in Taiwan and Korea, but 
also significant in ASEAN broadly as well as in Mexico. Mexico’s high-tech exports have 
roughly doubled over the past year, recently surpassing USD 100bn annually to the US. 
However, as AI investment and adoption broaden, the impacts on EM economies will 
become increasingly multifaceted. Commodity exporters will benefit increasingly as the 
AI build-out adds a structural demand growth vector on top of typical industrial cycles. 
Countries that are well positioned to absorb foreign direct investment (FDI) in new data 
centre capacity will benefit from inflows. Countries that adopt AI quickly may reap the 
rewards of productivity gains. 
Of course, AI’s impact on EM will not be positive across the board. AI may prove 
increasingly disruptive for EM economies that count human capital in services as a key 
strength. A prime example is India, whose call centres and general tech services offshoring 
are a significant percentage of GDP and may well be disrupted by agentic AI replacing the 
need for cheap labour in these areas. The 40% fall in MSCI India IT in dollar terms from its 
January 2025 high reflects this risk, although the downturn so far has been entirely 
valuation driven, with sector growth still coming through.  
To map countries’ exposure to AI investment and adoption, we scored 25 EM economies 
across five key pillars. The net score gives us a read of country-level AI exposure in 
relative terms. 
The upside is clear. Demand for electronics, energy, connectivity and the associated 
commodities is real, happening now and modelled closely by analysts. The output of these 
goods is simple to measure across countries. The potential negative impacts are more 
abstract and harder to measure due to the uncertain ways AI will shape labour markets and 
the potential for countries to offset AI displacement through adoption. This quantitative 
framework is therefore illustrative of risk skew rather than an attempt at predicting outcomes.
Framework
We score 25 EM countries on four channels through which AI boosts growth and one 
through which it threatens it. Each indicator is put on a 0-100 scale and combined into pillars 
by weighted average. The gain score is a weighted average of the four gain pillars; the loss 
score is the services pillar. The net score is gain minus loss. For investors, the individual 
pillars matter at least as much as the net score. Macro resilience is shown alongside it, 
because a negatively exposed country with strong growth and ample reserves is in a 
different position from one with weak buffers.

4. The winners & middle group 
Hardware carries 40% of the gain score, the largest income channel. In 2025, semiconductor 
exports were about 25% of GDP in Taiwan, 23% in Malaysia, and about 8% in Korea and 
Vietnam. Memory prices have lifted values sharply since: Korea’s chip exports hit a record 
USD 60bn in September, up 263% on the previous year. Malaysia’s chip exports rose 82% 
in the first eight months, and its total exports rose 54.5% in September, led by electrical 
and electronic goods (+69%), as per Fig 8. Net of chip imports, Taiwan’s 2025 chip trade 
surplus was roughly 9% of GDP, Korea’s 4% and Malaysia’s 7%, while Vietnam, Thailand 
and China were net importers, capturing the boom through assembly.

Power, construction and property: the data centre hosts
Data centre build-out follows demand and AI adoption, so will naturally grow alongside 
compute demand in emerging markets. However, another factor is that US power and 
permitting constraints are incentivising hyperscalers to build offshore, where reliable, ideally 
clean, power is plentiful. This trend is likely to accelerate after the US midterm election results, 
which showed the Democratic Party taking a clear majority in both houses of Congress as 
well as the governorships of key states for AI build-out, including Texas. 
When considering alternative data centre locations, the World Bank finds power reliability 
matters far more than its price. For now, we find Malaysia (+27) is the biggest direct beneficiary: 
credible projects and domestic AI capex are worth roughly 0.56% of GDP a year, more than 
twice any other EM, and capacity is set to roughly double to 2GW by end-2026. Korea (0.22%), 
Thailand (0.18%), Taiwan (0.15%) and the UAE (0.11%) follow. Data centres employ few people 
once built, but the gains flow to utilities, contractors, equipment makers and real estate. 
Capital and commodities: the Gulf, Chile, Peru
Saudi Arabia (+33) and the UAE (+24) score on adoption, sovereign investment and data 
centres with almost no negative services exposure. Humain targets about 3GW by 2030 and 
more than 6GW by 2034 and Stargate UAE a 1GW campus. The gains accrue mainly to the 
state. Chile (+23) is a key copper supplier and scores well on clean power. AI adds marginal 
copper demand, though even a doubling of data centre use by 2030 would be about 2% of 
global consumption. However, AI is only one of three themes. Energy and defence security 
and supply-chain resilience are behind a multi-year cycle that should bring miners long-term 
contracts and FDI.
For the middle group (Indonesia, Türkiye, Brazil, Peru, Mexico and others), AI is a modest 
tailwind through adoption, data centres, assembly or commodities. Their ability to implement 
structural reforms allowing them to be inserted into global supply chains will be key to boosting 
their potential across different areas. In Indonesia, the nickel supply chain is key. Türkiye has 
another opportunity for deeper integration with Europe, particularly through the North Atlantic 
Treaty Organization (NATO), where technology (and AI integration) will be key for the long term. 
Brazil is a renewable energy superpower, with a BRL 500bn (c. USD 90bn) data centre capex 
pipeline. It is a country of continental dimensions and an early adopter of digital technologies, 
and can benefit from adoption. Mexico’s boom in exports of tech products to the US is 
compensating for slower growth in the auto industry. Implementing reforms to house a larger 
share of the supply chain with more value-add locally would be the obvious next step.

5. The downside, and why exposure is not destiny
Net negative exposure is concentrated in India (-60), the Philippines (-53), Poland (-45), 
Hungary (-30) and Czechia (-25). All five have large, outsourced services sectors serving 
foreign clients, worth about 6-7% of GDP in India and the Philippines, and 4-5% in Central 
Europe. This work was outsourced to cut costs, so the risk is that clients look to cut costs 
further via AI. The Philippines gets the highest loss score (90) because call centre work 
is the most substitutable. India combines a high loss score with weak gain pillars. 
Strong adoption potential offsets part of the risk in Central Europe. 
An important nuance is that while the sectors look relatively small against the whole 
economy, they are larger against the pool of graduate jobs. India’s tech workforce is about 
6 million (about 1% of employment) but a leading graduate employer, while the Philippines’ 
AI IT and business process management (IT-BPM) sector employs 1.9 million (4%), another 
key source of well-paid graduate employment. A rise in graduate unemployment is a 
social risk as well as an economic one, raising risks of social discontent and brain drain. 
However, there are various paths to this negative outcome being mitigated. 
What has happened so far
Markets have priced the risk through valuations: MSCI India IT is down roughly 40% in dollar 
terms from its January 2025 high, while its forward earnings have risen 14%. But so far, there 
is little sign of AI-driven job losses in Indian tech. Employment in India’s IT and business-
process sector rose from 5.4 million in FY24 to around 6.0 million in FY26, according to the 
National Association of Software and Service Companies (NASSCOM). The slowdown in 
hiring at the large IT firms since 2023 mostly reflects the unwinding of a post-COVID hiring 
boom. The five largest employers (TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech and Cognizant) added 
about 286,000 staff in FY22, cut about 68,000 in FY24 as demand normalised, and have 
added a net 16,000 or so since. Part of the recent restraint also reflects restructuring, 
including job cuts of about 2% at TCS in FY26 and reorganisations at HCLTech and Cognizant.
Hiring has also shifted toward global capability centres (GCCs), the in-house technology 
and operations hubs that multinationals run in India. GCC headcount has grown roughly 
10% a year to around 2.3 million, more than a third of the sector’s workforce. Some clients 
appear to be replacing higher-cost staff at home with lower-cost staff in their own Indian 
centres, while taking productivity gains from AI.
Job-postings data tell a similar story. The Naukri JobSpeak index of new listings is back on 
its pre-COVID trend after the 2021-22 boom. IT-software postings are above pre-pandemic 
levels, though still below their long-run trend, while postings in business process 
outsourcing (BPO) and IT-enabled services, often cited as the most exposed to AI, are at 
a record high. Fresher hiring at the large firms has fallen from its FY22 peak but, on 
management targets, is broadly back to pre-boom levels in FY27, with Wipro the exception.
Where AI is showing up is in productivity, growth and pricing. India’s four largest IT firms 
have grown dollar revenue about 12% since mid-2022 while headcount fell about 2%. 
Revenue per employee at TCS, Infosys, HCLTech and Cognizant has risen 11-16% from its 
2023 low and is back near or above its previous highs, after rising slowly, if at all, for most 
of the previous 15 years (Fig 10). Revenue growth itself has slowed to 1-3% a year, and 
analysts expect prices to fall by around 3.5% as contracts are renewed with AI built in, 
holding sector growth near 3% against a ten-year average of 5%. 
In the Philippines, the outsourcing industry earned about USD 40bn and employed about 
1.9 million people in 2025. In July, its industry body, the IT and Business Process Association 
of the Philippines (IBPAP), cut its 2028 targets from USD 59bn of revenue and 2.5 million 
jobs to about USD 47bn and 2.0 million (mid-points of its new ranges), citing AI. In its 
downside case, the sector would employ fewer people in 2028 than it does today. HSBC 
finds no collapse yet, however: employment and business-services exports are still rising, 
though more slowly than before COVID.

So far, AI has slowed hiring at the large IT firms, especially of graduates, while total 
employment in the sector has kept growing. The larger risk to GDP is that clients pocket 
AI savings as lower prices without buying more work. That would show up as falling dollar 
revenue at the big firms, which is the number to watch.
Exposure is not destiny
There are a few factors that suggest this negative exposure may not end up being 
translated into economic and asset price downside. 
The first is the possibility that AI adoption and productivity growth in tech service industries 
offsets job disruption or loss of business via clients reshoring. The World Bank finds that 
South Asia’s exposed jobs are mostly complementary to AI: only 7% of jobs in the region 
combine high AI exposure with low complementarity, against 15% elsewhere. 
They also find that AI skills already command a wage premium of nearly 30%. Indian IT is 
turning AI into revenue (about USD 3.1bn a year at TCS, over 10% of sales), and an AI-enabled 
Indian firm still costs less than an AI-enabled British or American one. Adoption is rising fast: 
the share of Indian firms using AI went from near zero in mid-2024 to 23%. US firms with 
more AI exposure have also expanded links with South Asian suppliers and the World Bank 
finds reshoring is not the dominant effect. Again, the open question is whether new work 
grows fast enough to offset price deflation on the old. 
The second key point is economic resilience and growth in non-tech sectors. In many 
cases, non-tech growth may more than offset any loss in tech or business services 
momentum. India is the most resilient economy in our sample, with GDP growth near 7% 
driven by domestic demand, foreign exchange (FX) reserves covering nearly eight months 
of imports and only about 10% of jobs at risk on the IMF measure. The Philippines also 
scores highly at 72, again due to higher GDP growth. 
India is also a data centre beneficiary in its own right. Operational capacity is about 1.6-1.8GW 
(JLL; Cushman & Wakefield), with record-low vacancy and most new supply pre-let to 
hyperscalers. Forecasts put capacity at 6-10GW by 2030 and investment at around 
USD 200bn, about 4-5% of GDP over five years. That makes India one of EM’s largest data 
centre markets, but relative to its economy, the build-out is still a fraction of Malaysia’s, 
where capacity is already roughly ten times larger as a share of GDP.
Central Europe scores low on economic resilience (12-32), mainly on slower growth, but the 
measure misses EU membership and the European defence and infrastructure cycle, which 
cushion Poland, Czechia and Hungary.

Overall, we think it likely that AI disruption will not materially affect growth. The services 
pillar measures exposure, but revenue can hold up if clients buy more work or firms move up 
the value chain. Innovation and productivity gains from AI will also likely create new jobs in 
other industries, potentially more than offsetting losses in information and communications 
technology (ICT) services roles (such as software engineering) over the long run. 
Passive versus active
The argument for favouring active over passive management in EM equity is simple to make. 
The consistently larger dispersion of country returns relative to MSCI World, as well as 
information asymmetry for less well-researched companies in these markets, means the 
scope for outperformance is simply greater. Historical data has proven this; EM managers 
have outperformed benchmarks far more frequently than DM peers in recent years. 
The S&P 500 has outperformed around 80% of US equity managers over three, five and ten 
years. MSCI EM, by contrast, has ranked between the 62nd and 78th percentile of EM 
equity managers over the same periods, and the largest passive EM fund lower still: over 
ten years, close to 90% of EM managers have beaten it (Fig 11).

The impact of AI on the index makes active advantage even larger in our view, for two key 
reasons. First, there are alpha opportunities from the uneven impact of AI across EM 
economies, which is reflected in our scoring method, and across companies, with those 
best able to cut costs and target revenues using technology at a competitive advantage. 
Second, a passive allocation now gives investors exposure of close to 30% of the index to 
just three stocks: TSMC and the two Korean memory giants. However, this may miss smaller 
companies, or off-index stocks, which are entrenched in the same supply chains yet may 
offer much more value compared to globally well-owned mega-cap stocks. There are also a 
number of indirect ways for an active manager to benefit from the tailwinds of an AI cycle; 
for example, around power generation, thermal management and the associated 
infrastructure, as well as raw materials. 
For now, we think selective exposure to EM AI giants is ultimately attractive. It comes at 
around 7x earnings and is pricing a cyclical peak in earnings which may not arrive. However, 
the sector remains prone to sharp swings in earnings and sentiment, as the valuation 
drawdown in Korean stocks over the past four months shows.

Summary & Conclusion
Emerging markets provide exposure to a diverse array of countries and earnings drivers. 
However, there is no escaping the fact that AI is likely to become an increasingly 
influential force in EM equity returns, in North Asia and beyond. Most EM economies 
are likely to benefit from the AI investment wave, via tech hardware or through other 
channels, such as commodity exports or data centre investment. Some may be more 
challenged by AI-driven disruption to their services sectors, although the extent of that 
disruption remains highly unpredictable. Countries like India that look exposed to AI 
disruption have the opportunity to offset the risk through AI adoption. Macro factors 
such as economic resilience and reform may also prove more important than AI-driven 
risk. That could leave value opportunities in sectors that sell off in anticipation of 
lower earnings that do not materialise, as in India today.
In our view, the best way to navigate these rapidly evolving dynamics is active 
management. Active management in EM has historically had a high success rate 
because of the dispersion between countries (Fig 11), and AI is likely to widen that 
dispersion, providing further opportunities to generate alpha.
