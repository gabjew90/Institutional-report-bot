Christmas, delegated: a 
glimpse of your festive 
season with a personal 
AI assistant
Imagine Christmas without the 
endless to-do list. Your personal AI 
assistant finds the perfect gifts, books 
the family getaway, coordinates 
everyone’s calendars and makes 
dinner reservations — all while you 
focus on the festivities. Sounds like 
a Christmas wish? AI agents are 
bringing us closer to that reality, 
moving beyond answering questions 
to actually getting things done.
What a personal AI agent is, 
and who is building them
A chatbot answers questions. An 
agent carries out tasks. It plans, uses 
websites and connected services, 
works through several steps and 
keeps going after you close the app, 
returning when something changes 
or when it needs your approval. The 
idea is to replace a dozen apps and 
browser tabs with one conversation 
in a place you already use, such as a 
messaging app.
The agent is always on. It runs in 
a single, persistent chat in which 
the context from all previous 
conversations stays loaded, so you 
don’t need to repeat yourself. It also 
runs on its own dedicated computer, 
so it keeps working while you’re 
offline. This may sound like a minor 
feature, but in our testing it made a 
substantial difference.
The category is only weeks old, and 
several providers launched within a 
short window:
Meta’s Muse launched on 8 
September. It runs on a private cloud 
computer that holds the agent and 
the user’s data, and is rolling out 
first in the US on iOS, Android, the 
web and WhatsApp. Third-party 
estimates put it above 2 million daily 
users within five weeks of launch.
•	 OpenAI’s Dots launched on 29 
September for ChatGPT Pro 
and Business Premium users in 
eligible markets. Personal Pro 
access initially excludes the EEA, 
Switzerland and the UK. 
•	 Instinct, a start-up, launched its 
agent in August. You text or call it, 
and access is invite-only. 
•	 Google’s Gemini Spark has also 
rolled out. It is a personal agent 
that works in the background.

But trust is the product
A personal agent is only as useful as what it knows about 
you. To book the right villa, it needs your dates, your 
budget and your family’s dietary needs. To buy a gift, it 
needs your payment details and a sense of your relatives’ 
tastes. To coordinate calendars, it needs access to yours. 
The more it can see, the more it can do for you.
That is also why security and trust are critical to the 
success of these tools. An agent that reads your inbox, 
remembers your preferences, signs in to your accounts 
and spends money on your behalf holds the keys to a 
large part of your life. Coverage of the launches notes 
that giving an agent more access to personal data 
widens what it can do but also creates safety and 
reliability risks. Productivity and security pull in opposite 
directions, and trust sits where they meet: users will share 
more only if they believe the agent is safe, and the agent 
becomes more useful only if they share more.
Providers say they have built protections around each 
part of the interaction. 
Below are their own descriptions; we found no 
independent audit of any of them.
•	 Memory and personal data: Muse lets users inspect 
and edit stored memories; Dots and Instinct offer 
controls over shared or indexed data.  
Open issue: granular memory management remains 
inconsistent across providers. 
•	 Secure environments: Muse and Dots run in isolated 
cloud environments that separate user data and limit 
agent access.  
Open issue: independent security audits remain limited, 
and Meta says it is developing additional privacy 
protections. 
•	 Permissions and autonomy: Muse uses a dedicated 
permission agent to authorise actions; Dots lets users 
choose when approval is required.  
Open issue: the balance between seamless automation 
and sufficient human oversight is still unsettled.

The usual advice for users is to start small and review 
each authorization individually.
In agentic AI, model intelligence and a mature 
ecosystem of connected services drive capability, but 
trust determines how much of that capability users 
will actually adopt. We are in the early innings of the 
agentic transformation, and much remains unknown: 
how agents will be regulated, which use cases will prove 
most valuable and whether consumer behaviour will 
fundamentally change. Like every technological shift, 
this one will create winners and losers. Who they will be 
remains to be seen.
