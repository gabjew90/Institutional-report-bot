I was going through my travel folder recently, and discovered a stockpile of old European 
currency.  Beautifully illustrated Dutch guilders, French francs, Italian lira and Deutsche 
marks offered wistful memories of my early European travels. They were also a reminder 
of an inefficient financial system that faded away when the euro came into being. 
The common currency and its underlying economy have been managed by the European 
Central Bank (ECB) for almost three decades.  The ECB has brought benefits to the bloc, 
but it has also had its struggles.  With a leadership transition at hand, the ECB is facing a 
challenging future. 
Well-established now, the euro and the ECB were seen as grand experiments when they 
were proposed. The perceived benefit was improved commerce: it was costly for firms to 
manage the exchange rate risk of doing business in lira, pesetas, marks and francs. The 
common currency brought together 11 countries with nearly $10 trillion of real gross 
domestic product (GDP). 
The euro combined nations where inflation had been historically low (like Germany and 
the Netherlands) with others where it had been historically high (like Italy and Spain). 
Under unified monetary policy, inflation and interest rates in euro area countries began to 
converge.  This unleashed substantial amounts of investment into the southern part of the 
euro area, boosting their economic fortunes.

During its first decade, the euro and the ECB worked very well.  Euro area growth advanced, 
productivity reached parity with the United States, interest rates fell, and inflation was steady.  But 
the Global Financial Crisis ended the reverie. 
Failing to diagnose the depth of the difficulty, the ECB raised rates in the summer of 2008 at the 
same time that the Federal Reserve was easing aggressively.  When the depth of the crisis 
became apparent, the charter of the ECB made it difficult for it to initiate quantitative easing; fiscally 
conservative countries were hesitant to support those who were not.  In the end, a complicated 
series of programs was introduced, each with strict limitations. 
The ECB was too quick to raise rates after the GFC, at a time that the euro area economy was 
fragile. That contributed to the European debt crisis of 2010-12, where several member nations 
came dangerously close to default.  The tide turned with ECB President Mario Draghi’s pledge to 
do “whatever it takes.” Sluggish growth over the balance of the last decade kept eurozone inflation 
well below target, seemingly impervious to the ECB’s efforts. 
Christine Lagarde, the current ECB president, succeeded Draghi in 2019.  Lagarde has announced 
her plans to step down next February, before her term expires.  Her timing may have been 
informed by major elections that will take place in Europe next spring; planning a transition may be 
easier under current leadership.  Spots will also be open for a new chief economist and a head of 
the ECB’s market operations. 
Christine Lagarde will go down as one of the foremost economic policymakers of her generation, 
having served previously as the Managing Director of the IMF and as the French Finance Minister.  
(She was the first woman to hold all three roles.) As the ECB considers who will lead the 
organization into its fourth decade, it faces a number of difficult challenges: 
• 
Like other central banks, the ECB has struggled to achieve its inflation target.  Europe has 
been hit with two energy shocks in the last four years, and is struggling to define a path to 
better resilience.  Europe may lag in achieving productivity gains from artificial intelligence (AI). 
Rates may have to be kept higher for longer in the years ahead, which will be costly for 
member states.

Even though its balance sheet is larger than it would like, the ECB is under renewed pressure 
to ameliorate debt difficulty within the euro area.  The Growth and Stability Pact has been 
reworked on several occasions, but member states are running large budget deficits.  Fiscal 
stress in France has led to suggestions that the central bank use one of its emergency bond-
buying programs to settle markets.  But the bar for doing so is very high, absent a broader 
financial crisis. 
• 
The efforts of the ECB have been hamstrung by a lack of economic reform in the euro area.  
Progress in implementing the Draghi Report has been frustratingly slow, leaving the bloc to 
underperform. The result has been a form of stagflation that no one is comfortable with. 
• 
The ECB now sets policy for 21 member states.  Defining a policy that is acceptable to such a 
diverse set of countries is not easy.  The ECB’s Governing Council does not announce the 
outcomes of monetary policy votes, but securing a plurality of support for policy has been 
difficult at times.  Achieving consensus may become even tougher in the future. 
• 
The ECB is a supranational institution.  The politics of Europe, however, are becoming more 
nationalistic, and authorities in power have been the object of protest. The ECB may find that 
sustaining support within Europe’s political power structure may be more challenging in the 
years ahead.  And while the ECB charter makes it very difficult for a member to depart, the 
possibility of such an occurrence cannot be dismissed. 
The opportunity to exchange my old European notes for euros has long passed.  I certainly hope 
that the opportunity to use them again does not arise. 
Too Much Of A Good Thing? 
Farmers understand the risks of monoculture: planting only one crop, year after year. While initial 
yields may be strong, they will weaken as the soil’s nutrients are depleted. Sustainable farming 
requires variation in which seeds are sown. 
The U.S. economy is gaining at an impressive pace, but with a risk of too much concentration in AI-
related investments. A healthy economy should see growth across sectors. Though AI is still a 
dominant theme, we’d like to see green shoots of more diverse investment growth. 
Business investment has led economic gains in the U.S. in the year to date. In the third quarter, 
nonresidential investment contributed 1.8% of the strong 3.4% gain in annualized gross domestic 
product.  Data centers and computing equipment were the top investment items.  
Markets have also borne out this trend. Investor enthusiasm for AI and its supporting technologies 
has been unabated since ChatGPT’s launch in 2022. Capital markets stood ready to support the AI 
buildout. Debt issuance by the six major hyperscalers (large-scale cloud computing providers) is on 
track to nearly double this year. The technology sector drove two years of equity market 
outperformance in 2024 and 2025, but hyperscalers’ values have become more volatile this year. 
We have kept an eye on this market dynamic as a risk to the wealth effects that have enabled 
robust consumer spending. 
But the intensity of activity surrounding AI leaves the market and economic performance heavily 
reliant on this sector. A tech downturn could spur a broad recession if it is the only source of 
growth; better diversity is needed.

Last quarter’s GDP report reveals the extent of this concentration. Apart from computer equipment

specialty industrial equipment and turbines, all of which are associated with the AI buildout.  
The concentration risk presented by a heavy emphasis on AI came into focus around the midterm 
election, when candidates favoring more restrictions on data center construction did very well.  
While we do not expect the outcome of election 2026 to meaningfully alter the trajectory of the AI 
buildout, it cannot continue upward at such elevated rates indefinitely.  We need a more well-
rounded set of investment drivers. 
There are some nascent signs that capital spending is broadening. The White House boasts a list 
of investments launched since 2025, counting 522 projects totaling $383 billion of investment. 
Though semiconductors lead by value, the majority of identified projects are in sectors like metals 
and materials, energy, pharma, vehicles, defense and machinery. These projects account for 
165,000 new jobs.  
The AI investment boom helped to carry the economy through a dry spell. Now, a healthier variety 
of investment will ensure that yields will be sustained. 
Transporting Inflation 
Headline commodity price spikes capture instant market attention.  The effects of shipping 
disruptions are slower to surface: they move gradually through freight costs, inventories and 
margins before reaching consumers.   
Freight rates, port congestion, and delivery times all point to elevated shipping pressures.  
Container rates have increased sharply as diversions around the Red Sea have lengthened 
shipping times, while insurers have raised premiums substantially and withdrawn coverage from 
some routes.  Longer voyages also burn more fuel and tie up vessels and containers, tightening 
capacity elsewhere.

The ripple effects of maritime bottlenecks and high energy costs now extend well beyond ocean 
transit.  As shippers seek faster, more reliable alternatives, demand and rates have climbed sharply 
for both air cargo and overland logistics networks.  
While it takes time for those costs to come ashore, the delay is temporary.  Goods arriving today 
may have been shipped under earlier contracts or drawn from existing inventories.  Businesses 
may absorb higher costs through margins rather than pass them straight to customers.  
Nevertheless, the impact is already starting to show in pipeline pressures, with import and producer 
prices trending up.   
An OECD analysis suggests that a permanent doubling of container shipping costs lifts inflation in 
imported manufactured goods by 1.3 percentage points across OECD economies in the first year.  
According to the International Energy Forum, higher shipping costs account for an estimated 40% 
to 70% of the rise in global inflation since February 2026.   
International Monetary Fund research adds another dimension, suggesting that longer transit times 
themselves can fuel inflation: a 100-hour increase in shipping time is estimated to raise U.S. 
consumer inflation by around 0.5 percentage points at its peak, roughly five months later. 
The longer ships stay off course, the greater the risk that higher costs travel from shipping lanes to 
shopping lanes.
