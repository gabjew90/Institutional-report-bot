Overview 
Quality remains one of the most intuitive and widely adopted equity factors. 
Investors naturally prefer companies that generate consistent profits, allocate 
capital efficiently, maintain strong balance sheets and produce sustainable 
cash flows. These characteristics have historically been associated with 
stronger long-term performance and greater resilience through market cycles. 
The economic foundations of Quality remain intact. However, the market 
expression of Quality has evolved. Over the last two decades, the rise of 
asset-light, highly scalable Technology businesses has reshaped the sectoral 
and business-model composition of global equities. As a result, unconstrained 
Quality portfolios increasingly reflect exposure to highly profitable Technology 
companies and US mega-cap growth stocks. 
Our analysis highlights five key findings: 
– Quality remains economically relevant and empirically robust. 
– The realised exposure of Quality has become more Technology-led and 
less inherently defensive. 
– Return on Assets (ROA) is now the dominant driver of broad Quality 
performance. 
– After neutralising sector, country and style exposures, Accruals emerge as 
the strongest pure stock-selection signal. 
– The growing divergence between profitability and earnings quality reinforces 
the importance of understanding implementation choices. 
Quality still works. But implementation increasingly determines the exposure 
investors actually receive.

1. Introduction 
The Quality factor is one of the most widely adopted investment styles within modern 
factor investing. At its core, Quality reflects the belief that companies exhibiting strong 
profitability, efficient capital allocation, robust financial positions and sustainable earnings 
should outperform weaker businesses over the long term. 
Academic research and practical investment experience have identified several 
characteristics commonly associated with high-quality companies: 
– Persistent profitability 
– Efficient use of assets 
– Strong cash-flow generation 
– Conservative leverage 
– Earnings supported by cash rather than accounting adjustments 
Such businesses often display greater resilience through economic cycles, stronger 
competitive positions and a greater capacity to reinvest capital at attractive rates. 
While the intuition behind Quality remains straightforward, implementation is considerably 
more complex. There is no universally accepted definition of Quality. Different providers 
place different emphasis on profitability, balance-sheet strength, earnings stability and 
cash-flow measures. 
Within the FTSE Russell framework, Quality combines profitability and leverage through 
four complementary measures: 
– Return on Assets (ROA) 
– Change in Asset Turnover 
– Accruals 
– Operating Cash Flow to Debt 
Together, these measures seek to identify companies that are not only profitable, but 
whose profitability is sustainable, efficiently generated and supported by strong financial 
foundations. 
The importance of this broader perspective has increased in recent years. Structural 
changes in global equity markets, particularly the rise of Technology companies and the 
growing importance of intangible capital, have altered how traditional Quality metrics 
behave in practice. 
2. What do we mean by Quality 
Quality investing is built on a simple idea: investors should prefer companies that are 
profitable, efficient, cash-generative and financially resilient. The intuition has not 
changed. What has changed is where those characteristics are concentrated within global 
equity markets.

3. Accruals are the link between profits and cash 
Quality investing is built on a simple idea: investors should prefer companies that are 
profitable, efficient and these characteristics are sustainable.  
Among all Quality components, Accruals are often the least understood. Yet decades of 
accounting and investment research suggest they may be among the most valuable 
indicators of earnings quality and future stock performance. At their core, Accruals 
measure the difference between accounting earnings and the cash generated by a 
business. 
Accounting standards are designed to measure economic activity rather than simply 
record cash transactions. Revenue may be recognised before cash is received. Expenses 
may be recognised before cash is paid. These timing differences create accruals. 
Non-cash earnings may be entirely legitimate. They may arise because customers have 
not yet paid invoices, because inventory has increased, because depreciation patterns 
differ from investment spending or because expenditures have been capitalised rather 
than expensed immediately. However, those earnings are inherently less certain than 
cash already sitting in the bank. 
This distinction matters because cash ultimately determines a company’s ability to fund 
growth, repay debt, return capital to shareholders, finance acquisitions and weather 
periods of economic stress. 
For this reason, investors often distinguish between high profitability and high-quality 
profitability. A company can generate strong reported earnings while simultaneously 
producing relatively weak cash flow.

When a large proportion of earnings is derived from accounting accruals rather than cash 
generation, future earnings may prove less persistent than current earnings suggest. High 
accruals can arise from: rapid growth in accounts receivable, revenue recognised ahead 
of cash collection, inventory accumulation, capitalisation of expenditures, aggressive 
accounting assumptions, or large investments whose future economic benefits remain 
uncertain. 
None of these developments are inherently problematic. In fact, many rapidly growing 
companies naturally exhibit some of these characteristics. The issue is that accounting 
assumptions must eventually convert into realised cash flows. If they do not, earnings 
often disappoint. 
Accruals therefore act as a measure of earnings sustainability rather than profitability 
itself. 
The importance of Accruals was first formalised by Sloan (1996), whose research 
demonstrated that investors often fail to adequately distinguish between the accrual and 
cash-flow components of earnings. Companies with low accruals subsequently generated 
superior stock returns, while companies with high accruals tended to underperform. 
Subsequent research by Richardson et al. (2005) reinforced those findings by 
demonstrating that the least reliable portions of reported earnings are often associated 
with accrual-based accounting estimates rather than realised cash flows. This body of 
evidence became known as the Accrual Anomaly, one of the most robust findings in the 
Quality-investing literature. 
The importance of Accruals may actually be increasing because modern equity markets 
are increasingly dominated by businesses whose value is driven by intangible assets. 
Traditional industrial firms generated profits primarily through physical assets and tangible 
capital. Today’s leading Technology companies generate profits through software, 
intellectual property, data assets, cloud infrastructure, network effects, brand capital. 
These business models often produce extremely high ROA, relatively low reported asset 
bases, rapid investment cycles, significant capital commitments, greater reliance on 
accounting estimates and assumptions. As a result, profitability and earnings quality can 
diverge more meaningfully than they did in traditional industrial sectors. 
This does not imply that Technology companies are low quality. Many remain among the 
most profitable and cash-generative businesses ever created. However, it does mean that 
profitability alone may no longer provide a complete picture. ROA tells investors how 
much profit a company generates. Accruals help investors assess how much confidence 
they should place in that profit. 
Viewed differently, ROA measures the quantity of profitability, while Accruals provide 
insight into the quality of profitability. 
High Accruals should not be interpreted as a bearish signal. Nor should they be viewed as 
a market-timing indicator. Instead, they provide context. When ROA is high and Accruals 
are low, profitability is generally supported by strong cash generation. For this reason, 
Accruals are best viewed as a risk-monitoring tool rather than a prediction tool. It is about 
identifying companies whose profits are most likely to endure.

4. Quality has become technology-led 
The most important structural change in Quality investing has been the rise of Technology 
as the dominant Quality sector. 
Modern Technology companies often exhibit exactly the characteristics rewarded by 
Quality metrics: 
– High margins 
– High ROA 
– Strong cash generation 
– Limited leverage 
– Scalable business models 
– Low physical capital intensity 
Because global equity markets are market-capitalisation weighted, these characteristics 
become even more influential when the largest companies possess them. 
The mechanism is straightforward: 
High benchmark weights multiplied by high Quality scores naturally produce larger 
Technology exposures in unconstrained Quality portfolios. This does not imply that the 
Quality signal is flawed. 
Many Technology companies genuinely are highly profitable and financially strong. 
However, it does mean that unconstrained Quality increasingly captures exposure to a 
particular business model: asset-light, scalable, highly profitable and often US-listed 
mega-cap growth companies. 
For investors, that materially changes the nature of the exposure.

The evidence suggests that ROA now drives much of what investors experience as 
“Quality.” 
This is both a strength and a potential risk. ROA is an exceptionally powerful measure of 
profitability. However, it is also deeply influenced by sector composition and business-
model structure. Asset-light firms can appear extraordinarily profitable because their 
reported asset bases frequently understate internally generated intangible assets such as 
software, data, intellectual property and brand value. As a result, today’s Quality portfolios 
increasingly reflect the economics of modern business models rather than traditional 
notions of defensiveness.

7. The AI investment cycle – a rhyme not a forecast 
The current AI investment cycle makes the distinction between profitability and earnings 
quality particularly relevant. 
Leading Technology firms are investing unprecedented amounts into AI infrastructure, 
semiconductors, data centres, networking capacity and ecosystem development while 
simultaneously reporting exceptional profitability. 
A broadly similar tension emerged during 2012-2014 when Technology profitability

9. Signal overlap is rising 
The component analysis also highlights increasing overlap within the Quality framework. 
ROA and the broader Quality composite exhibit extremely high realised correlation. 
Operating Cash Flow to Debt has increasingly moved in line with ROA and Quality, 
although its standalone contribution has been weaker (Chart 6).

Accruals remain more differentiated. This differentiation helps explain why Accruals 
provide meaningful incremental information after other exposures are removed. The 
practical lesson is that factor design should focus not only on economic intuition but also 
on incremental information content. A component should justify its inclusion not simply 
because it sounds sensible, but because it contributes unique insight. 
10. Quality is not automatically defensive 
Historically, Quality has often been viewed as a defensive equity factor. 
That perception is only partly correct today. As Quality has become increasingly linked to 
profitable Technology companies and mega-cap growth leadership, factor beta has risen. 
Recent diagnostics indicate rolling betas above one for broad Quality and ROA-based 
portfolios. 
Investors seeking defensive Quality exposure increasingly require explicit controls around: 
– Beta 
– Volatility 
– Downside risk 
– Drawdown sensitivity 
Quality alone is no longer automatically defensive.

12. Conclusion 
Quality remains one of the most intuitive and economically grounded equity factors. 
Investors continue to prefer profitable companies, efficient capital allocators, strong 
balance sheets and sustainable earnings. What has changed is the market expression of 
those characteristics. 
The rise of highly profitable, asset-light and scalable Technology businesses has 
increasingly shaped realised Quality exposure. Consequently, broad Quality has become 
more closely tied to Technology leadership, profitability and mega-cap growth dynamics 
than many investors realise. ROA remains the dominant driver of broad Quality 
performance. 
However, once sector, country and style exposures are removed, Accruals emerge as the 
strongest pure stock-selection signal. This distinction is particularly relevant today, as 
some of the market’s most profitable companies simultaneously exhibit elevated accrual 
characteristics during an unprecedented AI investment cycle. 
The lesson is not that Technology is low quality. Nor is it that profitability has lost 
relevance. High profitability and high-quality profitability are not always the same thing. 
ROA tells investors how much profit a company generates. Accruals help investors 
assess how sustainable that profit may be. 
Quality remains fit for purpose but implementation defines the exposure. Standard Quality, 
Pure Quality and Defensive Quality should be viewed as distinct investment objectives 
with different sources of risk, return and portfolio behaviour. 
Understanding the difference between them is increasingly important. Above all, investors 
should understand what they own.
