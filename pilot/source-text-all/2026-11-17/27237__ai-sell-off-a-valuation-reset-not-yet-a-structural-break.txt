AI Sell-Off: A Valuation Reset, Not Yet a Structural Break 
 
 
Michele Morganti, Vladimir Oleinikov, Mattia Mammarella 
12 November 2026 
 
 
 
• The November 5 sell-off was historic: the VIX jumped 65% in a single day, while the Nasdaq and the US Semiconductor 
Index fell 6% and 10%, respectively. Volatility has since normalised, with the VIX back to around 19, and we read the 
rout as a valuation reset rather than a structural break. 
• Investors had already started demanding proof that record capex would translate into revenues, margins, and free 
cash flow. 
• In addition, the Democratic midterm sweep increased the regulatory risk for AI-related data-centre (DC) buildouts. 
That said, the presidential veto and the lack of a 60-vote Senate majority limit the damage. Washington’s strategic 
imperative to maintain its AI lead over China, alongside neoclouds’ leverage in negotiations with individual states, 
should also constrain the regulatory downside. Moreover, a somewhat slower DC buildout could also ease M7 cash 
burn and support token pricing. 
• Q3 earnings raised the bar: since July, revenue forecasts for the five hyperscalers for 2026–2028 have risen by $22–
235bn, yet FCF estimates have fallen by $62–163bn, respectively. Strong growth is no longer enough without spend-
ing discipline. By contrast, the 18 largest IT names outside the hyperscalers have seen upward revisions to both sales 
and FCF. 
• US Tech now trades at a rare, slight discount to the S&P 500 on three-year forward EPS, close to the 2012 and 1995–
97 lows. We favour diversified exposure to the AI value chain: capital-light AI adopters and bottlenecks such as data-
centre cooling. Overall, we remain cautiously overweight (OW) equities, with constructive PMIs, resilient GDP and 
earnings growth, relatively light investor positioning, better valuations, and supportive seasonality providing a fa-
vourable backdrop. Even so, given lingering risks, diversifying beyond S&P Tech remains essential: S&P 493, US 
mid-caps, a US gold basket, and ex-US markets (Europe, Japan, and EM). 
 
Volatility spiked on November 5 from subdued levels, as a concentrated sell-off hit US technology, semiconductors, and AI 
infrastructure. It has since retraced much of the move, with the VIX back near 19, although still above its pre-sell-off lows. Two 
forces drove the correction: a shift in the AI debate and higher regulatory risk after the Democratic sweep. Investors no longer 
reward investment commitments alone; they want evidence that unprecedented capital spending can generate sufficient rev-
enues, margins, and free cash flow. Rising bond yields and wider credit spreads for hyperscalers amplified the repricing. After 
the steep decline, the US semiconductor index trades on a 12-month forward PE of 12x, down from more than 30x a year ago. 
Beneath the surface, the market is rotating. Investors now pay a premium for firms with disciplined cash flow (Microsoft, Apple) 
and for the S&P 493, mid-caps, and ex-US markets, all of which have outperformed the S&P 500, helped by good earnings

momentum. While the rotation may have gone too far in the short term, we expect it to persist as long as investors continue to 
reward spending discipline. 
The current Q3 earnings season reinforced these concerns. Cloud demand remains robust, but the market reaction to Alpha-
bet, Amazon, and Meta shows that strong revenue growth no longer suffices when investment needs rise just as fast. Mi-
crosoft’s results were more reassuring, supported by strong Azure growth and greater spending discipline, while Apple’s results 
underscored the resilience of its earnings profile. The message is clear: execution standards have risen substantially. 
Analysts' revisions illustrate the tension. Since July, hyperscaler revenue forecasts have risen by $22bn for 2026, $105bn for 
2027, and $235bn for 2028, while FCF estimates have fallen by $62bn, $162bn, and $163bn, respectively. The market is 
therefore questioning not the existence of AI demand but the timing and profitability of its monetisation. That said, the 18 largest 
IT names outside the five hyperscalers look much better placed: sales forecasts have risen by a comparable amount ($21bn, 
$105bn, and $336bn for 2026, 2027, and 2028, respectively), while FCF estimates improved by $7bn in 2026, fell by only 
$16bn in 2027, and rose by $90bn in 2028. 
Politics adds another layer of uncertainty. The Democratic midterm sweep raises perceived regulatory risks around AI, DCs, 
permitting, and energy consumption. Yet the presidential veto and the lack of a 60-vote Senate majority make strict federal 
regulation that could derail the AI boom unlikely. The bigger risk is tighter state and local regulation of DCs, although this is 
more likely to impose additional conditions on DC projects rather than halt them outright. Several developments support this 
view: a bipartisan proposal to speed up DC construction, CoreWeave's letter to the Texas governor outlining local benefits, 
and states' interest in avoiding a slump in tax revenues from delayed or cancelled projects. The US also remains determined 
to maintain its AI lead over China. Regulation may make the buildout slower and costlier, but it is unlikely to derail the invest-
ment cycle. Slower DC construction may even reassure investors by curbing cash outflows and potentially supporting token 
prices.

We therefore read the sell-off as a valuation reset, not as evidence of broad weakness in AI demand. The Nasdaq 100 fell 
around 15% from its early-October peak and has since rebounded by 7%. US semiconductors corrected by around 30% from 
their highs and have recovered 9.5% since November 5. Technology multiples have also compressed significantly, although 
crowded positioning and lingering high bond yields remain important near-term vulnerabilities. Based on three-year forward 
EPS, the Tech sector's PE relative to the S&P 500 has dropped 35% from its August 2024 peak to 0.95. US Tech thus trades 
at a slight discount to the broader market, close to the lows of 2012 and 1995–97. By comparison, the relative multiple fell 40% 
between 2000 and 2002. Furthermore, while Tech margins have broadly tracked the sector's rapidly rising capex contribution 
to GDP, Tech PE multiples have derated by around 30% over the past year.  
Despite these risks, we stay neutral on Tech for now, given the sharp sell-off and significant PE derating. We see the most 
attractive opportunities among less capital-intensive beneficiaries of AI adoption and productivity gains (Phase 4, EU AI ben-
eficiaries), alongside selected structural bottlenecks, such as data-centre cooling. 
Overall, we remain cautiously overweight (OW) equities, as fundamentals still support risk assets: constructive PMIs, resilient 
GDP and earnings growth, lighter investor positioning than the norm, ongoing sector rotation, improved valuations (PE vs. 
rates, see chart below), and good seasonality. Solid new orders and a recovering IFO are tailwinds for Europe in particular. 
Given lingering risks, diversifying beyond S&P Tech remains essential: the S&P 493, US mid-caps, a US gold basket, and ex-
US markets (Europe, Japan, and EM). 
Key risks include higher-for-longer rates amid sticky inflation and the Iran war, a further 50 bps of central-bank tightening 
through Q1 ’27, additional equity and debt issuance, and signs that ROE may be nearing a peak. Tech PE is below its post-
1995 average, but price-to-book remains high, reflecting exceptional ROE expansion. Rising leverage and tighter regulation 
could bring forward the ROE peak and trigger a significant P/B de-rating towards historical norms, particularly in semiconduc-
tors (see chart above).
