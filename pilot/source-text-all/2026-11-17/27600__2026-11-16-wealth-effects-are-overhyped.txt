"WEALTH EFFECTS" ARE OVERHYPED
Dario Perkins
16 Nov 2026
With the massively popular and critically-acclaimed Perkini Report short-listed for a Nobel prize
(probably), “haters” have been pushing back on some of the underlying analysis. The main
complaint is about why we dismissed “wealth effects”… you know that thing that exists in the
minds of investors but doesn’t appear in any data. Was the Perkini Report wrong to downplay this.
Could that prevent the Nobel prize going to a worthy winner (rather than another textbook-writing
academic)? 
We think not
1/7
A while back I had a heated dispute with former White House staffer
Jason Furman. Well I say “dispute”, but it was a bit one-sided. He was
saying something wrong in the financial press, and I was complaining
to the journalist who published it (who then passed on my
comments...). The “argument” was about the role of AI in US GDP.
Furman was saying the US would be in recession without it. I said he
was ignoring imports. Inevitably, I was right, as JF finally acknowledged
last week. (0.3? Really?)

2/7
So, another Perkins win against someone who was wrong on the
internet? Well, not quite. Because although Prof Furman has now
admitted his mistake (sort of...), he has come up with another claim that
irritates me – “wealth effects”! The big AI GDP boost is still there; it’s just
invisible. (Funny how academic economists have a penchant for things
we can’t see…). And the basis for his claim? The Fed model. In fact,
when you model e.g. a 30% rise in US stocks, that adds roughly 50bps
to GDP.  
3/7
To be fair, Furman is not the only one who believes in imaginary “wealth
effects” – it is v much the consensus among investors. (Like the idea
that tech was rate insensitive – whoops.) The Fed’s forecasting model,
based on data since the 1960s, backs that up. When stock prices rise,
people feel wealthier and that encourages them to save less. The
trouble is, we don’t see this relationship in the data. There used to be a
link (inverse) between wealth and savings but it disappeared after the
GFC.

4/7
My contention is that the relationship broke down because all those
post-GFC wealth gains have come from stocks, and the wealth effect
from equities is much weaker than for housing. Why? Because equity
wealth is much more heavily concentrated among the top income
groups, where marginal propensities to consume (MPCs) are v low. To
test that, I estimated my own consumption function and looked at how
the coefficient on wealth changed over time. It collapsed. In fact, it is no
longer significant:

5/7
The Fed's model misses this because it is estimated over a very long
time horizon, with parameters that were flattered by the 2000s, when
consumers could extract equity from their homes and spend it in the
economy (when the saving rate turned negative). In fact, the good
professor acknowledged that there is now a residual in the Fed’s
equation. Not only does that suggest the AI boost is smaller than
everyone thinks, but it also provides some hope about what might
happen when the bubble bursts. 
6/7
Another point to bear in mind about “wealth effects”. The idea first
gained prominence during the Dotcom era. Alan Greenspan was one of
the biggest advocates of this theory, even giving a speech that included
precise estimates of how much the wealth effect was boosting the
economy (unusual for Mr I’m-Not-Telling-You-What-I’m-Thinking

Greenspan). My question: where did those mythical wealth effects go
when Dotcom bust? Stocks plunged and yet consumption just kept on
rising… Its not a real thing!
7/7
Does that mean investors can stop worrying about the macro impact of
a market crash? Not exactly, because wealth effects are only one of the
channels through which equities can affect the economy. Later this
week, we will publish a sequel to the (brilliant) "Perkini Report”, that will
look at the risks around our crash scenario. It will address the client
feedback we received, both from investors who thought we were too
bearish and those who claimed we weren’t bearish enough.
Look out for it.
