The US midterm elections delivered a Democratic sweep of 
Congress, and markets responded with a dramatic rotation. 
The shift was swift and decisive: investors moved out of high-
growth, technology-heavy businesses into areas of the 
market that had long been overlooked. The clearest losers 
were semiconductor and AI-linked stocks, as investors 
worried about increased regulation in the sector and 
demanded more clarity on the massive spending plans of 
large technology companies. The Japanese market, heavily 
exposed to the global technology supply chain, was also 
caught in the sell-off. By contrast, European equities held 
their ground, and the Indian market posted strong gains, 
reflecting a broader search for alternatives to the US. 
Defensive global equities — think utilities, healthcare, and 
consumer staples — quietly outperformed. The message 
from the markets seems to be that the era of undisputed US 
technology dominance is being questioned.

technology stocks. Europe, however, was broadly flat. Bond yields declined 
across major regions as markets sought safer assets. In commodities, oil 
prices dropped sharply on expectations of diplomatic progress in the

BoE Raises Rates as Inflation Outlook Worsens
The Bank of England raised its key interest rate by a 
quarter of a percentage point to 4% on Thursday — its 
first increase since August 2023. Governor Andrew Bailey 
signalled that a further increase is more likely than not if 
energy costs remain elevated, with the Bank now 
forecasting inflation could reach close to 5% in the 
second quarter of next year — well above its 2% target.
US Factory Activity Gains Momentum
The Institute for Supply Management's manufacturing 
index rose to 55.5 in October, above expectations and its 
second-highest level in over four years. Production 
reached its best level in several years, a sign of robust 
industrial momentum. On the labour market front, latest 
private-sector hiring and weekly jobless claims points to 
a resilient labour market that supports the broader 
economic expansion.
Japan's Wages Grow at Fastest Pace Since 1997 
Nominal wages rose 4.6% year on year in September, 
the fastest pace in decades. After adjusting for inflation, 
real wages grew 2.2%, well above 
economists' 
expectations. These figures reinforce the view that the 
wage-growth cycle is becoming more durable, which 
supports household purchasing power and is a key 
factor the Bank of Japan monitors when assessing its 
monetary policy.
