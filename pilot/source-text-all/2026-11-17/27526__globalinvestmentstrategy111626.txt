Diversification matters as markets shift 
The global market portfolio changed over the past two decades 
The concept of a Global Market Portfolio (GMP) is a hypothetical portfolio that combines globally investable assets. 
Chart 1 shows a proxy, based on the market capitalization of various global asset-class benchmark indices. Based 
on these indices, we estimate this hypothetical portfolio at a value of $276 trillion as of 10/30/2026, nearly four 
times its value on 12/31/2006. The GMP has also become broader over time, and history shows that the value of 
that breadth becomes most evident when market leadership changes during periods of stress. 
Chart 1 illustrates the path of GMP value accumulation, and from 2022 to 2026 equities grew from 47% to 52% of 
the GMP, while fixed income declined from 41% to 35%. Meanwhile, real assets expanded from 6% to 8% share, 
and alternatives stayed around 5% to 6%. Although their shares remained relatively steady, real assets and 
alternatives grew in absolute value as the GMP expanded. This growth broadened the opportunities available to 
investors for diversification across a wider range of asset classes during this period, and we expect those 
opportunities broadly to continue.

Diversification matters when markets shift 
Even beyond the portfolio's growth, however, we want to note that market stress events can increase uncertainty 
and trigger a flight to safety, resulting in meaningful shifts in asset-class allocations. Four major stress events 
occurred in the past two decades, and in each case, equities lost proportional value in the GMP while others gained 
share: 
• 
Amid the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, investors shifted away from risk assets toward more defensive 
positions. The GMP reflected this shift, as equities relative portfolio weight declined from 55% to 39% 
between 2007 and 2008, while fixed income securities increased from 40% to 54%.  
• 
In 2011, markets faced another period of heightened global volatility as European sovereign-debt concerns 
intensified alongside weaker global growth expectations. Heightened risk aversion favored defensive 
assets as equity’s relative portfolio weight fell from 43% to 39% between 2010 and 2011 while fixed 
income increased from 49% to 53%. Real assets and alternatives remained relatively steady rather than 
contracting substantially.  
• 
In 2018, Federal Reserve (Fed) rate hikes and slowing global growth tightened financial conditions and 
pressured equities. As equities relative portfolio weight declined, fixed income grew from 45% to 47% 
between 2017 and 2018, while real assets held steady and alternatives grew from 3% to 4%. This shift 
extended beyond the traditional rotation into fixed income, as alternatives also gained share as the overall 
portfolio expanded.  
• 
Towards the end of the COVID-19 global pandemic in 2022, a slowing economy and tighter monetary 
policy as a response to rising inflation pressured both equity and fixed income. Equities’ relative portfolio 
weight fell from 49% to 47% between 2021 to 2022, while rising yields limited the diversification benefit 
from bonds, whose relative weight increased from 40% to 41%. Meanwhile, real assets held steady and 
alternatives rose from 5% to 6%. This shift highlighted the diversification value of other asset classes as 
inflation and rising rates challenged a traditional portfolio made primarily of stocks and bonds. 
Investor implications 
Our analysis here illustrates that no single asset class has provided the same diversification benefit in every period 
of market stress. Maintaining strategic exposure across fixed income, real assets, and alternatives alongside 
equities can provide multiple potential sources of diversification as market leadership changes. Our historical 
analysis illustrates that different asset classes gained or lost relative portfolio share during periods of market stress, 
supporting the potential value of maintaining diversified exposures as market leadership changes. This tendency 
has been a key objective of strategic asset allocation. Investors today have greater exposure to asset groups like real 
assets and alternatives that have gained portfolio share over the past 20 years. 
We do not know when the next event may cause equity prices to fall sharply, but we do believe that diversification 
allows strength in some areas to at least partially offset weakness in other areas of global markets. Just as 
important, our analysis implies that moving money out of global markets altogether — into cash or cash 
alternatives, for example — may miss the gains in other asset groups during the period of risk aversion, and may 
then miss the opportunity to gain from equity exposure, once risk appetite returns.1

Election results could moderate, but extend, AI buildout 
Recent election results could reinforce a more measured approach to AI infrastructure development. Power 
availability, grid interconnection, infrastructure costs, permitting, and community requirements increasingly 
determine when, where, and at what cost capacity can be delivered. We believe these constraints are more likely to 
change the pace and economics of the buildout than its long-term direction. We believe funded and permitted 
projects may be better positioned, while later-dated projects requiring power or approvals may face longer 
timelines. Google’s acquisition of Crusoe’s pre-developed Wyoming site illustrates the value of de-risked, power-
secured capacity and the flexibility to directly control development. 
For Communication Services, Software, and the broader Information Technology sector, we continue to believe AI 
adoption and monetization, earnings growth, competitive positioning, and valuation remain the primary long-term 
drivers. Slower capacity deployment does not necessarily imply weaker demand. If demand and returns remain 
attractive, a more measured buildout could extend the capital spending cycle while increasing the strategic value of 
operating and power-secured capacity. Delays could also defer cloud revenue and development costs. For software, 
we would continue to judge AI investment primarily on adoption, monetization, and customer returns. 
Within AI infrastructure, we continue to favor established hyperscalers. In our view, their access to capital and 
power, geographic flexibility, enterprise relationships, and integrated cloud platforms should become increasingly 
valuable as incremental capacity becomes harder to deliver. We believe hyperscalers are well positioned to 
navigate constraints, shift investments across markets, and monetize AI capacity through broader cloud and 
enterprise platforms. Our preference therefore remains with companies that can fund, deliver, and monetize AI 
infrastructure rather than simply pursue the largest buildouts. We would become less constructive if monetization 
slows, project delays become cancellations, or sustained capital spending fails to generate adequate returns and free 
cash flow.

High-yield stress shows why quality matters 
Many investors know high-yield bonds as "junk bonds," but the name can be misleading. It suggests every borrower 
is equally risky, when in reality, high-yield companies vary widely in their financial strength. That difference is the 
key to understanding recent market stress, and why bond quality matters. 
A credit spread is the additional yield investors receive for owning a corporate bond instead of a comparable U.S. 
Treasury. When spreads rise, investors generally demand more compensation for the risk that a borrower may not 
repay its debt. Recently, spreads have risen sharply for CCC-rated bonds, one of the lowest-quality areas of high 
yield (see Chart 3). 
Chart 3. CCC spreads spike, but BB remains contained 
 
Source: Bloomberg, data as of November 9, 2026. Credit spread represents the difference in yields between a bond and a similarly dated Treasury. BB is represented 
by the Bloomberg Ba US High Yield Average OAS Index and CCC by the Bloomberg Caa US High Yield Average OAS Index. An index is unmanaged and not available for 
direct investment. Past performance is no guarantee of future results.  OAS = options-adjusted spread. 
So far, however, the strain appears contained. Spreads on BB-rated bonds, the highest-quality segment of high 
yield, have not increased significantly. That matters because BB-rated bonds make up more than half of the 
Bloomberg U.S. Corporate High Yield Bond Index. Many BB-rated issuers are also financially stronger than the 
“junk bond” label might suggest. During broader periods of market stress, CCC and BB spreads typically rise 
together. That has not happened this time. For now, the warning signs remain concentrated among the weakest 
borrowers.     
For investors, the lesson is to focus on quality. When risks rise, quality can provide a stronger first line of defense. 
Weaker borrowers tend to feel market swings and earnings worry first, even when the broader economy looks 
healthy. High yield includes some sturdier companies than the “junk bond” name implies, but it still carries 
exposure to the weakest borrowers. We therefore remain neutral on high yield and prefer taking credit risks in 
higher-quality areas of the bond market, including investment-grade corporate and securitized bonds.

Industrial Metals — An uneven path ahead 
Industrial Metals could face a more challenging backdrop in 2027, as the sector becomes increasingly caught 
between competing forces — supportive long-term fundamentals and growing near-term risks.  
The long-term fundamentals remain constructive. Demand continues to grow from the AI buildout, electrification, 
and growing power generation needs. Meanwhile, the supply of mined copper continues to lag demand, creating a 
structural imbalance. Given the average mine takes roughly 17 years from exploration to production, these 
shortages cannot be fixed overnight, supporting the case for higher copper prices over time.2  
Investor enthusiasm has grown alongside this strong backdrop (see Chart 4). Speculative positioning3 more than 
doubled between March 2024 to its peak in September 2026. This persistent optimism, however, could leave 
copper more susceptible to disappointment if the outlook becomes less supportive. 
Chart 4. Copper positioning shows signs of cooling 
 
Sources: Bloomberg and Wells Fargo Investment Institute. Weekly data is from January 7, 2022. through November 3, 2026. Net speculative positioning represents 
speculative longs minus speculative shorts. Bars above zero represent when positioning is net long, while bars below zero represent when positioning is net short. 
Several factors could challenge bullish expectations. We expect higher interest rates and elevated energy costs, 
which could eventually slow the global economy and reduce demand for industrial metals. Meanwhile, growing 
uncertainties around the pace of AI infrastructure spending could temper an increasingly important source of global 
demand. The recent roll over in speculative positioning from its peak on September 4, 2026 through October 30, 
2026 suggests some of this optimism may already be fading as investors account for a more balanced outlook in 
2027.  
We remain favorable on Industrial Metals, as long-term demand trends pulling on limited supply growth should 
support higher copper prices over time. However, with speculative positioning elevated and much of the “good 
news” potentially already reflected, the path forward could become more uneven if economic conditions weaken or 
the pace of the AI buildout moderates.

Higher-for-longer rates may expose vulnerabilities 
The battle to tame inflation may prove more difficult than many policymakers have expected. After peaking near 
9% in mid-2022, inflation, as measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI), declined sharply over the following 
year, helped in part by the Fed’s aggressive interest-rate hikes from near zero to roughly 5%. Inflation initially 
appeared to be on a steady path toward the Fed’s 2% target. However, the final stretch has proven more 
challenging, with inflation remaining stubbornly above the Fed’s goal. 
Persistent inflation has also changed the outlook for interest rates. With Fed Chair Warsh reiterating the central 
bank’s commitment to price stability, investor expectations have shifted from lower rates toward the possibility 
that rates may remain elevated or move higher. For many small- and mid-sized businesses, the borrowing-cost 
relief they had anticipated may not materialize anytime soon, in our view. 
At the same time, many businesses continue to face higher operating and input costs, putting additional pressure 
on profit margins just as elevated interest rates make existing debt more expensive to service. These pressures 
have already contributed to elevated levels of financial stress among more highly leveraged companies and could 
intensify if borrowing costs remain higher for longer. 
Chart 5 shows that financial stress is already elevated. The distressed ratio, which measures the percentage of loans 
trading below 80% of par value, has been rising since mid-to-late 2025. If interest rates remain elevated or move 
higher, we believe financial stress could broaden as a growing number of vulnerable businesses struggle to absorb 
higher financing costs and weaker margins. 
Given these dynamics, we remain favorable on Distressed Credit, as greater financial stress may create compelling 
opportunities for investors to provide capital and help challenged businesses establish more sustainable paths 
forward.

Risk considerations 
Each asset class has its own risk and return characteristics.  The level of risk associated with a particular investment or asset class generally correlates with the level 
of return the investment or asset class might achieve. Stock markets, especially foreign markets, are volatile.  Stock values may fluctuate in response to general 
economic and market conditions, the prospects of individual companies, and industry sectors.  Foreign investing has additional risks including those associated with 
currency fluctuation, political and economic instability, and different accounting standards. These risks are heightened in emerging markets. Small- and mid-cap 
stocks are generally more volatile, subject to greater risks and are less liquid than large company stocks. Bonds are subject to market, interest rate, price, 
credit/default, liquidity, inflation and other risks. Prices tend to be inversely affected by changes in interest rates. High yield (junk) bonds have lower credit ratings 
and are subject to greater risk of default and greater principal risk. The commodities markets are considered speculative, carry substantial risks, and have 
experienced periods of extreme volatility.  Investing in a volatile and uncertain commodities market may cause a portfolio to rapidly increase or decrease in value 
which may result in greater share price volatility. Real estate has special risks including the possible illiquidity of underlying properties, credit risk, interest rate 
fluctuations and the impact of varied economic conditions. 
Asset allocation and diversification are investment methods used to help manage risk. They do not guarantee investment returns or eliminate risk of loss including in 
a declining market. 
Alternative investments, such as hedge funds, private equity/private debt and private real estate funds, are speculative and involve a high degree of risk that is 
appropriate only for those investors who have the financial sophistication and expertise to evaluate the merits and risks of an investment in a fund and for which the 
fund does not represent a complete investment program. They entail significant risks that can include losses due to leveraging or other speculative investment 
practices, lack of liquidity, volatility of returns, restrictions on transferring interests in a fund, potential lack of diversification, absence and/or delay of information 
regarding valuations and pricing, complex tax structures and delays in tax reporting, less regulation and higher fees than mutual funds.  Hedge fund, private equity, 
private debt and private real estate fund investing involves other material risks including capital loss and the loss of the entire amount invested. A fund's offering 
documents should be carefully reviewed prior to investing. 
Hedge fund strategies, such as Equity Hedge, Event Driven, Macro and Relative Value, may expose investors to the risks associated with the use of short selling, 
leverage, derivatives and arbitrage methodologies. Short sales involve leverage and theoretically unlimited loss potential since the market price of securities sold 
short may continuously increase. The use of leverage in a portfolio varies by strategy. Leverage can significantly increase return potential but create greater risk of 
loss.  Derivatives generally have implied leverage which can magnify volatility and may entail other risks such as market, interest rate, credit, counterparty and 
management risks.  Arbitrage strategies expose a fund to the risk that the anticipated arbitrage opportunities will not develop as anticipated, resulting in potentially 
reduced returns or losses to the fund. 
Private infrastructure investments are speculative, illiquid, and intended for long-term investment. They are subject to risks including construction and development 
delays, cost overruns, leverage, changes in interest rates, regulatory or political developments, operational failures, changes in demand, and limited exit 
opportunities. Valuations may be uncertain, and investors could lose some or all of their investment. 
Cryptocurrency and other digital assets often involve significant risks and can be highly volatile. Their value may fluctuate substantially, and investors could lose all 
or a significant portion of their investment. Digital assets may be subject to market, liquidity, regulatory, and operational risks and may be subject to heightened 
risks of fraud, cyber incidents, and market manipulation. Digital assets may not provide the same regulatory protections as traditional investment products and 
often are not insured by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), the Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC), or any government agency. 
Investors should carefully consider their investment objectives, risk tolerance, and financial circumstances before investing in cryptocurrency or other digital assets.

the index include: underpinned by PitchBook LCD data sourced from both public and proprietary sources, comprehensive and precise coverage using 20+ years of live 
index history, and weekly rebalancing to reflect current loan market activity.
