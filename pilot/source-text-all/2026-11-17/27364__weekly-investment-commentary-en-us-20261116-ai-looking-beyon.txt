The valuation picture for U.S. equities depends on the measure used. The Shiller 
cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (CAPE) suggests valuations are near 
dot-com bubble highs. See the chart. The S&P 500’s 12-month forward P/E looks 
less stretched by historical standards, but only because investors expect strong 
earnings growth. Whether those expectations are justified is an open question. 
The key uncertainty is how the AI buildout evolves relative to demand and 
adoption. Falling computing costs could squeeze suppliers’ profits, while 
bottlenecks could delay AI adoption. Both could cause near-term pressure, but 
neither necessarily undermines the longer-term opportunity, in our view.

Ample uncertainty still hangs over the longer-term picture. That’s a key feature of the polyfurcated environment we’ve long 
described, where multiple scenarios are plausible. Overcapacity is a risk, and builders' earnings could slow before adopters 
see meaningful gains. Our strategic conviction doesn't assume that current winners will remain winners. Instead, our 
starting-point scenario explicitly sees valuations declining without assuming a permanent growth breakout. Yet AI’s 
improving capabilities could drive broader adoption, boosting productivity, profit margins and earnings over time. We see 
particular opportunities in U.S. health care, IT, tech, and financials, where stronger longer-term earnings prospects help 
underpin our strategic overweight in U.S. equities, a key driver of our broader developed-market equity overweight. 
The longer-term AI opportunity, however, requires substantial investment to make it a reality. Companies financing the AI 
buildout are competing with governments borrowing heavily from the same pool of savings, intensifying competition for 
capital. That backdrop has led us to revise up our interest rate expectations in our CMAs. We expect central bank policy rates 
to stay elevated for longer, reflecting a higher expected neutral interest rate. Yet stronger growth and earnings can coexist 
with higher borrowing costs, and a higher-rate environment is increasingly reflected in market prices.
That repricing has improved bonds’ strategic appeal, with higher starting yields offering better compensation for longer-term 
interest rate risk. For the first time since 2020, we are taking a more constructive view on duration risk. We have moved to 
neutral on developed-market government bonds and strengthened our overweight in inflation-linked bonds, which offer 
attractive yields and protection against inflation risks tied to supply constraints. Yet government bonds are no longer the all-
weather diversifiers they once were. Persistent inflation pressures limit central banks’ ability to cut rates and drive bond yields 
lower, reducing the scope for bond prices to rise. That’s also why we favor building portfolios granularly around specific 
exposures and convictions rather than asset class labels, reflecting a broader industry shift toward a total portfolio approach. 
Bottom line: We maintain our strategic overweight in U.S. equities, backed by our conviction in AI’s longer-term earnings 
potential. Higher yields also strengthen the case for bonds, particularly inflation-linked bonds.

Market backdrop 
U.S. stocks recovered nearly all their post-midterm election losses last week as an AI-led rebound gave the S&P 500 its best 
week since early August. Chip stocks led the gains, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor index surging 8%. The rebound 
suggests AI earnings optimism is outweighing pressure from higher interest rates, for now. Two-year U.S. Treasury yields 
jumped 33 basis points to 4.86% after hawkish comments from Fed officials and a hotter-than-expected inflation report.

We are neutral. We see opportunities in physical AI. Cheap, open-source AI could drive adoption,

We are overweight. Much of the repricing we anticipated has occurred, and expectations for

We are neutral. Higher yields offer attractive income and a bigger cushion against further rate

Global inflation-linked 
bonds
We are neutral. We see inflation settling above prepandemic levels, but markets may not price 
this in the near term as economic growth could slow.
Euro area govt bonds
We are neutral on short- and medium-term bonds. Current ECB rate pricing looks fairly valued 
given the balance of risks. Higher energy prices could push rates higher. We prefer to deploy 
risk elsewhere. 
UK gilts
We are neutral. We expect periods of elevated volatility given political uncertainty, longer-term 
bonds’ growing market share and buyers’ increasing price sensitivity.
Japanese govt bonds
We are underweight. The substantial repricing in yields has improved the risk-reward, but 
further rate hikes and heavy issuance could push yields higher.
China govt bonds
We are neutral. Chinese bonds offer stability and diversification, but developed-market yields 
are higher. A shift in investor sentiment toward equities limits upside. 
U.S. agency MBS
We are neutral and lean into the stable income MBS provide despite tight spreads.
Short-term IG credit
We are neutral. Spreads are tight due to corporate strength, but attractive overall yields help 
offset the risk of increased issuance.
Long-term IG credit
We are underweight. We prefer short-term bonds less exposed to interest rate risk over long-
term bonds. But attractive overall yields help offset the risk of increased issuance.
Global high yield
We are neutral. High yield offers attractive income. We prefer higher-rated U.S. and European 
high yield over investment grade and see dispersion of returns increasing. 
Asia credit
We are neutral. Overall yields are attractive and fundamentals are solid, but spreads are tight. 
Emerging hard currency

EM local debt.
