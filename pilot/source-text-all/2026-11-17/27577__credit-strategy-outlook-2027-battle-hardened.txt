2027 set to end on a positive tone 
The resilience of the credit markets was tested throughout the year and, after over three and a 
half years of a very solid performance, they finally began to crack.  Credit spreads widened from 
the very tight levels seen up until the end of August, and the move, while not dramatic, has not 
reversed in a meaningful way (although the start of November looks promising).  This is a 
departure from previous periods of intense volatility, when spreads recovered almost as quickly 
as they widened.  
Credit spreads have yet to recover meaningfully 
 
Source: SPICE, SG Cross Asset Research/Credit 
Moreover, with under two months left in 2026, but realistically just four to five weeks of any 
significant activity, it is difficult to see how the credit markets can improve sharply from what was 
a rocky end to 3Q and start to 4Q.  The broader market volatility finally spread to the credit 
markets and pushed excess returns into negative territory.  Total returns have fared even more 
poorly, courtesy of the rising sovereign bond yields, among other factors.  The chart below shows 
how the USD IG index has seen a much sharper total return contraction than its euro counterpart, 
despite milder spread widening due to the sell-off in US Treasuries. 
Total returns struggled as the sovereign bond sell-off intensified over the year

The positive news is that credit spreads are no longer as expensive as they were for well over a 
year although they remain just below their long-term average.  Yields, however, are much more 
attractive, and we believe they provide a strong reason to buy corporate bonds. 
Nevertheless, the case for credit is currently mixed.  Or rather, it is a question of timing, in our 
view.  On the one hand, we believe appetite for the asset class remains strong, as evidenced by 
the positive dynamics in the primary markets.  Granted, new issues have recently been coming 
with higher new issue premiums than earlier in the year. But yields remain very attractive, new 
issue volumes are heading towards a fresh record, and the market is absorbing the deals with 
ease.  Additionally, for now, credit fundamentals remain solid, with companies posting strong 
earnings but with no signs of widespread or aggressive releveraging. 
On the other hand, uncertainty remains high, and we believe the markets need to see clear 
progress towards resolving the war in the Middle East.  Oil prices have eased recently but remain 
very high and confirmation is needed that sovereign bond markets have found a new floor 
(ceiling).  To a large extent, the questions that pushed spreads wider in September/October 
remain unanswered.  And, we may also need greater clarity on the AI expansion. 
A year ago, we wrote in these pages that we expected modest spread widening in 2026. We 
believed companies would start to releverage amid a benign environment and healthy economic 
growth, and that this would lead to the beginning of a new credit cycle.  Now we know better, as 
the spread widening was the result of uncertainty and not an environment supported by more 
benign trends. However, we think that the ongoing uncertainties will keep companies from 
releveraging aggressively any time soon.  We also believe that we will see answers to the 
questions currently plaguing the markets. Once we start getting some clarity, we believe credit 
spreads will tighten again and end 2027 on a positive note.

Lessons from 2026 
Even in trouble, credit puts in a resilient performance 
For the first time since 2022, the credit markets are set to end the year with spreads wider than at 
the start.  This is not too surprising given another eventful year, with a new war in the Middle East 
pushing up oil prices and disrupting supply chains across the board.  Rising oil prices, in turn, 
have put upward pressure on inflation measures, eliciting a response from central banks.  We 
have also had a flood of new issues from AI-related companies (and, since the US midterm 
election results, questions about their future), more Private Credit woes, and volatile and rising 
sovereign bond yields. 
Through it all, the credit markets posted a largely resilient performance, with spreads moving in 
narrow ranges and mostly recovering after the various shocks to the markets.  In fact, this strong 
resilience has characterised the credit markets for over three and a half years, with spreads 
mostly moving in narrow ranges and an ongoing tilt towards tighter levels.  This process slowed 
down this year, as spreads became increasingly tight, making each further basis point of 
tightening harder to achieve.  And once more, events outside credit rattled spreads albeit just 
briefly, including the war in the Middle East and the rise in oil prices.   
However, in October, the credit markets began to crack. The combination of rising oil prices (with 
futures at their highest levels in decades) and very high sovereign bond volatility, with yields at 
multi-year highs, sent uncertainty levels soaring, finally leading credit spreads to widen.

The issues come from outside credit 
Geopolitical tensions and war 
Throughout the year, the headlines have repeatedly highlighted the challenging geopolitical 
environment.  There have been more discussions on trade and tariffs, persistent tensions 
between the US and Canada as well as other countries, and recently even discussions about 
Canada becoming an “associate member” of the EU.  But the major event was the war in the 
Middle East.  The human toll so far has been very high and, as we write these lines, it is not clear 
how the war will evolve now that the US midterm elections have given Democrats full control of 
Congress. 
Moreover, we’ve seen supply chain disruptions and a sharp rise in oil prices, which remain 
elevated.  The chart below shows the cash prices of Brent and WTI contracts, which have tested 
the high levels we saw back in 2022, although they have eased following the US midterm 
elections, largely on hopes that the results would help bring about a resolution to the war in the 
Middle East. 
Oil prices remain high (6 November) 
 
Source: Bloomberg, SG Cross Asset Research/Credit 
Sovereign bonds extend falls 
The sovereign bond sell-off was another feature of the markets this year.  In reality, sovereign 
bond prices have been broadly on a falling trend since 2020, although yield levels were much 
higher on both sides of the Atlantic in the early 2000s.  However, the rapid sell-off from what were 
already relatively high yield levels, combined with rising sovereign debt levels, pushed 
uncertainty higher and weighed on fixed income markets in general.

Higher yields haven’t spooked issuers 
Rising sovereign bond yields have meant higher corporate bond yields, but the good news is that 
the higher all-in costs have not had a significant negative impact on the new issue markets.  The 
chart below shows that the primary markets have remained open all year so far, despite sharp 
bursts of volatility, with new issue volumes remaining robust even as yields have risen.   
Higher yields haven’t kept issuers away (6 November)

Looking at the activity in October, new issue volumes were relatively low in the first half of the 
month, which we attribute to a combination of blackout periods and, more importantly, ongoing

strong volumes again.

Credit spreads weaken but the move remains moderate 
The weakness in September and October saw credit spreads widen sharply, and we had the iBoxx 
Corporates index spread to benchmarks widening from around B+90bp in the final week of 
August to B+122.5bp two months later.  Over the same period, the iTraxx Main index widened 
from 50bp to 68bp (although the roll in late September accounted for 4bp of that increase).  
However, the chart below shows how moderate the moves were compared to the swings seen in 
previous years.   
Credit spread widening is moderate by historical standards 
 
Source: SPICE. Bloomberg, SG Cross Asset Research/Credit 
In fact, the latest spread widening looks very mild and has so far been relatively brief compared 
with the moves seen in 2018 (when spreads widened all year long), the very sharp jump in 2020 
due to COVID-19, the severe widening in 2022, and even the rapid sell-off in 2025 following the US 
tariff announcements. 
Regarding the current level of spreads, last year we posed the same questions: 
Is credit fairly priced? … 
The good news is that the spread to benchmarks of the iBoxx Corporates is now much more 
attractive than it has been in well over a year.  As we can see in the chart below, the spread 
widening we saw in September and October means that the index spread is now close to the long-
term mean (although still a touch below).  As a reminder, we believe that the current market 
environment started around late 2013 to early 2014, after the sovereign bond crisis of 2011-13 
and the earlier subprime crisis and collapse of Lehman Brothers.

Credit metrics show credit-friendly results 
Rating dynamics confirm companies’ good health 
One of the consequences of companies’ positive earnings performance is that we continue to see 
more rating upgrades than downgrades.  This is evidenced by Moody’s rating drift ratio, which 
has remained positive in Europe throughout the year, extending the run to 43 months in a row 
and counting.  The US ratio had been negative since January 2023 but finally turned positive in 
June and has been on an upward trajectory since.  Granted, the measure is backward-looking, 
but we take heart in the recent trends, which, in our view, highlight how companies have 
managed to navigate crisis after crisis over the past few years.

Default rates on the right track 
Looking at default rates, the results are more mixed.  On the one hand, the latest levels remain

little better, at just under 4%, but the good news is that both seem to be slowly falling.  The US 
rate is now well below the recent peak seen in early 2025 (6.4%) and the European rate has fallen 
rapidly recently.

How the scorecard works 
The scorecard assigns a value to each of the five areas that impact spreads. These areas are 
macroeconomic fundamentals, corporate fundamentals, the technical factors of supply and 
investor demand, and, finally, valuations. Within each area there can be several subsectors; thus, 
profits, liquidity & spending and ratings & defaults combine to make up corporate fundamentals. 
We forecast the impact of each area by comparing our expectations to the consensus. The impact 
ranges from +++ (very positive) to --- (very negative). We then assign a weight to specify how 
important this indicator is at this point of the credit cycle. The impact multiplied by the weight 
gives the score for each indicator. The overall scorecard is the average of the five sector weights.

1. Macro fundamentals: The economy holds still 
This year has seen its fair share of problems and hurdles, including the war in the Middle East, 
rising oil prices, inflationary pressures, and central-bank tightening policies, in addition to the 
sovereign bond volatility and generally rising yields in the fixed income complex.  Yet economies 
around the world continued to grow highlighting the resilience of the global economy.   
Geopolitical tensions and oil prices 
The US midterm election results raised hopes of progress towards resolving the war in Iran, and 
oil futures quickly dropped, with Brent futures falling by almost $20 in four sessions, and WTI 
futures by some $16.  However, prices remain very high, as we can see in the chart below.  This is 
not too surprising, as global observed oil inventories have fallen significantly, with the IEA’s 
September oil market report estimating a drop of more than 500 million barrels since February, 
as our Commodities experts point out.  Moreover, oil futures prices remain below the physical 
prices paid by refiners, and following the recent sharp drop they have stabilised just below $100 
per barrel for now.  
Oil futures still high 
 
Source: Bloomberg, SG Cross Asset Research/Credit 
More elections to come 
The US midterm elections are now over, but next year we’ll have the French and Italian elections, 
among others.  In our view, the French elections are likely to hold the market’s attention, 
particularly given the large spread between OATs and Bunds.  The differential was relatively 
stable at around 60bp in the first half of the year, well below the 80bp seen in 2025.  But then it 
started to rise in the summer months, and the trend accelerated from mid-September onwards 
to reach 174bp in the second half of October.  Since then, the spread has fallen towards 150bp (at 
the time of writing), but remains extremely high, and not too far off the worst levels seen in 2011.

Risks to the credit markets 
Risk aversion hit its highest levels towards the end of October, and has since fallen, with not just 
credit spreads tightening but lower market volatility, rising equities and less volatile sovereign 
bonds.  Nevertheless, many questions remain, most recently around the future of the AI industry 
following the US midterm election results. However, in our view, the main risks to the markets 
haven’t changed from a few weeks ago. 
War in the Middle East and oil prices 
In our view, it is possible that, following the US election results, we could see an end to the war.  
Clearly, oil prices believe the narrative, and we believe that further signs of improvement would 
boost risk appetite across the board.  The risk is that the war continues, miscalculations occur 
and the situation on the ground deteriorates, with oil prices rising further, supply chains facing 
greater disruption and potentially a bigger hit to the economy.  This is not our central scenario, 
but it is a tail risk that could prove very damaging.   
Sovereign bond damage  
We believe that the rising sovereign bond yields and overall volatility we saw back in October 
were among the main reasons for the poor performance of the credit markets, particularly the 
euro IG and HY indices.  We hope we have seen the worst of the volatility, but we suspect that if 
oil prices surge again, sovereign bonds will come under renewed pressure.   
However, for us, the issue is not so much the actual level of sovereign bond yields as the speed at 
which they could rise. We note that our Rates colleagues still expect the 10y Bund yield to rise to 
3.75-3.85% in 1H27. In our view, if sovereign bond yields rise in a gentle and orderly fashion (if 
such a thing can be achieved) then credit spreads should withstand the deterioration with ease 
and even tighten gently on the back of the higher overall yields.  The problem, for us, would be a 
very rapid yield rise accompanied by a sharp increase in uncertainty. This would put pressure on 
credit spreads, although we suspect that the credit market would still prove more resilient than 
most if not all sovereign bonds. 
An economic recession 
We discussed this a year ago, and we look at it once again now, as we believe it is the worst-case 
scenario for the credit markets. The probability of an economic recession remains very low, in our 
view, but if the issues discussed above materialise, the margin for error would be much smaller.  
In such an event, we believe that credit spreads would react negatively very quickly, and test the 
widest levels we saw in the aftermath of the Credit Suisse collapse.

Ratings
