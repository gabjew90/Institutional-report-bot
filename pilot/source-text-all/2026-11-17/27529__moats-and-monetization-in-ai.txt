Executive Summary 
In the almost four years since the launch of ChatGPT, the question that our technology equity research 
team hears the most is alarmingly simple: If every large language model can write the same email, 
summarize the same earnings, and generate a coherent block of code, why should firms building these 
LLMs earn outsize economic profits?  
 
We began hearing this question in 2024 and 2025 as a way to assess whether public cloud vendors 
could earn reasonable returns on their large capital expenditures. In 2026, with investors eyeing 
potential IPOs in the artificial intelligence lab space, the question is more squarely aimed at labs such 
as Anthropic and OpenAI, as investors try to figure out whether these labs are on a fast track to 
commodification or building durable businesses. 
 
We come out on the more bullish side, as we think leading AI labs are building economic moats. While 
we do not view these moats as fully formed yet, early usage data, product development, and 
partnerships indicate that leading AI labs will be able to extract economic returns from developing and 
running LLMs, staving off threats of commodification. The structure of this report is straightforward: It 
first presents the market's concerns, then highlights our position and where we could be wrong. 
 
The Market's Worry 
Investors see the three classic markers of a commodity market forming at the model layer: First, raw 
capabilities are converging, with Chinese open-weight models closing the gap. Second, the price of a 
fixed level of intelligence has collapsed since 2023. Third, switching models on third-party platforms is 
nearly frictionless.

Why the Market's Concerns Are Valid 
If the labs cannot earn economic profits, the concern spreads well beyond Anthropic and OpenAI. The 
spending commitments rippling across chips, power, and data centers rest on the assumption that 
intelligence can be monetized at attractive returns, making the question of commodification, in effect, a 
question about the durability of the entire AI trade. 
 
Our View 
We think the leading AI labs are in the process of building economic moats and will stave off 
commodification. In our view, the bears are missing three things. First, model unit economics are 
inflecting as inference demand scales faster than training and research costs; Anthropic reached 
operating profitability in the first two quarters of 2026, well ahead of bearish expectations. Second, the 
labs are building data-and-product flywheels, an early form of network effects, in which usage improves 
models and tools, and better models and tools drive more usage. Third, the labs are building structural 
cost advantages through compounding training know-how, compute-scale discounts, concentrated 
research talent, and custom silicon. 
 
Alternative Outcomes Are Possible 
Widely adopted open models at or near the frontier (the most advanced AI systems), a migration of 
inference toward free small models on edge devices, and public-opinion-driven constraints on data 
center construction are the developments we watch as potential thesis breakers.

Before diving deeper into the specific dynamics that give us confidence that AI labs can build durable 
business models, it is worth providing a general overview of how LLMs work, especially the delineation 
between training and inference, and a brief overview of the two main pure-play AI labs, Anthropic and 
OpenAI.  
 
Building LLMs 
A large language model is the output of a four-stage process: data ingestion and processing, 
pretraining, post-training, and inference.  
 
Exhibit 1  How Large Language Models Are Set Up 
Source: Morningstar. 
 
During the first stage, data ingestion and preprocessing, AI labs source enormous volumes of text from a 
variety of sources, including the open web, books, code repositories, and synthetic data from prior large 
language models. The labs then clean the data and structure it into training-ready datasets.  
 
The second stage, pretraining, is the core model-building step, in which unsupervised learning runs 
across massive AI-accelerator clusters teach the model the language, grammar, and conceptual 
relationships from the data that it has access to. These training runs can take months at a time, with 
outputs only known after they’re complete. This time-delay dynamic is a contributing factor to why 
having the best talent matters for AI labs. Small tweaks in the training run can lead to materially

The third stage, post-training, is typically when AI labs teach raw LLMs to be helpful, honest, safe, and to 
follow instructions. Post-training is also the stage where reinforcement learning with human feedback 
plays a major role in helping models develop skills such as drafting documents or building models. This 
stage is also when AI labs set parameters for models to ensure they refrain from performing certain 
tasks, such as helping create biochemical weapons or infiltrating codebases.  
 
The fourth stage is inference and deployment, in which end users can submit prompts and the model 
generates outputs based on its pre- and post-training learning. 
 
The important thing to keep in mind for investors is that the first three stages are fixed costs incurred 
before the model generates any revenue, and the remaining costs are reserved solely for the inference 
stage. The cost structure in the inference stage, however, is variable and scales alongside usage.  
 
Training and Inference 
If we had to distill the development of LLMs even further, we’d think that investors can break it into two 
stages: training (stages one to three above) and inference (stage four). 
 
Exhibit 2  Training Versus Inference 
Source: Morningstar. 
 
We reiterate this point because investors often lump model development costs into one bucket and try 
to calculate AI labs’ economics as if training and inference costs were conceptually the same, which 
they are not.  
 
We see training as analogous to building a factory. Developing a frontier model requires substantial 
capital expenditures to access cloud infrastructure and AI accelerators capable of supporting massive 
training and post-training runs. As the labs build each factory or model, they do not collect any revenue, 
and the economic returns from this factory are wholly linked to the eventual quality of its outputs and 
customer demand for them.

Another dynamic to highlight here is that AI labs can train a large model and then use it to train 
downstream models that are cheaper and more efficient.  
 
Inference, on the other hand, is akin to running the factory. While you need cloud infrastructure and AI 
accelerators to run each model, the costs are a fraction of the costs of training runs. Also, for leading AI 
labs, most of their inference volume is directly monetizable through subscriptions, direct billing, or 
advertising.  
 
To get a clearer picture of the durability of AI labs’ business models, we must treat training and 
inference separately, as we do in other businesses, where we have different economic 
conceptualizations of fixed and variable costs.

have models either right at the frontier or in the vicinity, we believe the question of moats and 
monetization in LLMs is particularly prescient for Anthropic and OpenAI.

The core bear case against leading AI labs can be boiled down to three main arguments. One, we are 
seeing a convergence in capabilities. Two, we are seeing a decline in token prices. Three, model 
companies do not have high switching costs, making it easy to switch between models. These are 
admittedly the three indicators you would want to see to identify a typical commodity-like market.

Each leg of this three-legged bear stool is supported by observable data. Bears argue that if all three

model builders to firms that control scarce complements (chips, power, cloud infrastructure,

We find this to be a coherent bear case, as it does not seek to dismiss AI as a “fad” or merely a theme

Bear Leg One: Converging Capabilities and the Chinese Open-Model Risk 
The capability, or raw intelligence, as measured by industry researchers, data is clear: when we chart

have narrowed to the low single digits, with the order of who has the best model also regularly shuffling

Our Take 
We understand that investors remain focused on whether the proliferation of Chinese open-weight 
models commoditizes the model layer. Our view is that Chinese models will continue to lag US frontier 
models because of structural reasons, listed below. Further, we believe that the rise of open models 
capable of taking over low-value tasks will, in turn, create greater demand for orchestrators and frontier 
models to efficiently manage these utility-tier models. Finally, we think it is highly unlikely that US 
enterprises/government agencies will standardize their workflows on Chinese open models for a variety 
of reasons, including national security and cost-related factors that we discuss below.  
 
The Chinese open-model bear case is certainly top of investors’ minds, as we saw in investor worries 
about the prospects of frontier model companies following the release of DeepSeek’s R1 model in 
January 2025 and Moonshot’s Kimi K3 model in July 2026.  
 
While we don’t want to dismiss the impressive technical improvements the Chinese labs have made 
over the past couple of years, we’d like to remind investors that their progress comes down to two key 
factors.  
 
First, as Chinese labs develop models in a hardware-constrained ecosystem (because they don’t have 
access to the latest AI chips), the only way to remain competitive is to optimize their software and 
algorithms to train and run models efficiently on older hardware. As we saw in the US model launched 
following DeepSeek’s R1 launch, US companies can quickly see the algorithmic advancements made by 
their Chinese counterparts and incorporate them into their own training apparatus, achieving even 
better model outcomes because they have access to better hardware than Chinese labs. This dynamic 
means that as long as Chinese models remain open-weight and release technical papers detailing their 
algorithmic advancements, US labs can incorporate these methods into their models to accelerate their 
progress. 
 
Second, Chinese labs have been using outputs from Claude models, as well as the thinking traces that 
Claude provides, to further refine and improve their own models in a process known as distillation. If 
Chinese model roadmaps rely, at least in part, on leveraging frontier US models for improvement, then 
Chinese models, by definition, cannot surpass US labs in a meaningful way.  
 
We would concede that neither dynamic, on its own, is an argument against convergence. In our view, 
what these dynamics establish is a ceiling rather than a differentiator: a distillation-dependent 
ecosystem can track the frontier, but it cannot durably lead it.  
 
From a value-creation versus value-capture perspective, we think that the proliferation of Chinese open 
models may spur greater demand for intelligent orchestrator models to manage them. The analogy to 
keep in mind for investors is that of a competent manager, whose value increases when they have 
competent employees to whom they can delegate tasks. If an orchestrator model can manage a host of 
agents, keep them on task, and deliver real business value, we believe a disproportionate amount of 
value would accrue to this orchestrator agent. The natural rebuttal is that orchestrators should converge

just as base models have. We would note, however, that orchestration quality is less a function of raw 
weights and more a function of post-training investment, tool use, context handling, and reliability 
across long task chains, which have so far proved to be the layers hardest to replicate through 
distillation.  
 
Beyond the value-capture opportunity for frontier models, we do not believe that US enterprises, 
government agencies, or companies working with the US government will adopt Chinese open models 
to save on inference costs. The policy, security, and strategic realities are such that the US cannot afford 
to cede its intelligence layer to companies that operate out of an adversarial nation-state. While bears 
would love to show us OpenRouter usage data indicating adoption of Chinese open-weight models, we 
do not find that data representative of actual enterprise workloads, as it heavily skews toward hobbyists 
and individual developers.  
 
We would take the adoption argument further. First, we believe US procurement rules and sector 
regulations are likely to formally restrict the use of Chinese models in enterprise and government 
settings, and such rules can be written strictly enough to close the obvious backdoor of downloading 
open weights and running them on-premises without technically routing through a Chinese company. 
Second, even without strict rules, we doubt most enterprises want to stand up on-premises weights and 
build the surrounding serving, security, and evaluation infrastructure themselves. The revealed 
preference of enterprise IT is to pay a third party for a turnkey, supported package. 
 
It is also worth asking why the Chinese open-weight ecosystem exists in the form it does. In our view, 
the answer is partly legal: Chinese labs operate in a jurisdiction that does not meaningfully enforce 
intellectual property claims around model outputs, allowing them to distill from US frontier models in 
ways US companies could not replicate without inviting litigation. This asymmetry lowers Chinese 
research and development costs, but it also creates a dependency that US labs can attack. If frontier 
labs restrict their most capable models to private, enterprise-only channels, withhold reasoning traces, 
and deploy distillation-detection techniques, the pipeline feeding the Chinese open-model ecosystem 
narrows considerably. Should distillation become materially harder, the competitive set at the frontier 
effectively collapses to the two or three US labs, and convergence becomes a much smaller concern for 
the economics of the model layer. 
 
Beyond the national security argument, prior comparisons show that Chinese open models are not as 
cheap as sticker-price comparisons suggest. To put it in concrete terms, Kimi K3's API pricing is a 
blended rate of $5.40 per million tokens, which is roughly half of Opus 4.8 ($9) and GPT 5.5 ($10). While 
this may appear to be a big discount, we've seen prior Chinese open-source models be much more 
token-intensive and require many more steps to complete tasks, reducing the price advantage and, in 
some cases, even reversing it. 
 
Data from industry researchers supports this view, with cost-to-run comparisons showing that models 
with lower sticker prices often cost as much as, or more than, models with higher sticker prices to run 
real workloads once token intensity is accounted for. The broader point, in our view, is that the

fundamental cost input at the model layer is compute and the efficiency with which it is used. If the US 
frontier labs have better hardware and use it more efficiently, through caching, batching, and serving 
optimizations that come with scale as well as proprietary algorithmic work, these inference efficiencies 
can more than offset the research and development cost advantage that distillation hands the Chinese 
open-model ecosystem. 
 
Bear Leg Two: Price Compression 
The pricing data, if anything, is even more dramatic. When models are grouped into intelligence cohorts, 
and price per intelligence cohort is tracked over time, we see material price compression. Intelligence 
that commanded $17 per million tokens in late 2024 can be purchased for $0.50 per million tokens in 
2026, a 97% price decline in less than two years. 
 
Exhibit 8  Language Model Inference Price, by Intelligence Index Band, Over Time 
Source: Morningstar, Artificial Analysis. Data as of Sept. 18, 2026. 
 
Even in the frontier models, we have seen a similar downward trend in the months following their 
release. Models scoring above 40 on the Intelligence Index have seen prices compress from roughly $5 
toward $0.25 per million tokens within a handful of quarters of release. 
 
Our Take 
While it can be easy to look at these price charts and jump on the side of commodification, we’d urge 
readers to reserve judgment. Falling unit prices, when paired with exploding volumes, can lead to high 
sales growth (we did mention Anthropic’s meteoric sales growth, right?). For example, even if the price 
per token falls by 90%, if token usage increases by more than 10 times (as it has), the total amount spent 
on tokens still goes up. Another point worth noting is that the AI labs' ability to efficiently serve models 
has also increased dramatically over the past few years. As a result, these labs can effectively maintain 
margins while the price per token declines for certain models within their model families.  
 
While we concede the point of price compression to the bears, we’d argue that the volume response to 
falling prices and the model companies’ ability to serve models more efficiently matter more, as we will 
lay out in our unit economics section, which is just around the corner.

Bear Leg Three: Low Switching Costs 
The final leg of the three-leg bear argument for commodification, low switching costs, is also largely 
correct at the infrastructure layer. When using cloud platforms such as Microsoft Azure or AWS Bedrock, 
or coding tools such as Antigravity or Cursor, users can simply pick a different model from a drop-down 
menu, as shown in Exhibit 9. 
 
Exhibit 9  Switching Between Models Is As Easy as One-Two-Three 
Source: AWS Bedrock Console, Cursor Model Settings, Azure AI Foundry Model Catalog, Google Antigravity. 
 
This dynamic, especially when it comes to the hyperscalers, makes sense as these firms benefit from a 
competitive and interchangeable model layer.  
 
Our Take 
Our pushback on this bear case is more nuanced. While we agree that the cost of switching a model 
endpoint, such as a model on Cursor, is near zero, the cost of switching an enterprise AI deployment is 
not. Enterprises investing in areas such as prompt engineering, evaluation harnesses, fine-tuning 
models, and building agentic scaffolding are doing so by modeling these tasks after a specific model or 
model provider. If they were to switch models on the fly across their entire organization, they’d run into 
a ton of quality-control issues, as many of the processes that they’d developed won’t simply port over to 
the new model.  
 
While we see the argument for more competition on the model layer as companies rationalize their AI 
spending, we believe model providers’ price segmentation strategies will largely accommodate this 
rationalization while keeping enterprises within their larger ecosystems, which include apps, harnesses, 
artifacts, and so forth. In other words, the model companies will offer different models with varying 
levels of intelligence and speed at different price points. If you want the cheapest model for a simple, 
repetitive task, that will be an option, and vice versa, all offered as a complete menu within each model 
company.

This ecosystem point also previews what we view as a structural conflict between the model companies 
and the software industry. The value that sits outside the model weights, including harnesses, company 
context, workflows, and integrations, is precisely the layer software firms have historically been built to 
capture. We see two plausible equilibria: either software firms become the distribution and final 
integration layer for the model companies, with the model acting as a tolled input, or the model 
companies vertically integrate and effectively become software firms themselves. The same tension 
runs through the labs’ relationships with public cloud providers, as hyperscalers benefit from an 
interchangeable model layer while model companies work to deepen lock-in around their own 
ecosystems. 
 
Our Take on the Broader Bear Thesis 
We believe that AI lab bears make a valid point. We accept that raw model capabilities are converging, 
with Chinese open models being a clear competitive threat. We also accept that per-token prices are 
structurally deflationary, and that switching models on third-party platforms is basically frictionless.  
 
That said, as we laid out above, all three legs of the bear case deserve more attention. We also believe 
that by centering the argument around convergence, price, and switching costs, investors can lose sight 
of the two factors that matter far more.  
 
First, the unit economics of the model business are inflecting in a manner not typical of commodity 
businesses, with clear operating leverage emerging despite their nascent stage. Second, the sources of 
durable advantage in the model business are not going to be benchmark scores and raw intelligence. 
The true sources of competitive advantage and differentiation in the model layer, in our opinion, lie in 
the data flywheels (network effects) and the structural cost advantages these firms are building. These 
two factors constitute the remainder of our report. AI bulls, please enjoy the fun part.

To support our view that unit economics are much better than investors think, we first separate an AI 
lab’s cost base into three primary centers. The scalability and amortization of these costs are key to 
understanding these firms’ longer-term margin trajectories. 
 
Exhibit 11  The Three Primary Cost Centers for AI Labs 
Source: Morningstar estimates. Data as of Sept. 18, 2026. 
 
As a model company decides to divvy up its compute, it can choose among three buckets.  
 
Bucket one is training. This cost, as we discussed in the foundation section, is mostly fixed and is 
amortized over the trained model’s useful life, which is around a year. Investors may be surprised to see 
such a long useful life, considering model launches and improvements are far more frequent. To this 
point, we’d note that pretraining costs are mostly tied to training a new generation of models (an 
example would be a jump from Claude 3 to Claude 4). So, once you complete a successful pretraining 
run, you can leverage it to launch multiple models that build on the same initial run (for example, Opus 
4.6, Opus 4.7, and Opus 4.8 are all based on the same pretraining run). This teacher-student framework 
is why training costs are amortized over a longer period than investors may be thinking.

training costs, but they’re also amortized over a longer period. A good example of this timeline could be

Over the past few years, we have seen AI labs focus the vast majority of their resources on training and

on investments in training and research that made the models and relevant tools useful enough for 
companies and users to deploy.  
 
So, as investors, the real question about AI lab economics is not whether a lab loses or makes money 
during training and research (hint: they lose money), but rather what the company’s economics look like 
as inference becomes a larger part of the overall compute pie, and how much can inference scale 
relative to training. 
 
As Inference Scales: Revenue and Margins Will Inflect Upward 
Our core thesis on AI lab unit economics can be put in one sentence: inference demand is scaling 
exponentially, with a significantly steeper curve than training and research costs. As both inference and 
training and research costs continue to scale, the gap between the two curves widens, and that is where 
this industry's profits will be found.

Scaling on Compute, Not Labor, Provides Much More Leverage 
Before turning our attention to economic moats, we leave the reader with one more piece of unit 
economics analysis. A business whose principal cost center is people scales revenue mostly by hiring. 
While revenue per employee tends to improve with scale, the gains are often marginal rather than 
transformational.  
 
On the other hand, a business whose principal cost center is compute scales revenue by bringing more 
hardware online, unbound by headcount considerations. Again, this is a simplification, as the business 
would need people who are master operators and manipulators of that hardware, but the larger point 
still holds.  
 
As evidence of this dynamic, take a look at the chart below comparing Salesforce’s revenue per 
employee with Anthropic’s. Anthropic's revenue per employee has gone practically vertical, rising from

Open Models Surprise to the Upside 
Our current view is that open models, both Chinese and Western, remain behind the frontier, enabling 
frontier-model companies to build durable businesses and monetize at significantly higher rates than 
their open-model counterparts. If, due to a technological breakthrough in either the training or post-
training paradigms, research labs releasing open models were able to take the lead on the model layer, 
it would have a serious impact on our pricing and growth assumptions that underpin the frontier lab's 
unit economics.  
 
We would frame this risk as having two variants, and the second does not require open models to lead. 
The first variant is the one described above, in which open models outright surpass the frontier. The 
second is that open models simply remain close enough to the frontier that the leading labs cannot 
maintain any durable edge, a dynamic not far from what we observe today. Critically, either variant only 
challenges our views meaningfully if these advanced open models, which are likely to be Chinese given 
the intellectual property constraints on Western open efforts, are also widely deployed in the West. 
Preventing that outcome requires some combination of government action restricting their use and 
distillation-prevention measures that choke the pipeline feeding the open ecosystem. It also requires 
scaling laws to keep delivering frontier improvement from here.

Research Methodology for Valuing Companies 
 
Overview 
At the heart of our valuation system is a detailed projection of a company’s future cash flows, resulting from our analysts’ 
research. Analysts create custom industry and company assumptions to feed income statement, balance sheet, and capital 
investment assumptions into our globally standardized, proprietary discounted cash flow, or DCF, modeling templates. We use 
scenario analysis, in-depth competitive advantage analysis, and a variety of other analytical tools to augment this process. We 
think analyzing valuation through discounted cash flows presents a better lens for viewing cyclical companies, high-growth firms, 
businesses with finite lives (mines, for example), or companies expected to generate negative earnings over the next few years. 
That said, we don’t dismiss multiples altogether but rather use them as supporting cross-checks for our DCF-based fair value 
estimates. We also acknowledge that DCF models offer their own challenges (including a potential proliferation of estimated 
inputs and the possibility that the method may miss short-term market-price movements), but we believe these negatives are 
mitigated by deep analysis and our long-term approach.  
 
Morningstar’s Equity Research Group (“we,” “our”) believes that a company’s intrinsic worth results from the future cash flows it 
can generate. The Morningstar Rating for stocks identifies stocks trading at a discount or premium to their intrinsic worth—or fair 
value estimate in Morningstar terminology. Five-star stocks sell for the biggest risk-adjusted discount to their fair values, whereas 
1-star stocks trade at premiums to their intrinsic worth.  
 
Four key components drive the Morningstar rating:  
▸ our assessment of the firm’s economic moat.  
▸ our estimate of the stock’s fair value.  
▸ our uncertainty around that fair value estimate.  
▸ the current market price.  
 
This process ultimately culminates in our single-point star rating.  
 
Economic Moat 
The Morningstar Economic Moat Rating is a structural feature that Morningstar believes positions a firm to earn durable excess 
profits over a long period of time, with excess profits defined as returns on invested capital above our estimate of a firm’s cost of 
capital. The economic moat rating is not an indicator of the investment performance of the investment highlighted in this report. 
Narrow-moat companies are those that Morningstar believes are more likely than not to achieve normalized excess returns for at 
least the next 10 years. Wide-moat companies are those that Morningstar believes will earn excess returns for 10 years, with 
excess returns more likely than not to remain for at least 20 years. Firms without a moat, including those that have a substantial 
threat of value destruction-related risks related to environmental, social, and governance; industry disruption; financial health; or 
other idiosyncratic issues, are more susceptible to competition. Morningstar has identified five sources of economic moats: 
intangible assets, switching costs, network effect, cost advantage, and efficient scale. 
 
Fair Value Estimate 
Each stock’s fair value is estimated by using a proprietary discounted cash flow model, which assumes that the stock’s value is 
equal to the total of the free cash flows of the company is expected to generate in the future, discounted back to the present at the 
rate commensurate with the riskiness of the cash flows. As with any DCF model, the ending value is highly sensitive to 
Morningstar’s projections of future growth. 
 
Fair Value Uncertainty 
The Morningstar Uncertainty Rating represents the analysts’ ability to bound the estimated value of the shares in a company 
around the fair value estimate, based on the characteristics of the business underlying the stock, including operating and 
financial leverage, sales sensitivity to the overall economy, product concentration, pricing power, exposure to material ESG risks, 
and other company-specific factors. Based on these factors, analysts classify the stock into one of several uncertainty levels: Low, 
Medium, High, Very High, or Extreme. Our recommended margin of safety—the discount to fair value demanded before we’d 
recommend buying or selling the stock—widens as our uncertainty of the estimated value of the equity increases.

Morningstar Rating for Stocks 
The Morningstar Rating for Stocks is a forward-looking, analyst-driven measure of a stock’s current price relative to the analyst’s 
estimate of what the shares are worth. Stock star ratings indicate whether a stock, in the equity analyst’s educated opinion, is 
cheap, expensive, or fairly priced. To rate a stock, analysts estimate what they think it is worth (its “fair value”), using a detailed, 
long-term cash flow forecast for the company. A stock’s star rating depends on whether its current market price is above or below 
the fair value estimate. Those stocks trading at large discounts to their fair values receive the highest ratings (4 or 5 stars). Stocks 
trading at large premiums to their fair values receive lower ratings (1 or 2 stars). A 3-star rating means the current stock price is 
close to the analyst’s fair value estimate.
