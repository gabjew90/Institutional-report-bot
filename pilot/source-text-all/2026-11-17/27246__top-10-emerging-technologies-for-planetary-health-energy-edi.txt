Foreword
Planetary boundaries offer a window into 
understanding the conditions that make modern 
human life on Earth possible. They describe limits in 
the Earth systems that regulate climate, freshwater, 
land, nutrients, oceans and the living biosphere, 
regulating stability, resilience and life support on 
Earth. Together, they define the safe operating space 
within which humans can thrive. These boundaries 
are not a checklist of separate environmental 
problems. They are connected processes, and 
pressure in one can increase pressure in another. 
Earlier this year, we published our third annual 
Planetary Health Check, confirming that seven of the 
nine boundaries have been breached, with no clear 
evidence of recovery.1 We continue to move further 
away from safety and deeper into danger. The 
consequences will unfold unevenly, but the direction 
is clear: action is needed now to move towards 
recovery and mitigate the worst impacts.
Humanity has a profound, documented impact on 
these systems. We shape them through what we 
extract, build, consume and waste, and it is in our 
power to change our influence. Agriculture can 
restore soils or exhaust them; cities can protect 
watersheds or seal them; and industry can reduce 
pollution or spread pollutants through air, water 
and ecosystems. Technology is embedded in 
all of these choices. It can substitute damaging 
processes, make resource use more efficient or 
repair ecosystems. Technology can also accelerate 
extraction, lock in wasteful demand, shift impacts 
from one boundary to another or create new 
dependencies whose consequences are difficult to 
anticipate. There is no silver bullet, and technologies 
need to be assessed by their net impact across 
planetary pressures, dependencies and costs, 
in addition to their potential and realized benefits.
Energy systems are particularly consequential 
because they intersect with so many of the 
boundaries. Energy systems are a primary 
determinant of how much carbon enters the 
atmosphere and, through that, how quickly oceans 
acidify. They also shape how land, water, minerals 
and infrastructure are used across the economy, 
influencing air quality, the production of goods, 
transport, buildings and food, and communities’ ability 
to withstand heat, disruption and price shocks. More 
than a single-sector intervention, the energy transition 
is a lever that can ease pressure across several 
boundaries, provided it is designed around material 
limits, ecosystems’ health, resilience and fair access.
Last year’s edition of this report examined emerging 
technologies across food, water, ecosystems, 
industry and energy, showing the breadth of 
innovation driving planetary health. This year, 
its focus on energy technologies reflects both 
the scale of the challenge and the possibility of 
gains compounding when generation, storage, 
materials, buildings, industry and digital systems 
are considered together. The technologies that 
follow should not be read as a substitute for proven 
solutions, policy or changed patterns of production 
and consumption. They are options whose value 
will depend on how responsibly they are developed, 
combined and deployed.
Johan Rockström
Director, Potsdam Institute for 
Climate Impact Research (PIK)

Introduction
Building a cleaner, more secure and 
equitable energy system requires innovation 
across technologies and infrastructure.
The way the world generates, moves and stores 
energy affects nearly every planetary boundary 
under strain. This report looks at 10 technologies 
best positioned to ease that strain and at what 
it will take for decision-makers in government, 
finance and industry to scale them. These 10 
were chosen from dozens of contenders based on 
how much they could ease pressure on the planet, 
widen access to reliable power and strengthen 
the systems that deliver it, each with a credible 
path to deployment by 2030 (see Figure 1).
This edition arrives at a moment of growing 
pressure on energy systems. Markets are volatile, 
supply chains are concentrated, and the push for 
energy independence could even drive a return to 
coal in places where cleaner alternatives cannot 
keep up with demand. The energy transition needs 
to cut emissions and also reduce the dependencies 
that leave any one country exposed to shocks it 
cannot fully control.
The 10 technologies in this report meet that need 
in different ways. A battery built from materials 
common enough to source almost anywhere 
keeps working in cold that would stall a lithium 
battery entirely. A blend of clay and limestone 
can now replace roughly half of the core material 
of ordinary cement, cutting emissions from 
concrete production, one of the world’s most 
carbon-intensive materials. Each chapter includes 
an overview of the technology and three short 
assessments of its impact on planetary boundaries, 
equity and security, weighing the benefits and real 
trade-offs for the planet, people and systems.
Three patterns emerge from the list of technologies 
that are worth tracking while reading.
First, they address different kinds of bottlenecks. 
Several solve a materials problem, replacing a 
scarce, concentrated input with one common 
enough to find almost anywhere. Others solve a 
systems problem instead, closing the gap between 
when the grid generates power and when it is 
needed. Understanding which kinds of problems a 
technology solves is often the fastest way to identify 
what could accelerate or stall its advancement from 
a mineral deal to a regulator’s decision.
Second, they reach the world in different ways. 
Granular technologies spread through many small 
deployments that each get a little cheaper to build, 
so their progress shows up gradually, one project 
at a time. Infrastructure-scale technologies depend 
instead on a smaller number of large, capital-
intensive projects, so their progress often turns on 
a single decision, e.g. a construction approval or 
a procurement standard that can unlock multiple 
subsequent projects at once. This difference 
shapes their effects on the wider system. Granular 
technologies often improve existing systems from 
within, while infrastructure-scale technologies have 
the potential to reshape them from the ground up.
Finally, they are not designed to work in isolation. 
Examined individually, each addresses a specific 
problem. Paired together and combined with other 
enabling technologies, their value often compounds 
instead. A subsea cable carrying an offshore wind 
farm’s power ashore is only as useful as the battery 
that can absorb what it generates. A heat battery 
only banks power that a software-coordinated grid 
has already made available. The technologies in this 
report are building blocks, meant to work together 
rather than stand alone.
Reaching this combination of technologies took a 
wide range of expert perspectives. This report was 
developed in collaboration with 18 experts from 
business, academia, government and international 
organizations, drawn from 10 countries and six 
regions. They were essential in shaping the selection 
and analysis of the technologies featured here.
Every technology in this report can help ease 
pressure on the planet. How far that benefit 
reaches, and how many people actually get to 
share in it, depends on how these technologies 
are built, integrated and put to use from here.

Calcined-clay limestone cement 
Lower-carbon cement for 
tomorrow’s roads and bridges
01

A $100 billion near-zero-carbon 
cement market by 2035
International Energy Agency (IEA). (2025). Demand- and supply-side measures 
for the steel and cement transition: Executive summary.
Today’s cement starts with limestone and clay, 
crushed together and fired in a kiln hot enough 
to break the limestone.2 This releases carbon 
stored in the rock for millions of years. Reaching 
that temperature also takes enormous amounts of 
fuel, adding to the process’s total emissions. Hard 
grey pellets called clinker come out of the other 
end. Ground fine and mixed with small amounts 
of gypsum, clinker becomes Portland cement, 
the powder that binds sand, gravel and water into 
concrete. It is also the material behind many of the 
roads, bridges and buildings on Earth.3 Cement is 
responsible for about 8% of global carbon dioxide 
(CO2) emissions,4 making it one of the most critical 
materials to decarbonize in order to achieve global 
net-zero ambitions.
One way to cut those emissions is to use less 
clinker. Calcined-clay limestone cement (LC3), 
still uses clay and limestone but processes them in 
a radically different way. The clay is calcined and 
heated enough to drive off water locked inside its 
structure and break down its original crystal form, 
leaving it chemically unstable and highly reactive. 
The limestone is ground fine instead, which gives 
it far more surface area for reaction. Neither does 
much on its own, but the two put together activate 
each other. When combined with the remaining 
clinker, the three react to seal the pores, giving 
concrete its strength.5 In early testing, cement made 
this way, with clinker cut nearly in half, has already 
outperformed ordinary Portland cement in strength6 
while cutting emissions on two fronts. Less clinker 
means less of the chemical reaction that releases 
CO2. Because clay is heated at roughly half the 
temperature required for clinker production, LC3 
also directly reduces industrial energy demand, 
not just process emissions. These two effects 
combined bring emissions down by up to 40% 
compared to ordinary Portland cement.7
LC3’s chemistry, developed by a research 
partnership led by EPFL in Switzerland with 
institutions in India and Cuba,8 has been proven 
in labs for years, but getting it into an actual 
building requires the paperwork to catch up. In 
2021, Europe adopted a new cement standard, 
allowing up to half of a cement’s mass to come 
from calcined clay and limestone combined.9 India 
followed in 2023, publishing a standard that sets 
a minimum clinker content of 50% and clears 
calcined clay for up to 35% of the mix.10 A builder 
specifying cement in Paris or Mumbai can now 
reach for LC3 without applying for an exemption.
Two projects already show that shift at work. In 
Colombia, a plant at Rio Claro has been producing 
calcined clay cement since around 2020; by 2024, 
it had made more than two million tonnes of it, 
used in bridges, tunnels, highways and high-rise 
buildings.11 In Denmark, a version of this cement 
called FUTURECEM has been sold commercially 
since 2021, and within a year it spread to France, 
Belgium and the Benelux region, poured into 
foundations, cast into precast panels and used on 
at least one bridge.12 The rules are keeping pace 
with the material. In the US, an existing standard 
already allows for LC3 to be used in construction.13,14 
While Europe’s old codes didn’t permit this kind of 
blend, regulators issued a new code in 2021 that 
did.15 A similar wave of regulatory revisions has 
been required across emerging economies, such 
as India, Brazil and Colombia, where LC3 presents 
an opportunity to address high clinker costs and 
carbon emissions simultaneously.16
However, scaling low-carbon cement production 
globally could take over $1 trillion in investment.17 
Standards are emerging, but procurement, testing 
and buyer confidence remain key bottlenecks. In 
the US, an existing standard already permits far 
higher clinker replacement than the roughly 5% 
most contractors specify by default, but state 
transport departments and federal buyers have 
been slow to translate that flexibility into contracts.18 
Public buyers can close that gap directly. They 
need performance-based specifications that judge 
a cement by its strength and durability rather than 
its ingredient list, plus the testing infrastructure to 
support them. Federal programmes such as Buy 
Clean, together with low-carbon procurement rules, 
are already pushing procurement in that direction.19 
How far that reach extends into state-level cement 
specifications over the next few years will determine 
how fast LC3 can scale.
If LC3 reaches global scale, a different challenge 
could emerge. LC3 shifts cement’s dependence on 
quarried rock from limestone burned into clinker to 
clay burned at a lower heat instead. Which deposits 
end up carrying that new weight, on whose land 
and under whose oversight, are the questions the 
industry is only beginning to answer.

Equity
LC3 can make construction more affordable. Replacing up to 
half of the clinker with limestone and calcined clay can reduce 
production costs while strengthening the cement. This is particularly 
important for growing cities in need of more homes, roads and 
hospitals. Processing local clay can also create new jobs in cement-
producing regions and shift more value creation into countries 
that currently import clinker but possess clay resources. However, 
lower production costs do not automatically mean cheaper 
construction. Savings may not be propagated down the value 
chain, as new processing equipment requires investment, reliable 
heat, testing facilities and skilled workers. The clay must also have 
the right properties, while building rules, public procurement and 
affordable financing must accept LC3 before smaller companies 
and lower-income communities can benefit. Public procurement 
and building standards will play a major role in determining the rate 
at which lower-cost cement reaches affordable housing and public 
infrastructure, breaking out beyond niche projects.
Security
LC3 can strengthen industrial and energy security by reducing 
dependence on fuel-intensive clinker production and imported 
materials. Because it cuts clinker production, which often relies on 
imported coal, petroleum coke or gas, it reduces exposure to fuel 
shocks and import bills. Most countries also have direct access to 
clay and limestone and can often adapt existing kilns to produce 
LC3. However, resilience still depends on local geology, transport 
and steady heat for clay calcination. Plants still need gypsum, 
electricity, laboratories and precise process control. A country that 
lacks these capabilities may swap fuel dependency for imported 
equipment, expertise or calcined clay.
Planetary boundaries
Climate change pressure can be reduced because LC3 uses about 
half as much clinker as conventional cement, decreasing both process 
emissions and the energy required to produce cement. This can cut 
carbon emissions by up to 40%.20
Biosphere integrity pressures may shift rather than disappear. LC3 
reduces limestone extraction but increases demand for calcined clay, 
which can still damage habitats, underscoring the importance of 
responsible sourcing and land restoration.
Ocean acidification may slow when LC3 replaces conventional cement 
because lower CO2 emissions mean less CO2 is absorbed by the 
oceans. Growth in total cement production can offset those savings.
Land system change may be affected. Quarrying for clay adds new 
land disturbance even as limestone quarrying falls, so the net effect 
depends on where clay deposits are opened and whether sites are 
restored afterwards.
Atmospheric aerosol loading can fall because burning less coal and 
petroleum coke in cement kilns cuts particulate and sulfur emissions.

Virtual power plants and grids 
Coordinating batteries, cars 
and buildings as one power plant
02

Wherever air conditioning drives demand, the 
hottest days of the year bring the heaviest load 
on the grid. Utilities plan for those days using 
known relationships between heat and power 
consumption. Every added degree pulls a certain 
amount of extra power on to the grid, and that 
calculation tells a utility how much reserve capacity 
to hold and how many backup plants to keep 
ready. That relationship has grown steeper and less 
predictable over the past five years as both average 
temperatures and the frequency of heat waves 
climb. Grids now draw more power at the same 
temperature than they used to. Recent growth in 
electric-vehicle charging and data centre demand 
is adding to the pressure. When the combined load 
outruns the plan, the result is blackouts in some 
places and dirtier backup power in others.
Closing that gap increasingly relies on two kinds 
of software working together to coordinate the 
grid directly: virtual power plants and digital twins. 
A virtual power plant links real devices already in 
homes and businesses, such as home batteries, EV 
chargers and smart thermostats, into a single system 
a grid operator can call on directly.21 Each device 
constantly reports its own status, how charged 
a battery is and whether a car is plugged into a 
shared platform.22 When the grid needs power, that 
platform sends a single signal telling specific devices 
exactly how much to release and when,23 much 
like how a conductor cues different sections of an 
orchestra rather than shouting instructions to each 
musician separately. A digital twin works differently. 
It is a continuously updated model of the grid itself, 
fed by live data on how power is actually flowing, so 
operators can test an action like drawing down a set 
of batteries or rerouting a line to see what it would 
do to the system before it happens in reality.24 Where 
a virtual power plant acts, a digital twin predicts.
Recent breakthroughs have made it possible to treat 
distributed devices as a grid resource rather than 
just a pilot experiment. For years, a home battery 
or an EV sitting in a driveway faced two limits as a 
grid resource. Storage was too expensive to deploy 
at meaningful scale, and the software meant to 
coordinate thousands of devices at once was too 
slow to trust with a real-time grid event. Both limits 
broke within the same year. Battery pack prices for 
stationary storage fell 45% in 2025 to an all-time 
low of $70 per kilowatt-hour (KWh) – the sharpest 
drop of any battery category, for the first time 
making them cheaper than electric-vehicle packs.25 
Coordination software caught up on the same 
timeline, moving from updating every few seconds 
or minutes to responding in under a second.26
California showed what a virtual power plant can 
already do. In July 2025, three utilities coordinated 
more than 100,000 home batteries into a single 
two-hour pilot test, delivering enough power to run 
hundreds of thousands of homes during a peak-
demand event.27 In Germany, Next Kraftwerke’s 
platform now controls more capacity than a dozen 
full-sized power plants, without owning one.28 
Digital twins are already preventing blackouts, but 
they exist as isolated tools rather than a single, 
all-knowing simulation. For example, at the asset 
level, the New York Power Authority runs live digital 
twins to monitor the health of physical hardware, 
tracking vibrations and temperatures to catch failing 
turbines or transformers days before they cause 
outages.29 Managing the wider grid has traditionally 
relied on decades-old, static what-if models known 
as contingency analyses, but the Department of 
Energy (DOE) is already planning the transition from 
these legacy, snapshot-based models to a true 
system-level digital twin, a live simulation capable of 
modelling grid behaviour in real time while linking it 
to the micro-physics of power plants.30
Virtual power plants and grids reach full scale when 
the two tools work as one system, with a digital 
twin testing each move a virtual power plant makes 
before the grid feels it. Grid operators need to open 
their markets so that households and businesses 
are paid for the power their devices supply, just 
as a power plant is. Some power markets already 
pay these devices directly, while others have set 
opening dates years after regulators first required 
it. Before a digital twin can rehearse a bigger virtual 
power plant action, it needs a live, shared picture 
of the grid to test that action against, built on data 
every utility describes in the same way. That data-
sharing layer is what grid operators are now piloting. 
With those two pieces put together, a digital twin 
could check a virtual power plant’s next move 
before it happens, rather than after.
Every large-scale test of virtual power plant software 
so far has been a planned event, a scheduled pilot 
or a forecast peak. None has yet faced a failure like 
the April 2025 blackout, which cut power across 
Spain and Portugal without warning. Holding a grid 
together through one would mean relying on devices, 
and data, owned by millions of households.

10

Equity
Virtual power plants link household and industrial equipment, 
so participants can shift when they use or supply electricity. 
Digital twins use live data to create a virtual copy of the grid, helping 
utilities detect faults, plan upgrades and connect more renewable 
energy without building as much extra capacity. Together, these 
tools can reduce outages and lower costs while creating jobs in 
installation, software and operations. However, these gains come 
with their own conditions. Customers need equipment that can 
be controlled remotely, smart meters, reliable internet, fair contracts 
and privacy guarantees. Utilities need sensors, compatible 
data systems and trained staff. Public funding, protections for 
renters and transparent payments are essential to ensure that 
these systems drive an increased uptake of renewable energy 
and benefit poorer and rural communities, not just households 
already equipped with costly batteries and electric vehicles.
Security
Virtual power plants can make it easier to use renewable energy 
at scale, reducing reliance on imported fuels, gas-fired plants 
used during periods of high demand and single large power 
stations. Digital twins also support resilience by allowing operators 
to simulate extreme events, identify weak points and test repairs 
without disrupting the live grid. However, both systems depend 
on communications networks, sensors, cloud services and 
software. False data, cyberattacks or inaccurate models could lead 
to poor decisions affecting many parts of the grid at once. Systems 
that rely heavily on batteries may also remain dependent on a small 
number of battery manufacturers. True resilience requires secure 
data sharing, compatible systems and manual backups if networks 
fail or suppliers stop providing support.
Planetary boundaries
Climate change pressure can be reduced when digital tools 
help integrate renewables and reduce fossil fuel backups. 
However, the net benefit depends on the generation displaced, 
storage losses and the additional energy and materials required 
for computing and communications.
Ocean acidification can be reduced indirectly when virtual power 
plants and digital twins help replace fossil-fuel generation with 
renewables, reducing the amount of CO2 absorbed by the oceans.
Biosphere integrity can be affected in different ways. Digital tools 
may reduce the need for new power plants and grid infrastructure, 
but producing batteries, sensors and servers requires mining 
and creates waste.

Abundant materials batteries
Everyday metals for a more 
secure energy supply
03

Winter is hard on clean power grids. A spell of calm, 
overcast weather can last for days, leaving wind 
turbines idle and solar panels under cloud just as 
long, cold nights push electricity demand up. Most 
batteries on these grids hold two to four hours of 
power. Once they run out, gas and coal plants tend 
to fill the gap.
Grid batteries are now being installed faster 
than ever, and most of them use lithium-ion, 
a technology engineers built to be small and 
light – tuned for phones and cars, where every 
gram of weight costs range.31 Grid storage does 
not share that weight constraint, and dropping 
it opens the door to batteries built from entirely 
different materials. This is why battery makers 
are increasingly turning to sodium, zinc and iron, 
elements abundant enough to let chemistry diversify 
along two axes at once: what a battery is built to do, 
and where its materials come from. On the functional 
side, that means trading weight for the property 
that matters on-site, whether that’s cold tolerance, 
fire safety or multi-day duration. On the materials 
side, sodium, zinc and iron are common enough to 
source almost anywhere,32 unlike lithium and cobalt, 
whose supply chains are narrow and concentrated. 
Choosing these minerals also reduces dependence 
on the handful of countries that currently control 
today’s scarcer materials and eases the pressure 
on land and water near extraction sites. 
Sodium, zinc and iron have each been known 
battery candidates for decades;33 sodium-ion 
research alone predates the smartphone.34 
Getting them out of the lab has taken years 
of manufacturing build-out. Production lines 
have gone in, yields have improved, and costs 
have come down,35 in one case backed by a 
$305.3 million federal loan guarantee.36 As a result, 
CATL (Contemporary Amperex Technology), Eos 
Energy and Great River Energy have each brought 
projects online at the scale of 1 gigawatt-hour 
(GWh) in the past two years. They are building a 
portfolio of batteries, each suited to a different job, 
rather than betting on one winning chemistry.
A sodium ion is larger and holds a weaker grip 
on its electrons than a lithium ion, so it keeps 
moving through a battery even as the surrounding 
chemistry slows in the cold. The newest commercial 
cells hold on to more than 90% of their charge at 
-40°C,37 a temperature that stalls most other battery 
chemistries. However, that mobility has a cost: 
a sodium ion carries less charge for its size than 
lithium, which means that a sodium battery stores 
about 30% less energy in the same volume.38 That’s 
enough to rule sodium out of long-range electric 
vehicles, but doesn’t matter for a fixed installation. 
A 60 GWh supply agreement signed by a major 
Chinese battery company in 2026, the largest 
sodium-ion order to date, shows how fast that 
market is moving.39 
Zinc solves a different problem – fire. A lithium-ion 
battery catches fire because its electrolyte, the fluid 
ions move through, is flammable. A zinc battery 
replaces that fluid with water, removing the fuel a 
fire needs to spread.40 The trade is energy density. 
Water can’t hold as much charge as the liquid it 
replaces, which keeps zinc’s energy density too 
low for compact uses.41 With ample energy density 
for stationary uses, the US zinc battery maker Eos 
Energy is building a 400 megawatt-hour (MWh) 
system for Marine Corps Base Camp Pendleton, 
where fire risk is not a trade-off the base is willing to 
make.42
Iron-air trades on cost rather than performance. 
The battery runs on rust. Discharging lets iron 
oxidize and release electrons; charging reverses the 
reaction and pulls the oxygen back out.43 Iron costs 
almost nothing,44 so iron-air batteries can be built to 
hold a charge for around 100 hours45 – long enough 
to carry a grid through days of dead calm or heavy 
cloud. The trade is efficiency. Charging diverts 
some current into a side reaction that splits water 
into hydrogen, energy that never comes back,46 
holding recharge efficiency to about half of what a 
typical battery achieves.47 A Minnesota utility, Great 
River Energy, has brought the first commercial iron-
air system online, backed by an 80 GWh pipeline

Equity
Zinc-based, iron-air and sodium-ion batteries could expand access to 
electricity storage in different ways. Sodium-ion batteries can manage 
daily changes in solar power, water-based zinc batteries can provide 
safer storage, and iron-air batteries can supply power during cloudy or 
windless periods. However, all three technologies require factories, 
grid connections, supporting equipment and trained technicians. 
Iron-air batteries also need large sites, and all three remain relatively 
expensive to produce at scale. Government purchasing, long-term 
investment and local training will determine whether communities 
gain reliable electricity and jobs or simply import finished systems, 
reinforcing today’s dominant battery hubs.
Security
While different types contribute to supply chain diversification, they 
reduce different vulnerabilities. Sodium-ion batteries can reduce 
demand for lithium and use many existing battery factories. However, 
their key materials and production are still concentrated in a few 
countries. Zinc-based batteries rely on widely traded metals, and 
water-based designs are less likely to catch fire. However, some 
require high-quality zinc, manganese, bromine or specialized 
components. Iron-air batteries use widely available iron and can 
store electricity for up to four days. This could reduce the need for 
gas-fired power when renewable energy is unavailable. However, 
the technology is still new and depends on large sites, electronic 
equipment and software, creating potential new vulnerabilities.

Climate change pressure can fall when sodium-ion batteries manage 
daily changes in electricity use, iron-air batteries provide longer-term

Extracting those materials and producing these batteries still creates 
emissions, but far less than the fossil fuels they displace.
Biosphere integrity pressures may shift as iron-air and many 
zinc-based and sodium-ion batteries avoid lithium and cobalt. 
However, mining iron, zinc and manganese can still damage habitats. 
Net effects depend on material quantities, sourcing locations, battery 
lifetime and recycling.

avoid water-intensive lithium supply chains, but mining and processing 
zinc, iron and manganese also require water. The outcome depends 
on local water availability, extraction methods and material demand

creates additional risks for ecosystems and communities.
Ocean acidification can be reduced indirectly when zinc-based, 
iron-air and sodium-ion batteries help renewable electricity replace 
fossil fuels, reducing CO2 entering the oceans. Emissions from 
manufacturing reduce, but don’t outweigh, this benefit.
Novel entity pressure may ease with water-based zinc and iron-air 
designs, which avoid the flammable, fluorinated electrolytes used in 
lithium-ion batteries. Sodium-ion cells still rely on similar fluorinated 
salts and binders.

Thermal energy 
storage (heat batteries)
Storing clean power as 
heat until it’s needed
04

Making steel, cement or chemicals means keeping 
a furnace at extreme heat around the clock, and 
most of that heat still comes from burning gas or 
coal. Fuel suits the job because it can be stored 
and available on demand. Solar and wind now 
produce some of the cheapest electricity available, 
but only when the weather allows. Chemical 
batteries can deliver that power for a few hours, but 
keeping a furnace running for days would require so 
much battery capacity that generating heat this way 
would cost more than burning gas.
Instead of relying on rare, temperamental minerals 
like lithium and cobalt, thermal energy storage, 
often referred to as a heat battery, routes electricity 
through low-cost, durable elements with high 
heat resistance, such as sand, firebrick, carbon 
blocks or molten salt. These cores soak up thermal 
energy at temperatures ranging from 1,000°C to an 
incandescent 2,400°C, depending on the design,53,54 
and hold that energy for hours or days, discharging 
it on demand as high-pressure steam or scorching 
air directly into factory boilers.55 The energy doesn’t 
need to be consumed the millisecond it’s generated. 
It gets locked in as heat and tapped whenever the 
furnace demands it.
As more renewable capacity has come online, 
the moments when power is nearly free, or even 
costs money to offload, have become routine on 
many grids. A heat battery charges during those 
hours and stores energy until the furnace needs 
it. Within about three years, heat batteries have 
gone from small pilot projects to installations 
running at nearly a thousand times that scale – a 
speed few other pieces of industrial hardware can 
match.56,57 Regulation is starting to catch up too. 
The European Commission’s Electrification Action 
Plan proposes cutting network charges for industrial 
users who shift their power use to off-peak hours, 
narrowing the gap that has long kept gas cheaper 
than electricity.58
Thermal energy storage is already running inside 
operating factories across a range of industrial 
sectors, financed increasingly by mainstream 
energy companies and public development 
banks. In Germany, Rondo Energy is building a 
100 MWh battery for Covestro, a major chemicals 
manufacturer, backed by €75 million from the 
European Investment Bank and Breakthrough 
Energy Catalyst.59 In Hungary, a corn-processing 
plant that had run on gas for years switched 
to heat batteries in 2025, without paying any 
upfront capital cost. Kyoto Group built the 
battery, owns it and takes on the risk if anything 
goes wrong. The plant pays for the heat it uses 
the same way it always paid its gas bill, under 
a 15-year agreement.60
Brick, sand, molten salt and carbon blocks all 
reliably hold and release industrial-grade heat, 
but every factory requires heat to be delivered 
differently. One might need steam while another 
needs blistering hot air, so each installation is still 
its own custom engineering job rather than an off-
the-shelf product.61 Even once those engineering 
challenges are addressed, another barrier 
remains: electricity markets were not designed 
for how heat batteries operate. Most industrial 
tariffs are still built for steady, predictable demand, 
while a heat battery draws power in fast, flexible 
bursts whenever electricity is cheap. Today’s 
pricing rules were never designed for that 
pattern. For heat batteries to scale, regulators 
will need to go beyond tariff cuts and introduce 
binding policy.62 
A heat battery doesn’t need fossil fuel to keep 
the furnace running around the clock, just a grid 
handing off more electricity than it can use when 
the battery is ready to bank it as heat. Furnaces 
burn roughly a fifth of the energy the world uses, 
and nearly all of it comes from fuel shipped, piped 
or mined in from somewhere else.63 Electrifying 
that share would simultaneously reduce industrial 
emissions, improve energy security and create 
a new source of flexible demand for increasingly 
renewable electricity systems.
 The energy 
doesn’t need 
to be consumed 
the millisecond 
it’s generated. 
It gets locked 
in as heat and 
tapped whenever 
the furnace 
demands it.

Equity
Heat batteries store cheap electricity or waste heat and 
provide a steady heat supply for factories and other industrial 
facilities. Simple materials such as sand, brick and concrete 
can cost much less than chemical batteries and help users 
avoid expensive peak-hour electricity prices. This could 
improve the competitiveness of energy-intensive industries 
while preserving skilled industrial jobs. However, these 
benefits will not be shared equally without support. Projects 
need investment, strong grid connections, good insulation, 
heat-transfer equipment and trained workers. Smaller 
manufacturers and low-income households may need 
targeted funding and fairer electricity rates for lower system 
costs to reduce their bills.
Security
Heat batteries provide a buffer when electricity supply is 
uneven. They store extra wind and solar power or waste 
heat and release it when electricity is scarce. This can reduce 
peak demand and reliance on imported gas. Systems using 
sand, brick or concrete also avoid many minerals needed 
for chemical batteries and can be built close to users in 
many regions. However, some risks remain. Electric heating 
depends on reliable grids, control systems, insulation and 
heat-transfer equipment. Molten salts and other specialized 
materials must be safely contained and maintained by trained 
workers. During a prolonged blackout, a heat battery can 
support only the connected equipment and only until its 
stored heat is used.

reducing CO2 entering the oceans. 
Atmospheric aerosol loading can fall where heat batteries 
replace fuel burned on-site in industrial boilers, especially coal, 
thereby cutting particulate and sulfur emissions close to where 
people live and work.

Perovskite-silicon 
tandem solar cells
Converting more of the 
sun’s light into electricity
05

Getting a new solar installation permitted, 
connected and built can take years. For a country 
trying to build more solar power, especially where 
space is limited, getting more electricity from 
each new solar site can be valuable. Right now, 
silicon’s own physics sets the limit for whether that’s 
possible. Even under ideal, theoretical conditions, 
a conventional single-junction silicon solar cell can 
convert no more than about 29% of the sunlight it 
receives into electricity.64 
A perovskite-silicon tandem cell breaks through 
that wall by putting two solar cells to work instead 
of one. Picture two sieves stacked on top of each 
other, each with a different mesh size. The top 
layer, a manufactured crystal called perovskite, 
catches the high-energy blue and green light.65 
Whatever slips through it, mostly red and infrared 
light, falls on to an ordinary silicon cell underneath, 
which catches that instead.66 Working together, 
the two layers can realistically reach 42–43% 
efficiency.67 Oxford PV says its tandem panels 
already generate up to 20% more energy than a 
standard silicon panel from the same site.68 How 
the cell gets manufactured also matters. Beyond 
operational efficiency gains, perovskites could 
also change how solar cells are manufactured. 
Growing silicon crystal requires high-temperature 
furnaces and substantial energy, while perovskites 
can be deposited as a thin coating directly on to 
a finished silicon cell using industrial techniques 
such as evaporation or printing.69 They also require 
only small quantities of their active materials, 
potentially reducing reliance on the energy-
intensive production of high-purity silicon. This 
technology can plug into an industry that already 
manufactures at enormous volume, rather than 
requiring a new one.
Until recently, only fingernail-sized tandem cells, 
built at lab scale, could beat silicon’s limit. A cell 
of that size first broke past it in 2020, and by 
2025, the record had climbed to 34.85%.70,71 
Reproducing that result at commercial size was a 
separate challenge, and manufacturers only solved 
it in 2026. A cell scaled up to roughly the size of a 
small tablet still reached 30% efficiency, just short 
of the lab record.72 Investors did not wait for further 
experimentation. Swift Solar committed $27 million 
to build its first US tandem factory, years before 
a full-sized panel, around the size of a twin bed, 
performed anywhere near the lab number.73
That bet has already produced concrete outcomes. 
Oxford PV has shipped commercial tandem panels 
to a US utility customer, and GCL Optoelectronics 
has switched on a factory in Kunshan, China, built 
to produce them at gigawatt scale.74,75 A single 
customer’s first order became industrial-scale 
supply within about a year.
Solar technology durability and longevity 
factor heavily in energy and financial efficiency 
calculations. Independent modelling suggests 
a smaller tandem system could still match an 
equivalent silicon system’s total lifetime output, but 
only if the panels last roughly 25 years outdoors,76 
well past what today’s accelerated ageing tests can 
confirm.77 That 25-year threshold is the number 
to watch. Once testing can credibly project past 
it, banks can underwrite the technology instead of 
merely watching it. Two things stand in the way: 
1.	 Manufacturers still have to iron out defects, 
including pinholes and uneven residue, that 
show up when the coating process moves 
from a small lab sample to a full-sized panel.78 
2.	 Testing bodies need to publish a shared ageing 
standard specific to perovskite, one that reflects 
the heat, ultraviolet light and dust of the hot, 
dry regions where much of the world’s new 
solar is being built.
Squeezing more power from land already secured 
is one of the things this technology has to prove. 
The physics is increasingly working. The remaining 
question is whether manufacturers, lenders and 
regulators can prove the technology durable enough 
to become a mainstream part of the solar industry.

Perovskite–silicon tandem panels capture more sunlight than 
standard silicon panels, producing more electricity from the same 
roof or piece of land. This could lower costs for households and 
businesses with limited space and improve access to reliable 
electricity when combined with storage or flexible electricity use. 
Manufacturing could also create skilled jobs in panel production,

to finance. Without affordable loans for lower-income households,

Perovskite–silicon tandem panels shift some energy-security risks 
from fuel supply to manufacturing. Because each panel produces 
more electricity, they can reduce pressure on land, equipment 
and limited grid connections. Smaller, distributed solar installations 
can also reduce fuel imports and reliance on large power plants. 
However, tandem panels cannot consistently produce electricity 
as sunlight changes. Reliable supply still depends on batteries, 
flexible electricity use and strong grids. The technology also 
remains dependent on a concentrated silicon supply chain, 
and adds specialized coatings, precise quality control and lead 
management. Until the panels are proven to last for decades 
outdoors, early deterioration could reduce their reliability, make 
them harder to finance and lower their expected electricity output.

Advanced subsea 
HVDC interconnectors
Sharing surplus power 
across seas and borders
06

Across the world, clean electricity is increasingly 
abundant in the wrong place at the wrong time. 
Wind output often peaks overnight, when demand 
is lowest; solar output peaks at midday, hours 
before the evening surge in use. When a region has 
nowhere to send its surplus, or nowhere to draw 
from when its own generation dips, grid operators 
throttle back turbines, disconnect panels and leave 
hydropower reservoirs full. The limiting factor sits in 
the wires between one grid and the next.
A subsea high-voltage direct current (HVDC) 
interconnector helps direct that surplus to where 
it can be used. A cable running along the seabed 
links two national grids, so a country can export 
power when it has more than it needs and import 
it when it doesn’t. At each end, a converter station 
changes the grid’s alternating current (AC) into 
direct current (DC) for the crossing, then changes it 
back on arrival. Over distances this long, AC would 
waste much of its energy repeatedly charging 
the cable itself, while DC avoids that loss.79 While 
single links have connected pairs of countries for 
decades, recent advances have enabled higher-
voltage cables to connect multiple countries into 
shared networks.
Raising the voltage depends on the insulation 
wrapped around the cable’s copper core. Newer 
forms of insulation use a tougher, heat-resistant 
plastic, lined with thin layers that stop electric 
charge from building up inside it. This allows cables 
to operate at higher temperatures and enables 
a single cable to carry more power, which can 
reduce the number of parallel cables required for 
a project.80 One cable can now carry roughly the 
output of two large coal-fired power plants, more 
than twice what the previous generation carried. 
That extra capacity is starting to make shared 
cable networks possible, and regulators have 
begun replacing project-by-project connections 
with shared infrastructure planned in advance.81
Cables now reach far enough to connect places 
with significantly different weather and resources. 
Viking Link, which opened at the end of 2023, 
runs under the North Sea from Denmark to 
England, helping both countries balance supply 
and demand as renewable generation fluctuates.82 
Tunisia and Italy have now contracted the first 
HVDC cable between Europe and North Africa, 
which could open a route into Europe for Tunisia’s 
solar power.83
Two things need to happen at once for HVDC 
to scale. Shipbuilders and cable-layers need to 
keep adding capacity to lay all this cable, since 
orders are already outpacing what the industry 
can build and install.84 Manufacturers also need 
to prove that equipment from different companies 
can work together on the same line, so a fault in 
one place doesn’t take down the whole system.85 
The milestone to watch is the first time the 
technology is proven on a live, multi-country grid, 
followed by a contract that actually requires open 
competition among suppliers. Regulators are 
moving in the same direction. The UK’s energy 
regulator approved its first shared, multi-purpose 
interconnector in 2024, instead of treating each 
new cable as its own one-off project.86
Whether subsea HVDC becomes the connective 
tissue of an integrated offshore electricity system or 
remains a patchwork of bilateral links depends on 
whether interoperability can be demonstrated over 
the next few years.

Equity
Subsea HVDC connectors allow regions to trade electricity 
over long distances. They can expand access to cheaper 
power, reduce price spikes and create skilled jobs in ports, 
cable installation and grid operation. However, lower energy 
bills are not guaranteed. Projects require substantial long-

specialized workers. Regulators determine whether the 
benefits go mainly to consumers or investors, and whether 
coastal communities benefit from hosting cable landings. 
Lower-income markets may need public guarantees, 
fair pricing and training to participate. In addition, coastal 
communities and fishing groups should participate in 
route selection and decisions on livelihood protection 
and benefit sharing.
Security
Subsea HVDC connectors can reduce reliance on imported 
fuels by connecting countries to neighbouring power 
systems. They allow regions to share backup capacity, 
reduce the impact of local shortages and balance wind and 
solar power across areas with different weather conditions. 
This can reduce dependence on gas and the need for 
backup plants, but it also creates new vulnerabilities beneath 
the sea. A damaged cable or converter station can remove a 
large amount of electricity, while repair ships and replacement 
equipment are limited. Cross-border electricity flows also 
depend on secure digital systems, compatible rules and 
political trust. A network with several connections offers 
greater flexibility, while reliance on a few large cables can 
create strategic weak points.

and laying pairs close together, so their magnetic fields partly 
cancel, reduces that exposure. Marine spatial planning, 
routing that avoids sensitive habitats, appropriate installation 
methods and monitoring of cumulative effects are needed 
to limit these pressures.

cables replace fossil-fuel generation and reduce the amount 
of CO2 absorbed by the ocean. The benefit depends on the 
source of the electricity being traded.

Modular nuclear reactors
Building nuclear power in 
smaller, repeatable units
07

Most places need power that runs every hour, 
whether or not the wind is blowing or the sun is out. 
Today, one of the easiest ways to guarantee that 
continuity is a gas plant that will continue burning gas 
for the next thirty years. Modular nuclear reactors 
are a bet that a cleaner option can be built fast 
and cheaply enough to compete with that default.
Every nuclear reactor follows the same basic 
process. Splitting uranium releases heat, the 
heat turns water into steam, and the steam spins 
a turbine that generates electricity – the same 
process a coal or gas plant uses, with fission 
replacing combustion.87 The idea behind a modular 
reactor is to build that same machine in the same 
way as a manufactured product. Standardized 
parts come off a factory line, ship to a site and are 
assembled there, the way a modular home goes up 
in weeks instead of the months a site-built house 
takes. That approach splits into two families. Most 
small modular reactors adapt the water-cooled 
designs that already run most of the world’s nuclear 
fleet, scaled down to roughly a third the output of 
a conventional plant. Microreactors shrink the idea 
further, small enough that some are designed to 
ship in a handful of standard containers.88
Small modular reactors have moved from drawings 
to working plants. Russia has operated a floating 
plant in its Arctic since 2020, and China has 
operated a demonstration plant since 2023.89 
The first in North America is under construction 
at Ontario Power Generation’s Darlington site in 
Canada, where four identical reactors are planned.90 
Technology companies that need round-the-clock 
power for data centres are now paying to bring 
more online, with Amazon backing X-energy91 
and Google contracting with Kairos Power.92
The microreactor side of the bet is being tested 
differently. A US Department of Defense programme 
called Project Pele has finished building a 
transportable reactor and delivered its first full load 
of fuel to a national laboratory, aiming to generate 
electricity by 2028, a target that has already slipped 
three years from its original schedule.93 For it to 
replace the diesel generator that would otherwise 
power a base or mine with no other option, it has 
to prove that it can run reliably enough, for long 
enough, without refuelling.
To help settle the bet, reactor builders need to 
show that costs fall as they build more of the same 
design. NuScale’s first planned US plant collapsed 
in 2023 when the expected price of its electricity 
climbed by more than half and the Utah towns that 
had signed up to buy it pulled out. Ontario Power 
Generation expects its fourth Darlington reactor 
to cost a third as much as its first. Governments 
need to fund a domestic supply chain for the more 
highly enriched uranium fuels needed by many 
microreactor designs. The US has already started, 
since the country doesn’t yet produce this fuel at 
commercial scale.94 At least one dedicated fuel-
production line is already under construction, with 
the goal of opening before the end of the decade.95
To accelerate project timelines, regulators are also 
starting to allow later projects to reuse already-
approved reactor designs, skipping a full review 
for each new project. 
If costs do fall, modular reactors could be built by 
the hundreds by 2050.96 That would bring reactors 
to many communities that have never hosted them, 
often displacing fossil fuel plants while creating new 
challenges around waste storage and disposal.

Equity
Nuclear microreactors could replace costly diesel at mines and 
in remote communities. Small modular reactors using familiar 
pressurized-water designs could provide electricity and heat to 
smaller grids and industrial sites. Both could create skilled jobs 
and reduce exposure to fuel-price swings, but neither guarantees 
affordable energy. Microreactors may need specialized fuel, transport 
arrangements and trained operators. Small modular reactors still 
require cooling systems, grid connections and supporting nuclear 
infrastructure. Early projects face high financing and licensing costs, 
and factory production will reduce costs only after many units are 
built. Public funding, community consent, local training and fair 
pricing will determine who benefits.
Security
Nuclear microreactors could keep essential facilities running during 
grid failures and replace diesel dependencies with reactors that 
operate for years. Small modular reactors could add generating 
capacity gradually while using well-established reactor skills and 
fuel supplies. However, they change rather than eliminate security 
risks. Many microreactors require more highly enriched uranium 
or specialized fuels made by only a few suppliers. Small modular 
reactors also depend on a concentrated global system for processing 
and enriching uranium. More reactor sites could increase exposure 
to physical attacks, cyberattacks and failures in safety controls. 
Resilience will require diverse fuel suppliers, secure transport, well-
trained operators, credible plans for handling nuclear waste and 
strong international regulations, including safeguards for maintaining 
nuclear safety during conflicts.
Planetary boundaries
Climate change pressure can decline when microreactors replace 
diesel and small modular reactors replace coal or gas. Uranium mining, 
fuel processing and construction still produce emissions, but these are 
far outweighed by the benefits over the reactors’ lifetimes.
Biosphere integrity pressures may decrease where reactor types 
better avoid habitat conversion and fossil-fuel extraction compared 
with the electricity sources they replace. However, uranium mining, 
multiple reactor sites, heated water discharges and rare accidents can 
still damage habitats.
Freshwater change depends on the reactor design, cooling method 
and local water availability. Some microreactor designs can reject 
heat without cooling-water withdrawals, while water-cooled systems 
vary in their withdrawals and consumption. Heat and drought can 
constrain cooling and electricity output, making climate-resilient siting 
and cooling design important.
Ocean acidification can be reduced indirectly as both reactor 
types replace fossil-fuel generation and reduce CO2 emissions. 
However, slow or expensive deployment could delay these benefits. 
Novel entities include radioactive waste. Small modular reactors 
use established fuel types, while many microreactors may use 
newer fuels that require different systems for treatment, transport 
and disposal. 
Land system change pressure can decline because nuclear 
power needs less land per unit of electricity than almost any 
other source, although uranium mines and waste storage sites 
still occupy land.
Atmospheric aerosol loading can fall where microreactors replace 
diesel generators at mines, military bases and remote communities, 
cutting soot and other particulate emissions.

Building-integrated photovoltaics
Generating electricity from 
roofs, walls and windows
08

A city’s rooftops are among its largest unused 
surfaces. When a solar developer looks for land to 
build new generating capacity, they typically look 
past the city towards farmland at its edge, even 
though the roof of the building next door receives 
the same sunlight. Cities occupy just 2–3% of the 
world’s land yet drive 70–80% of its energy use.97
Building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPV) put a 
photovoltaic layer to work as a roof, façade or 
even window.98 Manufacturers laminate silicon cells 
directly into the glass, metal or polymer a roof or 
façade would need anyway and wire the whole 
assembly into the structure as the building goes up. 
The same principle extends to windows. A thinner, 
transparent photovoltaic layer can capture invisible 
infrared light while letting visible light through, so 
a window still lets daylight in even as it generates 
power.99 Colour is a separate engineering challenge, 
since a photovoltaic surface is naturally dark. 
Coatings can now bend light into colour using a 
microscopically textured surface, the same principle 
behind a butterfly wing’s blue. That avoids the paint 
or dye layer that would otherwise block light before 
it reaches the cell underneath, so manufacturers 
can colour-match a panel to a building’s brick 
or stone while it still generates power.100
Several recent developments are now helping turn 
BIPV from bespoke architectural projects into a 
repeatable construction product: a recognized 
international standard, faster prefabricated 
installation methods and new building regulations 
requiring solar-ready construction. Until recently, 
a BIPV panel had to satisfy electrical safety codes 
built for solar panels and, separately, fire, structural 
and weatherproofing codes built for roofs and 
walls, with no single standard covering both. 
A single new international standard now closes 
that gap, giving certifiers one set of requirements 
to check against.101 Assembly is catching up 
too. A prefabricated, insulated façade panel 
demonstrated in Germany in 2024 snaps into a 
building without a separate supporting frame and 
installs in under an hour and a half per panel, a 
fraction of the time a custom-built façade usually 
takes.102 The European Union has added its own 
push. New buildings there must now be solar-ready, 
with installation required starting in 2026.103
Two working buildings already show what BIPV 
construction looks like in practice. Copenhagen 
International School is clad in 12,000 panels of 
coloured solar glass, each set at a slightly different 
angle so the façade shifts between shades of green 
as the light changes. Together, they supply about 
half of the school’s electricity.104 At King’s Cross 
Station in London, the roof was already built and in 
daily use by roughly 40 million passengers a year 
when custom glass panels went in to cover part of 
the Grade I-listed structure (the UK’s highest level 
of heritage protection). The panels now generate 
roughly 10% of the station’s electricity, and the 
station never closed during installation.105
The remaining challenge is whether BIPV can 
be valued both as an energy technology and a 
construction material. A BIPV project is usually 
judged only on the electricity it produces, even 
though it’s also the roofing or façade a building 
needs anyway. Only 13% of BIPV projects make 
financial sense under electricity-only accounting. 
Counting the roofing or cladding they replaced as 
a cost saving pushes the share of viable projects 
to 62%.106 Manufacturers can also bring costs 
down by building BIPV at real volume. Real-world 
installations currently show only one in 10 BIPV 
projects using fully prefabricated construction,107 
even though a leading German research institute 
has already proven a system that works.108 If both 
pathways move together, BIPV could eventually 
cost about the same as a standard roof or façade 
with solar panels installed separately on top. 
Every building already standing is a potential 
power plant. BIPV turns existing roofs and walls 
into part of the energy system, without requiring 
new land. Whether BIPV remains a niche 
architectural feature or a standard part of new 
construction now depends less on the technology 
itself than on standards, manufacturing scale and 
how buildings are valued.

Equity
BIPV replace conventional building materials with solar panels 
that generate electricity from roofs, façades and windows. By 
producing power where it is used, they can reduce energy bills 
and ease pressure on electricity grids, particularly in dense cities 
with limited land. They can also create skilled local jobs in design, 
construction and installation, while giving architects and designers 
greater scope to integrate sustainability into their work. However, 
the benefits depend on upfront investment, building design, local 
sunlight, electricity prices and access to financing. Property owners 
typically receive most of the financial gains, while tenants may not 
benefit much unless leases or regulations ensure savings are shared. 
Affordable finance, incentives for social housing, clear standards 
and workers trained across construction and electrical trades 
are essential to broadening access.
Security
By generating electricity directly on buildings, BIPV can reduce 
reliance on imported fuels, large power plants and long-distance 
transmission lines. It can also help relieve pressure on local electricity 
grids during the day. However, most grid-connected systems 
stop operating during power outages unless they are paired with 
battery storage and suitable inverters. BIPV also depends on 
concentrated solar supply chains and connected inverters that 
introduce cybersecurity risks. Because the panels also serve as 
roofs or façades, failures can affect both electricity generation 
and the building itself. A diverse supplier base, secure technical 
standards and a skilled maintenance workforce will be critical 
to ensuring BIPV strengthens energy resilience.
Planetary boundaries
Climate change pressure can decrease when BIPV replaces 
both fossil-fuel electricity and conventional building materials. 
Manufacturing silicon, glass and other components still creates 
emissions, but these are far outweighed by the avoided emissions 
over BIPV’s lifetime.
Land-system change pressure may decrease where BIPV uses 
existing buildings and avoids conversion of forests or other natural 
vegetation for solar farms. 
Biosphere integrity can be benefitted by reducing the need to convert 
natural land for energy generation. However, mining silicon, silver and 
copper can still harm ecosystems unless supported by responsible 
sourcing and land restoration.
Freshwater change pressure may decrease when BIPV replaces 
water-intensive electricity generation. However, producing silicon, 
glass and metals still requires water and can shift water demand and 
pollution to manufacturing regions.
Ocean acidification can be reduced indirectly when solar panels built 
into buildings replace fossil-fuel electricity, reducing CO2 entering the 
oceans. Embodied emissions and lower output on shaded surfaces 
reduce this benefit, but don’t outweigh it.

Floating offshore wind
Turning strong deep-
sea winds into power
09

Most offshore wind turbines stand on foundations 
driven into the seafloor, the way pilings hold up a 
pier. The deeper the water, the taller and heavier 
that foundation has to be, and beyond about 60 
metres, the cost stops making economic sense. 
The strong, steady winds over deeper water mostly 
go untapped.
Floating offshore wind works the same way any 
turbine does, with blades catching the air and 
spinning a shaft, and the shaft driving a generator 
that produces current. The difference is what holds 
the turbine up. A floating turbine rests on a platform 
riding on the surface, held in place by mooring lines 
and anchors rather than a rigid connection to the 
bottom, the way a ship stays put at anchor rather 
than being built into the harbor floor.109 Replacing 
a rigid seabed foundation with a moored floating 
platform lets floating turbines reach water past 300 
meters deep110 – water that has held some of the 
world’s strongest offshore wind completely out of 
reach until now.111
Several technical solutions make this possible. 
Where the seabed drops away completely, 
engineers weigh a long, narrow spar cylinder at 
the bottom, letting it hang deep underwater so 
the turbine at the other end rides upright like a 
fishing bobber, no matter how rough the surface 
gets. A semi-submersible platform takes a different 
approach, distributing buoyancy and ballast across 
several connected floating columns for greater 
stability. Where the seabed can hold a taut line, 
anchor lines staked into the bottom pull a tension-
leg platform down tight instead, using buoyancy 
and tension together to limit movement, more 
like a tent pitched hard into the ground.112
The amount of steel and cable a floating wind 
project needs per megawatt produced is falling 
rapidly for two reasons. One large turbine can now 
generate what used to require several smaller ones. 
Building fewer turbines means building less of 
everything that holds them up, cutting total project 
costs 15 to 20%.113 Anchoring has also gotten 
smarter on its own. Platforms placed close enough 
together can now share a single anchor point 
instead of each one needing its own, cutting how 
much anchor hardware a project needs, regardless 
of turbine size.114 As a result, floating offshore wind 
has fallen down the cost curve, and multi-turbine 
floating arrays are now running at scale.
One of those arrays, WindFloat Atlantic, sits 
off Portugal on three floating platforms. It has 
produced power continuously for four years and, 
along the way, ridden out a storm with waves as 
tall as a six-story building and winds strong enough 
to flip a truck, without any damage.115 Off the coast 
of Norway, 11 floating turbines built by Equinor, 
the country’s state-controlled energy company, 
power five oil and gas platforms far out at sea. 
The platforms otherwise burn some of the gas 
they produce to make their own electricity, and the 
turbines are designed to supply about a third of it, 
and more when the wind is strong.116
To capture steady, strong winds blowing over the 
deep ocean at scale, the industry needs to coalesce 
around a small number of platform designs so 
certification bodies can publish shared standards 
for anchors and underwater power cables.117 
Once a design can be certified against a common 
standard, manufacturers can build the same platform 
repeatedly instead of engineering each project from 
scratch. That transition from bespoke engineering 
to repeatable manufacturing is what will determine 
whether floating offshore wind follows the same 
cost curve as fixed offshore wind. Ports also need 
to evolve. A floating platform is built and assembled 
onshore, then towed out to sea fully formed, so the 
port itself has to be large enough to construct and 
launch something that size, not just receive finished 
turbines. To address this issue, the UK is already 
building a dedicated assembly hub at one of its ports, 
due to open in 2027.118 Other governments with deep 
coastal water need ports built to the same scale.
Floating wind costs roughly 1.5 to 2 times what 
fixed-bottom offshore wind costs today. Forecasts 
now put those two costs on a converging path 
by around 2030 to 2032, contingent on the 
development of shared platform standards and 
factories beginning to build identical platforms at 
scale.119 Closing this gap on schedule is the real test.
Not every floating wind project announcement 
leads to actual construction. Some companies have 
already walked away from projects with government 
backing already secured, taking a payout instead of 
building anything at all.120 Whether floating offshore 
wind follows the cost curve of fixed offshore 
wind now depends less on engineering than on 
standardization, manufacturing capacity and the 
infrastructure needed to build projects at scale.

Equity
Floating wind turbines can use stronger winds farther offshore, 
providing new electricity for coastal regions and islands. 
They could diversify electricity supplies, reduce the impact of 
fuel-price increases and create jobs in ports, manufacturing 
and marine services. However, floating wind projects are currently 
more expensive than turbines fixed to the seabed. They also need 
upgraded ports, specialized ships, new connections to the grid 
and long-term investment. Electricity-market rules and pricing 
determine whether future cost reductions benefit households and 
businesses. Early consultation, local training and fair procurement 
can help ensure that coastal communities gain skilled jobs rather 
than mainly bearing disruption to fishing and other livelihoods.
Security
Floating wind can access large areas of deep water, reduce reliance 
on imported fuels and spread electricity generation across more 
locations. Because the turbines need no fuel to operate, they are 
less exposed to sudden changes in commodity prices. However, 
changing wind conditions still require flexible grids, batteries or other 
backup power. Some risks are also shifted offshore. Electricity may 
depend on underwater cables, sometimes with limited alternative 
routes. Repairs require suitable ports, specialized ships, favourable 
weather and trained crews. Turbines also require large amounts of 
steel and copper, while some use rare-earth magnets made through 
supply chains concentrated in a few countries. Resilience, therefore, 
depends on spare cables, repair capacity, diverse suppliers and 
coordinated grid planning.
Planetary boundaries
Climate change pressure can be reduced because floating wind 
produces few emissions over its lifetime and can replace fossil-fuel 
power. Producing steel, concrete and cables, as well as operating 
construction and repair ships, can still create emissions, but far less 
than the fossil fuels they replace. 
Biosphere integrity outcomes are site-specific. Floating turbines 
may reduce some seabed disturbance relative to fixed-bottom 
designs as they avoid pile-driving, one of the loudest sources 
of underwater noise and a major concern for porpoises, dolphins 
and whales. Colonization of their structures does not by itself 
establish a net biodiversity benefit. Mooring lines and dynamic 
cables suspended in the water column can catch lost fishing gear, 
creating an entanglement risk for whales and turtles, while anchors, 
vessel activity and turbine blades can affect habitats, fish and seabirds. 
Siting, cumulative effects, fisheries displacement and long-term 
monitoring must inform deployment.
Ocean acidification can be reduced indirectly when floating wind 
reduces CO2 emissions from electricity generation, provided it replaces 
fossil-fuel power rather than simply adding to total electricity supply.
Land system change pressure may decrease where floating wind 
avoids the conversion of forests or other natural vegetation for land-
based power generation. However, ports, substations and electricity 
lines can still take up coastal land and divide habitats.

Next-generation lithium batteries
Storing more energy in less weight
10

Lithium is light enough to float on water. That same 
lightness is why it holds more energy for its weight 
than any other practical battery material, and why 
it became the standard chemistry for nearly every 
battery built today. Engineers have pushed that 
design close to its limit. Getting more now means 
changing what’s inside the battery without giving up 
the energy density that made lithium the choice in 
the first place.
Next-generation lithium batteries keep lithium 
ions carrying charge between two electrodes and 
rebuild a different part of the battery around it. In a 
conventional cell, lithium ions slide in and out of the 
electrodes, always returning to the same fixed spot. 
One electrode is made mainly of graphite, while the 
other is made of a heavier compound containing 
lithium and other metals such as cobalt and nickel. 
A flammable liquid sitting between the electrodes 
carries those ions back and forth.121
Lithium-sulphur batteries replace the heavier 
electrode with sulphur and swap graphite for pure 
lithium metal. As the battery discharges, sulphur 
reacts with lithium and dissolves, much like ice 
melting into water. Charging reverses the reaction, 
and the sulphur reforms, much like water freezing 
back into ice. That direct reaction, dissolving and 
reforming rather than sliding in and out of a fixed 
spot, is what lets the cell pack more energy into 
the same space than a conventional design can.122 
Solid-state batteries leave the electrodes in place 
and target the liquid instead, replacing it with a 
solid material that conducts lithium ions but blocks 
electrons, and cannot catch fire the way the liquid 
can. Removing that liquid also clears the way for 
pure lithium metal in place of graphite, the same 
upgrade lithium-sulphur makes, from a different 
direction, without the fire risk.123 Both approaches 
pack more energy into the same weight of material, 
so a lighter battery can do the same job, or the 
same battery can do more.
In the past few years, coatings have begun to 
address the specific failure modes that kept 
next-generation lithium batteries locked in the lab 
for decades. In a solid-state cell, thin threads of 
metallic lithium called dendrites grow through the 
solid electrolyte during charging, much like tree 
roots finding and widening a crack in pavement, 
until they connect the two sides and short the 
battery. New coatings and layered materials now 
block those threads before they can spread.124 In 
a lithium-sulphur cell, the reaction at the sulphur 
electrode throws off a byproduct that drifts to the 
lithium side like silt settling into a pipe, coating it 
and cutting the battery’s life short. A coating tested 
at Argonne National Laboratory now traps that 
byproduct before it reaches the lithium side and has 
kept a test cell running for 700 charge cycles.125
Next-generation lithium batteries have already 
left the lab for the road, the sky and orbit. Lyten, 
a US-based battery start-up, has cells flying in 
commercial drones and has been selected for a 
demonstration aboard the International Space 
Station.126 Solid-state has moved further still, 
without leaving Earth. In September 2025, a cell 
built by QuantumScape and PowerCo powered 
a motorcycle around a demonstration circuit at 
a major auto show, charging from 10% to 80% in 
about the time it takes to fill a gas tank.127 Toyota 
and its battery supplier Idemitsu have gone a step 
further again, committing to solid-state cells in a 
production vehicle by 2027 or 2028 – the clearest 
signal yet of how soon this technology could reach 
an ordinary car.128
Lithium-sulphur will prove itself once it can hold 
far more charge than today’s batteries and survive 
years of daily charging. The coating that already 
protects a small lab cell needs to carry over into 
a full-size battery, with the same reliability it shows 
in the lab. Producers are now building and testing 
cells at that larger scale to prove it can work.129
Solid-state will prove itself once car factories can 
build it repeatedly at a cost close to that of today’s 
batteries. Getting there means mastering exact 
control over layer thickness, pressure and humidity 
– the kind of precision that turns one successful 
cell into thousands of identical ones. Every cell that 
comes off the line at that same quality closes the 
gap between today’s higher production cost and 
the price point automakers are building towards.130 
Both chemistries are converging on the same 
milestone. A factory that can build the exact same 
battery, flawlessly, run after run, is what turns a 
working cell into a technology ready for real scale.
Both routes retain a dependence on lithium. 
Lithium-sulphur cells avoid nickel and cobalt, 
while solid-state cells may still use cathodes 
containing these metals. Higher energy density 
can open new applications, potentially increasing 
total lithium demand. Where that deepening 
demand gets met, and under what conditions, 
is a question next-generation lithium batteries 
have only begun to raise.

Equity
Lithium-sulphur and solid-state batteries could store more 
energy in a smaller, lighter battery. This could extend the range 
of electric vehicles, improve backup power and reduce the 
amount of equipment needed for energy storage. They also 
have distinct advantages: lithium-sulphur batteries replace 
expensive nickel and cobalt with low-cost sulphur, while solid-
state batteries may be less likely to catch fire. However, none of 
these technologies guarantees lower electricity or vehicle costs. 
Lithium price, new manufacturing equipment, high levels of 
early production waste and uncertainty about cycle lives could 
keep costs high. Affordable financing, strong warranties, repair 
and recycling networks, skilled workers and clear manufacturer 
responsibilities will determine where the jobs and savings go.
Security
These batteries could reduce oil use and help store renewable 
electricity, but each technology has different supply risks. Lithium-
sulphur batteries avoid nickel and cobalt but still depend on lithium 
metal. Solid-state batteries may use fewer flammable liquids, but 
they require specialized materials and highly precise manufacturing. 
Both technologies still depend on lithium processing and battery 
production concentrated in a small number of countries. While 
circular models could help mitigate these dependencies, batteries 
that wear out quickly, have fragile internal components or produce 
too many defective units could still prove unreliable and expensive. 
Supplier diversity, common standards and battery-specific recycling 
systems will determine which technology can scale, and which can’t.
Planetary boundaries
Climate change pressure can be reduced if these batteries speed 
up the shift to electric transport and renewable electricity. Energy-
intensive materials, production waste and batteries that need early 
replacement could offset some of these gains, but not enough to 
reverse the overall benefit. 
Freshwater change pressure may increase where additional lithium 
extraction and processing alter water availability, particularly in water-
stressed regions. Impacts depend on the deposit, extraction method 
and local hydrology. Contamination can also harm ecosystems and 
communities, while recycling can reduce the need for new lithium.
Biosphere integrity can be harmed as new lithium mines take up land 
and disturb habitats. On the other hand, lithium-sulphur batteries may 
reduce the need for nickel and cobalt mining. The effect of solid-state 
batteries will depend on the materials eventually used at scale.
Novel entities remain a concern because lithium-sulphur batteries 
use liquid chemicals that can become hazardous waste, including 
the PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) family of persistent 
chemicals, which recent research has traced in the environment 
around battery manufacturing sites.131 Solid-state batteries may be 
safer because they use fewer flammable liquids, but they introduce 
other less familiar materials, including sulphides, oxides and polymers.
Ocean acidification can be reduced indirectly when advanced lithium 
batteries help renewable power and electric vehicles replace fossil 
fuels, reducing the amount of CO2 entering the oceans. Manufacturing 
emissions reduce this benefit, but not enough to reverse it.

Conclusion
The 10 technologies featured in this report offer 
benefits beyond emissions reduction. Together, 
they could strengthen resilience, improve energy 
security and reduce pressure on the planet’s natural 
systems. Their contribution depends on the systems 
they replace, where they are deployed and how 
impacts are managed across their full life cycles. 
Lower carbon emissions do not automatically imply 
lower pressure on every planetary boundary.
Today’s conditions make that promise even more 
compelling. Fuel markets are more volatile than they 
were a decade ago, mineral supply chains have 
concentrated in fewer hands, and more countries 
are choosing to generate power at home rather 
than depend on others for it. At the same time, 
energy infrastructure must withstand intensifying 
heat, drought, storms and coastal flooding. 
The technologies best positioned to benefit from 
that situation are the ones that trade a fragile 
dependency, an imported tanker, a single overseas 
supplier, for an energy source a country can build 
and hold on to itself, or share with neighbours on 
terms it can rely on.
That trade is real progress, but it rarely ends the 
story. Sodium and zinc can be sourced in many 
places on Earth, yet nearly all of the world’s 
sodium-ion battery cells are still built inside a single 
country’s factories. A grid coordinated by software 
can call on thousands of batteries and thermostats 
at once, solving a problem no fuel tank ever could, 
and simultaneously creating a single system a bad 
actor could disrupt at scale. Dependencies can be 
reduced but rarely removed entirely.
How a technology reaches scale carries its own 
version of the same trade-off. A building-integrated 
solar panel or a sodium-ion battery installation 
succeeds or fails one project at a time, so one 
weak deployment barely dents the average, and 
progress compounds across thousands of small 
wins even when no single one makes headlines. 
A modular reactor or a subsea cable is tested by 
a handful of projects – or sometimes only one. 
When that’s the case, the entire bet rides on that 
project succeeding, and a single failure (e.g. a 
cancelled reactor order or a stalled cable contract) 
can look like proof that the technology doesn’t 
work even when the underlying economics haven’t 
changed. These two paths put the risk in different 
places – spread thin across many small bets, or 
concentrated in a few large ones – and reward 
different kinds of patience accordingly.
Whichever bets pay off, the more important 
question will remain unanswered. Getting the 
technology right does not guarantee getting the 
outcome right. A solar façade may lower a building’s 
electricity bill, but tenants will not capture their share 
of the savings unless a lease or regulation distributes 
them differently. An offshore wind farm may create 
jobs, but they will not necessarily go to the fishing 
communities that use those waters unless local 
hiring is built into the contract. A cement plant using 
a lower-carbon blend may reduce costs for public 
buyers, but only if procurement rules assess cement 
on performance rather than habit. A battery factory 
that uses abundant, widely sourced minerals may 
still concentrate jobs in one country unless the 
investment agreement addresses how benefits will be 
shared. Communities also need a voice in siting and 
benefit-sharing decisions, protection of livelihoods 
and access to affordable energy. Every one of these 
decisions has to be made while the technology is 
still being built, not once it is already running.
The same test will apply to whichever technologies 
join this list next. Enhanced geothermal offers a 
domestic, always-on source of power that AI-
assisted exploration is making steadily cheaper to 
find, though it featured in last year’s edition and was 
set aside on that basis rather than its merits. Cooling 
already accounts for more than a third of electricity 
demand in some tropical economies, a share only 
growing as cities warm, but the most cutting-edge 
approaches, such as solid-state cooling, remain too 
early-stage to offer a credible path to deployment by 
2030. Nuclear fusion could reshape energy systems 
entirely if it ever closes the gap that still separates 
it from commercial reality. Each will eventually face 
the same question that the 10 in this report already 
do: what dependencies will it create and who will 
decide where that dependency lands?
The window to answer those questions is short. 
Every technology in this report was chosen for a 
credible path to deployment by 2030,132 so the 
contracts, standards and siting choices that will 
settle those questions are being made now. Energy 
produces roughly three-quarters of the world’s 
greenhouse gas emissions, and those emissions 
will keep adding up for as long as today’s systems 
stay in place.

Appendix 1: Planetary 
boundaries primer
The planetary boundaries framework identifies 
nine critical Earth system processes that regulate 
the stability and resilience of the planet. Scientists 
have defined safe operating limits for each of 
these processes to ensure a stable environment 
for humanity. 
Biosphere integrity (boundary crossed) measures 
the health and diversity of ecosystems and species. 
Loss of biodiversity undermines the resilience of 
Earth’s systems and the benefits they provide.
Climate change (boundary crossed) refers to 
the increase in the concentration of greenhouse 
gases in the atmosphere, primarily CO2. Crossing 
this boundary increases the risk of severe and 
irreversible climate impacts.
Freshwater change (boundary crossed) concerns 
the consumption and management of freshwater 
resources. Overuse can lead to water scarcity 
and ecosystem degradation.
Land-system change (boundary crossed) 
involves the conversion of forests, grasslands 
and other natural habitats into agricultural 
or urban areas. Excessive land-use change 
disrupts ecosystems and carbon storage.
Modification of biogeochemical flows (boundary 
crossed) tracks the flow of nutrients, especially 
nitrogen and phosphorus, from fertilizers into the 
environment. Excessive flows cause water pollution 
and dead zones in oceans and lakes.
Novel entities (boundary crossed) covers the 
release of new substances, such as synthetic 
chemicals, plastics and elements whose 
abundances have been altered by humans, 
including heavy metals. These can have unknown 
or harmful effects on living organisms and 
ecosystems and contribute to climate change, 
atmospheric aerosol loading and stratospheric 
ozone depletion.
Ocean acidification (boundary crossed) 
results from increased CO2 dissolving in oceans, 
lowering pH levels. This threatens marine life, 
especially organisms with calcium carbonate 
shells or skeletons.
Atmospheric aerosol loading refers to the 
presence of tiny particles in the atmosphere 
from pollution, dust or natural sources. High 
concentrations affect climate and human health.
Stratospheric ozone depletion involves 
the thinning of the ozone layer, which protects 
life on Earth from harmful ultraviolet radiation. 
Ozone depletion increases health risks and 
damages ecosystems. 
Staying within these boundaries is essential 
for maintaining a stable and resilient planet.

Appendix 2: Methodology
The 10 technologies featured in this report 
were selected through a combination of AI-driven 
discovery, thematic prioritization and expert 
consultations. The detailed methodology is 
outlined below.
Phase 1: Technology discovery
Frontiers’ AI Discovery Tool was used to identify, 
assess and rank a longlist of 80 emerging 
technologies according to the following instructions.
Scope and definitions
	–
Subject: emerging technologies that could 
enable human activities to remain within 
planetary boundaries while remaining affordable, 
scalable and accessible globally (the study 
covers both entirely novel innovations 
and established technologies applied in 
transformative new ways)
	–
In scope: scientific, engineering, digital, biological, 
chemical or materials-based approaches
	–
Out of scope: policy, finance, governance 
or behaviour-change interventions
Eligibility gating 
Technologies failing any of the three gates below 
were automatically excluded from the longlist.
	–
A current technology readiness level (TRL) 
of 5–8
	–
A credible commercial deployment pathway 
by 2030 
	–
A stated, plausible mechanistic link to at least 
one planetary boundary
Assessment criteria and relative weights
Technologies passing the three gates were 
assessed and ranked according to the following 
weighted criteria.
	–
Academic momentum (20%): ecosystem trend, 
tech-specific trend, basis used, growth figures
	–
Commercial momentum (15%): patents, 
funding, start-up/company activity, milestones
	–
Deployment viability (25%): TRL/anchor, 
developer breadth, cost/trajectory, critical 
materials, barrier, regulatory status, concentration 
risk, supply chain trajectory, project pipeline
	–
Planetary boundaries impact (40%): the breadth 
and magnitude of each technology’s positive 
and negative effects across the planetary 
boundaries, with greater weight given to 
boundaries that are already significantly overshot
