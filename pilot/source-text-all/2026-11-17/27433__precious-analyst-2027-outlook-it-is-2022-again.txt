Q3. Gold did not rally during the 2026 Hormuz shock despite higher inflation. Has 
gold’s role as an inflation hedge or safe haven changed? 
No, in our view. We have long argued that gold is not a universal stagflation hedge 
because the source of inflation matters. There are two distinct stagflationary regimes in 
which equity-bond portfolios tend to perform poorly, and gold behaves differently 
across them. 
The first regime is one of growing market concerns about institutional credibility. 
When markets doubt the central bank’s willingness or ability to contain inflation, 
inflation may run high, and both bonds and equities sell off. The 1970s are the classic 
case: US fiscal expansion and Fed subordination let inflation run out of control, and gold 
surged as investors sought value outside the system. 
The second is when supply disruptions drive both weaker growth and higher prices, as 
in 2022 when Russia, then supplying ~40% of Europe’s gas, cut flows, and commodities 
— as the disrupted input —were among the few assets with positive real returns. Like in 
2022, gold typically underperforms initially in supply disruption episodes. Supply‑driven 
inflation raises the risk of tighter monetary policy, higher yields increase the opportunity 
cost of holding gold and weigh on ETF demand, and potential equity market drawdowns 
trigger margin‑related liquidations.  
The 2026 Hormuz shock fits this second regime, as the inflation shock came from 
disrupted commodity supply.

Q6. Could China’s gold allocation ultimately exceed your 20% plausible target? 
Yes. We see risks to our estimate of China’s 20% gold allocation target as skewed to the 
upside because larger gold reserves could support RMB internationalization. The path 
toward a more important international currency broadly has three steps: increasing its 
use in third-country trade, increasing its role in international debt markets and, 
ultimately, establishing it in other countries’ reserves. While the first two steps are 
already underway, the final step is typically the hardest for any emerging reserve 
currency, as reserve managers seek diversification from the existing reserve system while 
also considering the composition of assets underpinning the alternative. History 
suggests gold can help bridge this transition: as a neutral reserve asset that does not 
depend on any issuer’s creditworthiness or political alignment, gold can strengthen 
confidence in an international currency. Large gold holdings helped underpin confidence 
in the USD as it overtook the GBP as the dominant reserve currency after World War II, 
reinforcing the notion that the currency was “as good as gold.” Whether or not an 
emerging currency ultimately achieves dominant reserve status, gold accumulation can 
therefore form part of the strategy, suggesting China’s desired gold allocation could 
ultimately exceed 20%.

We think broader central bank gold selling is unlikely — with our nowcast instead

Türkiye’s reserve reporting is unusual because gold swaps can appear as declines in 
reported gold reserves even when the gold has not been outright sold, potentially 
accounting for part of the 129-tonne decline at the onset of the Middle East conflict and

More fundamentally, Türkiye is an outlier: as an EM central bank, it faces potential FX

therefore view these countries as outliers where gold swaps or sales are more plausible, 
rather than as a signal of broader EM central bank selling. 
We are skeptical about concerns that Gulf countries might also sell gold going forward.

Q8. Could DM central banks with large legacy gold holdings become sellers? 
We view that risk as less likely now. If anything, interest in gold and reserve security is 
broadening beyond EM central banks. DM central banks have largely remained on the 
sidelines of the buying wave since 2022, partly because they already hold substantial 
gold reserves for historical reasons, typically diversified across domestic and foreign 
custody locations, notably the Bank of England and the New York Fed, to manage 
jurisdictional, political and physical risks. Concerns about geopolitical instability were 
among the reasons some European central banks moved part of their gold holdings to 
New York during the Cold War. 
But DM central bank feedback suggests that the debate is increasingly shifting from 
whether to own gold to where to store it: for instance, France and the Netherlands have 
recently reportedly moved gold holdings away from New York. The greater focus on 
reserve security suggests DM reserve managers are increasingly considering the tail risks 
that give gold its strategic value in the first place, reinforcing the case for retaining rather 
than selling gold.

Platinum and Palladium: Still Fragmented 
Q11. Platinum and palladium remain under Section 232 investigation for US tariffs. 
What does that mean for the outlook?  
We think the main risk is continued supply fragmentation and volatility from 
persistent US tariff uncertainty (Exhibit 18), but see outright US tariffs on platinum and 
palladium as unlikely. 
 
Platinum and palladium are critical inputs, particularly for the automotive sector, but 
global supply is exceptionally concentrated: Southern Africa accounts for roughly 
82%/46% of platinum/palladium supply and Russia another 11%/39% — creating 
significant disruption risk. Reducing reliance on foreign critical mineral supply and 
rebuilding domestic capacity appear to have become increasingly important US policy 
objectives.  
In theory, tariffs are a way to achieve this policy objective: make imported minerals more 
expensive, protect domestic producers and ultimately incentivize investment in US 
mines, smelters and refineries. In practice, the PGM supply response is limited. US 
domestic mine production is limited, scaling output is technically and geologically 
challenging, and new mines and refineries take many years to permit build and bring 
online. Because tariffs can change much faster than production capacity can be built, 
the resulting policy uncertainty can undermine the long-term investment they are 
intended to encourage. 
But the risk of tariffs still achieves a national-security objective by pulling metal 
inventories into the US without requiring large-scale government purchases or 
immediately raising costs for downstream consumers. The result is lower liquidity 
outside the US — and thus price volatility — even if global inventories remain 
comfortable on paper.

Q12. Does ongoing fragmented supply mean higher platinum and palladium prices 
in 2027? 
Not necessarily. Supply fragmentation keeps volatility risk elevated, but the 2027 
demand backdrop is more mixed. Fed hikes should weigh on investment demand, 
particularly for platinum, which has a larger investment component than palladium. The 
EV transition should also continue to reduce PGM demand, although the impact may 
differ between the two metals. 
Platinum faces the greater European EV headwind, but also more upside from 
defense spending. Europe has historically been more diesel-heavy, making its vehicle 
fleet relatively more platinum-intensive. Rising EV penetration therefore weighs 
particularly on platinum demand. Our auto analysts reported BEV sales in Europe up 
66% yoy in August 2026, while implied ICE sales fell 10% yoy. However, military vehicles 
remain heavily diesel-based, making higher defense spending a potential offset. 
Palladium has relatively more support from hybrids and the slower US EV transition. 
Gasoline vehicles are more palladium-intensive, and because HEVs are mostly 
gasoline-engine vehicles, hybrid growth supports palladium demand. In the US, BEV 
sales fell 45% yoy in 3Q26, while hybrid sales rose 27% yoy in 3Q26. This suggests 
gasoline-linked catalyst demand may persist for longer as hybrids gain share and BEV 
adoption slows.
