3
The equity rally of recent 
years has been dominated 
by AI-related companies and 
mega-cap technology stocks. 
However, durable bull markets 
rarely sustain themselves 
indefinitely on the back of 
a narrow group of leaders. 
As earnings growth expands across sectors and investors 
look for additional sources of return, market leadership may 
begin to broaden. Healthcare appears increasingly well 
positioned to participate in that transition.
Despite several years of relative underperformance, 
the sector remains attractive due to a combination of 
depressed valuations, improving fundamentals, powerful 
secular growth drivers, and significant opportunities 
for innovation. As investors look beyond the first-order 
beneficiaries of artificial intelligence, healthcare may 
represent one of the most compelling opportunities 
within a broadening equity rally.
Healthcare: a forgotten 
sector in a narrow market
Healthcare has been one of the clearest casualties of the 
narrow market leadership that has characterized the current 
bull market. As investors concentrated capital in AI-related 
beneficiaries and mega-cap technology companies, the 
sector steadily fell out of favor despite continuing to 
generate attractive earnings and cash-flow growth.
Healthcare also faced a series of idiosyncratic challenges, 
including uncertainty surrounding drug-pricing reform, 
pressure on Medicare Advantage reimbursement rates, and 
post-pandemic normalization across diagnostics, medical 
devices and life-science tools. The result has been one of 
the sector’s longest periods of relative underperformance in 
decades.
At the same time, healthcare's weight in global equity 
benchmarks has declined materially, leaving many investors 
underexposed relative to historical levels.
Ironically, many of the factors that contributed to 
healthcare's underperformance may now be creating the 
foundation for future outperformance as market leadership 
broadens beyond a narrow group of technology stocks.
Historically, new market leaders tend to emerge when 
improving fundamentals coincide with attractive valuations 
and subdued investor expectations. Healthcare increasingly 
exhibits all three characteristics.
The healthcare outlook 
is improving
Several headwinds that weighed on healthcare over recent 
years are becoming more manageable.
Specifically, many of the sector's biggest uncertainties are 
giving way to greater visibility.
1.	
Policy clarity is improving. Investors now have 
greater clarity around the Inflation Reduction Act's 
drug-pricing provisions, reducing one of the largest 
sources of regulatory uncertainty facing the sector. 
Greater visibility is also beginning to emerge around 
pharmaceutical tariffs and Most Favored Nation (MFN) 
drug-pricing proposals.
2.	
Managed-care fundamentals are stabilizing. 
Recent reimbursement updates have become more 
supportive while insurers continue repositioning 
businesses to restore profitability.
3.	 Utilization trends are becoming more predictable. 
Several years removed from the pandemic, healthcare 
companies and investors have a clearer view of 
underlying demand patterns.
Importantly, these improving healthcare fundamentals are 
emerging as investors begin to examine opportunities 
beyond mega-cap technology. As a result, investor focus is 
beginning to shift from uncertainty toward fundamentals.

4
Longevity: a powerful driver 
of healthcare's structural 
growth advantage
While healthcare has struggled to capture 
investor attention in recent years, the 
sector's long-term growth drivers have 
remained remarkably resilient. 
The long-term investment case for healthcare remains 
supported by several powerful secular trends. Demographic 
aging, rising chronic disease prevalence, expanding access 
to healthcare, and medical innovation continue to drive 
durable demand growth.
Longevity is emerging as a force multiplier across these 
trends. Advances in medicine have transformed many 
acute and fatal diseases into chronic conditions that 
can be effectively managed for decades. 
The global population is not only aging but also living 
longer. As the number of people over the age of 60 
continues to rise, a growing share of the population is 
entering the life stage associated with the highest levels 
of healthcare utilization.
At the same time, rising life expectancy is extending 
the number of years during which individuals consume 
healthcare. While people are living longer than previous 
generations, gains in lifespan have often outpaced gains 
in healthy, disease-free years. 
This dynamic is likely to support growing demand for 
pharmaceuticals, medical devices, diagnostics, procedures, 
and healthcare services as a larger share of the population 
enters its highest-utilization years.

As a result, longer lives do not necessarily 
translate into fewer healthcare needs. 
Instead, they can extend the period over 
which individuals require treatment, 
monitoring, and medical intervention.
This demographic shift is being accentuated by the rising 
burden of chronic disease. Obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular 
disease, cancer, and mental health disorders continue to 
increase globally, while longer lifespans mean more people 
are reaching ages at which conditions such as dementia, 
Parkinson’s disease, osteoporosis and degenerative joint 
disease become more prevalent. Together, these trends are 
increasing the need for diagnosis, treatment and long-term 
care, creating sustained demand for healthcare innovation 
and services.
Obesity and metabolic disorders have reached epidemic 
proportions, while mental health conditions now affect 
roughly one in seven people worldwide. Unlike acute 
illnesses, these conditions typically require long-term 
management, ongoing consultations with physicians, 
pharmaceuticals, diagnostics, and interventions involving 
medical devices.
Together, these trends support sustained growth in demand 
for healthcare services. Unlike many growth opportunities, 
longevity-related demand drivers are highly durable and 
largely independent of the economic cycle.
The result is a powerful long-term demand backdrop: more 
people reaching their highest-utilization years, living longer, 
and requiring healthcare support for a greater portion of 
their lives.

7
Could healthcare become one 
of AI's biggest beneficiaries?
Much of the investment discussion surrounding AI has 
focused on companies building the underlying 
infrastructure. Less attention has been paid to sectors that 
may ultimately create value through AI adoption. The first 
phase of AI rewarded the builders; the next phase may 
increasingly reward the adopters. Healthcare sits at the 
center of that opportunity.
The sector is both data-rich and labor-constrained. AI has 
the potential to improve diagnostics, accelerate drug 
discovery, automate administrative workflows, and enhance 
clinical decision-making.
1.	
From data to diagnosis 
Healthcare generates vast amounts of data, much of 
which remains underutilized. AI has the potential to 
unlock value from medical imaging, electronic health 
records, genomics, and other datasets by improving 
disease detection, accelerating drug discovery, and 
enabling more informed clinical decision-making.
2.	
Extending the healthcare workforce 
As aging populations increase demand for care, many 
healthcare systems face shortages of physicians, nurses, 
and other healthcare professionals. AI can help alleviate 
these pressures by automating administrative tasks and 
improving workflow efficiency, allowing clinicians to 
spend more time with patients while enhancing 
resource utilization.
3.	
Accelerating innovation and improving efficiency
AI's benefits extend well beyond providers. 
Pharmaceutical companies can use AI to accelerate 
research and development, providers can improve 
operational efficiency, and payers can better identify 
high-risk patients and optimize care management. As a 
result, AI has the potential to drive productivity gains 
across the healthcare value chain.
Unlike many industries where AI’s primary impact may be 
disruption, healthcare has the opportunity to combine strong 
secular demand growth with significant productivity gains.
This creates an unusually attractive combination: AI could 
accelerate the pace of healthcare innovation while also 
helping the sector respond more efficiently to rising demand. 
Capital is returning to 
healthcare innovation
Another encouraging sign is the recovery in healthcare 
capital markets.
Biotech financing improved during 2025, follow-on activity 
increased, IPO markets resumed, and healthcare M&A 
accelerated as large pharmaceutical companies sought to 
replenish their pipelines ahead of patent expirations.
While activity remains below prior-cycle peaks, the trend is 
notable. Improving access to capital supports clinical 
development, accelerates innovation, and signals growing 
investor confidence in the sector's long-term growth prospects.

Biotech as a potential early 
indicator of recovery
Biotechnology is often one of the most sentiment-sensitive 
areas of healthcare, responding quickly to changes in 
investor confidence, capital availability, and expectations 
for future innovation. As a result, biotech performance can 
provide an early indication of broader shifts within the 
sector.
Recent strength suggests that sentiment is improving. As 
concerns around drug pricing, reimbursement, and 
government agency stability become more manageable, 
capital is returning to innovation-driven areas of healthcare.
Historically, biotech recoveries have often coincided with 
broader improvements in healthcare sentiment. If that 
pattern holds, recent outperformance may indicate that 
investors are becoming more willing to reward healthcare 
growth once again.
Where is innovation creating 
value? 
Two blockbuster markets in the making: 
obesity and liquid biopsy
Medical innovation remains one of healthcare's most 
important drivers of long-term growth. Advances in 
oncology, immunology, metabolic disease (including GLP-1 
and related incretin therapies), robotic surgery and 
precision medicine are expanding the number of treatable 
patients and increasing the value of treatment per patient. 
While innovative therapies often carry higher upfront costs, 
they have the potential to improve outcomes and reduce 
long-term healthcare spending.
	– Obesity: The success of GLP-1 therapies has transformed 
obesity from a lifestyle-management market into one of 
the largest pharmaceutical opportunities in decades by 
demonstrating clinical benefit across multiple settings 
such as cardiovascular mortality. With penetration rates 
still relatively low and clinical applications continuing to 
expand, the addressable market remains substantial. 
Importantly, we believe today’s GLP-1-based therapies 
represent only the first step in addressing metabolic 
disease, much as statins began to address cardiovascular 
disease nearly four decades ago. We see significant 
opportunity in next-generation therapies with multiple 
targets and profiles. These therapies could improve on 
GLP-1-based medicines by enhancing tolerability and 
treatment adherence while preserving lean muscle mass.
 
	– Liquid biopsy: Liquid biopsy has the potential to 
reshape cancer care by enabling earlier detection, 
optimizing treatment protocols, and allowing for more 
effective monitoring. If broadly adopted, these 
technologies would fundamentally transform the 
diagnosis and treatment of cancer. In the process, this 
would create a new category of recurring diagnostic 
testing while simultaneously shifting treatment toward 
earlier, more targeted intervention.
Importantly, neither theme is simply taking share within an 
existing market. Both have the potential to create entirely 
new markets by redefining the standard of care. They are 
powerful examples of healthcare's ability to generate long-
term, innovation-driven growth, much as the technology 
sector has done in recent years.

9
Healthcare’s next chapter 
of leadership
The market's obsession with AI has been 
understandable. But in focusing on the 
companies building the future, investors 
may have overlooked a sector 
increasingly positioned to benefit from it.
Healthcare enters this period from an unusual starting 
point. Expectations are low, valuations remain attractive, 
and investor positioning is light. Yet the sector's underlying 
growth drivers have rarely been stronger. Aging 
populations, increasing longevity, rising chronic disease 
prevalence, and continued innovation are driving demand 
across the healthcare ecosystem.
At the same time, many of the headwinds that contributed 
to the sector's recent underperformance are beginning to 
fade. Policy uncertainty is becoming more manageable, 
capital is returning to innovation, and new growth 
platforms such as obesity therapies and liquid biopsy are 
moving from promise toward commercial reality.
The paradox is that healthcare's fundamentals remained 
intact even as investors looked elsewhere. If the first phase 
of the rally rewarded the builders of AI, the next phase may 
reward sectors applying innovation to solve real-world 
challenges. By that measure, healthcare may have spent 
the last four years lagging the market but may now be 
positioning itself for its next chapter of leadership.
