 
"Déjà Vu": JPMorgan Finds 3.8GW Of Malaysia's 
Data Center Pipeline Tied To Chinese Operators As 
Washington Moves To Shut The Backdoor 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 15, 2026 - 10:20 PM 
For three years, Malaysia's pitch to the AI industry was simple: cheap land, cheap power, and 
no awkward questions about who the tenant is. 
This week, Washington started asking. 
With Jensen Huang out at Nvidia, 14 sellside desks hunting for the stock's floor and the "Bank of 
Nvidia" missing its chairman, the next leg of the scandal was always going to land in Southeast 
Asia, where Megaspeed and its Malaysian unit Speedmatrix did their shopping. And now we 
have the first hard number on how much is at stake. In a note titled "Malaysia Data Centers: 
Déjà vu of potential US chip controls?" (available to pro subs), JPMorgan's Malaysia research 
team writes that roughly 3.8GW of data center capacity in the country is associated with 
Chinese operators, or about 25% of the entire pipeline. 
In other words, one in every four megawatts Malaysia plans to build is tied to exactly the 
customers Washington is now trying to lock out. And, as we explain below, that's almost 
certainly the floor. 
One In Four (Or Is It Closer To One In Three?) 
Start with the size of the prize. JPM's proprietary tracker now puts Malaysia's data center 
pipeline at roughly 12.7GW, more than double the 5.9GW it was tracking in 2025. However, just 
like in the US where only a fraction of the pipeline is "credible", only 1.3GW is live and another 
2.2GW is under construction. The rest is either "committed" (4.0GW) or early-stage (5.2GW), 
which the bank defines as announced or speculative capacity with land and power yet to be 
secured. 
 

 
 
Put differently, 72% of the pipeline hasn't broken ground, and 41% of it is little more than a press 
release. That's the part that tends to evaporate first when the tenant can no longer get chips. 
Against that backdrop, here is the bank's headline finding, and the caveat it delivers, all in the 
same quote: 
Our proprietary Malaysia DC tracker identifies ~3.8GW of capacity associated with Chinese DC 
operators, representing ~25% of Malaysia’s pipeline. Actual Chinese end-demand exposure 
could vary, as our tracker maps operators rather than tenants, and does not capture Chinese 
tenants leasing capacity from non-Chinese colocation providers (e.g. Yondr, BDC and K2). 
Translation: the 3.8GW counts only data centers where the operator is Chinese. It says nothing 
about Chinese tenants renting space from everyone else. 
And that's a rather large blind spot. JPM doesn't name names, but the usual suspects are well 
known: DayOne, the former GDS unit that sits on our casualty list after mulling whether to halve 
its $5BN US IPO, operated 29% of Malaysia's built IT capacity at the end of 2024, according to 
Data Centre Dynamics. The biggest operator of all, at 32%, was Bridge Data Centres, a 
Bain-backed unit of ChinData whose parent has historically counted ByteDance as its largest 
tenant. JPM lists "BDC" (presumably Bridge) among its non-Chinese colocation providers, which 
means whatever TikTok's parent rents there is outside the 3.8GW. 
There's also some curious math. Divide 3.8GW by the tracker's own 12.7GW and you get 30%, 
well above the "~25%" in the text (which would need a 15GW pipeline). Maybe the bank is using 
a wider denominator, maybe it's rounding generously, or it could just be using a hyperactive 
LLM. Either way, the real exposure to Chinese end-demand is higher than one in four: 
 

 
 
How The Backdoor Works (And Why It Was Legal) 
Regular readers know the story, because we have been telling it since January 2025, when we 
asked "Did DeepSeek Use Shell Companies In Singapore To Procure Nvidia Blacklisted 
Chips?", and again five weeks later when Singapore started probing middlemen shipping Nvidia 
servers to Malaysia. Back then Jensen Huang's answer was that he saw no evidence of 
diversion. That one has aged about as well as his employment contract. 
There are really two backdoors. The first is plain smuggling: buy the chips through a front 
company and ship them on, which is what Megaspeed is accused of (it denies wrongdoing, and 
as we noted yesterday in "Who Whacked Jensen Huang?", even the CIA isn't fully sold on the 
spy part). The Information puts the value of Nvidia chips that moved through Megaspeed and 
Speedmatrix at no less than $7.5 billion, and by Bloomberg's count Nvidia's own tally to the US 
government missed 50,000 of them. 
The second backdoor is the big one, and it's perfectly legal: US export controls govern where a 
chip is shipped, not who rents time on it. So a Chinese AI lab that can't import a Blackwell can 
simply lease one in Johor. Goldman's Asia economics team, led by Andrew Tilton, had a polite 
name for it back in September, in a note that Bloomberg dusted off this week: "export-control 
arbitrage" (more on that note in a moment). Epoch AI estimates some 275,000 Nvidia 
accelerators were imported into Malaysia in 2024 and 2025 alone. 
That arbitrage was already closing before the scandal: Oracle killed a $7BN chip-leasing deal 
with Tencent across its Southeast Asian data centers after a visit from Trump officials, and 
 

 
Nvidia quietly told cloud customers that 51% of their services must go to non-Chinese clients. 
Then came Alice Huang, and Washington found its excuse to finish the job. The Megaspeed 
blacklist fight we flagged on Tuesday... 
... has since turned into a full rewrite of the rulebook. According to the WSJ, Commerce is 
looking at new export rules and licensing requirements for Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and 
Singapore. The Information goes further: it reports that BIS is in the final stages of a rule adding 
seven countries (those four plus Brazil, Norway and South Africa) to the license list for anything 
at or above Blackwell, with importers having to name the end user, and that it could land this 
month. On the Hill, both parties are racing to bolt chip curbs onto the must-pass defense bill: 
And with Huang gone, the industry has lost the one man who could talk the White House out of 
it. 
Goldman: Nobody In Asia Has More Riding On This Than 
Malaysia 
So how much does Malaysia stand to lose? For that we go to the Goldman note itself, "Exports 
and Beyond: Asia's Broadening AI Dividend", published two months ago, when Alice Huang was 
still just a face in Jensen's Taipei party photos. 
The bank's ASEAN data center thesis rests on two legs. One is the respectable one: power and 
grid constraints in tier-1 markets are pushing hyperscalers to places where land, electricity and 
regulators are all more accommodating. The other is the arbitrage, and Goldman is refreshingly 
blunt about who is doing it: a footnote names ByteDance as reportedly among the Chinese firms 
training their newest models in Southeast Asian data centers, and notes that TikTok's $25BN 
data-infrastructure project in Thailand, approved in May, is worth 4.3% of that country's GDP. 
On the back of both, Goldman's TMT team expects ASEAN capacity to quadruple in five years, 
with Johor the region's largest hub by 2030. Malaysia already has more live capacity than 
Singapore, Korea or Hong Kong, at about 1.3GW (which, reassuringly, matches the live figure in 
JPM's tracker): 
 

 
 
The physical evidence is hard to miss. Malaysia's net imports of diesel generators, the backup 
power of choice for data centers, were running more than $200 million above their 2018-22 
average on the latest reading, far ahead of Thailand, Indonesia or Australia: 
 

 
 
What does that do for growth? Goldman estimates data center construction adds an average of 
30bps a year to Malaysian GDP over 2026-2028 (20bps for Thailand, 10bps for Indonesia), with 
Malaysia getting the single biggest impulse next year, at roughly 36bps: 
 

 
 
If that sounds small next to RM385 billion of approved investment, it's because about two-thirds 
of what goes into a data center is imported chips and electrical gear. The part that stays in 
Malaysia is concrete, cabling and construction wages, and once the building is up, permanent 
headcount is in the low hundreds. 
The more interesting number is what happens after the servers are switched on. Malaysia's ICT 
services balance swung from a deficit of 20bps of GDP to a surplus of 30bps between Q2 2025 
and Q2 2026, and 2025 was the first year since 2011 that the country ran an overall services 
surplus: 
 

 
 
Goldman reads that as Johor selling cloud and inference back to Singapore. Maybe. But think 
about what an "ICT services export" from a Malaysian data center actually is: a foreigner renting 
compute that sits in Malaysia. That is the remote-access loophole, showing up in the balance of 
payments. 
Put it all together and the bank concludes that Malaysia is the ASEAN economy with the most to 
gain from AI. Its summary table has AI contributing 3.4 percentage points of Malaysia's 5.2% 
growth this year, i.e. roughly two-thirds of it: 
 

 
 
To be fair to Kuala Lumpur, most of that (2.8 points) is tech hardware exports, mainly chip 
assembly and testing, which no Commerce rule on Blackwells will touch. The slice that runs 
through data centers is the 0.2 points of construction plus the 0.4 points of ICT services exports: 
call it 0.6 points, or a bit more than a tenth of this year's growth. So the macro hit is 
manageable. The hit to the handful of Bursa-listed contractors and utilities that have been living 
off it is another matter. 
And here is the part that has aged best. Goldman closes with a warning about the region's AI 
dependence: 
This positioning is inherently double-edged. While the region is highly leveraged to the AI supply 
chain and has so far delivered robust growth thanks to the investment boom, such concentration 
leaves the region vulnerable to any reversal in AI investments. A moderation in hyperscaler 
capex, whether driven by higher financing costs, greater investor scrutiny of ROI or 
slower-than-expected progress in AI adoption, might generate a meaningful growth headwind 
for the region. 
Higher financing costs, ROI scrutiny, slower adoption: all present and accounted for. What's 
missing from the list of risks is the one thing the same note had named, six pages earlier, as a 
driver of the boom. Arbitrage, after all, only works until the other side notices. 
Kuala Lumpur Discovers Paperwork 
Malaysia, which has approved RM385.7 billion ($95 billion) of data center investment since 
2021 and whose construction sector grew 6% last quarter on the back of it, per Reuters, is now 
scrambling to look like a responsible custodian. 
 

 
On Thursday, Trade Minister Johari Abdul Ghani said the government is drafting a law forcing 
data centers to disclose their operators, locations, power use and how many GPUs they bring 
in. He also said he saw no legal basis to investigate Speedmatrix. By Friday, his ministry was 
working with customs to find out whether Speedmatrix's chips had been re-exported, while 
stressing that it had never given the company the all-clear. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim, for his 
part, says there is no sign any chips left the country. 
The most revealing detail is in the ministry's own statement: a data center tenant can bring AI 
chips into Malaysia without any permit or license (only exporting them has required one, since 
July 2025). Just 55 of the country's 117 approved projects are even operating. So nobody in 
Kuala Lumpur actually knows how many GPUs are in the country, who owns them, or who is 
logged into them from Hangzhou. Well, somebody is about to find out. 
Déjà Vu, With One Big Difference 
Why "déjà vu"? Because Malaysia has seen this movie. In January 2025, Biden's AI diffusion 
rule capped chip exports to the country and local data center names were routed: in the first two 
weeks of that year, as the rule took shape, YTL Power lost 13% and Gamuda 9%. Four days 
after the rule, JPMorgan cut Sunway Construction and YTL Power to Underweight and Gamuda 
to Neutral, all on the same day (we'd guess that was no coincidence). Trump then scrapped the 
rule, and the downgraded stocks did this: 
 
SunCon more than doubled. Which is probably why, this time around, the bank is in no mood to 
repeat the exercise: all five stocks in the note are rated Overweight, even as the market does 
 

 
the downgrading for it. On Thursday alone YTL Power shed 8% and more than RM3 billion of 
market cap, taking its slide since the end of September to roughly 26%, with the contractors in 
tow (Gamuda lost another 3% on Friday): 
 
The one big difference with 2025 is who is writing the rules. Last time a lame-duck 
administration published a rule on its way out the door, and its successor scrapped it four 
months later with Nvidia's CEO cheering from the front row. This time the rule comes from 
Trump's own Commerce Department, Congress wants an even tougher one, and the 
lobbyist-in-chief is unemployed. 
Nor are valuations pricing in a rerun. JPM's own charts show SunCon at 20x forward earnings 
against a 10-year average of 17x, and Gamuda at 18x against 15x (chart source: Bloomberg, 
via JPM): 
 
 

 
Who Gets Hit, According To JPMorgan 
The bank's answer is, more or less: not the stocks we cover. Start with its argument for why the 
builders are mostly insulated: 
While the implications of potential US measures remain unclear, we see relatively low direct 
exposure for GAM and SCGB, as most of their DC clients are Western hyperscalers developing 
capacity primarily for their own use, rather than third-party operators potentially exposed to 
chip-diversion restrictions. 
The numbers behind that: JPM counts 24 hyperscaler packages in Selangor and Negeri 
Sembilan worth 1,554MW and RM46.6 billion, of which 14 packages (828MW, RM24.8BN) have 
yet to be awarded. Gamuda, with a US hyperscaler client and only ~15% of its orderbook in 
data centers, is the safer of the two; for SunCon, every RM1BN of data center wins gained or 
lost moves FY27/28 earnings by 3-4%. 
 
Fair enough, but note the hedge buried in the same note: contractors may escape direct 
restrictions, yet a change in customers' investment plans or timelines would hit everyone. More 
than half that table is still to be awarded, after all. 
Next, the bank's defense of YTL Power: 
In colocation, GPU procurement sits with tenants, limiting YTLP’s direct exposure. Long-dated 
contracts with bank-guarantees offer downside protection, while YTLP’s integrated land, power 
and DC infrastructure lets it target US tenants for future capacity. 
 

 
YTL's 150MW of live capacity is split evenly between US and non-US customers, JPM says, 
without spelling out who the non-US half is. Tenaga, meanwhile, is covered by five-year 
take-or-pay supply agreements, so it gets paid whether or not the servers ever show up. And 
NationGate gets a clean bill of health: 
Nationgate’s Nvidia GPU assembly lasted about three quarters, and we stripped all GPU 
revenue from forecasts at end-2025. Any licensing risk sits with exporters, not contract 
manufacturers, delivering locally. 
Add it all up and the latest price targets in the note's own tables sit 26% to 64% above 
Thursday's close (YTL Power: RM7.20 vs RM4.40). Which is either a screaming opportunity, or 
a reminder that price targets tend to follow price, not the other way around. 
The Other 4GW 
To be fair, there is a bull case, and it comes from Morgan Stanley. In its Nov 2 global power deck 
(also available to pro subs), the bank estimated that about 15GW of America's data center 
power shortfall will spill over to Asia, with Malaysia taking the single biggest slice at 4GW: 
 
That was before the blue tsunami froze a good chunk of the US pipeline. So maybe the 
Americans chased out of Texas and Ohio simply backfill whatever the Chinese vacate in Johor: 
4GW in, 3.8GW out, and nobody gets hurt. One Singapore chip executive told Reuters as much, 
arguing that fewer chips for China means more for everyone else. 
 

 
Maybe. But there's a catch: under the rule The Information describes, every Blackwell headed to 
Malaysia needs a US license, whoever the tenant is. An American hyperscaler will get one. It 
will just take longer, and the whole point of Johor was that it was fast. 
What to watch: 
●​ The Commerce rule: could be announced as soon as this month. The detail that matters 
is whether it regulates only shipments or also remote access, i.e. existing capacity. 
●​ The defense bill: three chip-control bills, including mandatory location tracking, are being 
lined up for the NDAA. 
●​ Nov 18: the date three House committee chairs set for a closed-door briefing on Huang's 
dealings with Alice Huang. 
●​ Malaysia's customs findings on Speedmatrix, and how fast the GPU-disclosure law 
moves. 
●​ The unawarded 828MW of hyperscaler packages: any slippage shows up in Gamuda 
and SunCon's orderbooks first. 
Nobody Asked Who The Tenant Was 
JPMorgan's conclusion is that the scope of any US measures is uncertain, direct exposure for 
its stocks is low, and the committed investments provide support. That's one possibility. The 
other, and to us the more likely, is that a pipeline which is 72% unbuilt, at least a quarter 
Chinese by operator and an unknown amount more by tenant, in a country that only this week 
decided to start counting its GPUs, is about to get a lot smaller. 
We said in December that Johor had become a magnet for AI data centers. Magnets, it turns 
out, also attract the Bureau of Industry and Security.
