particularly in the US and UK; Bunds likely more constrained by trade-offs between policy

A range of drivers contributed to the broad-based selloff across G10 bond markets in 
2026. The energy shock was the spark, but resilient growth, stable labor markets, a debt 
financed investment boom, and episodic fiscal sustainability concerns supported the 
yield move, with bearish impulses coming from all markets at some point during the year. 
Our baseline does not argue for opening up the tail to deeply lower yields. It should, 
however, see the risk of significantly higher front-end rates moderate through the year. 
As disinflationary evidence accumulates, we expect the balance of risks to shift from 
higher to lower policy rates across most markets, with our central bank policy 
expectations generally dovish versus end-27 market pricing (Japan being the exception; 
Exhibit 1). That evolution supports a baseline path for yields that leans more bullish to 
the forwards as the year evolves.

part on sustained energy relief, but the starting point of tighter financial conditions and 
a smaller fiscal impulse compared with 2026 points to a shorter runway to increased 
growth concerns. The balance between hawkish risks and medium-term growth 
downside should keep belly and longer-term yields better anchored and lead to greater 
curve flattening if upside risks to policy rates materialize.

European Sovereign Spreads: Between energy and French tail risks  
Sovereign spread tightening likely staggered over 2027 as BTPs stand to benefit most from 
energy relief, further OAT-Bund compression hinges on credible improvements in fiscal policy. 
The initially contained repricing in sovereign spreads amid higher energy prices gave way 
to greater pressure on weaker sovereigns as market focus shifted towards 2027 electoral 
deadlines and the impact of higher rates on debt sustainability. 
We think this sequencing can provide some insights into what sovereign credit relief may 
look like in 2027. Although timing is highly uncertain, we expect a gradual relaxation of 
geopolitical risks to be a tailwind for sovereign spreads, particularly for markets with 
high betas to energy, such as Italy and Greece. Breaking down sovereign spreads moves

France is where political uncertainty is likely to be most durable—potentially extending

Australia: Accelerating inflation prompted the RBA to start raising rates even before the 
onset of the Iran conflict, with the subsequent energy shock leading to further 
tightening. The perception that the previous cycle’s policy stance was insufficiently 
restrictive exerted upward pressure on terminal rate pricing, culminating in five RBA 
hikes. Although inflation remains above target, cumulative policy tightening has begun 
to constrain activity—most visibly in the housing market. That has shifted the 
asymmetry around rates, and we expect 10y yields to decline to 4.75% by YE27 on a 
modest reduction in risk premium and the eventual pricing of rate cuts. Our economists 
expect cuts to commence in 4Q27—later than market expectations—though we see 
near-term risks of a reversal of the recent rate decline should energy prices rebound or 
underlying inflation pressures prove hotter than expected. On the downside, we expect 
global spillovers and growth concerns around the AI outlook to be the clearest catalysts 
for a durable reduction in yields across the curve. While more pronounced curve 
flattening is likely to accompany any further upward pressure on policy rate 
expectations, our baseline of gradual easing amid normalizing inflation points to a 
resumption of curve steepening next year. 
New Zealand: In contrast to the RBA’s more front-footed approach to tightening, the

RBNZ has signaled greater caution amid the trade-off between a softening labor market 
and persistent inflationary pressures. Our economists expect two additional hikes—in 
February and June—representing a slower pace and lower terminal rate than market 
pricing implies, reflecting ongoing domestic economic slack The RBNZ’s willingness to 
assess the lagged effects of tightening delivered to date, while tolerating near-term 
inflation, should bias the NZD curve steeper: front-end policy expectations have room to 
recalibrate lower, while elevated macro uncertainty should support term premium from 
a relatively low level. Eventually, we expect NZD rates to also benefit from the global 
disinflationary theme of energy relief, with the domestic macro backdrop supportive, 
too; we project 10-year yields at 4.60% by YE27. 
Sweden: Resilient growth and continued fiscal support should allow the Riksbank to 
move gradually away from an accommodative stance in 2027, with our economists 
expecting two further hikes to 2.5%. Stronger demand should lift inflation from a soft 
starting point, with potential pass-through from energy-driven supply shocks adding 
upside risk. That said remaining labor-market slack and recent inflation undershoots 
limit the urgency to tighten. This leaves Sweden better positioned to sustain policy 
normalization than much of the Euro area, where growth remains more uneven despite 
the tailwinds from German fiscal expansion. We retain a flattening bias as Sweden moves 
through the early stages of tightening, while inflation softness and global spillovers help 
anchor forwards further out. Overall, we expect 10y Swedish bond yields to remain 
range-bound and settle around 3.10% by YE27. 
Norway: With September’s hike to 4.5% likely marking the peak, Norges Bank’s focus is 
likely to shift from further tightening towards eventual policy normalization. Subdued 
demand, reinforced by the largest tightening in financial conditions across the G10 this 
year, should help inflation moderate alongside a stronger NOK and fading supply 
pressures. This should pave the way for easing from 4Q27, although lingering inflation 
risks constrain the pace of cuts. As attention shifts towards easing, we see scope for 
modestly lower yields and a steeper curve. Historically, NOK curves have tended to 
steepen after the final hike. Given that adjustment has been limited so far this cycle, we 
have a preference for steeper curves relative to Sweden. We expect 10y Norwegian 
bond yields to decline to 4% by YE27. 
Switzerland: Contained underlying inflation should allow the SNB to keep its policy rate 
at 0% throughout 2027, leaving Switzerland the exception to the G10 tightening cycle. 
Recent upside inflation surprises have been concentrated in energy and volatile goods, 
while labor market slack is helping to contain broader domestic price pressures despite 
resilient growth. Nevertheless, risks to our policy forecast remain tilted towards hikes if 
inflation pressures broaden or energy prices remain elevated. Our FX strategists also 
expect some CHF weakness next year as European sovereign credit concerns ease, 
which could add modestly to imported inflation. With our unchanged policy forecast 
dovish relative to market pricing, we see limited justification for a sustained sell-off, 
although upside inflation risks leave room for episodic bearish pressure. Conversely, the 
relatively low sensitivity of CHF yields to energy prices should limit their decline as 
energy pressures normalize, leaving Swiss bonds lagging a broader global rally. We 
therefore expect 10y yields to remain range-bound and settle around 0.50% by YE27. 
With little scope for capital gains, prospective returns remain modest, while Swiss yields 
should remain the lowest in the G10.
