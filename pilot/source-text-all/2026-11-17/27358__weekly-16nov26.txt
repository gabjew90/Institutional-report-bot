Volatility in technology stocks continued last week, with a sharp recovery between Monday and Wednesday interrupted by a
hot CPI print and the resignation of NVIDIA’s chief executive, Jensen Huang on Thursday. As we highlighted last week,
valuations in tech stocks, particularly emerging markets (EM) tech stocks, are very compelling, and earnings growth
estimates continue to inflect higher. This is a good moment to add exposure, in our view, particularly while positioning
remains light. But while the medium-term case is intact, it may be difficult for tech stocks and other risk assets to regain
sustained momentum in the short term while financial conditions keep tightening. A rising US dollar, up nearly 2% last week,
high UST yields and what increasingly looks like the start of a longer rate-hiking cycle make for a difficult backdrop for
animal spirits.
US CPI for October, released on Wednesday, rose 0.4% mom against a 0.3% consensus, with core up 0.3% for a third
straight month. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) published no annual rates because of the 2025 government
shutdown, but estimates put headline inflation at around 4.0%, up from 3.7%, and core at around 2.8%, up from 2.6%.
Importantly, the rise in core last month was driven as much by demand-sensitive services components as by energy or
housing costs. This raises the risk that inflation is becoming broader and less transient, making it more difficult for the US
Federal Reserve (Fed) to delay further hikes while labour markets remain robust. A 25bps hike is now fully priced for
December, with two more fully priced by September 2027.
After two days of speculation, Huang resigned on 12 November after admitting to an affair and amid an independent board
investigation that Alice Huang, a former managing director of Singapore-based Megaspeed with whom he was reportedly
entangled with, was passing information to Chinese intelligence. Jensen Huang is more than the (former) chief executive of
one of the largest and most important companies in the world. He is a key figure in the AI story, as a visionary operator,
communicator, dealmaker and supply-chain orchestrator. His departure is unlikely to set back the roll-out of NVIDIA’s
product pipeline, which looks set for the next two to three years. But NVIDIA has also become a key investor in the broader
AI ecosystem and provides financial backing to neoclouds. How far it continues in that role without Huang is uncertain.
NVIDIA’s earnings on Tuesday should provide some clarity on how the company operates from here and represent another
clearing event for markets. A broader US government crackdown on NVIDIA’s business with China is also possible after the
departure of Huang, who advocated a more open and collaborative approach with China. NVIDIA is about 20% lower mtd
and 32% below its all-time high, at just 11x 12-month forward earnings, the cheapest in more than a decade. TSMC, NVIDIA’s
key supplier, is down around 7.5% since news of Huang’s possible departure broke.
A geopolitical breakthrough in the Middle East remains the key trigger for cross-asset performance into year-end. Despite
US President Donald Trump’s more conciliatory tone after the midterms, it looks further away than it did last week. Trump
says he sees “positive momentum” and wants to maintain the “status quo”, but Tehran has called the US reply to its seven
conditions “insufficient and deceptive”. Iran continues to treat the US naval blockade, and US efforts to push tankers
through the southern shipping lane, as acts of war. It is unlikely to accept them indefinitely and may instead try to raise the
cost of the blockade through further attacks on shipping and other targets. Iran launched drones at Bahrain on 13 and 15
November, its first attacks there in months, saying they were retaliation for US strikes on Iranian sites including Qeshm
Island. Even if neither side wants a wider war, the current dynamics are unstable. With oil in the USD 90s, markets are
pricing balanced risks, but the next move in the risk premium may be tilted to the upside.

China:  Exports jumped 35.2% yoy in October and imports 41.6%, partly on a recovery in crude shipments from the Gulf,
narrowing the monthly trade surplus to USD 105.3bn from USD 133.5bn in September. Semiconductor exports more than
doubled, up 137% by value, and auto exports rose 37.6%, taking the surplus for January-October to USD 1.04trn and putting
China on track to exceed last year’s record. Exports to the US rose 32.9% and to the EU 24.3%, keeping trade imbalances in
focus.
Indonesia:  Economy Minister Airlangga said the budget has enough funds to keep fuel subsidies in place through year-end,
even if oil rises to USD 100 a barrel, despite the 2026 budget assuming USD 70 and the rupiah weakening to around
USD/IDR 18,200-18,300. With prices frozen this year, a hike in subsidised fuel prices in January looks likely, though it would
be deeply unpopular and further dent President Prabowo’s approval ratings. The 2027 budget assumes oil at USD 75 and
USD/IDR 17,500, both well below current levels. Separately, Airlangga said Indonesia has completed 80% of its OECD
accession agenda, with reforms to competition, statistics and anti-bribery laws to be fast-tracked by year-end, and now
aims to join in 2028, a year later than previously targeted.
Malaysia:  The Megaspeed graphics processing unit (GPU) smuggling scandal is set to bring tighter scrutiny of Malaysia’s
data-centre industry. The Wall Street Journal reported that US lawmakers may impose new export rules and licensing
requirements on Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Singapore to stop Chinese firms accessing advanced NVIDIA chips
through rented overseas capacity, and local consultancy Sprint DC warned of a “short- to medium-term market shock”. The
government is considering a law requiring operators to report their chip counts. There is no sign yet of investment slowing,
with USD 95bn of data-centre projects approved since 2021, and tighter rules on US data centres at home could push more
US operators towards Malaysia.

South Korea:  Minutes of the October meeting showed all seven board members backed holding the base rate at 3.00%
after hikes in July and August but retained a tightening bias, with most members leaning hawkish. The Bank of Korea (BoK)
expects growth in 2026 and 2027 to significantly exceed its August forecasts of 3.3% and 2.9% on the semiconductor
upcycle, with inflation also above forecast and core inflation, at 2.8% yoy in September, the central concern. The board
noted that chip bonuses and dividends could lift private consumption by around 0.4%, but add only about 0.1pp to GDP, and
that gains have yet to feed through to broader consumption and jobs. Members also flagged risks from high global long-
term yields and doubts over AI profitability. Another hike in November remains likely.
Taiwan:  Exports rose 58.9% yoy to a record USD 96.8bn in October, the 36th straight month of growth, driven by AI
demand. Information and communications technology (ICT) and audiovisual products rose 72.9% and integrated circuits
63.0%, together making up 83% of exports, while shipments to the US hit a record USD 32.9bn, 34% of the total. The
finance ministry expects November exports to grow 47-51% and full-year exports to exceed USD 900bn for the first time.
Vietnam:  The government raised the annual cap on medium- and long-term foreign commercial borrowing by companies
and banks to USD 10bn from USD 6.1bn, and lifted the permitted growth in short-term foreign debt to 27% from end-2025
levels, from around 19-20%. The decision, effective immediately, amends the 2026 public-debt borrowing plan and gives
borrowers more room to tap offshore funding. The loans carry no government guarantee, and borrowers bear the full
exchange rate and interest rate risk.

On the fiscal side, President Abelardo de la Espriella said Colombia will drop plans to extend value-added tax (VAT) to more
goods and services, despite the government’s need for revenue amid a deepening fiscal crisis. The fiscal adjustment bill will
no longer apply VAT to basic consumer goods and agricultural inputs, and the finance ministry is instead studying a cut in
the headline VAT rate to 17-18% from 19%, the president said in a televised address on Sunday marking his first 100 days in
office. Separately, Fitch said El Niño alone is unlikely to drive rating pressure, with the fiscal adjustment the key factor, while
the finance minister put the cost of rebuilding after the August earthquake at around COP 50trn.
Ecuador:  Fitch named Ecuador among the Latin American economies most exposed to El Niño, warning that a severe event
could bring a repeat of the 2023-24 electricity rationing. The central bank estimates a moderate El Niño would cut 0.5pp
from 2027 growth and a severe one around 1.4pp. Reinstated diesel subsidies are straining the fiscal position, and Fitch
warned that further easing could delay the next IMF review. To shield households from blackouts, the government has cut
large industrial users from the grid, and it trimmed fuel prices by USD 0.05 until 11 December. The 29 November local
elections, the ruling National Democratic Action (ADN) party’s first, will shape fiscal and energy policy.
Mexico:  Headline inflation rose for a third straight month to 3.75% yoy in October from 3.45%, above the 3.65% consensus,
as the end of a summer electricity subsidy and higher fruit and vegetable prices lifted non-core prices. Core inflation
reached 3.80%, keeping inflation well above the Banco de México (Banxico) 3% target a week after its surprise 25bps cut to
6.25%. Separately, Trump threatened to double tariffs on Mexico unless it stops “migrant caravans” heading to the US,
despite the US embassy crediting Mexico last month with cutting border apprehensions by 95%. The market largely ignored
the threat, with the peso little changed, but it marks a return to trade threats and suggests limited US urgency on the United
States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) review. Deputy Finance Minister Bonilla said stronger investment should lift
growth to 2.0% in 2027, in line with the budget.
Peru:  The central bank raised its reference rate by 25bps to 4.50%, its first move since August 2025, as Lima inflation rose
to 5.25% yoy in October, the highest since August 2023 and above the 1-3% target range for an eighth month. Food prices
rose sharply on El Niño-related weather and fuel prices on the Middle East shock, and the board said the price pressures
can no longer be seen as purely temporary, with 12-month inflation expectations at 3.1%. It said the hike does not
necessarily start a tightening cycle, ahead of its next decision on 10 December.

Egypt:  Urban inflation slowed to 12.7% yoy in October from 13.9%, the lowest since January and below JP Morgan’s 13.8%
forecast, helped by a favourable base effect. Monthly core inflation accelerated to 1.2% from 0.8%, however, pointing to
lingering cost pressures in non-food goods and services, and transport prices rose 25.8% yoy. With the overnight deposit
rate at 20.0% after last month’s 100bps hike, the central bank has room to hold at its 17 December meeting.
Nigeria:  S&P said Nigeria’s goal of regaining investment grade by 2029 may be too ambitious, noting that no sovereign has
moved from a single-B rating to investment grade within three years. Nigeria remains five notches below investment grade
at S&P and Fitch, and six at Moody’s. Finance Minister Oyedele set the target last month alongside a return to JP Morgan’s
flagship EM bond index. S&P acknowledged significant reform progress, including the removal of petrol subsidies and the
tax overhaul, which drove its upgrade to ‘B’ in May, the first since 2012. This was followed by positive outlooks from Moody’s
and Fitch and FTSE Russell’s restoration of Nigeria’s frontier-market status in September.
South Africa:  The unemployment rate edged up to 33.7% in Q3 from 33.6%, with the number of unemployed rising by
97,000 to 8.6m as the labour force grew. Employment rose by 101,000 qoq to 16.8m, led by finance, manufacturing and
mining, but was down 215,000 yoy. The expanded unemployment rate, including discouraged job seekers, held at 46.3%.
