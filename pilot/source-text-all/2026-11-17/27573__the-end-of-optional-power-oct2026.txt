Executive Summary 
For about two decades, power demand in developed markets barely grew, and grid investment could be put off 
with few visible consequences. That has changed. The same grid is now expected to support four goals at once: 
rebuilding industry, keeping data and compute at home, protecting households from more extreme weather, and 
reducing dependence on imported energy. Power has gone from a background input to a binding constraint. 
Much of this constraint is a legacy of globalization. Developed economies moved energy-intensive production 
offshore, so their grids were never asked to support a new wave of domestic investment. Flat demand reflected 
that as much as it reflected efficiency gains. Deglobalization, reindustrialization, and data localization have now 
reversed the direction, and the grid was not built for it. 
AI data centers are speeding this up, but they are not the whole story. Their biggest effect is on who gets served 
first. Hyperscalers can pay more and move faster than anyone else, so they take up the remaining room in 
interconnection queues, transformer order books and skilled labor, and slower industrial projects end up waiting. 
Beneath them sits a second, politically durable demand leg from heat, electrification and energy security, which 
does not cycle with AI capex.

1. How the Constraint Was Built: Two Decades of Flat Demand 
For most of the period since the global financial crisis, electricity demand in developed markets barely moved. 
US retail electricity sales peaked in 2007 and stayed roughly flat for the next 15 years, and EU demand is still 
around 10% below its 2008 level.5 The usual explanation is efficiency: LED lighting, better appliances, and more 
efficient air-conditioners and electronics. That is part of the story. 
The other part is what stopped being built. Under globalization, energy-intensive production moved to wherever 
labor, land, and power were cheapest, very often to Asia, and developed-market grids were never asked to support 
a new wave of domestic factories. Flat power demand was, to a large degree, a result of deindustrialization. 
According to the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), electricity sales to industry decreased at an annual 
average rate of 0.7% over 2000–2015, as the sector’s share of total retail electricity sales fell from 31.2% to 
25.8%.6

2. What Changed: Sovereignty Makes Power Demand Intentional Again 
Three things have changed since the 2010s, and none of them is a short cycle. Governments want strategic 
production back at home, they want data and compute inside their borders, and they want to depend less on 
imported fuel. Each turns into requests for grid connections on a network that now has to carry two-way 
renewable flows, very large single-site loads, and sharper weather-driven peaks, with little spare capacity. 
1. Supply-chain security and reindustrialization. The pandemic, the 2022 energy shock, and US-China 
rivalry turned supply-chain resilience into policy. Industrial programs now target semiconductors, 
batteries, defense, chemicals, and other strategic manufacturing across the US, Europe, Japan, Korea, 
and India. A leading-edge fab cluster or a battery gigafactory can draw as much power as a mid-sized city 
and needs it without interruption.  
2. Data sovereignty. Governments increasingly treat data, models, and compute as strategic assets. Data-
residency rules, latency requirements, security reviews, and customer preferences mean compute can 
no longer simply go wherever power is cheapest. It has to be built inside the border, and once built, it 
needs electricity. Beyond the US and China, Japan, Korea, India, the Gulf states, and the EU are all funding 
sovereign compute of their own.  
3. Energy security. Europe’s 2022 gas crisis showed that imported fuel is a geopolitical liability. The near-
closure of the Strait of Hormuz from late February 2026 made the same point for Asia: about a fifth of 
global LNG shipments normally pass through the strait, much of it bound for Asian buyers.7 Generating

The US leads the build-out 
Traditionally, industrial competitiveness comes down to labor cost, logistics, tax, and market access. Nowadays, 
time-to-power is joining that list. A few years ago a developer would pick a location and then ask the utility for a 
connection; today many start with a map of where power is available and work backwards. Governments have 
noticed, and “power-ready” industrial parks with pre-built substations are becoming a selling point. A region that 
can deliver reliable power quickly can win projects without the lowest costs, and a region that cannot may lose 
them despite generous subsidies. 
The US has the clearest intent. It is home to most of the leading frontier AI models and the largest cloud providers. 
Washington openly frames AI as a race with China; its 2025 AI Action Plan was built around winning it.8 In that 
framing, electricity becomes a strategic input: the country that can power the most compute, fastest, has an edge. 
China, which built generation and transmission ahead of demand, does not face the same constraint. US buyers 
pay the most and move first, so qualified equipment, turbine slots and engineering capacity go to the US first. 
Other markets have to wait longer. Tightness elsewhere therefore persists for longer. 
 
3. Business Demand: Data Centers Set the Pace and Jump the Queue 
The usual power trade runs in a straight line: more compute, more data centers, more electricity, higher earnings 
for utilities and equipment makers. The chain is directionally right, but it’s also too narrow. If the power cycle were 
only an AI spike, investor conviction would die the moment hyperscaler capex normalizes. We think two separate 
claims need to be pulled apart. 
AI is the fastest-growing load, but not the only one 
There is no doubt that AI is adding load quickly. The International Energy Agency (IEA) expects global data-center 
electricity use to roughly double by 2030 vs. 2024.9 Goldman Sachs expects global data-center power demand to 
rise about 170% between 2025 and 2030, with more than 60% of the increase in the US.10 What sets AI apart is 
not only the size of the demand but its shape: very large loads, concentrated in a few places, that need full power 
from day one. 
Yet data centers are only one of several sources of growth. Of the 3.5% annual US demand growth Goldman Sachs 
forecasts to 2030, data centers contribute 2.6 percentage points, and the rest comes from homes, offices, 
industry, and transport (Exhibit 3). Reshoring of manufacturing, electric vehicles, heat pumps, and air-
conditioning also contribute to the power demand growth. This matters for how long the cycle lasts. A story built 
only on AI depends on hyperscalers continuing to spend; a story built on several loads at once does not.

Hyperscalers jump the queue 
The bigger effect is on who gets served first. For a hyperscaler, a month of delay on a gigawatt campus costs far 
more than the electricity, and power prices are a small line in its budget. Goldman estimates that paying a 
premium for round-the-clock low-carbon power on all new data-center demand would cost the hyperscalers 
about 3% of their 2028 EBITDA.11 So they prepay for transformers, reserve turbine slots years in advance, fund 
substations, and sign take-or-pay contracts. 
That drains whatever slack the system had. Interconnection studies, transformer order books, turbine capacity 
and electricians all go to the buyer who pays most for time-to-power. A battery plant or chemical facility on thinner 
margins cannot outbid a hyperscaler for the next available substation. This is why the industrial side of the story 
is easy to overlook, and why it represents latent demand that will keep the system tight even if AI spending slows. 
 
4. Household Demand: Peaks, Electrification, and Politics 
Households add a second leg of demand that has little to do with AI and will not fade if hyperscalers slow down. 
Three drivers sit behind it, and each stresses a different part of the grid. 
1. Hotter summers raise the peak. Grids are sized for the busiest hours of the year, and those hours are 
getting busier. A system that looks comfortable over a full year can still run short in a heatwave, a cold 
snap, a dry year for hydro, or a still evening, which are also the moments when an outage does the most 
political damage. Europe is where this has changed fastest. Repeated heatwaves, including an early and 
intense one in May and June 2026, have households buying portable units, often in the same hot week,

2. Electrification loads the local network. Electric vehicles and heat pumps add to total demand, and they

3. Energy security keeps electrification funded. For households, energy security shows up in the bill. After 
Europe’s gas crisis in 2022 and the 2026 Hormuz disruption, governments have leaned harder on 
electrification backed by domestic generation to shield consumers from imported fuel prices. That gives 
it backing from voters who may care little about climate targets. 
All three drivers widen the gap between average and peak demand. Building firm capacity for every extreme event 
is expensive, and so is falling short. Most systems will use a mix of batteries, gas peakers, demand response, 
distributed generation, interconnectors, and better grid software. Batteries are usually discussed as a partner for 
solar and wind, but they are just as useful for shaving peaks. Households are adapting too by installing residential 
power sources and battery storage for backup during outages and protection against rising bills. In aggregate, 
these systems give the grid a small but growing pool of flexibility. 
AI spending will move in cycles and industrial policy will change with elections. Voters will still expect the lights 
and the air-conditioning to work in a heatwave.

Five frictions explain why that gap is slow to close. 
1. Grid timelines are much longer than project timelines. A data center takes two to three years from plan 
to operation; a transmission line can take ten (Exhibit 5). Connecting a new site involves studies, permits, 
land, equipment orders, substation work, and commissioning. At the end of 2024, about 2,300 GW of 
generation and storage projects were waiting in US interconnection queues, and only 13% of the capacity 
that applied between 2000 and 2019 had been built.13 
2. Grid equipment cannot be bought off the shelf. A large power transformer is custom designed, tested 
for months, and expected to last 40 years or more. Because a failure can black out a whole area, utilities 
buy only from suppliers they have qualified, often over several years, and paying more does not get a 
transformer sooner. Lead times for large transformers now run between 2.5 to 4.5 years, with orders 
booked for 2027–28 delivery.14  
3. Permitting depends on institutions. A new transmission line may cross several jurisdictions and face 
local opposition and environmental review. Approvals can take longer than construction, and new factory 
capacity does nothing to speed them up. 
4. Skilled workers are scarce. Goldman estimates the US needs more than 500,000 additional workers to 
meet power demand growth, with over 200,000 of them in transmission and distribution. Even if every 
energy apprentice went to the grid, the US would be about 78,000 transmission and distribution workers 
short by 2030.15 
5. Key materials are concentrated. Transformers need grain-oriented electrical steel (GOES), copper, and 
specialized insulation. GOES is made by a handful of producers worldwide, and the US has a single 
domestic supplier, Cleveland-Cliffs, which caps how fast transformer output can grow.

These problems feed on each other. A shortage of transformers holds up substation upgrades, which slows grid 
connections. Slow connections push developers to build their own power, which adds orders for turbines, 
generators, switchgear, and batteries from the same stretched suppliers, and more on-site generation makes the 
grid harder to coordinate. 
Meanwhile, equipment makers are adding capacity slowly. Many lived through the overcapacity and weak pricing 
of the 2010s, and a new production line takes two to three years to build, qualify, and ramp up. That caution 
supports today’s pricing and margins. An aggressive, industry-wide capacity push would be the clearest sign that 
the cycle is maturing, and it is the signpost we watch most closely. 
 
6. How Users Are Adapting: More Compute per Watt, and Their Own Power 
Faced with slow connections, power users are doing two things: getting more useful output from the power they 
have and generating their own until the grid arrives. Neither ends the shortage, and both create demand for 
equipment. 
More compute from each watt 
Power passes through transformers, switchgear, uninterruptible power supplies (UPS), busways, distribution 
units, and rack-level power supplies before it reaches a processor, and each step loses some of it as heat. For a 
data center with a fixed grid connection, the measure that matters is how much of that power ends up as compute 
it can sell. Hence, the industry is moving toward 800V direct current to the rack, which removes several AC-to-DC 
conversion steps. NVIDIA estimates the design improves end-to-end efficiency by up to 5% and cuts copper use 
by about 45%, because higher voltage carries the same power with less current.16 NVIDIA plans to introduce it 
with its Kyber racks in 2027, at a pace set by standards, safety approvals, and server design. 
Cooling is another critical constraint. The average data-center rack still draws less than 10 kW, but NVIDIA’s 
current GB200 and GB300 racks draw about 120 kW, and its Kyber design for 2027 is planned at around 600 
kW.17,18 Air cannot remove that much heat. NVIDIA says the GB200 generation is more than 90% liquid-cooled, 
and Kyber will be fully liquid-cooled, with no fans.19 Better cooling also leaves more of each grid connection for 
the servers. The industry’s average power usage effectiveness (PUE) has stayed above 1.5 for years, which means 
roughly a third of the power a site draws never reaches its IT equipment.20 New liquid-cooled halls are designed 
to run much closer to 1.0. 
Bringing your own power 
When a project cannot wait years for a utility upgrade, the developer brings its own power (BYOP): aero-derivative 
gas turbines, reciprocating engines, fuel cells, temporary power modules, sites next to existing plants, or a mix of 
on-site generation, batteries, and a later grid connection. 
On-site power began as a bridge to the grid, but it is turning into something more lasting. Communities and 
regulators increasingly expect data centers to accept interruption when the grid is stressed, so much of the

7. Asia: A Demand Region and a Supply Response 
Asia ships transformers and batteries to the West, and it is also building power systems of its own, for its own 
security reasons. Both matter for investors, and the second gets less attention. Asia already uses as much energy 
as the rest of the world combined but produces only about a third of it at home.23 Morgan Stanley expects the 
region’s power demand to grow about 5% a year to roughly 19,000 TWh by 2030, with data-center demand nearly 
tripling from about 310 TWh in 2025 to about 870 TWh. To meet it, Asia’s annual energy investment is set to roughly 
double from the past decade’s average of about US$660 billion, and grid spending alone should reach about US$1 
trillion over 2025–30.24

Demand: Every country wants its own capacity 
The localization logic behind US investment applies across Asia, from different starting points. With data 
sovereignty in play, none of these countries can opt out of the build-out. They will wait behind the US for qualified 
equipment, turbine slots, and engineering capacity, which keeps the market tight for everyone else. 
• 
India is investing on several fronts at once: transmission to bring solar and wind power from Rajasthan 
and Gujarat to the cities, rail electrification, manufacturing clusters supported by production-linked 
incentives, and a data-center market shaped by new data-protection rules. With a target of 500 GW of 
non-fossil capacity by 2030, India’s main constraint is increasingly the grid that links new generation to 
demand.25 
• 
In ASEAN, demand comes from urbanization, rising incomes and first-time air-conditioning, as well as 
factories relocating under China+1 and data localization. Johor’s data-center boom grew partly because 
Singapore had run short of capacity, and the 2023 power cuts in northern Vietnam, which disrupted large 
electronics plants, showed how quickly unreliable supply can hurt a destination.26 
• 
North Asia has mature grids under new pressure. Japan’s power demand forecasts have turned up for the 
first time in decades because of data centers and chip plants.27 Korea’s planned semiconductor mega-
cluster needs gigawatt-scale power and new transmission lines. Taiwan has to keep supply to its fabs 
extremely reliable on thin reserve margins. 
• 
China does not fit the underinvestment story. It built ultra-high-voltage transmission ahead of demand, 
adds more generating capacity each year than any other country, and has comfortable reserve margins in 
most provinces. Even so, it has announced a record RMB 5 trillion (~US$720 billion) of grid investment for 
2026–30, 40% more than in the previous five years, to connect more than 500 GW of new renewables a 
year.28 It also leads the world in solar components, batteries and inverters, and its transformer and 
switchgear makers are winning more orders in the Middle East, Southeast Asia, Latin America, and parts 
of Europe.  
Supply: Who makes what 
Asia’s supply strengths vary by country: 
• 
Korea has heavy electrical equipment makers with long US track records in transformers and switchgear, 
and is building capability in large gas turbines and nuclear components. 
• 
Japan brings grid technology, HVDC, gas turbines, nuclear and high-reliability engineering, along with 
high-voltage cable and power semiconductors. 
• 
Taiwan is strong in power electronics, data-center power systems and cooling, closely tied to the AI server 
supply chain. Its transformer makers are also winning US business. 
• 
India has domestic leaders in transformers, switchgear, cables and engineering, procurement and 
construction (EPC), as well as listed local arms of global grid companies.

8. Investment Implications: Asia’s Potential Listed Beneficiaries 
As power infrastructure becomes central to economic execution, we focus on suppliers with long backlogs and 
technological scale in the bottlenecks described above. The names below are illustrative of each exposure and 
are not recommendations. 
Power Electronics and Advanced Energy Systems 
This focus encompasses the critical components that convert, regulate, and optimize electrical energy within 
high-density facilities, alongside utility-scale energy storage architectures. As computational densities climb and 
renewable generation introduces structural intermittency to regional grids, these systems act as a vital defense 
against power instability. The primary growth driver for these companies is the exponential increase in 
component-level complexity, such as the migration to high-voltage DC and smart power distribution, which 
substantially raises the dollar-content value per project. 
• 
Delta Electronics (Taiwan): A global leader in power management systems and thermal components. 
Delta is well positioned to capture increased dollar-content per data center rack through its advanced 
power blocks, smart busways, and proprietary liquid cooling architectures. 
• 
CATL (China) & Sungrow (China): Key anchors of utility-scale energy storage and clean grid stabilization. 
As intermittent renewable assets scale globally, CATL’s advanced lithium-ion battery architectures and 
Sungrow’s high-capacity power inverters provide the crucial fast-ramping storage capacity needed to 
maintain global grid stability and fulfill 24/7 carbon-free corporate mandates. Geopolitics limits their

High-Growth Regional Grid Infrastructure and Components 
This focus targets companies capturing localized re-industrialization capex within hyper-growth emerging 
markets. Unlike global exporters, these businesses are highly levered to domestic infrastructure mandates, urban 
grid overhauls, and the immediate deployment of localized point-source power systems. The key driver is the 
accelerating shift toward BYOP architectures, where factories and data center operators purchase localized 
generation assets to run independently of backlogged utility connections.

9. What Could Make Us Wrong 
A long-term thesis is only useful if it says what would break it. Four risks follow from that. 
1. A pause in AI spending. If hyperscalers cut back, queue pressure would ease and the most AI-exposed 
stocks would likely de-rate. The broader drivers in this paper would remain, but the market is unlikely to 
separate them in the short term. 
2. Too much new supply. Korean, Japanese, Indian, Chinese, and Western equipment makers have all 
announced capacity that comes online around 2027–28. If expansion turns aggressive, or Chinese 
suppliers win approval in markets that now exclude them, prices could fall faster than backlogs imply. 
3. Pushback on bills and sites. Grid investment ends up in household bills, and community opposition over 
power, water, and noise is already delaying US projects. In September 2026, Loudoun County in Virginia, 
the world’s largest data-center market, voted to advance a 12-month pause on new data-center 
applications. Large-load tariffs, interruptible contracts and state audits slow approvals; they delay 
demand more than they remove it. 
4. Valuation. Many grid-equipment leaders trade on multiples that assume the shortage lasts for years, so 
stock selection matters more than owning the theme. 
 
Conclusion: The Wire Is the Bottleneck 
For most of the past two decades, investors could treat electricity as a utility bill. That no longer holds for a factory 
that cannot get connected, a data center waiting in a queue, or a household facing a summer peak. The physical 
response, in wires, transformers, turbines, and storage, runs on timelines of five to ten years, longer than most 
investment cycles and longer than the current AI boom has existed. 
That mismatch is the opportunity, and much of the equipment that closes it is made in Asia. The bottleneck is no 
longer only the chip or the factory floor. It is the wire.
