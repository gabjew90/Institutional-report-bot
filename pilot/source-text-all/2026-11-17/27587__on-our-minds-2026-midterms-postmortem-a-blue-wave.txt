2026 Midterms Postmortem: A Blue Wave 
 
The midterms delivered a comfortable Democratic victory, with the party winning at 
least 54 Senate seats and more than 235 House seats, while two Senate races and 
several dozen House races remain uncalled. Affordability remained at the center of 
voters' concerns heading into Election Day, as Republicans lost key red-state races in 
South Carolina, Texas, Kansas, and Iowa. 
 
A Blue Wave 
Voters came out to polls yesterday with affordability concerns acting as an overarching 
theme. Democrats won control of the Senate, taking over even key red states in numbers. 
The final run of the SG election model predicted Democrats to win around 51-52 seats, 
although that final did signal a higher likelihood of an above-52 seats results compared to 
preceding week. Notwithstanding that, the winning party beat polls and expectations 
handsomely by gaining 54 seats, with Alaska and Mississippi yet to be called – see the two 
charts below.

More of an interesting note is the results around the local elections that took place. Midterm 
elections for House and Senate are always a statement about the President’s popularity and 
public’s satisfaction, which this time around clearly seeped into the local elections. Texas 
surprised with a Democratic governor, but the state legislature in many contested states still 
needs to be determined. The Republicans hoped for a greater voter turnout in their favor, but 
that failed to materialize in deep red states like South Carolina, fuelling their loss.  
Regulation Uncertainty 
Historically, with a political gridlock like now after yesterday’s results, policy becomes more 
uncertain. As Democrats take over Congressional control, many negotiations and policies 
that the Republican party has already passed will come into question. For example, for the 
negotiations around debt ceiling increase, Democrats can demand reversal for many of the 
policies passed through the One Big Beautiful Bill. Although no new legislation is likely to 
pass from Congress due to the filibuster in the Senate as well as presidential veto power, 
there are likely to be many hearings and testimonies with House oversight committees likely

US Growth Outlook  
With a Democrat Congressional control, there has been uncertainty around what the fiscal 
policy for the final two years of President’s term could look like. Historically, primary deficits 
(as percentage of potential GDP) became less loose when the President’s party lost control of 
the House. A House loss for the current administration has been a key assumption for our 
GDP outlook for 2026-2027, with a positive fiscal impulse driven a major growth acceleration 
this year, but with the loss of the House after the midterms taking fiscal policy discretion out 
of the picture for the remainder of this presidential term. Consequently, this year’s fiscal 
impulse will be a one-off with the impact waning going into 2027.

In terms of growth this means in our outlook that GDP in 2026 is to have above trend growth 
at 2.6% (Q4/Q4) due to the large 1H boost from larger-than-usual tax refunds and OBBBA, 
with 2nd round effects from the fiscal impulse keeping consumption and business investment 
spending elevated in 2H. But by the end of 2026 growth is projected to slow, as this fiscal 
impulse wanes and Fed gradually tightens its policy stance. In 1H 2027 GDP growth will slow 
below trend, with 2027 GDP growth ending up at 1.7% (Q4/Q4) – just below our trend GDP 
growth rate for 2027 (1.8%).  
 
Overall Assessment 
As expected, the Democrats gain control of both Chambers of Congress, although the extend 
of their victory was larger than expected. Nonetheless, the likely sizes of their majorities in 
the House and Senate will not be enough to either circumvent the Senate filibuster nor 
presidential veto power. Gridlock will be the main outcome of these midterms, where further 
discretionary fiscal policy measures are unlikely to materialize during the remainder of this 
presidential term. Instead, we will likely see a pro-active House in terms hearings and 
subpoenas of current and former administration officials and business executives, whereas 
the administration will double down on areas where the executive branch has still a lot of 
power relative to Congress, such as foreign policy and tariffs.
