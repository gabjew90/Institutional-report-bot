 
Port Of LA Imports Spike 58% As US Restocking 
Binge Peaks: Goldman's Maps The Coming Freight 
Hangover 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, NOV 13, 2026 - 06:25 PM 
The freight bull market isn't dead yet. But according to Goldman, it just had its best month. 
In September the Port of Los Angeles handled 1.04 million TEUs, the busiest month in its 
118-year history, as holiday goods hit the docks (per Reuters) and importers rushed to refill 
depleted shelves. October and November were shaping up to be even hotter: on a 4-week 
basis, inbound boxes into LA were up almost 60% yoy heading into November! 
Which is why Goldman's transport team led by Patrick Creuset, which every quarter puts out its 
must-read "Around the world in 80 charts" mega pack (available to pro subs), opened this 
week's edition with a two-word verdict: "Freight bull market continues in Q4." Then it spent much 
of the next 80 charts explaining why the party is about to wind down. 
Regular readers will recall we have been tracking Creuset's high-frequency trade data since he 
first introduced it in September 2025, and back in March we used his pack to show the Middle 
East shockwave rippling through energy, air travel and freight networks. Eight months later the 
shockwave has morphed into something else: a US import boom running at restocking-cycle 
peaks, rates into America near COVID-era highs, and a wall of new ships set to arrive just as 
the boom fades. 
 

 
 
Let's take a closer look at the dozen or so charts that matter most. 
Restock Everything 
The headline number is strong almost wherever you look: global air and ocean freight volume 
growth "remains strong around HSD% on our latest readings," Creuset writes. The drivers: 
The underlying drivers remain, on the supply side, Chinese manufacturing growth, and on the 
demand side, the positive inflection in the US inventory cycle since Q1 and growing 
datacenter-related volumes. Chinese port volumes have rebounded strongly 10-20% yoy in 
Sep/Oct catching up on earlier weather related disruptions. 
Mirroring the Chinese rebound, Goldman counts a roughly 20% yoy jump in US West Coast 
import volumes in recent months, with October's estimate built on IMF Portwatch data. 
 

 
 
Zoom in on LA alone and it gets wild. Goldman's own title for the chart below is "Huge spike in 
imports into LA this fall," and for once the sellside is underselling it: the 4-week average of LA 
imports and future vessel schedules peaked at roughly +58% yoy before easing back to around 
+30% (both read off the chart, so approximate). 
 
 

 
Where is all this stuff going? Into warehouses. The Logistics Managers' Index for inventory 
levels just posted its fastest month-on-month expansion since September 2022, i.e., the tail end 
of the post-COVID glut that ended in tears (and a lot of discounted patio furniture). 
 
Put differently, after a year of tariff whiplash in which importers front-ran, then froze, US 
companies are now restocking as fast as they did at the 2020/21 and 2024 peaks. 
The Hangover Is Already On The Calendar 
Which brings us to the problem with peaks. In Goldman's words: 
The current US import surge looks to start tapering off during Nov at growth levels consistent 
with prior re-stocking cycle peaks in 2024 (pre tariffs) and 2020/21 (surge in goods vs. services 
spend). That said, an earlier Chinese New Year in 2027 should keep volumes well-supported 
from a seasonal point of view into year-end. 
Creuset shows the last three major restocking cycles side by side, and the current spike is 
sitting right where the prior ones rolled over: 
 

 
 
And after year-end? Goldman lists three headwinds for Q1 2027: i) a maturing restocking cycle, 
ii) the seasonal payback from an early Chinese New Year, and iii) a capex cycle that the bank's 
US economists model as "cont'd positive but sequentially lower" in 2027. Meanwhile US 
retailers themselves are guiding lower: the NRF's latest import forecast has volumes going from 
+13% yoy in September to roughly -11% by March. 
 
 

 
The spot market appears to have gotten the memo early. Per Drewry's latest World Container 
Index, as reported by The Loadstar, Shanghai-Los Angeles rates just posted their steepest 
one-week drop since June 2025, falling 6% to $7,514 per 40ft box, while Shanghai-New York 
slid 5% to $9,631, with Drewry citing the NRF's forecast of an 11% month-on-month drop in 
November imports. 
Translation: the boxes ordered for Christmas are already on the water, and nobody is ordering 
the next batch. 
COVID-Era Rates... But Only If You're Shipping To America 
For now, though, US-bound shippers are still paying through the nose. As we noted back in July 
when Asia-US rates first blew through $7,900, and again in September when ocean freight 
costs exploded to rival COVID-era crisis highs (BofA's Lorraine Hutchinson flagged a 201% 
jump in rates, closing in on the 250% spike of the 2021 boxship shortage), the transpacific has 
been the place to be if you own ships. Back in August we put it more bluntly: 
Goldman's container section is titled, appropriately, "Current trading strong, outlook weaker." 
The China Containerized Freight Index spot rate has spent the fall around $3,600-3,700 per 
box, back to levels last seen after the Red Sea crisisand within sight of the 2021-22 mania: 
 
But the averages hide a split. Rates into the US are "close to COVID-era highs reflecting the 
import volume surge," with Far East-US West Coast around $8,300/FEU and the East Coast 
 

 
near $10,000 (approximate, read off Clarksons data), while rates into Europe and LatAm have 
declined. 
 
Why is Europe cheaper? Because the ships are coming back through Suez. Goldman notes that 
on Asia-Europe, "capacity is being freed up by a fast-moving return of services to the 
Suez-route." Southbound containership transits, which collapsed some 90% after the Houthis 
turned the Red Sea into a shooting gallery in late 2023, are now only around 30% below 2022 
levels, and northbound transits are back near par based on early-November data. 
 

 
 
Cutting thousands of miles off every Asia-Europe voyage is the equivalent of conjuring new 
ships out of thin air. The only thing still soaking up that slack is friction: port congestion remains 
elevated, ships are steaming slower to save fuel, and Goldman's measure of liner network 
speed (port calls minus fleet growth) is roughly 30% worse than in 2019. 
 
In other words, a big chunk of today's "tight" market is ships stuck in queues. Clear the queues 
and the slack shows up all at once. 
 

 
AI Takes The Plane 
If ocean is the restocking story, air cargo is the AI story. Goldman's air section is titled "Strong 
market driven by tech/DC-related volume," and the numbers back it up: air cargo rates are up 
roughly 30% yoy into year-end, with Middle East & South Asia origins up around 60% as the 
war continues to choke Gulf capacity. 
 
Rising load factors (demand growing faster than capacity) explain the move in rates, but the 
more interesting chart is the one tying Asian air freight to chips. Taiwan's air cargo tracks 
semiconductor IC unit growth, and the latter is running above 20% yoy. GPUs, servers and 
networking gear for datacenters are high value, time-sensitive and light enough to fly, which 
makes the 747 freighter the hyperscalers' favorite delivery truck. 
 

 
 
Which also means that air cargo is now one more asset riding the AI capex cycle, so if the 
capex impulse slows in 2027 as Goldman's economists model, air freight is levered to it. 
Diversification at its finest. 
44% Of The Global Fleet Is On Order 
And now for the chart that should keep shipping investors up at night. While everyone was busy 
celebrating record rates, the carriers did what carriers always do with record profits: they 
ordered ships. Lots of them. 
 

 
 
Goldman's title says it all: "44% of existing global capacity now on order, most of which for 
delivery in the next three years." That is the highest orderbook-to-fleet ratio since the 2008-09 
bust (and we all know how that cycle ended). Napkin math: with the global fleet around 34-35 
million TEU, roughly 15 million TEU of new boxships are coming, approximately the size of the 
entire global fleet back in 2010. 
Shipping companies "remain on a buying spree" according to Goldman, scrapping is minimal, 
and the idle fleet is near zero, so there is no buffer. The timing is the kicker: 
From a bigger picture, we're currently at an inflection point of the ocean capacity cycle (low point 
in Q426), which is set to accelerate during 2027, before slowing again during 2029. 
 

 
 
So to summarize: demand from US restocking peaks in November, the Suez route frees up 
capacity on the biggest east-west trade, and net fleet growth re-accelerates through 2027. 
Xeneta's Peter Sand recently told The Loadstar that the Red Sea is pivotal for 2027 and that "if 
there is a full return, freight rates could plummet." Goldman, for its part, still sees "forwards 
remain around current rate levels on Asia-Europe," and charter rates "remain firm." For now. 
One market where the supply cliff is further off: crude tankers. VLCC rates have gone parabolic 
as the Hormuz war rewired oil flows, something we have been tracking since before it was cool, 
and as Goldman's commodity team showed last month, more oil is now leaving the Gulf than 
before the war, and the freight bill is shocking. But even here, the VLCC orderbook has jumped 
from near zero to over 30% of the fleet, and "implies growth will accelerate." 
 

 
 
Nothing Cures Record Rates Like Record Ships 
Goldman's own summary is careful to keep both feet in the bull camp for now: volumes growing 
HSD%, "air cargo rates remain strong into year-end," and an early Chinese New Year to 
cushion the seasonal slowdown. Fair enough. Q4 will probably be fine. 
But look at the setup into 2027: a restocking cycle that has already hit the same growth levels 
where the last three rolled over; US retailers forecasting import declines; inventories growing at 
the fastest pace in four years; a capex impulse that is slowing at the margin; Suez reopening; 
and an orderbook equal to 44% of the existing fleet, with net capacity growth re-accelerating 
right as demand fades. Every one of those arrows points the same way, and we suspect the 
"outlook weaker" half of Goldman's container headline is the half that will age best. 
Then again, in shipping (as in everything else), nothing cures record rates like record rates. 
Here is what record rates bought: the biggest quarter of new containership orders ever, roughly 
2.2 million TEU (read off the chart).
