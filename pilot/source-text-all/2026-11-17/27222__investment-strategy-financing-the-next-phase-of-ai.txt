The AI narrative has largely been defined by demand. Whether discussing enterprise adoption, infrastructure 
buildout or returns on investment, investors have spent the past two years debating how large the opportunity 
could become. Increasingly, however, attention is shifting toward a different question: How will the next stage of 
growth be financed?
Recent earnings reports suggest the underlying economics remain supportive. While investor attention has 
focused on large capital raises and share price weakness among some hyperscalers, companies continue to 
reaffirm plans for significant increases in AI-related spending next year. Cloud and AI businesses continue 
to generate strong growth and attractive returns, while many of the operational challenges facing these 
companies are concentrated in slower-growing legacy businesses rather than in AI itself. Incremental AI-related 
investments also appear to be generating returns above existing corporate averages, supporting management 
teams’ willingness to continue investing despite rising funding requirements.
The more pressing issue today is financing rather than fundamentals. While the economic case for AI 
investment remains largely intact, the scale of capital required to sustain the buildout is becoming increasingly 
difficult to ignore. Across credit markets, the common theme is not deteriorating credit quality, but the growing 
challenge of absorbing an exceptional volume of issuance over a relatively short period of time.
The investment-grade market provides perhaps the clearest example. Hyperscalers now represent a rapidly 
growing share of the investment-grade universe, and investors are demanding increasingly large concessions to 
absorb new issuance. Recent debt offerings have generally been well received, but order books have become 
less oversubscribed and borrowing costs have gradually increased as investors seek additional compensation 
for growing concentration and supply risk. This appears to be a technical rather than a fundamental issue, 
reflecting the practical challenge of financing one of the largest capital investment cycles in modern history.
Similar dynamics are beginning to emerge across other financing channels. In asset-backed securities (ABS), 
investor demand for data center exposure has become increasingly saturated despite generally healthy 
asset performance. New transactions have frequently required wider spreads, shorter maturities or structural 
changes to attract capital. Likewise, leveraged finance markets have absorbed substantial volumes of AI-related 
issuance over the past year, yet investors have become increasingly selective as future funding needs continue 
to expand. Private credit markets have also adopted a more cautious stance, particularly toward technology-
related lending, reflecting a broader reassessment of risk across capital markets.
Importantly, there is little indication that these financing challenges are being driven by weaker end-demand. 
Hyperscalers continue to pursue ambitious expansion plans, NVIDIA chip pricing remains firm and rising, and 
activity levels across the broader AI ecosystem remain robust. Evidence from private markets suggests that 
suppliers supporting utility infrastructure, grid expansion and data center construction continue to experience 
significant pricing power and elevated profitability, highlighting the strength of demand flowing through the 
system.
The challenge, therefore, increasingly lies on the supply side of capital rather than the demand side of compute. 
Higher financing costs, investor concentration limits and a more selective funding environment are likely to 
increase the cost of future projects. As a result, the pace of AI infrastructure expansion may moderate from the 
extraordinary levels observed over the past two years. In our view, however, this should be viewed as a new 
phase of slower and more sustainable growth, rather than a reversal of the investment cycle. The adjustment 
process of this downshift in rate of investment can be painful for the market, but it sets up a longer cycle and 
lowers the risk of overbuild. 
Ultimately, the discussion reinforced a theme that has emerged repeatedly throughout this year’s AI debates. 
The primary risks facing the sector are becoming less about demand and more about execution, financing

and infrastructure. While the rate of growth is likely to decelerate as capital becomes more expensive and 
funding markets become more selective, there is little evidence to suggest that the broader AI investment 
cycle is nearing an end. Rather, the market appears to be transitioning from a period in which demand was the 
primary focus to one in which financing capacity is becoming an increasingly important consideration. The AI 
investment cycle remains intact, but its future trajectory is likely to be influenced as much by capital availability 
as by underlying demand. Such an environment favors the largest and strongest balance sheets, and may 
become increasingly challenging for smaller, leveraged players. Active credit selection takes center stage.

The U.S. economy is performing well. While its base remains relatively narrow, 
stemming from AI and the wealthiest 20% of consumers, there are hints of a 
broadening. The labor market remains quite strong, with lower-wage workers 
seeing the most rapid increase in wage growth. 
We expect the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) to raise rates in 
December and once more in 2027. Chair Kevin Warsh seems content to 
move deliberately, if not slowly. Meanwhile, oil prices appear likely to remain 
elevated well into 2027, versus prior forecasts. This would be growth-positive 
for the U.S. and other oil exporters, growth-negative for oil importers. Inflation 
risks, therefore, remain elevated globally, with developed markets continuing 
their hiking cycle. We expect two more hikes by the Bank of Japan (BoJ), the 
European Central Bank (ECB) and the Federal Reserve. Markets are pricing in 
the end of the hiking cycle via the two-year Treasury note. 
European growth is expected to be stable or slightly up into 2027, with France 
an obvious area of concern. Japan is also likely to see a modest improvement 
in growth. A risk is the potential for more dovish BoJ appointments.

Recent market weakness increasingly resembles a classic October risk-off 
correction rather than the start of a broader economic downturn. Investors 
are reassessing AI-related capital spending, higher interest rates, geopolitical 
risks and policy uncertainty. The result has been wider credit spreads, 
moderating equity valuations and a flight to quality in rates markets. 
Beneath the volatility, the fundamental backdrop remains resilient. Corporate 
earnings are constructive, consumer credit performance is stable and default 
rates are low across most sectors. We see the potential for volatility to 
persist over the coming weeks. But the broader growth trend remains intact, 
supported by a resilient economy and ongoing AI-driven investment. With the 
10-year Treasury still near 5% and the Fed expected to tighten only gradually, 
all-in yields are attractive across most credit markets for longer-term 
investors. We are mindful of the limits to that view. Inflation remains elevated, 
fiscal deficits are large and the capital needs of power, data centers and other 
infrastructure argue for a structurally higher term premium. We therefore 
expect any rally to be range-bound rather than the start of a sustained decline 
in yields.

CS 3.5 (unchanged) 
We are bullish on the U.S. dollar, reflecting Fed hikes and oil-related risks 
from Iran re-escalation post-midterms. While the dollar remains rich by both 
historical standards and recent ranges, near-term catalysts are likely to keep it 
strong, particularly against the euro. The latter remains under pressure as the 
end of the ECB hiking cycle comes into view and the risk premium from French 
credit spreads persists going into next year’s election. Low-yielder status also 
makes the euro a strong funder candidate if and when investors re-engage in 
carry trades.
The Brazilian election triggered a sharp selloff in the Brazilian real (BRL), yet 
high carry and potential signals about fiscal execution should keep the currency 
supported into 2027.

October was a volatile month for EMBI and CEMBI spreads. After a sharp sell-
off in late September driven by core rates, spreads recovered early in the month 
following softer data. By mid-October, however, spreads had once again moved 
wider on macro factors, with higher oil prices, AI concerns and escalating 
geopolitical risks in the Middle East all contributing to greater fears of weaker 
growth and higher inflation. In line with expectations, investment-grade credits 
outperformed high yield, with BBs outperforming Bs. Consensus overweights in 
sovereigns such as Argentina and credits with idiosyncratic challenges such as 
Cameroon and Gabon have performed worse. Countries, including South Africa, 
that have taken positive macroeconomic measures performed well. 
We continue to believe that underlying country fundamentals are robust, but 
the easy improvements are now behind us. The recent IMF meetings shifted 
attention toward preserving the improvements in credit metrics made following 
the 2020/2022 shocks. Improved funding visibility, stronger buffers and more 
credible policy frameworks have ensured that even lower-rated credits retain 
access to the market. However, with competition for capital increasing and 
external funding needs rising, partly due to higher oil prices, the market is 
looking for countries that are maintaining fiscal discipline and ensuring policy 
sustainability. Countries that remain on an improving trajectory or are taking 
credit-positive measures should outperform going forward. 
When we look at corporate fundamentals, credit trends remain broadly neutral, 
rating agency trends are broadly positive and default expectations are declining. 
On the technical side, primary issuance has slowed down, resulting in positive 
net financing, but the market continues to absorb supply. Overall, we continue to 
remain constructive on emerging markets corporates. 
In October, local currency assets suffered as domestic bond yields rose and a 
stronger U.S. dollar provided a headwind. Country performance was diverse. 
Brazil underperformed following the surprise re-election of President Luiz Inácio 
Lula da Silva. Frontier names including Uganda and Egypt, which have attracted 
considerable investor attention recently, underperformed on concerns that higher 
oil prices will cause their terms of trade to deteriorate. Nigeria has performed 
better than other popular names due to its refined and crude oil exports.
President Lula’s victory marked the first time since Brazil’s return to democracy 
that a candidate who lost the first round of a presidential election went on to 
win the second round. This surprised hard currency, local currency and foreign 
exchange markets, where long Brazil positions were a consensus overweight on 
expectations that a Flávio Bolsonaro victory would be credit-positive. Instead, 
markets now face concerns that without ambitious fiscal reforms, debt dynamics 
are likely to remain challenging during President Lula’s fourth administration 
given high interest rates and weaker growth. Our base case is that Lula initially 
adopts a moderate posture, seeking to reassure investors through his cabinet 
appointments and a limited fiscal adjustment package. Brazil’s strong external 
position and substantial reserve buffers mean that any sell-off in hard-currency 
assets should be relatively contained and if valuations widen excessively, we will 
view this as a buying opportunity.

CS 3.0 (unchanged)
The balance of factors driving growth is shifting from consumers to 
investment. Consumer spending is slowing as tax rebates and fiscal support 
fade, leaving AI investment as the main engine of growth and earnings into 
2027. U.S. activity remains firm and a solid base for the next phase.
The next test is whether AI funding and adoption can keep pace with the 
computing power being built. Hyperscalers have gone from funding their 
infrastructure with free cash flow to long IG debt, and now they appear to 
be broadening out to funding with equity. With capex at some hyperscalers 
already exceeding operating cash flow, spending should continue next year 
at a slower pace. Token demand is still forecast to grow about 385% in 2026 
and 126% in 2027, and a falling cost per token should broaden adoption 
and lift returns over time. As a result, expect a more selective AI trade, not a 
bust. Leveraged and richly priced names in semiconductors and neoclouds 
look most exposed; returns should favor companies that can fund their own 
buildouts. The focus is shifting from capex levels to the return on invested 
capital, and early margin gains are appearing. We still see 2027 as the likely 
inflection point, when AI productivity gains outweigh buildout-related inflation. 
Overall, we remain neutral with a positive bias. Risks are building: a Fed hiking 
cycle, tight energy supplies, political resistance to AI and financing strains. 
Forward earnings and an S&P 500 Index at 18.7x offset those risks for now. 
Being neutral lets us position either way as the facts develop, while we keep 
our conviction in the long-term AI cycle, which stays constructive for risk 
assets over the medium term.
What to watch ahead: the path of real rates; Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon 
capex and funding plans; evidence of AI returns on investment; and the 
impact of the new Congress and other political changes as a result of the U.S. 
midterm elections.

Chris Pettine
Research Analyst, 
Global Equities
CS 3.0 (unchanged)
AI-related spending and infrastructure buildouts have remained strong, 
with companies citing no weakness in demand and continued improvement 
in visibility. Investors increasingly are focused on the risk of spending 
decelerating in 2028, uncertain ROI, financing concerns, political pushback 
and power constraints. The recent White House AI Summit yielded a 
commitment to self-regulation. 
U.S. consumers remain quite healthy and continue to spend despite higher 
gasoline prices. Bank loan growth is relatively strong and credit is solid. 
Potential for AI to reduce deposit switching costs would be disruptive. 
Industrial stocks have underperformed on macro fears and rates despite solid 
industrial activity and strong bottom-up fundamentals. Healthcare stocks 
remain inconsistent, with the most recent outperformance in life science tools, 
driven by increasing AI adoption in drug discovery. 
We remain close to our benchmark in terms of top-down risks such as lifecyle 
categories, allowing us to look for bottom-up alpha opportunities.

Taras Shumelda

Equities
CS 2.75 (unchanged)
Our main themes — the conflict in Iran and the risk of a peaking in AI-driven 
capex — remain unchanged.
In India, demand continues to be strong despite inflationary pressures, 
the start of a rate-hike cycle and external challenges. Bank credit growth 
continues. In China, some consumer sectors are showing signs of 
stabilization, but not enough to make the overall picture more exciting. We are 
monitoring earnings risk due to a spike in raw material costs and appreciation 
in the renminbi. In terms of sectors, the team is selective on the AI supply 
chain and positive on upstream materials and semiconductor equipment. We 
are looking to diversify into select financials and consumer exposures. 
In Latin America, President Lula’s unexpected win upended markets and made 
some investment theses less convincing. We are awaiting an announcement 
about the administration’s economic team, but our mindset concerning Brazil 
is defensive. In the near term, investing in Brazil is too dependent on the 
macro picture, which in turn is shaped overwhelmingly by politics and policy. 
EMEA has just begun its earnings reporting and investors will continue to 
digest the results of the U.S. midterm elections and what they will mean for 
the peace process in Ukraine. 
The team sees attractive investment opportunities in the industrial, consumer, 
technology and financial sectors of emerging markets. We are less upbeat on 
utilities and real estate. In the light of the recent dislocations in Brazil, we are 
also reviewing earnings sensitivity and likely earnings forecast changes to 
many Brazilian stocks.
