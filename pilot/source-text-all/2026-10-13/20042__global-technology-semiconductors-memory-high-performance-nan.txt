Background - Why is there a need for High-Bandwidth Flash? 
 
 
The shift to inference requires much near-compute storage capacity, and HBF’s 
similar sequential read bandwidth vs. HBM positions it well as a potential solution 
to the memory constraint given NAND’s high density.  As more AI workflows shift 
away from compute-heavy training and toward memory-intensive inference, context 
windows are lengthening exponentially and scaling toward 10 mn+ tokens from a ~1.5 
mn baseline (see Exhibit 1).  The KV (key value) cache required for these more 
memory-intensive workloads is growing with larger context windows, broadly shifting 
the memory bottleneck from purely bandwidth-related to a combined 
bandwidth-capacity constraint.  Although we believe DRAM-based High Bandwidth 
Memory (HBM) - and its subsequent iterations - is likely to lead in bandwidth and 
performance, the technology is significantly capacity-constrained (current architectures 
typically include ~192 GB per GPU) given current DRAM supply constraints.  AI 
infrastructure operators are thus deploying additional GPU units to drive incremental 
compute-adjacent memory capacity, resulting in meaningfully underutilized compute 
capacity.  Through a strategic collaboration, SanDisk and SK Hynix have introduced High 
Bandwidth Flash (HBF) as a proposed solution to this near-compute capacity constraint.

we note that (1) HBM4E is currently in the sampling phase and delivers at least ~50% 
higher bandwidth; (2) although inference workloads are read-dominant overall, any 
write operations significantly reduces the effective bandwidth of HBF given its 
NAND-based design; (3) HBF’s structurally lower write performance and endurance 
relative to DRAM-based HBM results in its practical benefits being highly dependent 
on its read performance keeping pace with the latest generations of HBM. 
Variability of read/write ratio in inference: As context windows expand and 
n
workloads shift to read-heavy inference, occasional write operations in KV cache 
remain essential: if no prefix cache hit occurs (i.e., the prompt / prefix is not found in 
the existing KV cache), the read/write ratio is effectively zero.  Although this ratio will 
likely become increasingly rare over time and inference remains highly 
read-dominant, the variability of write operation frequency (and the resulting 
meaningful bandwidth penalty for NAND-based HBF) could render the effective 
bandwidth of HBF unpredictable in the early phases of any rollout. 
Endurance, energy intensity, and thermal concerns: Because it is based on NAND 
n
flash, HBF tolerates a finite number of program/erase cycles - whereas DRAM-based 
HBM offers far greater write endurance.  Also, the higher density of a HBF cube 
result drives higher energy consumption per stack (~160 W per cube in SK Hynix’s H3 
architecture, ~4X more power than HBM3E vs. ~2.5X more power than HBM4). Given 
the peak power of most flagship accelerators of ~1,000-1,200 W, and the utilization 
of 8 HBF stacks in H3, the power footprint of HBF alone can easily approach the 
accelerator’s own constraints and result in new thermal issues. This excess heat may 
further accelerate wear on the flash cells and complicate lifespan considerations. 
Manufacturing resource intensity: As with HBM, HBF uses design changes to 
n
maximize bandwidth, including increased internal structural parallelism and 3D 
packaging methods. It also uses ultra-wide on-package interconnect to alleviate the 
external data bottleneck.  These advanced packaging techniques, the use of hybrid 
bonding, and the ultra-wide interconnect all contribute to a more resource-intensive 
and higher-cost manufacturing process, including a significantly higher wafer 
consumption (most estimates suggest a 3X-4X higher cost relative to traditional 
NAND, similar to the DRAM/HBM “trade ratio”). 
In aggregate, we view HBF’s potential bandwidth limitations and resource intensity as 
the most significant challenges facing wide adoption of the technology.  As context 
windows lengthen and the share of inference workflows grows, we see improved 
visibility on the read/write ratio in KV cache and on pricing as key variables.

We are constructive on the broad set of NAND-based technologies set to address 
memory constraints in AI inference - and see alternative approaches such as XL 
Flash and zNAND-O ultimately driving similar implications.  While HBF remains one 
of the leading approaches to addressing current AI system memory constraints given 
public backing from SanDisk and SK Hynix (and ongoing development from Samsung) 
and significant peer-reviewed publications, other approaches have emerged, including

“XL Flash.”  Introduced by Kioxia and built on the company’s BiCS Flash 3D NAND 
architecture, the approach includes key architectural and design differences relative to 
HBF which could potentially offer easier packaging integration and/or lower cost, 
potentially at the expense of some performance.  We would also highlight Samsung’s 
development of zNAND-O - similar in concept such as using TSV to connect 4 or 8 layer 
vertical stacks of V-NAND dies, while targeting a different market segment in on-device 
AI (smartphones, PCs, etc.).  Samsung is also developing zHBM for data centers where it 
stacks the memory on top of the AI accelerator rather than next to it, to improve the 
performance and power efficiency.  Although it is too early to tell which approach might 
come to market first, we believe that all three approaches are ultimately aimed at 
solving the “memory wall” problem by applying NAND-based system approaches to 
increase context length while offering significant potential cost savings at the system 
level. 
XL flash may hold write, cost, and certain other advantages over HBF, although 
based on publicly-available literature HBF appears well-suited for read-heavy AI 
inference applications.  XL Flash utilizes standard PCIe, NVMe, and CXL system buses, 
but relies on less dense SLC-based NAND, smaller transfer blocks, and larger die 
architectures relative to traditional 3D NAND flash to maximize bandwidth, significantly 
lower random read latency, and meaningfully increase write endurance; characteristics 
that may render XL Flash better suited for write operations relative to HBF.  The 
technology’s reliance on existing approaches to 3D NAND manufacturing, its lack of TSVs 
(through-silicon vias, required for the stacking associated with HBF), and physical

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
