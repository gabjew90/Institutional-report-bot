ARE WE IN AN ERA OF FISCAL
DOMINANCE?
Dario Perkins
12 Oct 2026
Regular readers will know that I have a habit of getting myself into trouble on social media. Things
have gotten so bad that now, when we get company-wide advice on how to be a “good corporate
representative”, I can’t help but feel this unsolicited guidance is aimed at me personally. Anyway,
this time it wasn’t moron risk premia or Chinese SUVs and recycled Skoda jokes from the 1980s
that got me into trouble, it was my contribution to the debate about “fiscal dominance”.
1/8
The macro community on social media (especially X) is obsessed with
“fiscal dominance”. On those rare occasions when it gets tired of AI
boosterism (or belittling Europe), there is nothing it loves more than a
conspiracy theory about how the Fed and the US Treasury are in
cahoots to suppress government borrowing costs. But there is a
problem – most pundits don’t have any idea what fiscal dominance
(FD) is. Cue my innocuous contribution - which made a lot of macro
charlatans extremely mad. 

2/8
That definition triggered four or five days of intense debate. And when I
say “debate”, I mostly just mean people calling me various versions of
the word “idiot” (or worse). But my definition was correct. To be in an era
of fiscal dominance, the Fed must SURRENDER ITS INFLATION TARGET
– either because raising interest rates will make inflation worse, or
because the Treasury wont allow it to hike. (You may remember the
period immediately after WW2 when the Fed's main job was just to cap
yields.)
3/8
It is clearly not the case that the Treasury won’t allow the Fed to hike. I
mean, the central bank literally just hiked – and yes, right before the
midterms – despite all the macro gurus of X telling me for months that
this couldn’t happen. And it now looks like the Fed will keep raising
interest rates. But the modus operandi of social media is obviously to
never let the FACTS get in the way of a good conspiracy theory.

4/8
But that still leaves the other for of FD, where raising rates makes the
inflation situation worse. Weirdly, this idea is suddenly popular again,
among pundits on both sides of the political divide. On the right, we
have the deficit hawks and the perma equity bulls. Because public debt
is large, they argue that monetary tightening makes the deficit worse,
injecting more money into the system. And on the left, we have the
MMT crowd, who have always argued that higher rates are
“stimulative”. 

5/8
The hikes-are-stimulus thesis is partial analysis at best. While it is true
savers and bond holders benefit, it ignores every other form of monetary
transmission. What about the cost of capital? What about the incentive
to save? What about the incomes of the debtors, particularly the heavily
leveraged (eg most SMEs)? What about asset valuations? Worse, it flies
in the face of decades of empirical analysis. And you don’t have to go
back that far – did the 2022 rate hikes boost inflation? NO.

6/8
Some people say I am using the FD term too literally. (Economists tend
to do that..) They see a softer version, where the government's
persistently large deficits have made it impossible for the Fed to hit its
target. (I said we should this “fiscal prominence”, but they didn't like that
idea either.) This softer version is also full of holes. People forget that
the deficit needs to keep getting bigger to be a source of inflation. That
is not what is happening. Deficits have stabilized.

7/8
If persistent >2% inflation isn't the result of FD, why has the Fed been
missing its targets for so long? Having worked in policymaking myself,
I'll always choose cock-up over conspiracy. When employment slowed
and inflation dropped after 2023, officials thought that meant monetary
policy was very restrictive. They didn’t realize it was mostly just post-
COVID normalization. And with inflation headed in the right direction,
they didn’t need to speed things up and risk an unnecessary recession.

8/8
Unfortunately that calculus has changed in 2026. Not only is it clear
that inflation is stuck above 2%, but the labour market is starting to
recover. Monetary policy clearly isnt as restrictive as they thought, so
another monetary intervention is needed. Kevin Warsh likes to take the
credit for this pivot. He blames the “revealed preference” of his
predecessor. The reality is that Fed policy was already moving in this
direction. 66 months of missing your target is embarrassing, even for
doves.
