Summary 
 We find that the sustained rise in the 10 year US treasury yield in 2026 has largely been a 
reflection of the Fed shifting from thinking about cutting rates to actually starting to hike in 
September. This shift was caused by the US-Israeli war with Iran and subsequent supply 
disruptions leading to higher oil prices. The shift was facilitated by the resilient economy, 
which can partly be attributed to the AI boom. Meanwhile, inflation expectations have 
remained stable, suggesting that the Fed’s inflation target has stayed credible. This is the 
main story behind the gradual rise of the 10 year US treasury yield to 5.00%. 
 However, the recent rise in the 10 year US treasury yield well above 5.00% can largely be 
attributed to a rise in the term premium that was caused by concerns about the public debt.  
 Looking ahead, we expect the 10 year US treasury yield to move sideways through the 
remainder of 2026. We see some downward potential in 2027 as market pricing of Fed rate 
hikes seems overdone. However, as long as the economy remains resilient and geopolitics 
uncertain, downward potential may be limited. Meanwhile, the public debt is likely to remain 
a source of recurrent upward pressure in long-term rates. On balance, we conclude that 
longer-term US treasury yields have found higher ground. 
Introduction 
The 10 year US treasury yield has been above 5.00% since September 23, peaking at 5.31% on 
October 5. Several reasons have been mentioned for this surge. In this report we try to shed some 
light on this by looking at two decompositions of the 10 year yield: the term premium versus the 
risk-neutral yield, and break-even inflation versus the real yield. We also relate the term premium 
to bond market volatility (the MOVE index). We show that there are two stories: the first tells us 
why the 10 year yield climbed to 5.00% and the second why it jumped above that level and has 
stayed there. In the first story the Fed plays the lead role as it responded to the war in the Middle 
East. In the second the attention shifted to the US public debt, pushing up the term premium. 
The Fed and the term premium 
The first decomposition is based on the model of Adrian, Crump and Moench (ACM), and 
regularly updated by the New York Fed. It splits the 10 year US treasury yield in two components: 
the term premium and the risk-neutral yield. The latter can be interpreted as the average 
expected federal funds rate and the former as the risk premium that investors require for holding 
a 10 year government bond.

What immediately stands out from the chart for 2026 is that the risk-neutral yield has been 
steadily rising this year, while the term premium has been volatile and mostly moving sideways. 
Therefore, the sustained driver of the higher 10 year US treasury yield in 2026 has been the 
risk-neutral yield, rather than the term premium. If we zoom in on September, the month when 
the 10 year yield climbed above 5.00%, we can distinguish between the weeks before September 
23 and the weeks after.  
In the first three weeks of September, when the 10 year yield rose from 4.80% to 4.96%., the term 
premium fell from 0.78% to 0.57%, while the risk-neutral yield rose from 4.09% to 4.43%. This 
means that the Fed’s expected rate path was the main driver of the 10 year yield climbing 
toward 5.00%. 
However, after September 23, the dynamics changed. The term premium had fallen to 0.57% on 
September 22, but subsequently surged to 0.98% on October 7. At the same time, the risk-neutral 
yield fell from 4.43% to 4.39%. This means that in the last three weeks the term premium has 
been the main driver of higher rates. 
We can take a closer look at what moved the term premium by looking at the MOVE index, which 
is based on the implied volatility of options on US treasury yields. The chart for 2026 shows that 
the most recent rise in the term premium coincided with a spike in implied US treasury yield 
volatility, as measured by MOVE. The MOVE index jumped from 78.561 on September 22 to 
95.453 on September 23. At the same, the term premium rebounded from a 0.57% low to 0.64%.

This suggests that concerns about the public debt have played an important role in the 
recent rise of the 10 year US treasury yield well above 5.00%. The co-movement of the term 
premium and the MOVE index suggests the troublesome 5 year US treasury auction on that day 
was the main culprit. 
Note that on September 23, the risk-neutral yield also rose due to stronger-than-expected 
manufacturing and services PMI figures by S&P Global and a hawkish speech by Fed Governor 
Barr. However, a day later the risk-neutral yield started its descent, while the term premium 
continued to rise, closely tracking the MOVE. 
Inflation expectations and the resilient economy 
The second decomposition is based on TIPS and splits the 10 year US treasury yield in a break-
even inflation rate and a real yield. This should shed light on the role of inflation expectations 
versus real factors in the rise of the 10 year US treasury yield.  
 
Figure 3: Rising real yield, stable inflation expectations 
 
Source: Macrobond 
 
What is clear from the chart for 2026 is that break-even inflation has been relatively stable this 
year, despite an initial upward drift after the outbreak of the US-Israeli war with Iran at the end of 
February through May. In contrast, the real yield has been steadily moving upward in 2026, 
with a surge in September, both before and after September 23. The real yield rose from 
2.44% on September 1 to 2.63% on September 22, and continued to 2.95% on October 5. 
Meanwhile, the break-even inflation rate moved from 2.35% to 2.33% and then to 2.36%. In other 
words, inflation expectations hardly changed. This shows that the surge of the 10 year US treasury 
yield in September reflected a rise in the real yield and had little to do with inflation expectations. 
The rise in energy prices caused by the war in the Middle East and the resilient US economy have 
raised the Fed’s (real) policy rate path and the real 10 year yield. At the same time, the Fed’s 
hawkish reaction kept inflation expectations anchored. 
Wrapping up the decompositions 
The two decompositions show that the sustained drivers of the rise of the 10 year US treasury 
yield in 2026 are the risk-neutral yield and the real yield. Both drivers started their rise after the 
February 28 start of the war with Iran. The term premium has made large swings but has 
essentially moved sideways. In contrast, break-even inflation has remained largely stable. This 
suggests that the Fed has been able to maintain its credibility regarding the 2% inflation target by 
shifting from thinking about cutting rates earlier in the year to actually hiking in September and 
indicating another hike through the latest dot plot. Therefore, the sustained rise in the 10 year US 
treasury yield in 2026 reflects expectations of a higher Fed rate path.

In turn, this higher path has been caused by the US-Israeli war with Iran. Without it, the FOMC 
would still be thinking about cutting rates as the inflationary impact of the 2025 tariffs has faded. 
Instead, the supply disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz have pushed up global oil prices and 
domestic gasoline and diesel prices. At the same time, the US economy has been resilient in terms 
of GDP growth and the unemployment rate, which has made it easier for the FOMC to focus on 
the upside risks to inflation instead of downside risks to employment. In other words, geopolitics 
and oil prices, the resilient economy – which can partly be attributed to the AI boom – and 
monetary policy have led to a sustained raise in longer-term interest rates. This is the story of how 
the 10 year US treasury yield progressed to 5.00%. However, if we look at the recent rise above 
5.00%, the term premium is playing a major role and we showed that this was caused by concerns 
about the public debt. 
Higher ground 
Looking ahead, let’s consider the outlook for the Fed and the term premium. Before the outbreak 
of the war with Iran, the FOMC expected to make one rate cut this year, followed by another cut 
in 2027. In reality, the Fed hiked in September and expects to hike once more before the end of 
the year. They now expect to cut in 2028, with a two-year delay. On top of this, they recently 
made a modest upward adjustment to the neutral rate, but in light of the resilient economy we 
expect this won’t be the last. This means that the Fed’s rate path has moved upward compared to 
the start of this year, and that’s why we expect the 10 year US treasury yield to remain elevated.  
The downward potential is related to markets pricing in 3-4 hikes from now, which we think could 
be overdone. However, the supply shock from the Middle East and the resilience of the US 
economy has brought yields to a higher plateau through the Fed’s hikes, the two year delay in 
cuts, and the higher neutral rate. Due to its transformational nature, the AI boom could support 
the economy well beyond this year.  
Therefore, we think that downside potential from a revision of monetary policy expectations is 
limited and it’s not likely that the 10 year US treasury yield will revisit the levels below 4.30% that 
we saw in the six months prior to the outbreak of the war. Note that we are now about a full 
percentage point above that level.

What’s more, the most recent peak in the term premium was also the highest this year. In fact, if 
we look at recent years, the term premium – while exhibiting large swings – seems to have been 
trending upward. This may not be completely unrelated to the unstable geopolitical climate, lack 
of central bank demand and the ever expanding public debt. Since the most recent peak in the 
term premium was related to the public debt, and we do not expect any progress on this front,

the term premium may ease now and then when concerns about the public debt or geopolitics 
subside, but is likely to remain trending upward. Therefore, we think that if expectations of Fed 
hikes are scaled back, the downward impact on the 10 year yield will remain limited, 
because the public debt is not fading. In fact, it could become the most persistent driver of 
higher rates in coming years. 
Conclusion 
If we look at the near term, we expect longer-term interest rates to move sideways for the 
remainder of the year. We expect the Fed to make a second hike in December and the public debt 
to remain a concern. Looking further ahead, we think that it’s more likely that we see fewer rather 
than more rate hikes than the market has priced in. Therefore, we expect longer-term rates to 
ease somewhat during the course of 2027. However, as long as the economy remains resilient and 
geopolitics uncertain, the downward correction may be limited. Our rate forecasts are shown in 
table 1. We expect the 10 year US treasury yield to fall to 5.10% by mid-2027 as market 
expectations of additional Fed hikes subside and – when the decline in inflation to 2.2% that we 
forecast opens the door for the Fed to cut rates in 2028 – to 5.00% by the end of 2027. However, 
this leaves the 10 year US treasury yield still well above the below 4.30% levels for the 10 year US 
treasury yield that we saw in the six months prior to the outbreak of the war. We think that, at 
least over our forecasting horizon, longer-term US treasury yields have found higher ground.
