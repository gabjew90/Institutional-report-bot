The September employment report showed average hour-
ly earnings up 3.0% from a year earlier, the slowest pace 
since May 2021. On a year-ago basis, the Employment 
Cost Index (ECI) has also slipped and is below CPI infla-
tion for the first time since early 2023. At the same time, 
corporate profit margins sit at their highest level since the 
series began in 1947, and labor’s share of output is the 
lowest on record. We have long argued that a shrinking 
pool of available workers could put more upward pres-
sure on wages and threaten those margins. Companies 
responded to the labor shortage much as we expected, 
by investing in capital and lifting productivity. What has 
not followed is a meaningful recovery in wages. In this 
Economics Weekly, we look at why pay has lagged in a 
tight labor market, and what that means for the Fed 
and for investors.
Productivity Is Rising; Pay Is Not
For most of the past four decades, an abundant supply 
of workers helped keep wage growth in check and sup-
ported corporate profits. The labor shortages in the few 
years ahead of the pandemic, and during it, started to 
change that, with compensation’s share rising. Following 
the pandemic, workers remain harder to find, giving em-
ployees more leverage and putting pressure on margins, 
yet compensation’s share of output has been falling again 
sharply. Conversely, profit’s share of gross value added 
has continued to climb (exhibit 1).

The productivity data show the gap most clearly. Over 
the past four quarters, output per hour in the nonfarm 
business sector rose 2.2%, while real hourly compensa-
tion fell 0.1%. Workers produced more but received less 
in inflation-adjusted pay. The difference showed up in 
profits. Unit profits, the profit nonfinancial corporations 
earn on each unit of output, rose at a 43% annualized rate 
in the second quarter, the fastest pace since 2021. Public 
companies tell a similar story. The S&P 500 operating 
margins reached 15.7%—the highest level since 2022.
Margins can widen in two ways: companies can charge 
more relative to their labor costs or workers can produce 
more relative to what they are paid. Since 2020, produc-
tivity has been the larger contributor to rising margins 
than mark-ups (exhibit 2). That distinction matters 
because productivity growth creates room for wages to 
rise without adding to inflation. Yet, little of that room has 
been used. Within the ECI for private industry, wages and 
salaries rose 3.1% over the past year, leaving inflation-
adjusted pay down 0.4%. Over the same period, benefit 
costs increased 3.8%, suggesting a smaller share of com-
pensation growth is flowing through to take-home pay.  
All in all, workers are generating more output per hour, 
but their purchasing power is still declining.

Why a Tight Labor Market Hasn’t Lifted 
Pay
Small businesses still report difficulty finding workers. 
According to the NFIB’s September jobs report, 26% of 
owners cited the quality or availability of labor as their 
single most important problem, 14 points above the his-
torical average. Yet only 6% cited labor costs, the lowest 
percentage since December 2020, and the net share of 
owners raising compensation slipped to 28%. We see four 
reasons pay has lagged the shortage of workers. 
The first is a shift in spending from labor toward 
capital. Business investment has been one of the fastest-
growing parts of the economy since the pandemic, with 
real equipment spending up 13.6% at an annualized rate 
in the second quarter. Recent productivity gains have 
come largely from capital deepening. In our latest quar-
terly survey, our technology analysts noted that higher

compute costs were being offset by lower spending on 
labor, with headcount at most companies flat to down. 
Across the private sector, technology has led job cuts 
for much of this year, with companies citing AI-driven 
restructuring and the reallocation of budgets.
The second is a run of shocks to nonlabor costs. 
Final-demand producer prices rose 5.4% year-to-date in 
August, against 3.1% for average hourly earnings. Our 
latest analyst survey shows the same divergence: the 
nonlabor input-cost diffusion index rose to 80.6 in the 
second quarter from 76.2 in the year-ago period, while 
labor-cost pressures continued to moderate, with this 
index declining to 61.1 from 64.3.
The third is weaker bargaining power. With both 
hiring and layoffs low, fewer workers are changing jobs, 
and changing jobs is typically where the largest pay 
gains are won. Whether or not AI displacement ulti-
mately proves widespread, the prospect of automation 
could be reducing workers’ willingness to pursue higher 
compensation, particularly in occupations where firms 
are publicly discussing AI-driven productivity gains and 
headcount reductions. However, this could be changing. 
The Atlanta Fed’s Wage Growth Tracker shows that pay 
for employees switching jobs has been steadily rising 
relative to those who stay in their jobs. So far, this has 
not caused the quit rate to increase, though it is worth

A fourth explanation may be industry composition. 
Healthcare has accounted for a disproportionate share 
of recent job growth, yet wage growth in the sector has 
remained relatively subdued. Some of this likely reflects 
normalization from the extraordinary pandemic-related 
wage pressures seen in 2021 and 2022. Healthcare pro-
viders also face reimbursement constraints that can limit 
the pace of pay increases even when labor remains scarce. 
Because healthcare employment has grown faster than 
many higher-paying sectors, aggregate measures such as 
average hourly earnings may be understating underlying 
wage pressures elsewhere in the economy.

Whether the gap closes is the open question. Small-business 
reports of labor shortages remain well above their histori-
cal average (exhibit 5), and they have historically led wage 
growth by about nine months. Wage growth has started to 
respond: the Atlanta Fed’s tracker rose to 4.1% in August 
from 3.6% in June (exhibit 6), and our analysts expect 
labor cost pressures to pick up over the next six months, 
with the expected labor cost index rising to 66.7 from 61.1. 
After the 2021-2022 inflation surge, ECI growth trailed CPI 
for two years before overtaking it in mid-2023. The current 
shortfall is far smaller. Productivity gains need not pro-
duce a modern Engels’ pause, a prolonged period in which 
profits rise while real wages lag. If hiring continues to shift 
toward healthcare, where labor demand is likely to remain 
strong for demographic reasons, aggregate wage measures 
could begin to firm even without a broad acceleration 
across the rest of the economy.

What It Means for the Fed
One argument for keeping rates restrictive is to prevent 
successive “temporary” price shocks from becoming 
embedded in wage and price setting. That risk appears 
to have been limited up to now. Average hourly earnings 
growth has slowed to 3.0%, and the ECI has slipped below 
CPI inflation. Broad measures of wage growth have not 
reaccelerated, even though the Atlanta Fed’s tracker has 
firmed, and even though the labor market remains tight by 
most measures despite a soft September payroll report.
That does not necessarily mean the Fed is finished. 
Inflation remains above target, financial conditions are 
still relatively loose, and productivity growth, especially 
because of AI adoption, may be stronger than the data 
currently suggest. More important, recent inflationary 
pressures have come largely from the supply side of the 
economy, where monetary policy has less influence over 
the initial source of the shock.
The strength in margins, however, means that if wage 
growth strengthens, firms may have greater scope than 
usual to absorb higher labor costs through margins rather 
than pass them through to prices. The result is that a 
recovery in real wages does not need to be as inflationary 
as it would have been in prior cycles. The third-quarter 
ECI, due at the end of the month, will show whether com-
pensation is firming, while unit labor costs later in the 
year will show whether workers are beginning to share 
more fully in recent productivity gains.
What It Means for Margins and Markets
Whether or when this gap between compensation and 
margins closes matters well beyond the labor market. 
Investors have become increasingly comfortable with the 
current combination of strong productivity growth, rising 
capital spending, and record margins. 
The good news is that a wage catch-up would not neces-
sarily be a negative development for the economy. As 
noted above, with margins at record highs and labor’s 
share at a record low, firms have unusual room to 
absorb higher labor costs, so stronger wage growth 
could ease some of the pressure on households without 
reigniting inflation.
For investors, the question is whether margins can 
remain elevated if compensation begins to recover. The 
relationship between selling prices, labor costs, and mar-
gins bears close watching. As long as productivity growth 
remains strong, higher wages and healthy margins can 
coexist. If productivity slows while labor costs accelerate, 
margins become more vulnerable (exhibit 7).

At the same time, a prolonged shift of income from labor 
to capital carries broader consequences. Consumption 
ultimately depends on income growth, and profits have 
increasingly accrued to higher-income households with 
lower propensities to spend. If productivity gains contin-
ue flowing disproportionately to capital owners, the ex-
pansion becomes increasingly dependent on asset values 
and investment spending, rather than stronger household 
purchasing power.
That leaves the labor market as one of the most important 
variables to watch over the coming quarters. If wages be-
gin to follow increasing productivity, the current expansion

becomes more balanced and durable. If they do not, con-
cerns about affordability, inequality, and the distribution of 
growth are likely to play a larger role in both economic and 
political debates.
Conclusion
The labor shortage is producing the outcome many 
expected for productivity and capital spending, but not 
for wages. Companies have invested, productivity has 
improved, and margins have reached record levels. What 
has yet to follow is a meaningful recovery in labor’s share 
of output.
Whether that gap closes will shape the outlook for 
inflation, monetary policy, and margins. If productiv-
ity remains strong, wages can catch up without forcing 
prices higher or margins sharply lower, creating a more 
balanced expansion that is less dependent on invest-
ment and asset prices. If productivity slows before 
wages recover, the current balance among worker pay, 
corporate profits, and elevated inflation will become 
harder to sustain.
