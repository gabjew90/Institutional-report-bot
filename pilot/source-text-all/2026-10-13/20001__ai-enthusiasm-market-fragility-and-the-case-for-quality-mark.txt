Market Review 
The third quarter saw a reshuffling as the narrative around artificial intelligence (AI) became more nuanced 
and complex. We saw large capitalization stocks outperform small caps, with the S&P 500 up 2.30% versus 
a 7.23% decline in the Russell 2000 Index. Within small cap, there was a clear preference for value over 
growth (-4.91% return for the Russell 2000 Value versus a -9.39% return for the Russell 2000 Growth).
From a sector standpoint, energy was once more a top performer within the S&P 500 boosted by high oil 
prices. Health care and technology also outperformed. Utilities declined the most this quarter. Within

1.  Why is this time not different?
First, there is real wisdom in the saying history doesn’t repeat itself, but it does rhyme. There are real limits 
to using analogies for different time periods. While they can teach us lessons, they are not by themselves 
predictive. It is rare for all the same factors to be at play at the same time when you’re talking about events 
that are often decades apart. And correlation is not causation. There is a cottage industry of economists 
using correlation data to try to predict macroeconomic outcomes. Every cycle has its own unique 
reasons for why and when things change. It’s easy to look in the rear-view mirror and proclaim the data 
was screaming a crisis was ahead. The challenge is the relevant data was different each time. But again, 
correlation is not causation, as evidenced by the charts on the next page.

One of the things to pay attention to is how these capital expenditure booms are financed. Initially, mega-
cap technology companies were funding asset purchases from their gigantic free cash flow. Every time 
they announced an increase in their capital expenditure budgets, their stocks went up. It was a little 
confounding to watch investors cheering declining returns on capital. But there was so much conviction 
that the returns would more than justify the investment.
Eventually though, even the huge piles of free cash flow were not enough to fund the growth. We’ve 
transitioned to substantial debt raises in the market. We even saw Google come to market to raise equity. 
Prior to that, Google had been buying back its stock at a healthy clip. We’ve also seen an increase in 
complex off-balance sheet financing and special purpose vehicles to obscure the leverage in the system. 
I really thought we had learned our lesson in the Global Financial Crisis, but I might have been wrong. Once 
you are in this stage, you’ve created some fragility to the system. It is more vulnerable to economic shocks. 
And the shocks don’t have to come from the industry itself; they can be completely exogenous. 
To me one of the largest vulnerabilities of this capital expenditure boom is the dependence on two large, 
private, and unprofitable companies: OpenAI and Anthropic. According to news reports, Anthropic has solid 
margins on its inference compute (that is when users prompt the models and generate a response). While 
that is good news, it does not include the costs of revenue paid back to partners and the huge cost of 
training the models.

It is estimated that some 70% of AI cloud revenue is from Anthropic and OpenAI. A recent UBS report 
estimated that nearly half of all of Google’s cloud revenue next year would be from OpenAI and Anthropic.1 
Microsoft disclosed that 70% of its AI cloud revenue was from just OpenAI.2  However, the concentration is 
not limited to the hyperscalers generating revenue from the frontier labs. According to NVIDIA’s most recent 
10Q, nearly 70% of its receivables are from just five companies.3 
The other unusual element of this capital expenditure cycle is how quickly the lead in usage and capabilities 
changes. Most of the hyperscalers funding this cycle have operated in winner take all markets, typically 
with strong network effects. These competitive barriers generate strong economics that allow companies 
to invest to harden the competitive walls around them. This time it’s hard to argue that there are switching 
costs when you can change a model just by toggling a drop-down menu, and that many companies worried 
about the costs of tokens are choosing cheaper open-source models, many from China. If you think of the 
last two years, OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta, and Microsoft were each called a leader at one point and 
later a laggard and back again. Meta was thought to be a distant player until the recent release of their 
agentic product Muse.
It must give one pause in the absence of more information on the underlying economics for these frontier 
labs. And that’s before we get into all the recent reports on the models breaking free from their protected 
sandboxes to hack other companies and governments.
2. When is quality going to start outperforming?
Quality has had glimmers of a recovery this year. We saw this most notably in July, when overbuilding 
concerns roiled markets. We saw a drawdown in semiconductor names that had been substantial winners, 
and we saw by and large most of the KAR quality strategies outperform their benchmarks. 
As the summer continued into September, we saw investors return to the AI and cyclical themes they had 
favored, though with perhaps more measured enthusiasm. This caused a reversal in quality as a positive 
factor in performance.

If we examine the quarter, the month of July was revealing. Investors fled at a breathtaking speed from the 
more speculative corners of the AI trade. The abandonment of the momentum trade tells you that there are 
real concerns about the foundations of AI and that everyone wants to have a sturdy chair to sit on when the

In the meantime, we continue to take advantage of the current period to upgrade portfolio quality wherever 
possible because we believe the usual quality premium has been replaced with AI excitement. While it is 
frustrating to see portfolio characteristics stay consistent or improve and not be rewarded in the market, 
we do believe the regression to the mean will resume.

3. Given strong balance sheets, cash flow generation, and generally 
healthy fundamentals, what is preventing broader investor 
interest in quality small caps?
It was surprising to look at attribution data from this quarter and see a mixed bag in terms of what types of 
stocks outperformed during the period. Return on equity (ROE), generally a measure of high quality, was 
influenced by what we would term lower-quality, cyclical companies generating strong ROEs as a result of 
strong cyclical demand. This is why we prefer to let the qualitative strengths of a business take precedence 
in our analysis and recognize that the quantitative metrics are the outputs of a business’ strengths. 
What we’ve seen this past quarter is investors chasing bottlenecks across energy, shipping, or AI 
beneficiary themes. Investors will quickly exit a stock when it reports only good, and not stellar earnings. 
That indicates that narrative is driving the investment and not so much a deeper analysis on the longer-
term strength of a business. The swift exits are a recognition that many of these companies do not have 
the kind of differentiation to be durable.

We saw small caps generally start to underperform as the Federal Reserve announced its most recent 
interest rate increase. That is a typical reaction in the market, but we believe it matters why the Fed is 
raising rates. In this case, the Fed sees the strength in employment and the economy as evidence that 
rates at these levels are not restrictive. Considering the five years that inflation has been above target and 
the constraint of higher oil (particularly diesel prices), it is no surprise the Fed felt compelled to act and give 
some indication of an additional hike this year. 
A healthy U.S. economy is typically more meaningful to small cap earnings than interest rates. But to play it 
safe, in a higher rate environment, we believe favoring pristine balance sheets that are not subject to rising 
rates is another reason to focus on quality.

4. How healthy is the consumer today? What are the key indicators 
investors should be monitoring?
Today’s consumer is still spending but is also incredibly careful with every dollar. We would guess that as 
long as employment remains strong, the consumer will continue to spend. Right now, unemployment is at 
4.1%, the lowest level in one year.
Consumer balance sheets, despite a weak savings rate, still look benign. Revolving credit lines have stayed 
down, and consumer bank lending is soft, preventing overheating. The challenge is that average hourly 
earnings continue to trail inflation, so purchasing power is strained.
Strained purchasing power is supported by commentary from many retailers, such as Kroger, which 
noted customers are putting fewer items in the basket and trading down more to private label. Higher gas 
prices are certainly having an impact. Even luxury brands that don’t deliver the perception of value have 
underperformed. Hermes, once a standout in luxury, has seen its stock pressured on concerns if growth 
can continue in a more competitive luxury market. All consumers, regardless of economic standing, have 
felt the strain of lost purchasing power. Consumer-oriented companies must execute at very high levels to 
win business. In our eyes, the metrics to watch are employment, employment, and lastly, employment.
