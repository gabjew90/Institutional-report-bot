Three years of high interest rates have pushed US
private credit defaults to record levels on Fitch's
measure, while non-accruals at the largest listed
business development companies have reached
their highest level since 2017. At the same time,
private credit has become an important source of
funding for the AI infrastructure build-out, and
the two trends run through the same lenders,
counterparties and balance sheets.
Most of today's stress reflects higher rates
rather than AI, but lenders are now financing
the AI build-out from a position of already
weaker credit quality.
AI-related credit is not uniform. Loans backed by
long-term hyperscaler leases carry very different
risks
from
loans
to
sub-investment-grade
operators that depend on future demand.
Risk is also concentrating in a small network of
firms that are each other's customers, suppliers,
investors and lenders, while quarterly marks and
public bond indices give only a partial view of it.
This paper examines how these trends are
becoming intertwined, and why investors remain
divided on what happens next.

Executive Summary

4
US private credit default rates reached record
highs in 2025 and set a new record in the
second quarter of 2026. At the same time,
private credit has become an increasingly
important
source
of
funding
for
the
AI
infrastructure build-out, helping support more
than $1tn of committed spending. The two
developments are often treated as separate
stories. They are not. The same lenders
experiencing
rising
stress
in
their
loan
portfolios are also financing the AI build-out,
leaving the same balance sheets exposed to
both trends.
That
exposure
is
becoming
increasingly
concentrated. The commitments underpinning
AI infrastructure are increasingly concentrated
among
a
relatively
small
group
of
counterparties and are often supported by
revenue
projections
that
have
yet
to
materialise. At the same time, private credit's
size,
limited
disclosure
requirements
and
infrequent valuation practices can make risks
difficult to observe in real time. Loans are
typically marked quarterly against manager
models rather than continuously repriced in
public markets, muting signs of volatility.
Credit Stress Meets the AI Boom 
The implication is not that AI is driving today's
defaults. Most current stress reflects the
legacy
impact
of
higher
interest
rates,
plunging
software
valuations,
retail
redemptions
and
weaker
operating
conditions. Rather, lenders are entering a
period of unprecedented AI-related capital
deployment
from
a
position
of
already
deteriorating credit quality. It is against this
backdrop that UBS has raised its worst-case
default expectation for private credit from
13% to 15%, compared with 3-6% for US high
yield and 8-10% for leveraged loans.

The second quarter of 2026 was the quarter in which
mounting stress in private credit became harder to
dismiss. An FT analysis of Solve data, published on 17
August, put non-accruals across the 20 largest listed
business development companies (BDCs) at a median
2.8% of cost for the quarter, up from 2.0% three
months earlier and the highest level identified by the
paper since 2017, in the aftermath of the oil-price crash.
On PitchBook's Leveraged Commentary & Data (LCD)
figures, new lending at several of the largest vehicles again
fell short of repayments and loan sales, including funds
managed by KKR, Blue Owl and Apollo's MidCap Financial.
FS KKR reported 7.1% of its portfolio as troubled
investments. Blackstone and KKR also marked down their
exposure to software company Medallia after control
passed to lenders when Thoma Bravo wrote off roughly
$5bn of equity earlier in 2026. By the end of June 2026,
Blackstone was carrying the position at less than half of
par value, down from 60% in March. Weaker borrower
performance can also compound itself: where loans carry
leverage-based margin ratchets, rising leverage pushes
interest costs higher and adds pressure on borrowers that
are already stretched.
Managers differ on what these figures imply. David Golub
of Golub Capital told investors, in remarks reported by
the FT, that there was "elevated credit stress" and that
"we're in a credit cycle". Oaktree's Armen Panossian said
his firm was adopting "a more defensive and risk-averse
posture", adding that "beneath the surface, there's cause
for concern". Others struck a more reassuring tone. Blue
Owl's Craig Packer told investors that "credit metrics are
healthy and the issues we are managing remain isolated",
while Ares' Jim Miller said borrowers remained in "solid"
shape, with interest coverage and leverage "generally
consistent with our five-year average". Where managers
largely converge is on the source of the pressure. Much of
the stress is concentrated in loans originated in 2020 and
2021, when rates were near zero and valuations were
elevated. As Barings' Bryan High put it, higher borrowing
costs have "starved some businesses from investing".
The quarter also saw signs of stress beyond the loan
books themselves. Blackstone, Apollo and Blue Owl were
among the managers that limited investor withdrawals
from private credit vehicles after redemption requests
exceeded fund limits. Rather than reflecting weakness in
the underlying asset class, the restrictions highlighted the
tension between investor liquidity expectations and fund
structures investing in long-term private credit assets.
When Credit Stress Became 
Visible 
5

6
Where private credit sits in the AI build-out is
ultimately a question of credit quality rather
than financing channel, and the distinction is
often overlooked. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta,
Microsoft and Oracle are not simply bond
issuers operating outside the private credit
market.
Their
capital
expenditure
now
absorbs roughly 94% of operating cash flow
after dividends and buybacks, with some
spending
beyond
what
they
generate
internally. At the same time, BIS research
shows an increasing use of off-balance-sheet
financing structures, including joint ventures
and special-purpose vehicles, funded through
private credit and other private markets.
While
this
borrowing
sits
outside
the
sponsors' balance sheets, repayment is often
supported
by
investment-grade
lease,
capacity or offtake commitments from the
hyperscalers themselves.
The result is that AI-related private credit is
far from homogeneous. Common structures
include joint ventures, REITs, build-to-suit
arrangements
and
asset-backed
securitisations, but the credit supporting each
can differ materially. At the stronger end are
projects
underpinned
by
long-term
commitments
from
hyperscalers.
A
construction loan repaid through a 20-year
lease to Microsoft or Google is effectively
financing investment-grade counterparties,
albeit through a real-estate or infrastructure
structure rather than a corporate bond.
Not All AI Credit Is Risky 
The weaker exposure sits a tier below the
hyperscalers,
among
GPU-as-a-service
providers, "neocloud" tenants and other sub-
investment-grade operators. As Ropes & Gray
has noted, these borrowers face materially
more
challenging
underwriting
conditions
and depend to a much greater extent on
future
demand
continuing
to
meet
expectations.
A
loan
supported
by
the
contracted
cash
flows
of
Microsoft
or
Google carries very different risks from one
reliant
on
the
future
revenues
of
an
unproven
tenant.
If
AI
monetisation
disappoints or growth slows, that distinction
is likely to become increasingly important.

The public bond market is absorbing the other half of the
shift, and the scale is considerable. Vanguard's Lucas
Baynes estimates that combined issuance by Alphabet,
Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle averaged roughly
$35bn a year between 2020 and 2024, rose to $93bn in 2025,
and reached approximately $132bn in the first seven months
of 2026 alone, including a single multi-tranche offering of
about $53bn and a bond maturing 100 years from issuance.
Estimates for total AI-related debt issuance in 2026,
extending to chipmakers, data-centre developers and
utilities, range from roughly $300bn to $570bn.
Because much of this issuance is long-dated, it is extending
the duration of investment-grade corporate bond indices
while simultaneously increasing the weight of technology-
related issuers within them. In effect, concentration is
migrating into an asset class that many investors hold for
diversification. Baynes is careful not to overstate the point.
The issuers involved remain exceptionally strong credits,
and nothing in the issuance data suggests a deterioration in
solvency. The concern is more subtle: spreads remain close
to historically tight levels, leaving investors with relatively
little compensation for accepting greater concentration risk.
Nor do public bond markets capture the full picture. A
growing share of AI-related financing is being arranged
through private credit funds, joint ventures and other off-
balance-sheet structures that sit outside mainstream bond
indices altogether. As a result, public balance sheets and
benchmark indices provide an increasingly incomplete view
of how AI investment is being funded. The financing
obligations remain real, but the ultimate distribution of risk
is becoming harder to observe.
Concentration Migrates 
to Credit 
7

The commitments at the centre of the AI build-out are
increasingly being made between the same handful of
counterparties. Nvidia has agreed to invest up to $100bn in
OpenAI,
which
has
committed
to
deploying
Nvidia
hardware in return. OpenAI has separately signed a $300bn
cloud agreement with Oracle
and multi-year supply
arrangements with AMD and AWS, against roughly $13bn of
2025 revenue. Oracle's commitments, in turn, require
substantial purchases from Nvidia, while Microsoft, one of
OpenAI's largest shareholders, is itself a major customer of
the chipmaker. Revenue on the hyperscaler side is arriving:
Google Cloud grew 63% year-on-year in the first quarter of
2026 and Microsoft's AI business is running at a $37bn
annualised revenue rate. The structures, however, are
priced on that growth continuing at a pace sufficient to
support an accelerating debt load. Increasingly, the
obligations are being written between the same firms
rather than against independent third-party demand.
These so-called "circular deals" concentrate risk within a
small network of counterparties. While they can accelerate
investment
and
support
growth,
they
also
increase
dependence on a handful of firms continuing to spend and
fund one another. Should demand or capital availability
weaken, the effects could quickly spread across the
broader AI ecosystem, amplifying the impact of any
slowdown.
The durability of those arrangements depends on capital
continuing to flow into the sector at an extraordinary rate.
Apollo's
Torsten
Sløk
estimates
hyperscaler
capital
spending will rise from 1.4% of US GDP in 2025 to roughly
3.1% by 2027, close to twice the pace reached at the peak of
the US housing boom, and argues that spending on that
trajectory can reverse just as quickly. Bloomberg consensus
estimates compiled by Vanguard put capital expenditure at
almost $800bn in 2026 and above $1tn annually from 2027
through 2030, implying that the current issuance boom is a
multi-year shift in funding requirements rather than a one-
off surge. Jamie Dimon has separately argued that capital
demand on that scale is inflationary in its own right and
could keep long-term borrowing costs elevated for longer.
That matters because the defaults already emerging in
private credit are being driven by higher borrowing costs.
Fitch attributes much of the current stress in healthcare
and consumer borrowers to debt-servicing pressure rather
than AI-related factors. In that sense, the AI build-out is not
arriving in a benign credit environment. The same lenders
being asked to finance unprecedented levels of capital
expenditure are already grappling with rising defaults and
deteriorating credit quality, raising the question of how
resilient these interlocking commitments would be if
growth, funding availability or AI monetisation were to
disappoint.

The reason the disagreement persists is that the underlying
system is still working. Revenue continues to arrive,
agreements are being honoured, debt is being serviced and
the commitments binding together the AI build-out are still
being met. As long as companies generate enough cash flow
to honour leases, cloud contracts, hardware orders and
financing obligations, there is little reason for the structure
to break. Increasingly, however, the same handful of
companies
are
simultaneously
customers,
suppliers,
investors, borrowers and counterparties to one another.
The commitments are real, but so is the assumption
embedded within them: that future revenue will be
sufficient to support an expanding stock of debt and capital
expenditure. Ultimately, the debate is not whether the
model works today, but whether AI companies can
become
sufficiently
profitable
to
sustain
these
commitments without continued reliance on abundant
capital and financing. If profitability fails to materialise
before
growth
slows,
the
industry's
increasingly
interconnected obligations could come under significant
pressure.
The Question the Market 
Is Debating 
10

11
None of this points to an imminent reckoning, but it does sharpen a question every credit
manager and limited partner should already be asking: how much of this risk is actually
visible inside their own portfolio? Quarterly marks, manager models and fragmented GP
reporting were built for a steadier credit environment than the one described above. As AI-
related financing extends further into private credit, firms need a clearer view of
concentrations, counterparty exposure, covenant quality and valuation risk. The challenge is
not simply identifying where capital has been deployed but understanding how
interconnected
those
exposures
have
become
across
lenders,
counterparties
and
underlying assets. Alpha Alternatives helps managers and allocators build a clearer view of
portfolio exposures, concentrations, valuation practices and risk across increasingly
complex private-market structures.
