CAN AI SAVE THE TEX-MEX MARRIAGE?
João Pedro Oliveira Fachetti
11 Oct 2026
In June, we argued that the US and Mexico were heading for a “Tex-Mex divorce”. Trump’s tariffs
and trade uncertainty were putting pressure on one of the world’s most integrated manufacturing
relationships. Since then, Washington has refused to extend the USMCA, which has left the
agreement subject to annual reviews and cast further doubt over the future of North American
trade. Yet, Mexican exports to the US are booming again. Are we witnessing a revival of the Tex-
Mex marriage?
1/8
Mexican exports have staged a remarkable recovery after losing
momentum in the wake of Liberation Day. Annual export growth
accelerated from just 7.7% in January to 43.7% in July, the fastest pace
in more than five years, with shipments reaching a record US$81.4bn.
Mexico is now exporting considerably more than it did before the latest
round of trade tensions with Washington.

2/8
But the terms of the relationship have changed. Since George H. W.
Bush and Carlos Salinas shook hands over NAFTA in 1992, autos and
auto parts have anchored Mexico’s manufacturing ties with the US.
Today, Mexican-made vehicles face higher tariffs than some Japanese
and South Korean rivals. As the auto sector struggles, the US
investment boom in AI infrastructure offers a new growth engine for
Mexican exports. Computers and servers have overtaken autos as
Mexico’s largest export category.
3/8
Mexico’s industrial fortunes have long been tied to those of the US,
with the two countries’ manufacturing sectors moving largely in tandem
thanks to decades of closely integrated production chains. But that
relationship has weakened since 2025, as Mexican industrial activity
has increasingly diverged from that of its northern neighbour. Even the
surge in computer exports, fuelled by growing US demand for AI
infrastructure, has done little to restore the historic links between the
two economies.

4/8
But how Mexican are these Mexican-made servers? Not very, at least
when it comes to their components. Most of the parts used in computer
manufacturing are imported – mainly from Taiwan, China and other
Asian economies – before being assembled in Mexico and shipped
north to the US. Imported inputs account for a staggering 98% of the
materials used by Mexico’s electronics export industry.
5/8
But we could argue that none of this is particularly new. After all, the
maquiladora model, built around imported components and exports to
the US, has shaped manufacturing along Mexico’s northern border for
decades. Yet, the rise in computer exports has brought surprisingly little
growth to Mexican factories. Since January 2024, exports have surged
by nearly 1,300%, while output in the country’s computer and
electronics industry has risen by only 17% and employment by just
9%.

6/8
That disparity could shrink if Mexico followed the example of another
major maquiladora industry – the auto sector. While electronics
exporters rely almost entirely on imported inputs, the figure is closer to
70% in the case of autos, for which regional suppliers have emerged.
The USMCA’s 75% North American content rule fostered that integration,
and Washington now seeks similar rules for computers and servers. For
Mexico, this could spur local suppliers and help capture more of the
AI boom.
7/8
Turning this opportunity into a broader manufacturing revival will
require more than favourable trade rules. Mexico must attract private
investment, but business confidence remains weak, with the headline
index below 50 for 19 months. And while uncertainty over the USMCA
has weighed on sentiment, investors are  grappling with domestic

challenges, notably the insecure investment environment and weak rule
of law. The Sheinbaum government must address these issues to
improve the business climate.
8/8
For now, the Tex-Mex marriage remains on shaky foundations. The
five-year rolling correlation between US and Mexican manufacturing
growth fell from around 0.5 in 2021–24 to virtually zero in Q2 2026. The
AI-driven export boom has done little to revive Mexico’s domestic
economy or restore its ties to US growth. We expect the US economy to
expand by 2.5% this year, which would normally give a substantial
boost to the Mexican economy. But we are forecasting 2026 Mexican
growth at just 1.1%.
