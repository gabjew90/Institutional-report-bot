Global Macro
Bond yields steadied last week, with the 10-year US Treasury yield oscillating within its newfound 5.2–5.3% range and 
credit spreads broadly tightening. French OATs also stabilised, with the 10-year easing to c.4.8% and the spread over 
Bunds narrowing to c.133 basis points (bps) from a peak of 147bps on 2 October. The respite may prove short-lived. 
The National Assembly’s finance committee rejected the revenue half of the 2027 budget and will begin its floor 
debate on the original draft of the budget on Tuesday.
In the US, September’s consumer price index (CPI) inflation print, due on Wednesday, will be the next test of direction. 
Consensus estimates converge on an uncomfortable headline but a benign core. UBS projects headline CPI to rise 0.56% 
mom, the third-largest monthly increase this year after March and April, lifting the annual rate from 3.40% to 3.62%. Core 
CPI, by contrast, is projected to rise only c.0.2% mom, broadly in line with the pre-pandemic pace. On UBS’s numbers, 
core inflation edges down from 2.45% to 2.41% yoy, only 0.10-0.15pps above the level consistent with the US Federal 
Reserve (Fed) 2% personal consumption expenditures (PCE) target. The data continue to indicate an energy shock rather 
than a broadening of underlying inflation, at least for now. The key anchor remains wage growth, which is close to 0% in 
real terms. In our view, this backdrop leaves room to unwind more of the rate hikes priced into forward curves. That 
process has already begun over the last two weeks, and another hike is no longer fully priced by year-end.
Demand for duration at these levels is rising. Last week’s USD 39bn 10-year reopening stopped through by 1.7bps at 
5.30%, the highest yield at a 10-year auction since November 2000. The bid-to-cover ratio was 2.77x, against a 12-month 
average of about 2.5x. Primary dealers were left with just 2.5% of the issue, against a typical c.9%, meaning end investors 
absorbed almost all of the supply. Flows tell a similar story. According to BofA, long-term government and corporate bond 
funds took in USD 7.4bn last week, the largest inflow since May 2025, while municipal bond funds recorded their largest 
inflow on record.
Higher bond yields have compressed equity valuations this year, but earnings have more than made up the difference. 
The S&P 500 is up 15% year-to-date (YTD), even though its forward price-to-earnings (P/E) has fallen from nearly 22x in 
January to 19x. Emerging market (EM) equities are up 24% YTD despite their forward P/E falling 27%, from 13x to 9.5x.
Those gains, however, have become increasingly concentrated in AI. Nvidia, Apple and Microsoft now account for more 
than 21% of the S&P 500. At the end of Q3, three-quarters of the index’s constituents were trading below their 50-day 
moving average, the weakest breadth since April. The S&P 500 rose 2% in the quarter, while the equal-weighted index fell 
2%. Financials have lagged the most in recent weeks. September was the sector’s worst month relative to the S&P 500 
since 1990. Higher yields, slower M&A and tough trading comparisons all weighed, alongside a growing concern that AI 
will erode banks’ low-cost deposit base by steering household cash towards higher-yielding alternatives. 
Last week offered the first sign of a broadening. As yields stabilised, the equal-weighted index outperformed and rose for 
the first time in eight weeks. Whether that broadening continues depends on the Q3 earnings season, which starts with 
the US banks on Tuesday. Earnings need to do two things. First, the AI companies must keep delivering to justify the 
market’s concentration in them. Second, the rest of the market must show that the non-AI economy remains resilient, and 
so far, earnings growth there is broadly holding up. The stakes are high because markets are pricing stress in rates but not 
in equities. The MOVE index, which measures expected Treasury yield volatility over the next month, has risen to around 
100, well above its late-2025 low of c.55. The VIX, the equivalent measure for the S&P 500, is around 17, slightly below its 
long-run average of c.19-20 and consistent with daily moves of about 1%. Investors are therefore paying to hedge rate 
swings but not for protection against a sharp equity sell-off, even though the index is concentrated in a handful of AI 
names and Q3 earnings fall within the VIX’s one-month window. A disappointment from the largest companies could close 
that gap, with bond-market volatility spilling over into equities.
EM faces the same concentration risks. Korea and Taiwan have accounted for the bulk of this year’s gains. There is no sign 
that demand growth for semiconductors is slowing. In fact, it continues to accelerate: Taiwan’s September exports rose 
60.9% yoy to a record USD 87.2bn, and Samsung’s Q3 operating profit of KRW 107.4trn was nearly 2.5 times its full-year 
2025 result. Nevertheless, profit-taking and risk management are driving some reallocation away from the theme. 
According to BofA data, foreign investors sold USD 7.3bn of EM equities ex-China last week, led by Korea (USD 5.7bn), 
India (USD 2.3bn) and Taiwan (USD 0.7bn), while buying USD 2.8bn of Brazilian equities. Brazil is well positioned to be a 
key beneficiary of any push to diversify away from the AI theme, particularly now that a political transition looks very likely. 
A Bolsonaro victory in the runoff against President Lula, now priced at an 87% probability on Polymarket, would likely lower 
risk premia and raise the probability of fiscal consolidation and further rate cuts. Brazilian equities are up nearly 20% since 
the first round and 35% YTD, while the BRL has gained another 5%, taking its YTD appreciation to close to 10%.

Geopolitics and commodities
US President Donald Trump said on 8 October that the US “will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the midterm 
elections” taking place on 3 November, citing “productive discussions” with Tehran. Brent crude fell c.1% to around 
USD 103 per barrel on Friday. The military signals point the other way. US officials reportedly briefed Israel’s chief of 
staff on preparations to resume major combat operations within three weeks. The USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier 
group is heading to the region, adding to roughly 50,000 US personnel already there, with three carriers and two 
amphibious groups due near Iran by end-November.
Iran, meanwhile, is escalating at sea. The shipping intelligence service Marisks recorded at least eight tanker incidents 
in the week to 7 October, and the attacks are spreading. One tanker was hit off the coast of Qatar, the first attack there 
in more than six months, and another off Oman, injuring 12 crew. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) also 
claimed a strike on a liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) carrier using an ‘illegal’ route and warned that such vessels could be 
targeted across the region. In our view, the aim is to raise the cost of the bypass routes and ship-to-ship transfers that 
have kept Gulf exports flowing. No tanker has yet suffered major structural or cargo damage, but flows through Hormuz 
have fallen from c.15 million barrels per day (mbpd) to around 10mbpd (7-day average). Separately, the Houthis struck 
Riyadh’s main airport at the weekend, forcing its closure.
On Russia and Ukraine, Trump announced an “energy ceasefire” under which Ukraine would stop striking Russian 
refineries and Russia would stop hitting Ukraine’s, a deal that favours Russian President Vladimir Putin. US officials have 
reportedly warned Kyiv and European allies that arms sales could stop if they do not back it. Ukrainian President 
Volodymyr Zelenskyy says Ukraine will hold off if Russia does, while EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas urged Trump to 
sanction Russia instead. More surprising was Friday’s diesel deal. The Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) issued a 
licence lifting sanctions on Russian diesel exports, and Russian vessels are reportedly heading to the US, though it is 
unclear what they are carrying. Initial volumes of around 125kbpd are small against a c.1.6mbpd shortfall in internationally 
traded diesel. If the ceasefire holds, it eases pressure on product markets. If it breaks down, renewed refinery strikes 
would restore the risk premium.

Commentary
India:  The Reserve Bank of India raised the repo rate by 25bps to 5.50%, its first hike since February 2023, in a 
unanimous decision. The central bank also shifted its stance from neutral to “calibrated tightening” by majority vote. 
The move reflects rising inflation risks, with CPI inflation at 4.82% in August, crude prices near USD 100 per barrel and 
global yields elevated after the Fed’s September hike, while growth remains resilient at 7.8% in Q1 FY27. HSBC expects 
September CPI, due on Monday, to rise to 5.5%.
Malaysia:  Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim presented a MYR 459.8bn budget for 2027, 3.6% above the revised 2026 
budget, comprising MYR 376.8bn of operating and MYR 83bn of development spending. The fiscal deficit is targeted at 
3.3% of GDP in 2027, down from a revised 3.6% in 2026, which was raised from 3.5% because of a higher subsidy bill. 
Revenue is projected to rise 4.7% to MYR 380.8bn, including a MYR 32bn dividend from Petronas. The budget raises 
the minimum wage to MYR 2,000 per month from June, lifts individual income tax relief for the first time since 2010 and 
allocates MYR 16bn in cash aid for lower-income households, ahead of a general election due by February 2028. The 
government expects growth near the top of its 4.8-5.3% range in 2026 and 4.2-5.2% in 2027.
Pakistan:  The World Bank raised its FY27 growth forecast to 3.8% from 3.2%, only marginally above the 3.7% expected 
for FY26. Inflation is projected to rise to 8.2%, above the central bank’s 5-7% target for a second year, while higher 
import costs push the current account to a deficit of 0.8% of GDP and the fiscal deficit widens to 3.5% of GDP, still 
within the government’s 3.6% target. The report also highlighted the social cost of successive shocks, with poverty up 
6.4pps between FY19 and FY25.
South Korea:  Samsung Electronics reported preliminary Q3 operating profit of KRW 107.4trn, the first Korean company 
to exceed KRW 100trn in a quarter and slightly above consensus. Revenue rose 127% yoy to KRW 195trn and the 
operating margin reached a record 55.1%, with memory chips driving almost all of the gains on strong AI-related 
demand and rising DRAM and NAND prices. Q3 profit alone was nearly 2.5 times the company’s full-year 2025 result, 
although the foundry, LSI and consumer electronics businesses likely remained loss-making.

Commentary
Argentina:  Economy Ministry officials told foreign investors that local USD bond issuance has been temporarily 
suspended, and that alternative financing is being sought for 2027, with remaining 2026 payments already covered. The 
pause follows weak demand: the Bonar 2029 drew only USD 71m of bids in August and was then dropped from three 
auctions as secondary yields rose as high as 13.4%. Local USD bonds were due to cover USD 5.0bn of the USD 24.9bn 
of FX needs in 2027, so the Treasury may lean more on purchases in the spot FX market.
Brazil:  Flávio Bolsonaro took the lead over President Lula in a runoff for the first time in Datafolha’s series, at 49% against 
45%, after winning the first round with 47.0% of valid votes. Former candidates Ronaldo Caiado and Romeu Zema, along 
with Centrão parties including União Brasil and Progressistas, have backed or leaned towards him ahead of the 25 October 
runoff. His Liberal Party won the largest caucus in both houses, though he would still need allies to reach the three-fifths 
threshold for constitutional changes, including judicial reform. Separately, September IPCA inflation rose 0.82% mom, 
above the 0.73% expected, taking the 12-month rate to 4.58%, just above the 4.5% ceiling of the target band, as the end 
of the Itaipu electricity bonus lifted power bills by almost 8%.
Chile:  Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz acknowledged that weakness initially concentrated in mining and other 
supply-hit sectors has spread to domestic demand. He expects copper output to fall around 7% this year to its 
lowest since 2003, subtracting 0.8pps from growth in Q1 and 1.2pps in Q2 before spillovers, and blamed years of

Colombia:  The meteorological institute (IDEAM) officially declared the arrival of El Niño, putting the probability of

Peru:  President Keiko Fujimori announced a unified command bringing together the armed forces and national police 
to fight organised crime, along with an elite special-operations unit, tighter prison controls and an inter-ministerial

kidnapping in Trujillo. The government’s request for delegated legislative powers on security passed the Lower House 
and is expected to clear the Senate shortly. Separately, the central bank held its reference rate at 4.25% for a 14th 
consecutive month, despite inflation of 4.5%, as it expects the fuel-driven overshoot to fade.
Venezuela:  The government reportedly agreed to set up a fund with Qatar to settle USD 2.5bn of arrears to the 
Inter-American Development Bank (IDB), with Qatar paying immediately and Venezuela repaying on concessional 
terms. Clearing the default would allow it to seek a new USD 8bn IDB loan for roads, water, budget management and 
public administration, as the country contends with daily blackouts and earthquake damage. Separately, state-
owned oil company PDVSA signed agreements with Baker Hughes and partners to develop gas processing, transport 
and export infrastructure, subject to approvals including from the US Treasury.

Commentary
Poland:  National Bank of Poland President Adam Glapiński said the Monetary Policy Committee was ready to act if 
second-round effects emerge, after fuel prices pushed inflation to 4.0% yoy in September, ending a 14-month run within 
the target band. He did not expect a hike in November but dropped his September guidance that rates would stay on hold 
until mid-2027. Glapiński welcomed the government’s renewed fuel tax cuts but warned inflation would jump in January if 
they expire as planned, making the November projection and fiscal decisions key for early-2027 policy.
Romania:  President Nicuşor Dan nominated diplomat Luca Niculescu as Prime Minister-designate, the fourth attempt to 
form a government since the previous cabinet fell in May. He has 10 days to secure a majority, but the arithmetic is difficult: 
the Social Democratic Party (PSD) wants clarity on deficit targets, Save Romania Union (USR) rejects PSD-influenced 
ministers, the National Liberal Party (PNL) wants to see the programme first and the nationalist parties will vote against. 
S&P affirmed the rating at ‘BBB-’ with a negative outlook, noting that fiscal consolidation has stayed on track but warning 
that prolonged delay could trigger a downgrade.
Ukraine:  A KIIS poll found 53% of respondents think President Zelenskyy should not seek another term, against 42% who 
think he should, with support down from 59% in December 2023 amid war fatigue and corruption scandals. However, 71% 
oppose holding elections during the war. Only 40% think the country is heading in the right direction, down from 68% in 
May 2022.

to the regional crisis, though this marks a slowdown from 5.1% in 2025/26. Strong remittances and tourism provide 
support, but inflation of 13.3% remains high and public debt is above 80% of GDP, while the fiscal deficit is projected to 
narrow to 6.5% of GDP. Suez Canal disruption has cost an estimated USD 16.5bn in FX earnings since November 2023.
Ghana:  Moody’s upgraded the long-term foreign currency rating to ‘B3’ from ‘Caa1’ and moved the outlook to stable from 
positive. The ratings agency pointed to a sustained easing in domestic financing conditions, which has lowered borrowing 
costs and meaningfully improved debt affordability, albeit from a weak starting point.
Kazakhstan:  JPMorgan placed government debt on Index Watch-Positive, opening the way to inclusion in the GBI-EM 
Global Diversified index with a preliminary weight of 0.48%, following reforms including a primary dealer system. The 
review can last up to 12 months and will assess liquidity, foreign investor access and settlement infrastructure. Separately, 
Kazakhstan and Oman signed eight agreements during the Sultan’s visit to Astana, including a joint investment fund 
between Samruk-Kazyna and the Oman Investment Authority.
Morocco:  Moody’s completed a periodic review of the country’s ‘Ba1’ rating with a positive outlook, citing a diversified 
economy, effective policymaking and strong access to financing. It expects growth of 4.5% in 2026 and 4.3% in 2027, 
inflation near 2.2% and the deficit stable at around 3.5% of GDP, with debt near 61%. The main risk is higher social spending 
by the government formed after the 23 September elections, while continued consolidation could lead to an upgrade.
Nigeria:  Fitch revised the outlook on its ‘B’ rating to positive from stable, citing continued reform of the policy framework 
and greater confidence that momentum will survive the upcoming elections. Monetary and FX reforms have supported 
greater Naira flexibility, disinflation and faster-than-expected reserve accumulation, although inflation remains well above 
peers and weak governance, hydrocarbon dependence and low revenue still constrain the rating. Separately, the World

Saudi Arabia:  The World Bank now expects GDP to contract 2.0% in 2026, a sharp revision from 3.1% growth in June, 
after the Houthis entered the war in July, imposing a naval blockade and striking tankers and oil infrastructure. The non-oil 
economy remains resilient on consumption, low unemployment and infrastructure spending, and growth is projected to 
rebound to 7.9% in 2027 on base effects. The budget deficit is seen widening to 6.6% of GDP this year before narrowing 
to 3.7% in 2027.
South Africa:  The South African Reserve Bank (SARB)’s new programme of direct interviews with firms, discussed for the 
first time in the October Monetary Policy Review, shows momentum weakening and higher fuel and input costs squeezing 
margins. Firms have so far absorbed part of these costs given weak demand, with no clear evidence yet of widespread 
second-round effects, but the review concludes that risks of generalised inflation have increased. The squeeze is visible 
in the data: manufacturing output fell 4.3% yoy in August, led by petroleum and chemicals, and headline inflation is 
expected to stay above 5% until Q2 2027.
Türkiye:  Ankara and Islamabad agreed to prepare forces for rapid deployment to Saudi Arabia under the Mecca Joint 
Defence Agreement, following renewed Houthi strikes including on an Aramco facility in Riyadh. No troops have yet 
deployed, numbers and timing were not specified, and Turkish deployment would require parliamentary approval, with 
ratification of the pact expected this month. Separately, September inflation fell to 29.7% yoy, the lowest since November 
2021 and below expectations, as food and transport prices slowed.
Zambia:  The Treasury called for tighter fiscal management ahead of the 2027 budget, flagging the expansion of 
constituencies, maize arrears and El Niño as risks, with growth projected at 5.3% in 2026 and 6.9% on average in 2027-
29. Financing for Zambia’s leg of the Lobito Corridor road link has been secured, a project that could cut mine-to-port 
transit from 28 days to about five. The World Bank said Zambia could regain eligibility for loans next year, rather than 
grants only, if stabilisation continues.
