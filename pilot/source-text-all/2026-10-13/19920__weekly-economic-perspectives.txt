US: Conflicting Signals 
In a rather quiet data week, the three main releases highlighted different aspects of 
the US economy. Meanwhile, minutes from the Fed September meeting did not 
indicate urgency for an October follow-up hike. We look for the next move in 
December. Partly offsetting this were comments from Governor Waller suggesting 
he expects the Fed to hike twice more. 
The non-manufacturing ISM index came in at a robust 54.9 in September, little 
changed from the month before. After languishing close to the neutral 50 level for 
about six months in the middle of 2025, the index crossed above 54.0 in February 
and with one exception (April), it has remained at or above that level ever since. 
The message is straightforward: the services side of the economy is doing well, 
which confirms the story of resilient growth despite shocks. The components were 
solid across the board, although the employment metric (50.1) remains the soft spot 
in the story, also confirming the story of modest payrolls expansion. Price pressures 
intensified, with the price metric reaching the highest (74.0) since July 2022.  
Meanwhile, the trade deficit widened sharply to $105.6 billion in August, the largest 
since March 2025, when firms were dramatically boosting imports to front-run tariffs.
Excluding the readings for January-March 2025, the August trade deficit was the 
highest on record. The AI investment boom has a lot to do with that as just four 
categories of goods account for about half the entire goods deficit at the moment

In contrast to these indicators of robust demand, consumer sentiment is languishing 
under the combined weight of elevated inflation and modest wage gains. The 
preliminary October reading for the University of Michigan consumer sentiment 
index dipped for the third consecutive month to 46.3, not far from the record low 
recorded in May. Inflation expectations ticked up by a tenth of a percentage point 
both at the 1-year horizon (4.7%) and the 5-10 year horizon (3.5%).  
CA:  Labor Data Push 
Back on Hikes 
Canada’s labor market weakened further in September, as employment fell for a 
second consecutive month and unemployment rate edged higher. 
Beyond the disappointing headline loss, the key signal is that unemployment rate 
has returned to its June level. We expect it to remain near this level in the coming 
months as trade uncertainty and higher interest rates weigh on activity. However, 
the report was not entirely weak: private-sector employment remained resilient 
despite a smaller labor force. 
Employment fell by 68k in September, well below consensus expectations for a 10k 
gain. The decline was broad-based, with full-time and part-time employment down 
35k and 33k, respectively. By contrast, private-sector employment rose by 24k, 
partly offsetting public-sector losses. 
The unemployment rate consequently rose to 6.5% from 6.4% in August. 
Meanwhile, the participation rate fell by 0.2 percentage points to 64.8%, below the 
65% consensus forecast. Wage growth strengthened slightly, with average hourly 
earnings rising by 2.3% y/y in September, up from 2.0% y/y in August.

Overall, the data set a high bar for a near-term Bank of Canada rate hike. 
Consecutive job losses, rising unemployment rate, contained wage pressures and a 
weaker Q4 outlook all weigh against further tightening. Markets still appear too 
hawkish, with labor demand fading and growth slowing, we expect the BoC to hold 
in October. 
 
Japan: Continued 
Divergence 
We highlighted the growing divergence between nominal wages and household 
consumption in our recently published quarterly outlook. That divergence has 
persisted and, importantly, the evidence of weaker consumption is now becoming 
broader and more consistent across indicators. 
The Bank of Japan’s (BoJ) real travel-balance-adjusted Consumption Activity Index 
(CAI) fell 0.8% m/m in August, marking a third consecutive decline and taking the 
three-month annualized contraction to 8.1%. Weakness was broad-based, with 
durable goods consumption falling 5.2%, non-durables 0.9% and services 0.2%. 
While the sharp decline in durables may partly reflect payback from earlier strength, 
simultaneous weakness across all three major categories is harder to dismiss. The 
BoJ’s own consumption measure is therefore pointing to a softer Q3 and reinforcing 
the need for caution in assuming that higher wages will generate stronger domestic 
demand. 
This is an important signal because the weakness is no longer confined to the 
Family Income and Expenditure Survey (FIES), Japan’s long-running monthly 
survey of household income and spending. FIES covers roughly 9,000 households 
and provides a direct measure of household spending behavior, although its 
relatively small sample makes the monthly data inherently volatile. Persistent 
weakness in FIES could previously be discounted to some extent as survey noise.

That becomes more difficult when the direction of travel is increasingly corroborated 
by the BoJ’s separate consumption indicator. 
The latest FIES data reinforces that caution. Real household spending fell 3.1% y/y 
in August, while spending among workers’ households declined 5.0% in nominal 
terms and 6.8% in real terms. More tellingly, the average propensity to consume 
eased to 59.8% (figure 3). This suggests that the weakness in spending is not 
simply a mechanical consequence of inflation eroding purchasing power. 
Households may also be allocating a smaller share of income to consumption, 
pointing to incomplete transmission from wage gains into final demand. 
 
That caution is understandable given the renewed intensity of price pressures. 
According to Teikoku Databank, 9,940 food and beverage items saw price 
increases in Q3, the highest quarterly total since Q2 2023, when the number was 
only 76 items higher. The pressure intensified around the quarter-end, with more 
than 8,000 items seeing price increases across September and October combined. 
These repeated price increases risk keeping households defensive even as their 
incomes improve.  
The contrast with wages is increasingly striking. Nominal cash earnings rose 3.8% 
y/y in August, slightly above expectations, while real wages increased 1.5%, 
extending their positive run to eight consecutive months. In other words, the income 
side of the BoJ’s virtuous wage-price cycle is increasingly visible, but the crucial 
second stage, from higher real incomes to stronger consumption, remains less 
convincing. The government is responding to this pressure, with Prime Minister 
Sanae Takaichi estimating that the planned consumption tax cut on food would 
reduce household burdens by around ¥36,000 per person, potentially providing 
some relief to consumption once implemented.

We remain hopeful that sustained wage growth will eventually allow consumption to 
turn the corner. However, the latest data creates an increasingly important policy 
tension. Repeated price increases are likely encouraging households to restrain 
spending, which, if sustained, could eventually place a natural ceiling on inflation 
through weaker demand. At the same time, a slower pace of BoJ normalization 
risks further yen weakness, keeping imported inflation elevated and continuing to 
squeeze household purchasing power. The BoJ therefore faces an uncomfortable 
feedback loop: stronger wages are supporting household incomes, but persistent 
price pressures and yen weakness are preventing those gains from translating fully 
into stronger demand. 
This makes the Q3 consumption picture particularly important. The case for further 
normalization is strengthening on wages, but the case for doing it quickly is 
weakening on consumption. The fall in the BoJ’s CAI is particularly significant 
because it corroborates the weakness already visible in FIES and suggests that the 
soft patch is not simply a statistical quirk. Tightening into broadening consumption 
weakness risks suppressing precisely the income-to-spending transmission that the 
BoJ needs to see strengthen before concluding that the virtuous cycle is becoming 
self-sustaining. 
For now, we continue to see a December or January hike as a meaningful risk, 
particularly given the strength in wages. However, we favor a March hike, giving the 
BoJ more time to establish whether consumption and growth have recovered 
sufficiently from the apparent Q3 soft patch before tightening again. The crucial 
question is no longer whether wages are rising. They clearly are. It is whether 
Japanese households are willing and able to spend those wage gains, and the 
latest consumption data argue for caution in assuming that transition has already 
taken place.
