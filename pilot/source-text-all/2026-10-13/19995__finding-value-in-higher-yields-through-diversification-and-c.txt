Key takeaways
	·
Energy inflation, fiscal concerns and heavy debt issuance have pushed long-term yields 
sharply higher. The increase has been driven primarily by rising real yields and term 
premia, while markets already price substantial monetary tightening
	·
Unlike the rate-hiking cycle of 2022-2023, the global economy is not overheating and 
wage pressures remain contained. Resilient growth, improving productivity and healthy 
corporate fundamentals suggest that central banks are more likely to fine-tune policy 
than pursue aggressive tightening
	·
Higher yields have restored value across fixed income, creating more attractive entry 
points – particularly in high-yield and emerging-market corporate bonds. We favour 
gradually adding exposure through diversified duration and selective credit rather than 
relying on a precise call on the peak in yields
	·
France is a special case. Sovereign spreads have widened to levels last seen in 2011 
and yields now exceed Greece’s, reflecting political uncertainty. However, France is not 
Greece in 2010: savings are strong, external balances are broadly stable and market 
access remains intact. We expect the European Central Bank to intervene only if 
stress extends beyond France, keeping us close to benchmark weight until the budget 
is approved.

Markets are already pricing significant tightening. The Federal 
Reserve raised its policy rate to 4.00% in September 2026, its 
first increase since 2023, after lowering it from 5.50% to 3.75% 
between September 2024 and December 2025. Market expectations 
(as at 3 October) point to US policy rates of 4.25% by December, 
4.75% by June 2027 and about 4.85% by the end of 2027. This 
sequence of three to four 25bp increases over 14 months would 
take the US benchmark rate back to its late-2024 level (Figure 3). 
The ECB policy rate is priced to reach 3.50% next year. Much of 
the expected tightening is thus already reflected in bond markets. 
For yields to move materially higher, central banks would need to 
tighten more aggressively than investors currently anticipate.
Why are yields higher and what do markets already price in?
Fixed income faces pressure from three sources:
	·
Higher energy prices and headline inflation, driven by the Strait of 
Hormuz closure
	·
The Federal Reserve (Fed), European Central Bank (ECB) and 
Bank of Japan have all raised benchmark interest rates
	·
Persistent fiscal deficits have revived concerns over debt 
sustainability, increasing long-term premia in a manner that is 
unrelated to the cycle.
Governments also face growing competition for investors from AI 
hyperscalers, which are tapping debt markets at a similar pace to 
sovereign borrowers. In the bond universe we analysed, corporate 
debt grew 7% year-on-year to the end of July, close to the 6.6% 
increase in Treasury issuance.1
Duration-sensitive assets have borne the brunt of the selloff, 
as higher yields reduce the value of future fixed-rate cash 
flows globally. The 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.34% on 
1 October before easing to around 5.24%, a level last seen in 
2007.2 Meanwhile the 10-year real yield, which strips out inflation 
compensation, rose to 2.9%, its highest level since the 2008 Global 
Financial Crisis (Figure 1). Credit spreads have proved more resilient, 
indicating this has primarily been a government-bond selloff rather 
than a broader credit event, although corporate bonds remain 
exposed to moves in sovereign yields.
The rise has been overwhelmingly driven by real yields. 
Since the start of the year, the 10-year Treasury yield has risen by 
1.1 percentage points, of which 1.0 percentage point reflects higher 
real yields. Inflation compensation has remained broadly stable at 
2.2%-2.5%, suggesting markets are not pricing an inflation spiral.

Why we do not expect a repeat of the 2022-2023 hiking cycle
The tightening of 2022-2023 responded to an overheating economy, 
wage growth approaching 6% and policy rates starting from zero. 
None of those conditions exists today.
Economic activity remains resilient and improving across regions, 
with little sign of overheating. The ISM manufacturing index stood 
at 54.5 in September, with new orders at 55.3 and employment at 
52.7.3 Readings above 50 signal expansion, but these levels point to 
healthy growth rather than excessive demand. By comparison, the 
index spent much of 2021 above 604 before inflation accelerated. 
Currently, earnings are healthy, defaults are broadly contained and 
stronger investment is supporting both real rates and cash flows. 
This helps explain why government yields have risen without a 
corresponding deterioration in credit markets.
Inflation is above target, but energy rather than wages is driving 
the overshoot. Headline inflation reached 3.4% in August, versus 
3.0% for core inflation, which excludes food and energy, meaning 
the gap stems from energy prices. Core inflation is now around one 
percentage point above the Fed’s 2% target, compared to more 
than five percentage points at the 2022 peak. The latest monthly 
increase of 0.2% was also below expectations. 
The labour market tells a similar story: average hourly earnings 
rose by 0.1% in September, below the expected 0.3%, while 
unemployment stood at 4.2%.5 Productivity rose 2.2% year-on-year 
in Q2, ahead of unit labour cost growth of 1.4%,6 meaning firms can 
absorb higher wages without raising prices. Input costs remain a 
pressure point, with the prices-paid component of the ISM survey 
rising from 71.1 to 77.9. So far, however, this has not translated into 
broader wage or price pressures. In simple terms, energy inflation 
has not become wage inflation.
Policy rates also start from a very different position, being close to 
neutral, not at zero. In March 2022, the Fed began hiking from a 
0-0.25% target range and ultimately raised rates by 525 bps in 16 
months. The ECB, meanwhile, lifted its deposit rate from −0.50% 
to 4.00% in 14 months. Today, there is no such gap to close, even if 
the exact level of neutral is uncertain. Our base case is therefore a 
recalibration of policy rather than a prolonged, aggressive tightening 
cycle. In this scenario, any outcome in which central banks deliver less 
tightening than markets currently expect would be supportive for bonds.
Market rates are already doing part of the work. A US 10-year real 
yield near 3%7 and a 30-year mortgage rate of 7.28% – close to the 
7.8% peak of October 2023 – raise borrowing costs for housing, 
investment and consumption. Sustained at current levels, they 
tighten financial conditions and reduce the need for additional policy 
action. Productivity gains add further support by allowing growth 
without generating significant inflation. The key uncertainty is how 
quickly those gains can be sustained.

Locking in attractive income
High starting yields and already-priced policy tightening have 
improved entry points across fixed income. Investors can lock in 
attractive income for several years, improving prospective returns 
and increasing the appeal of fixed income relative to other assets.
Table 1 illustrates this dynamic. It shows the total return of a one-
year investment in global investment grade, global high yield and 
emerging-market corporate bonds when yields fall or rise by up 
to 1.5 percentage points over the year, a fall of 1.5 points broadly 
taking yields back to early-2026 levels.
The case for equities, meanwhile, remains solid. Higher rates are 
reflecting stronger growth prospects and a substantial investment 
cycle rather than a deterioration in fundamentals. However, when 
real yields rise, equity returns depend more on earnings growth than 
rising valuations – performance in 2026 follows that pattern.
Historical evidence supports this view. Since 1998, the S&P 500 
has delivered lower returns when real yields exceeded 1.5% than 
when they were lower or negative. Figure 4 groups the months since 
1998 into three categories according to the level of the 10-year 
real yield. The S&P 500 gained 13% per year when real yields were 
negative, 7.8% when they stood between zero and 1.5%, and 5.5% 
when they exceeded 1.5% – the range in which today’s 2.9% sits. 
Earnings growth was the same in the last two groups, at about 
7% per year.
The difference, however, comes largely from valuations, which 
added 0.6 point per year below 1.5% and subtracted 1.6 points 
above. This year follows the same pattern: the market multiple has 
fallen from 22x to 19x earnings as real yields have risen by around 
one percentage point. At the same time, expected earnings have 
increased by 30% and the index has gained 13%. At 19x earnings, 
equities have become less expensive in 2026. Elevated real yields 
may limit valuation expansion, but sustained earnings growth can 
continue to support equity returns.

The special case of France
France warrants special attention. Markets are pressing the 
government to deliver fiscal consolidation, with the spread over 
German bonds reaching about 60 bps at two years and 140 bps at 
10 years – levels last seen during the European debt crisis of 2011 
(Figure 5). The situation is concerning, but two questions need to be 
considered separately.
	·
The beta question: should investors hold French debt 
below benchmark weight? France represents about 8% of 
global sovereign bond indices and roughly 25% of European 
sovereign benchmarks. Wider spreads mean investors are now 
being compensated with significantly higher yields: spreads have 
widened by about 60 bps since December 2025, while German 
long-term yields have risen by as much. While public finances are 
under pressure, private-sector fundamentals remain considerably 
stronger. Household savings are substantial, there is no marked 
trade deficit and the ECB has the propensity to step in should 
fragmentation threaten, as it has committed to doing in the past. 
Given France’s index weight and yield advantage, a sustained 
underweight position carries meaningful risks.
	·
The alpha question: is there tactical value in adding or 
reducing French exposure? The autumn budget is an immediate 
test but the presidential election in spring 2027 represents the more 
significant political event for interest rates. This period will require 
nimbleness, but we believe long-term value is more likely to emerge 
from adding exposure to French debt than from increasing existing 
underweights. That said, timing matters. A sustained increase of 
150 bps in yields would put French debt on the trajectory of US 
debt, despite a better-controlled primary deficit of about 3% of GDP 
(Figure 5). The risk is self-reinforcing: higher yields deteriorate the 
fiscal dynamic, which can trigger further spread widening and could 
create the fragmentation that concerns investors.
For these reasons, we continue to see value in French debt, but 
maintain a temporary neutral position until uncertainty surrounding 
the budget process is reduced.
Comparisons with Greece have become common since French 10-year 
yields rose above Greek equivalents. However, the macroeconomic 
backdrop bears little resemblance to Greece in 2010 (Figure 6). At 
the onset of the sovereign crisis, Greece had a budget deficit of 15% 
of GDP, a primary deficit of 10%, a current account deficit of 11% of 
GDP, low national savings of 8% of GDP and an economy about to 
shrink by a quarter. It lost market access within months and the ECB 
lacked dedicated anti-fragmentation tools until 2012.
France today has a deficit of around 5% of GDP, a primary deficit 
below 3%, a current account that is close to being balanced, 
national savings of 21% of GDP, positive growth, uninterrupted 
market access and an ECB with dedicated instruments against 
fragmentation. The two countries’ debt ratios are similar – 128% of 
GDP for Greece then and 118% for France now – but the underlying 
fundamentals are very different. France has a fiscal adjustment 
challenge; Greece faced a systemic economic crisis.
The comparison becomes more relevant only if borrowing costs 
remain 150 to 200 bps higher for several years, significantly 
worsening debt dynamics (Figure 5).
What could prompt ECB intervention?
Investors fear a scenario in which budget negotiations drag on, 
restructuring rhetoric gains traction in parts of the opposition, spreads 
widen further, higher borrowing costs worsen France’s debt trajectory 
(Figure 5), and the market keeps pushing until the ECB steps in. We 
believe the ECB would intervene only if stress spread beyond France. 
Its tools are designed to fight fragmentation of the eurozone as a 
whole, meaning contagion to countries such as Italy or Spain would 
matter more than the absolute level of French spreads. 
Until then, the pressure remains on Paris and the adjustment must come 
through fiscal policy. We do not view debt restructuring as a realistic 
outcome: France retains market access and runs a primary deficit of 
3% of GDP. The country does not need to restructure, and the Greek 
example shows the cost of doing so. Our view is that French spreads 
could widen further in the coming weeks, with the budget vote rather 
ECB intervention likely to be the first catalyst. We would look to add 
French exposure once the budget is passed or at the first sign of central-
bank support, but not before. This underpins our current neutral stance.

Diversified exposure with gradual and selective credit 
positioning
We favour diversified exposure across duration, credit and equity 
risk. High yield and emerging-market corporate bonds offer the 
most attractive entry points, but dispersion between issuers calls for 
selective credit positioning and for adding exposure gradually. 
Our outlook remains constructive despite uncertainty around the 
peak in yields. Limited policy tightening, moderating underlying 
inflation or easing geopolitical risks would support yield stabilisation. 
In that environment, income would drive bond returns, while any 
decline in yields would provide additional capital gains. 
Conversely, a sustained energy shock, a broader acceleration in 
wages and prices, or a further increase in fiscal and supply premia 
could prolong the selloff. Heavy government borrowing could also 
keep long-term yields elevated even if policy-rate expectations ease. 
Soft wage growth in the September US jobs report supports the 
constructive case, but widening French spreads are a reminder that 
bond investors remain acutely sensitive to sovereign risk.
