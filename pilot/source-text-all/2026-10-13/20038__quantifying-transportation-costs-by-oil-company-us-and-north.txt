Freight rates have surged 10-50x across all tanker classes since year start, 
leading to a wide divergence in realized price and margins for E&Ps and Refiners 
across the globe, and a return to regional trading in a traditionally global 
market. We introduce a route-by-route analysis with an evaluation of the relative 
advantages of oil producers and refiners, based on their geographical location and 
their long-term tanker availability. 
Shipping crude costs have risen up to $40/bbl on the longest hauls (vs $1.6/bbl a 
year ago), increasing the delivered cost of crude to $10.4/bbl on average across our 
coverage (vs $1.0/bbl a year ago). On the upstream side, North Sea producers (VAR, 
Aker-BP, Harbour, Equinor) and North American producers are relative winners, 
due to end-market proximity. On the LNG side the effect is far smaller in unit terms, 
with longer voyages adding c.$0.75-0.85/mcf to delivered cost on the most-affected 
lanes (Qatar-Europe +76% YoY, US-Asia +75%); the longer routes have been offset by 
lower volumes from Qatar.  
On the refining side, US Midwest and Gulf Coast refiners remain largely insulated due 
to significant access to domestic crude delivered via pipeline, while European 
refiners face an estimated $11-17/bbl increase in freight costs versus historical 
norms. Mediterranean refiners are better positioned than Northwest Europe, 
benefiting from shorter-haul imports from CPC, Libya and other nearby sources, 
while Asian refiners face the greatest pressure given their reliance on long-haul 
imported crude, with incremental freight costs exceeding $20/bbl on average and 
approaching $25/bbl in some cases. 
At the company level, Equinor appears the most advantaged, while Repsol and Galp 
screen as negatively impacted due to their dependence on long-haul imports from 
Brazil, the US Gulf Coast and Venezuela. BP, Shell and TotalEnergies should benefit 
from their physical trading capability and from a meaningful amount of their fleet 
(>25% on our estimates) on long-term rates, although there is no good data available 
on their exact fleet contracting.

Shipping crude costs have risen up to $40/bbl on the longest hauls vs $1.6/bbl a year ago 
 
 
The cost of shipping crude has risen sharply this year and is now a material component 
of the delivered oil price, reaching up to c.$40/bbl on the longest hauls. A VLCC (Very 
Large Crude Carrier, c.2mn barrels) chartered from the Gulf to China currently earns 
c.$1.4mn/day, versus under $0.1mn a year ago, equivalent to c.$40/bbl of freight on 
that voyage against c.$1/bbl at year start. The cost scales with vessel size and distance: 
VLCCs carry the long-haul Gulf and Atlantic-to-Asia barrels where the impact is greatest, 
while Suezmaxes (c.1mn bbl) and Aframaxes (c.0.7mn bbl) serve shorter regional routes, 
leaving a North Sea-to-Continent cargo at only c.$6/bbl. The repricing extends beyond 
Asia and the Strait of Hormuz: with the larger vessels drawn east, Atlantic freight has 
also tightened, lifting Brazil-ARA Europe and West-Africa-ARA Europe to c.$20-25/bbl 
from $2/bbl a year ago.

On the refining side, US Midwest and Gulf Coast refiners remain largely insulated, while European 
refiners face an estimated $11-17/bbl increase in freight costs vs historical norms; Asian refineries the 
hardest hit 
 
 
We estimate the incremental freight cost embedded in global refining systems by 
comparing current spot tanker rates with 10-year averages. While VLCC rates in the 
Middle East-China route have risen to c.$1.4m/day versus a c.$40k/day historical 
average, the impact varies materially by refining system, depending on crude sourcing, 
transport distance and access to domestic supply. 
US refiners remain the most insulated, while Asia face the largest freight burden. US 
Midwest and Gulf Coast refiners benefit from domestic crude delivered via pipeline, 
limiting incremental freight costs to just $0-1/bbl. European refiners face an additional 
$11-17/bbl versus historical norms, although Mediterranean refiners are better 
positioned due to shorter-haul imports from CPC, Libya and other North African 
sources. Northwest European refiners benefit from access to North Sea crude, which 
accounts for c.25-30% of crude intake across the region and as much as 70-80% in 
Finland, providing a partial cushion against higher freight costs, though not enough to 
offset broader exposure to long-haul imports from the US Gulf Coast, Brazil, Venezuela 
and West Africa. 
Asian refiners face the greatest pressure given their reliance on imported seaborne 
crude. Incremental freight costs exceed $20/bbl on average in our analysis and can 
surpass $30/bbl on some long-haul routes. China is relatively better positioned, with 
c.30% of crude supply sourced domestically or via pipelines, while India and Thailand 
are only c.10-15% insulated. South Korea and Japan remain among the most exposed 
globally, relying on seaborne imports for nearly all crude supply. 
Headline freight costs may be partially offset by wider crude discounts, as 
producers and traders absorb part of the freight burden to maintain crude 
competitiveness and volumes. As tanker rates rise, refiners are increasingly seeking 
compensation through lower crude acquisition costs, while producers appear willing to 
offer larger discounts to maintain volumes and market share. Recent pricing moves from 
Middle Eastern exporters and market checks across Asia suggest part of the freight 
burden is already being absorbed through wider crude differentials, helping limit the full 
pass-through of higher transportation costs to refiners.

Company implications across EU and US coverage 
 
 
The analysis below assumes all crude transportation is exposed to current spot 
freight rates and therefore measures gross freight exposure rather than realized 
earnings impact. Companies with long-term charter coverage are likely to experience 
materially lower freight inflation than implied by the spot-rate scenario. Actual 
exposure to spot shipping rates varies significantly across companies. We estimate 
Galp is close to fully exposed to spot freight markets, Equinor has minimal spot exposure 
due to long-term charter coverage, TotalEnergies is exposed to spot markets for roughly 
70% of crude and product transportation, while Helleniq effective spot exposure is likely 
closer to 40-50%, reflecting regional crude sourcing, delivered crude contracts and 
shorter transportation distances. 
Across our upstream coverage, North Sea/Europe-weighted producers and 
US-weighted producers screen as the least exposed to higher freight costs, while 
companies with greater exposure to long-haul Middle East, West African and Latin 
American trade flows are the most affected. Under a spot-rate framework, North 
Sea/Europe-weighted producers (the E&Ps, OMV, Equinor) and US-weighted producers 
(ConocoPhillips, Chevron) screen as the least impacted by higher shipping costs, while 
TotalEnergies, Shell, Repsol and particularly Galp show the highest freight sensitivity. 
For refiners, exposure is driven by refinery geography, crude sourcing and fleet 
contracting structure. XOM (covered by Neil Mehta) screens as the least exposed 
given its predominantly US refining footprint and access to domestic crude supply, while 
BP and Shell also rank among the least affected in downstream due to c.40-45% US 
exposure. OMV screens among the least affected, with c.40% of refining capacity 
benefiting from access to regional crude supply in Romania and the Middle East, limiting 
reliance on seaborne imports. Mediterranean refiners such as HELLENiQ, ENI and Motor 
Oil remain relatively well positioned, benefiting from shorter-haul imports from CPC, 
North Africa and the Middle East. At the other end of the spectrum, Galp and Repsol 
screen as the most exposed European refiners due to their reliance on long-haul 
imports from Brazil, the US Gulf Coast and Venezuela, while Chevron (covered by Neil 
Mehta) ranks among the most exposed names in our coverage given its significant c.40% 
refining presence in import-dependent Asian markets (Korea, Thailand, Singapore).

Unlike crude, LNG shipping has experienced limited tonne-mile inflation despite ongoing disruptions 
 
 
LNG trade flows have been materially rerouted since the onset of shipping 
disruptions, with US exports increasingly replacing Qatari volumes across key 
consuming regions. US Gulf-Asia cargoes rose 110% YoY in Jan-Sep 2026, while US 
Gulf-Europe volumes increased 2%, compared with declines of c.70% for Qatar-Europe 
and Qatar-Asia. Despite mixed underlying cargo trends, freight costs increased across all 
major LNG trade lanes, averaging +60% YoY. In our view, the increase points to a 
reshuffling of global LNG trade flows with US suppliers emerging as the primary 
beneficiaries of changing trade patterns. 
Unlike crude and product tankers, LNG shipping has experienced limited tonne-mile 
inflation despite ongoing disruptions. Crude oil and distillate shipping activity 
expanded sharply as cargoes were rerouted around trade bottlenecks, driving a 
significant increase in volumes on the water and tanker demand. By comparison, 
LNG-on-the-water volumes have remained broadly stable throughout the period, 
suggesting LNG trade routes have not lengthened materially and vessel demand has not 
experienced the same structural uplift seen in oil markets. As a result, while LNG freight 
rates have risen, they are substantially lower than the dislocation observed across crude 
and refined product shipping. 
LNG freight markets reacted sharply to the initial disruption phase but have 
subsequently normalised. Freight rates across key LNG corridors spiked materially 
during periods of heightened geopolitical uncertainty before retracing as market 
participants adapted to revised trading patterns. While current rates remain elevated 
versus last year, averaging +60% YoY across the major routes analysed, they are well 
below the peak levels reached during the initial market adjustment. This, in our view, 
suggests today’s LNG shipping market is characterised by a higher but more stable 
freight rate environment, reflecting an industry that has largely adapted to rerouted 
global trade flows.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.
