US Equity Market Trading at a 10% Discount to a Composite of Our Fair Value Estimates
Based on a composite of our intrinsic valuations of the over 700 stocks we cover that 
trade on US exchanges, the US equity market was trading at a price/fair value of 0.90 as 
of Sept. 30, 2026. This indicates that the market was trading at a 10% discount to a 
composite of our fair value estimates. However, we caution that the discount is heavily 
skewed by seven mega-cap stocks tied to AI that account for almost the entire discount. 
Excluding those stocks from our calculation, the remaining composite is fairly valued.
Volatility in 2026 created multiple opportunities for investors to reallocate their portfolio 
weightings to take advantage of market dislocations. For example, coming into the year, 
we initially recommended a barbell-shaped portfolio, overweighting growth and value 
and underweighting core. After a sharp selloff in February and March, in our second-
quarter outlook, we advised investors to take gains in value and reinvest the proceeds 
into deeply discounted growth stocks. Growth staged a quick comeback, and as 
valuations rose toward fair value, in our third-quarter outlook, we advised locking in 
those gains and moving to an equal weighting across value, core, and growth. 
Current valuations point us back toward a barbell-shaped portfolio, slightly 
overweighting growth and value and underweighting core. Yet, with the preponderance 
of undervalued opportunities tied to only seven stocks, the market has increasingly 
become a stock-pickers market. Within the growth category, we would overweight those 
specific undervalued stocks driving most of the undervaluation and steer clear of long-
duration, non-AI growth stocks at risk of selling off if rates continue to rise.

US Stock Market Is Undervalued 
While the broad market appears to be trading at an 
attractive margin of safety, we think the US stock market is 
entering a potentially risky environment. Undervaluation is 
highly concentrated, momentum has reversed, and 
macrodynamics are increasingly negative.
Undervaluation is predominantly concentrated in just 
seven mega-cap stocks: Nvidia, Alphabet, Broadcom, 
Microsoft, Meta Platforms, Amazon.com, and Tesla. Each 
investment thesis depends heavily on our expectations for 
continued AI infrastructure spending and the long-term 
economic value ultimately created by artificial 
intelligence. Excluding these seven, our remaining 
coverage is trading at a fair value.
On the macrodynamics front, headwinds are building, 
leading us to be cautious. Interest rates have been rising 
in the US and internationally. The Fed is tightening 
monetary policy, with one more hike expected by year-end. 
Inflation will likely remain elevated as oil prices continue 
to climb higher. International economies are relatively 
stagnant , and the potential for systemic risk from rising 
interest rates on Japanese government bonds and a 
weakening yen continues to increase.

Diverging Fortunes Across the AI Economic Chain Lead to Wide Range of Returns
The Morningstar US Market Index edged up 1.69% in the third quarter as value stocks 
rose enough to more than offset losses in the growth category. 
The growth category fell 4.24%, with most of the loss coming from semiconductor 
manufacturing equipment manufacturers and several commodity-oriented technology 
hardware providers. Gains in the value category were driven by strong performance in 
the healthcare and energy sectors, partially offset by losses in the consumer cyclical 
sector. The number of stocks that declined in the core category was enough to offset 
otherwise healthy gains in Microsoft, Apple, Meta, and Amazon. 
The large-cap category trounced mid- and small-caps as the large-cap stocks most tied 
to the AI buildout boom continued to perform well. In addition, rising oil prices lifted the 
largest energy sector stocks. Within the mid-cap category, industrial stocks considered 
second-derivative plays on the AI buildout boom retreated, and several commodity-
oriented technology hardware stocks fell precipitously. Small-caps took it on the chin 
during the third quarter. Small-cap stocks with high debt leverage are especially 
vulnerable to rising interest rates as their debt is often floating rate. In addition, few 
small-cap companies’ businesses have been able to benefit from the AI buildout boom.

Returns Remain Balanced as AI Leaders Offset AI Followers
The Morningstar US Market Index rose 12.56% year to date through the end of the third 
quarter. Note that the broad market index is unconstrained and reflects the full market 
capitalization increase of mega-cap stocks, whereas the category indexes are 
constrained by market capitalization and do not reflect the full market capitalization 
increase of mega-caps.  
Each of the value, core, and growth categories has taken turns outperforming and 
underperforming thus far this year, yet has generally ended up in the same place. Value 
stocks have benefited from rotation into defensive sectors such as healthcare but also 
benefited from the surge in the energy sector as oil prices soared after the conflict with 
Iran began. The technology sector drove 80% of the core category's return, led by tech 
hardware stocks such as Micron, Intel, and Western Digital. Within the growth category, 
the tech sector contributed a larger gain than the overall sector, as communication, 
consumer cyclicals, and financial services detracted from the category return. Three 
stocks (NVDA, AMD, and SNDK) accounted for 60% of the category gain. 
Large-cap stocks have been the primary beneficiaries of the AI buildout, and that trend 
continued this year. Companies at the forefront of AI account for eight of the 10 largest 
contributors to large-cap performance. In the mid-cap space, gains have been driven 
largely by a small group of commodity-oriented technology hardware companies whose 
shares have surged several hundred percent amid severe supply shortages and windfall 
profits. We caution that many of these stocks now rank among the most overvalued in 
our coverage. Small-cap stocks started the year strongly but lost momentum in the third 
quarter, resulting in year-to-date underperformance.

Volatility Provided Multiple Opportunities to Capitalize on Distortions
Market volatility created multiple opportunities to capitalize on valuation dislocations. We began with a barbell-shaped portfolio, took gains in value stocks and redeployed capital into 
deeply discounted growth names, then harvested profits as those valuations recovered and shifted to an equal-weight stance last quarter. Current valuations favor a return to a barbell 
approach, with modest overweights in growth and value and an underweight in core. However, with most undervalued opportunities concentrated, the market has become increasingly 
driven by stock selection. Within growth, we favor those select undervalued AI names while avoiding long-duration, non-AI growth stocks that remain vulnerable to interest rates.

Surging Oil Prices Lead Energy Higher While AI Leaders and Rebounding Software Bolster Technology
The energy sector has been especially volatile this year as 
oil prices have soared, retreated, and surged higher as the 
conflict with Iran drags on into its eighth month.  
In the technology sector, after struggles in the first half of 
the year, MSFT's recovery accounted for almost 60% of the 
sector's return. Returns were bolstered by a combination 
of AI leaders and recovery in software providers. 
Detractors this past quarter were concentrated among 
semiconductor manufacturing equipment manufacturers 
and commodity-oriented hardware. 
Gains in the healthcare sector were widespread as the 
rotation into defensive-oriented names lifted most boats.
 
Starting the quarter in overvalued territory and often 
considered a fixed-income substitute, the utility sector 
was the worst-performing sector this past quarter. 
Similarly, the industrial sector was the most overvalued 
coming into the third quarter and sold off as several 
overvalued stocks considered second-derivative plays on 
AI retreated from their highs. Real estate retreated as the 
sector is negatively correlated with interest rates.

High Oil Prices Trounce Even AI Buildout Boom
The energy sector started the year as one of the most 
undervalued sectors, but a more than 50% rise in oil prices 
has propelled it higher. 
Technology has been the single greatest beneficiary of the 
AI buildout boom, yet investors should be wary of the 
sector's valuation, as it blends several significantly 
undervalued mega-cap stocks with some of the most 
overvalued commodity tech hardware stocks. 
Industrials considered a second-derivative play on AI have 
driven the sector higher, while non-AI industrials struggle 
in an economy heavily reliant on AI growth. Losses among 
device makers detracted from healthcare, yet rotation into 
the sector has lifted most other stocks. 
Shifts in consumer spending to experiences, high energy 
prices, and inflation have battered the consumer cyclical 
sector. Rising interest rates have taken their toll on utility 
stocks, as fixed-income rates have become increasingly 
attractive compared with utility dividend yields. 
While traditional bank stocks have held up, nonbank 
constituents have struggled as rising interest rates and 
credit concerns move to the forefront.

Attribution Analysis: Contributors Q3 2026
The top 10 contributors added more to the market index 
return than the quarterly index total return, highlighting 
the overall market's poor breadth. While prior-quarter 
contributors were technology hardware stocks tied solely 
to the AI buildout boom, this quarter we saw a more 
diverse set of contributors. 
After being pulled down with the rest of the software 
space since last fall and dropping all the way into 5-star 
territory, MSFT finally rebounded to its prior highs as the 
market came around to our view that AI would not 
displace the company’s software dominance. 
With its technological lead in AI GPUs, NVDA continued to 
perform well. Companies using AI to drive new economic 
value, such as META, PLTR, and AMZN, also performed 
well. Tertiary plays on AI, such as the increased need for 
cybersecurity, bolstered cybersecurity stocks such as 
CrowdStrike. 
Surging oil prices rounded out the contributors, as energy 
stocks such as XOM and CVX rode the upward trend.

Attribution Analysis: Detractors Q3 2026
Many of the third-quarter detractors were contributors 
earlier in the year whose stocks rose too far, too fast. 
Although semiconductor demand remains strong and 
manufacturing equipment is still needed to make 
semiconductors, stocks such as AMAT, KLAC, LRCX, and 
INTC retreated after running up too far in the first half of 
the year. 
Commodity-oriented technology hardware stocks peaked 
in June and rolled over as the market became more 
concerned that supply will catch up to demand in the next 
few years and the current excess profits will quickly 
dissipate. Examples include MU and GLW. Similarly, 
tertiary plays such as CAT, tied to data center buildouts, 
have pulled back.

Over Past Quarter, Most Sector Valuations Have Trended Toward Deeper Discount
The greatest changes in our sector valuations occurred in 
the industrial and utility sectors, which were the most and 
third-most overvalued sectors last quarter. Both sectors 
fell last quarter, with utilities the worst and industrials the 
second-worst performers.  Consumer cyclical and real 
estate were the next two worst-performing sectors, 
pushing their valuations deeper into undervalued territory. 
Energy was the top-performing sector last quarter, and the 
resulting appreciation erased the prior-quarter discount.  
Consumer defensive was the most overvalued last quarter, 
but after pullbacks in WMT and COST, the sector now 
trades at only a slight premium. We continue to see 
significant value in food stocks. 
Communication remains the most undervalued sector. 
While 4-star-rated GOOGL skews the sector valuation 
lower, we see a wide range of undervalued opportunities.

Growth Boost From AI to Fade by 2028
GDP growth has downshifted a gear after averaging 2.8% over 2022-24. This has been 
driven by lower population growth, the expiration of temporary factors that boosted 
growth, and the lingering effects of high interest rates. 
We expect the boost to GDP growth from AI to diminish as spending grows at a more 
reasonable pace. That causes GDP growth to slow a bit further through 2028. Slower 
GDP growth reduces the demand for workers, so we expect the unemployment rate to 
tick up to an average of 4.7% in 2028 from 4.3% in 2025. Easing monetary policy and 
other factors should drive a rebound in GDP growth in the later years of our forecast.
Inflation to Resume Falling After 2026
The oil shock from the Iran war contributes around 0.8 percentage points to inflation in 
2026. Tariffs are still exerting upward pressure. As these factors fade, inflation should 
resume falling in the coming years. Housing inflation continues trending down. And 
wage growth has already returned to levels consistent with inflation at or below the 
Fed’s 2% target.

Fed Ultimately Needs to Push Interest Rates Lower to Support Economic Growth
We Project 10-Year Treasury Yield of 3.50% in 2029 and Later Years
In our view, substantial further interest rate cuts will be needed to drive longer-run 
borrowing rates down and thereby support continued robust economic growth. 
Accordingly, the Fed will cut rates steeply, sufficient to drive the 10-year Treasury yield 
to an average 3.5% by 2029 (down from 5.3% as of early October).
We’re in line with the market in the near term, expecting one more rate hike through the 
end of 2026. After that, our view diverges from the market. We expect progress on core 
inflation along with weakening growth to push the Fed to cut rates twice in the second 
half of 2027. We expect four more rate cuts in 2028 as unemployment averages 4.7% 
that year, above consensus projections of 4.2%.
Altogether, that will bring the federal-funds rate down to 2.50%-2.75% by the end of 
2028, 1.25 percentage point below the current rate. Our expectations are 175 basis 
points below the market expectations. We believe the market’s terminal rate is much too 
high, reflecting an overestimation of the natural rate of interest.

to attract new buyers. Contagion from these lower prices

Investors Counting on Utilities’ Growth To Stop Recent Sector Slump 
US Utilities Headed in Reverse After 2024-25 Rally
Utilities’ Valuations Much More Attractive After Pullback
Utilities’ historic 2024-25 rally is now history. Since hitting an all-time peak in February, 
the Morningstar US Utilities Index is down 16% while the US market is up 13%, both 
including dividends. Utilities have gone from the best-performing sector during the last 
two years to the worst-performing sector since February. All of utilities’ relative gains 
against the market since 2024 are gone.
Two concerns have fueled the downturn. First, rising interest rates are finally catching 
investors’ attention. Utilities stocks rallied the last two years even as interest rates 
doubled. Dividend yields hit all-time lows below 3% early this year, an historically wide 
discount to interest rates. Utilities’ yields have come up from those lows but still have a 
long way to climb—and stocks a long way to fall—to catch interest rates.
Second, data center energy demand growth is hitting regulatory hurdles. We still expect 
utilities to benefit from data center growth, but that growth could take longer than 
investors initially expected. Power producers that led utilities’ 2024-25 rally have led the 
recent slide as they struggle to fulfill investors’ high growth expectations.
One upside for investors is that utilities’ valuations are more reasonable. The sector is 
9% undervalued as of late September based on our fair value estimates, the cheapest 
since early 2024. The sector’s median 19 P/E is in line with its 20-year average, yet 
growth prospects are much better, suggesting current valuations don’t fully capture the 
growth we think is coming. Investors could find some good bargains.
