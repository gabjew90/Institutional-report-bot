Market comment 
US and European equity indices were firmly in the black on Friday as the recent run up in bond 
yields took a breather. The S&P500 closed 46 points higher at 7811 with strong gains across the 
real estate, consumer discretionary, healthcare and financial sectors. US 10-year yields were just 
over 1bp higher on the day, but down by almost 7bps over the course of the week with most of 
the decline in yields arriving on Thursday. 
The yield on French 10-year bonds fell by 4.2bps to close the week slightly lower. That helped to 
propel the CAC40 to a 0.95% gain on Friday, with Arcelor Mittal, Capgemini, L’Oreal, Hermes, and 
Renault among the most prolific gainers. Renault shares got a boost from news that the European 
Union will enact a 350,000 quota against imports of Chinese hybrid and plug-in hybrid vehicles, 
above which much steeper tariffs will kick in.  
For context, Chinese exports of hybrid cars into the European market have risen from 
approximately 300,000 in 2025 to an estimated 800,000 in 2026 according to the Financial Times. 
EU Trade Commissioner Maros Sefcovic expects the new safeguard measure to reduce Chinese 
imports by more than 50%. Importantly, the measures were agreed bilaterally with Beijing 
(thereby minimising risk of retaliation) and do not require any evidence of market abuse to be 
enacted. 
Politico reported over the weekend that Germany would like Britain to raise tariffs on Chinese 
electric vehicles in exchange for preferential access to the European market amid a lingering sense 
on the continent that Britain should not be allowed to cherry-pick the benefits of EU membership. 
A desire to make Britons feel the consequences of emphasising their own sovereignty above 
economic access in 2016 is particularly acute in France, where temptations toward nationalistic 
chest-thumping are perhaps encouraged by centuries of rivalry with Britain and the context of an 
impending Presidential election where populist candidates are expected to do well. 
The German quid-pro-quo for Britain sounds eerily reminiscent of the deal offered to Canada by 
the United States, whereby a common external tariff vis-à-vis China seems to have been the price 
of admission for Canada to secure the most favourable terms of access to the US market of any 
country. There was plenty of nationalistic chest-thumping (or raising of elbows) in that case, too, 
but the Trump administration saw that arrangement as necessary to prevent China from back-
dooring goods into the American market via Canadian ports. In Britain’s case, the move seems to 
be more about signalling solidarity since the fact that the steering wheel is on the wrong side of 
the car already acts as a trade barrier of sorts preventing Chinese hybrids from flowing through 
the UK into Europe in large numbers. 
Canada famously spurned the United States’ offer so it could maintain full sovereignty over trade 
decisions, but this has come at a cost for Canadian exporters who had typically sent 
approximately two thirds of their products into the US market. Employment figures released by 
Statistics Canada last week confirmed that the Canadian economy shed 68,300 jobs in September, 
following on from a 41,700 decline in employment in August. The unemployment rate rose from 
6.4% to 6.5% despite a decline in workforce participation. The Canadian economy likely would 
have been in even worse shape had the wars in the Middle East and Eastern Europe not boosted 
demand for Canadian potash and energy products.

While the EU’s new protectionist measures vis-à-vis Chinese hybrid electric vehicles hold sectoral 
significance, they miss the structural problem of large and growing imbalances driven in large part 
by Chinese overproduction amid weak domestic demand. Unsurprisingly, captains of European 
industry from other sectors (notably, chemicals) are already agitating for similarly robust 
protectionist measures to be enacted against Chinese imports that compete with their own 
products. Eventually the European Commission may run out of sectoral policy fingers to plug the 
holes in the dike and simply opt for blanket protection – tariffs – in the way that the US has 
already done. 
The problem is unlikely to be solved on the Chinese end. Despite frequent nods towards domestic 
rebalancing in favour of greater consumption, policy continues to prioritise investment and 
production, especially in areas of high tech. Any large policy tilt towards consumerism faces 
ideological opposition as well as – arguably – financial constraints, given China’s rapid public debt 
accumulation and already-stretched fiscal balance. Raising taxes to address fiscal issues might be 
self-defeating if the intention is to boost consumption through more generous social programs, 
but China is raising taxes on wealthy Chinese who shelter assets offshore.  
The huge US investments in AI and other high-tech applications (mostly financed by private 
capital) have pushed China into an investment arms race of sorts that could leave limited fiscal 
room for social programs. This bears some similarities to the military arms race that Reagan 
enacted against the Soviet Union in the 1980s, but does the United States still hold the financial 
superiority that is required to win such a race? 
Not to be overlooked, geopolitical events over the weekend are again shaping up to be major 
drivers for energy markets. In a positive sign, Donald Trump announced that Russia and Ukraine 
had agreed to an “energy ceasefire”, but the ceasefire was promptly broken by Russian strikes on 
power plants in Kharkiv and Kiev. Trump had earlier indicated that it might be time for Zelenskyy 
to step down as Ukraine’s President after repeated requests from the United States to cease 
attacks on Russian oil refineries went unheeded. According to reports of statements made by 
anonymous Ukrainian officials, the energy ceasefire came about after US negotiators indicated 
that the US could withdraw intelligence sharing if those requests continued to be ignored. 
Another factor may have been a deal announced by Donald Trump between Russia and the USA 
that would see 1.8m tons of Russian diesel granted sanctions relief to be exported into the US 
market in coming months. President Zelenskyy described the deal as a “gift to Russia” and the 
deal faces resistance from lawmakers in Congress, but the White House may view the deal as 
killing two birds with one stone as it seeks to lower fuel costs for American consumers and 
pressure Ukraine not to engage in tactics perceived to run counter to US interests. 
The Middle East, too, continued to produce headlines that will influence energy markets. Yemen’s 
Houthis launched strikes on Saudi Arabia’s King Khalid international airport in Riyadh, killing at 
least twelve people and reportedly injuring hundreds more. The strikes follow a renewed offensive 
last week by Saudi Arabia and Yemeni government forces along the Red Sea coast to reclaim 
territory lost to the Houthis. The strikes coincide with Energy Week in Riyadh and have seen 
several oil and gas executives reconsider plans to attend conferences in the Saudi capital. 
There was also further news of strikes on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz over the weekend. The 
IRGC claimed that a ship attempting to navigate a route not approved by Iran struck a marine 
mine, while the UK’s Maritime Trade Office reported that two ships had been struck by projectiles 
off the coast of the UAE and in the Gulf of Oman. US CENTCOM also confirmed that it had 
immobilised a Panama-flagged vessel attempting to run the American blockade of Iranian ports. 
Nevertheless, Brent crude is trading lower this morning at $103/bbl at time of writing.

Week ahead 
Monday: China money supply figures for September are due out and the Fed’s Hammack will be 
speaking at the NABE Conference. 
Tuesday: Japan PPI and bank lending data for September gets the ball rolling. Following that we 
will see the NAB Business Survey and the September RBA policy meeting minutes out of Australia, 
the final read of September CPI out of Germany, and the results of the NFIB small business survey 
alongside the latest home sales figures for the USA. 
Fed’s Barkin and Collins are speaking, as is the BOE’s Dhingra. 
Wednesday: China CPI and PPI for September are the highlight of the Asian session, with 
supporting acts from NZ net migration figures, the Westpac leading index out of Australia and 
September money supply figures for Japan. Out of Europe we get the final read of September CPI 
for Spain and a host of ECB speakers (including Lagarde), and then its off to the USA where 
September CPI should provide the data highlight of the weak. Core CPI is seen rising from 2.4% to 
2.5%. The Fed will also be releasing its Beige Book. 
Thursday:  China trade figures for September are due out, as are Japan machine orders and 
September labour force figures for Australia. The latter will be interesting for local rate market 
dynamics as the unemployment rate tests the RBA’s estimate of the minimum sustainable level 
following four rate hikes this year. In the European session we will see August monthly GDP and 
manufacturing production figures for the UK, followed by the final read of French CPI for 
September. Out of the US we will see September retail sales figures, the usual weekly jobless 
claims, September PPI inflation, and the result of the latest Empire Manufacturing survey. 
Friday:  The bull market in central banker fireside chats rolls on with appearances by Fed Chair 
Kevin Warsh and BOE Governor Bailey. Elsewhere we get US industrial production figures for 
September, the final read for Eurozone September CPI, and New Zealand food prices and card 
spending data.
