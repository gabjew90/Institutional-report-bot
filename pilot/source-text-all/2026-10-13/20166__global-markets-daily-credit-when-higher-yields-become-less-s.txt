Credit: When Higher Yields Become Less Supportive 
 
 
For credit investors, the relationship between government yields and credit spreads 
is an important determinant of total returns. Higher government yields are not 
necessarily adverse for credit when they reflect stronger growth expectations, as 
there should be a positive read-through for corporate profitability. Moreover, tighter 
spreads in such an environment could provide a partial offset to the negative 
duration impact. This was broadly the pattern through much of 2024 and 2025. 
The recent price action has been different. Since July, USD and EUR government 
yields and credit spreads have moved higher in tandem rather than offsetting one 
another (Exhibit 1). The one- and six-month spread/rates correlations have also 
moved more firmly into positive territory in the USD market, after remaining largely

The more important question, in our view, is whether this reflects a temporary response 
to a rapid rates repricing or a more persistent change in the macro backdrop for credit. 
That distinction is particularly relevant given the importance of elevated all-in yields for 
credit demand in both USD and EUR IG credit markets. 
Oil, Fiscal Risks, and the Speed of the Move Help Explain the Shift 
Historically, the relationship between rates and spreads has been state-dependent. In a 
benign scenario of a positive growth shock, higher government yields can coincide with 
tighter spreads, as stronger growth supports corporate earnings and reduces downside 
macro risks. By contrast, in inflationary or fiscal shocks, both rates and spreads can sell 
off together. Two features of the recent macro backdrop help explain the latest move. 
First, the spread/rates correlation becomes less negative as oil prices rise. Higher energy 
prices make a rise in government yields more inflationary and less benign for credit, 
particularly where higher input costs and weaker real income growth may weigh on the 
growth outlook (Exhibit 3). 
 
Second, spread/rates correlations tend to turn positive when the rise in Treasury or Bund 
yields is rapid. The speed of the move matters because it gives investors less time to 
distinguish between a benign rise in yields associated with stronger growth and a more 
adverse move associated with inflation, fiscal risk, or higher term premia (Exhibit 4). 
In Europe, these pressures have been compounded by renewed fiscal concerns. French 
corporate issuers have remained relatively resilient during the OAT sell-off, with a record 
number trading inside comparable-maturity OATs. But the repricing in French 
government bonds has added to broader concerns around European fiscal risks, growth, 
and reform momentum (Exhibit 5).

Tight spreads and flat curves leave little cushion against another rates shock 
A further challenge for the spread/rates correlation this year has been the tight starting 
level of spreads, leaving limited room for additional spread compression to offset a move 
higher in government yields. Even after the recent widening, spreads remain tight 
relative to their post-2010 history across much of the IG and HY markets (Exhibit 6). 
 
Credit spread curves also offer limited additional compensation for extending maturity. 
The 2s10s credit spread curves remain relatively flat in both USD and EUR IG and have 
been resilient to both steepening and flattening in government bond curves in recent 
years (Exhibit 7). As a result, investors receive relatively little incremental spread 
compensation for extending either credit or duration risk, leaving unhedged portfolios 
more vulnerable to another rates-led risk-off.

Favor carry and neutral duration 
Overall, we think the spread/rates correlation is more likely to settle closer to zero than 
return quickly to a meaningfully negative regime. The sharp rise in global yields was 
partly driven by technical factors that exacerbated rates volatility, particularly pockets 
of negative convexity in Treasury futures and mortgages, according to our rates team. As 
these technicals become a diminishing source of further upside pressure on yields, the 
correlation should become less positive in the USD market. However, elevated oil prices 
and lingering fiscal uncertainty in Europe are likely to prevent a rapid return to a more 
supportive regime in both the USD and EUR market.  
Beyond the macro backdrop, the key signpost for credit is whether the supportive 
demand technical from all-in yield investors can remain intact. A sustained decline in 
rates volatility would improve confidence in carry-oriented strategies and reinforce the 
technical support that has kept spreads anchored. Conversely, persistently elevated 
volatility would make investors less willing to add duration-sensitive credit exposure. 
Reflecting this balance of risks, we favor carry with controlled duration exposure. Within 
credit, we retain a modest preference for USD over EUR, favor BBBs over AAs and As, 
and see better value in the belly of the spread curve than at the long end. 
More broadly, we think spreads can still tighten into year-end under a benign macro 
outcome, where rates decline gradually on disinflation without a material deterioration 
in growth. If rates volatility remains contained, elevated all-in yields should continue to 
support demand, particularly as 10-year and longer USD IG corporate bonds yield 
around 6.6%.
