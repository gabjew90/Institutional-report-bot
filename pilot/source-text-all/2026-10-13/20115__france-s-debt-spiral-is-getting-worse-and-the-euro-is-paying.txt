France's debt problem isn't going away
French bonds remain under serious pressure, with 10-year yields approaching the big 5% level and
spreads against Germany pushing to fresh highs.
The euro is paying attention, sliding towards levels not seen in some time.
And the underlying numbers are hardly reassuring.
Goldman highlights why France is particularly vulnerable to rising global borrowing costs, while Morgan
Stanley's latest budget analysis reveals just how ugly the debt math is becoming.
The worrying part? Higher yields are making an already difficult fiscal situation even worse, with little
sign of meaningful improvement.
Stubborn in France
French 10-year yields refuse to come down. The steep uptrend since late August remains intact, with
yields trading some 20bp above the 21-day moving average and even further above the 50-day.
The big level is 5%. A close above would likely put French rates firmly back in the spotlight.

France gets the pain, not the gain
Goldman's Rikin Shah highlights another problem for France. The AI investment boom is pushing up
borrowing costs globally, but the growth benefits are heavily concentrated in the US.
France is getting the higher cost of capital without much of the growth upside.
Making matters worse, the marginal cost of French borrowing is now around 250bp above the effective
interest rate on existing debt. As that debt rolls over, the fiscal math gets progressively uglier.

France's debt spiral
Morgan Stanley highlights some rather ugly French debt math.
The government's 2027 budget assumes 10-year yields averaging 4.3%, while OAT yields were already
around 4.9% when MS published its note.
A sustained 100bp increase in borrowing costs would add €3.4bn to annual interest expenses initially,
with the additional annual burden reaching a massive €33bn after ten years.
With French yields remaining elevated and the fiscal deficit going nowhere fast, the debt problem isn't
getting any easier.

The French budget illusion
France is promising €54bn of fiscal savings, but Morgan Stanley points out that the actual improvement
in the deficit is just €13bn.
MS isn't particularly convinced by the numbers either. The bank expects the deficit to remain at 5.4%
of GDP next year, unchanged from 2026, against the government's 5% target.
Meanwhile, public debt is projected to rise towards 122% of GDP.
Plenty of fiscal promises. Very little actual improvement.
