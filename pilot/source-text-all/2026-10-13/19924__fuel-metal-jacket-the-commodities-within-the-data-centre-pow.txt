At a glance: 
• 
AI data centres are a large and accelerating source of power demand, but there is significant 
uncertainty in their ultimate impact on markets. 
• 
Electrical load growth, power generation mix, and material intensity may prove more important 
than the data centres themselves in terms of sources of growth. 
• 
While copper and natural gas are the most exposed commodities, their supply and demand 
equilibrium are also affected by larger thematic trends and forces such as electrification and 
Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG). 
Artificial intelligence (AI) and the data centres that support AI are driving a major infrastructure 
investment cycle and significantly expanding demand for commodities. For commodity investors, 
the key questions are: where is the buildout of AI infrastructure already visible, how could it evolve, 
and what does that mean for energy and materials consumption?

Background 
AI is driving one of the largest private-sector 
infrastructure investment cycles in modern history. 
Major technology firms have collectively spent 
more than $700 billion USD in 2026 alone, up from 
$150 billion USD in 2023,1 and more than all mining 
exploration in the previous 20 years combined.2 As 
investors seek exposure to AI, the opportunity extends 
beyond software and semiconductors to the broader 
ecosystem of electricity generation, transmission, 
real estate, industrials, and commodities required to 
support the global expansion of data centre capacity. 
The U.S. is leading the North American buildout 
because of its vast domestic markets, established 
players, deeper power and fibre networks, and more 
mature permitting and interconnection ecosystem, 
while Canada has advantages from abundant 
clean power and a more favourable climate, though 
provincial grid and transmission constraints have 
limited the pace of expansion. 
Executive Summary 
AI data centres are emerging as a meaningful 
source of commodity demand because they 
require large amounts of reliable power, electrical 
infrastructure, cooling equipment, construction 
materials, and grid investment. For investors, 
the buildout of data centres and supporting 
infrastructure could contribute to demand 
pressure in electricity, metals, equipment,  
and energy markets, although the magnitude 
varies widely across forecasts. Copper and 
natural gas are the two most directly exposed 
commodities: copper is essential to the 
construction of data centres and the broader 
grid, while natural gas could play an important 
role in providing reliable dispatchable power  
as AI-driven electricity demand grows.

Why AI Matters for Power Demand? 
AI data centres are transforming electricity demand 
from a mostly stagnant end market into a new source 
of growth, with implications for investment portfolios 
as the exact numbers are far from finalized. For much 
of the past two decades, efficiency gains in lighting, 
appliances, motors, and industrial processes helped 
offset economic and population growth, keeping 
North American power demand relatively muted.  
The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) 
notes that U.S. electricity demand, measured as net 
energy for load, grew by only about 0.1% per year 
between 2005 and 2019, but accelerated to about 
1.7% per year between 2020 and 2025, with data 
centres identified as an important driver of that 
increase.3 AI changed the equation because training 
and running large models requires extensive amounts 
of reliable electricity, and meeting that demand may 
require new generation, transmission, transformers, 
cooling systems, construction materials, and backup 
fuel infrastructure. If demand for power and industrial 
inputs rises faster than supply can respond, the 
imbalance could be higher costs across electricity, 
equipment, metals, and energy markets.

Data Centres and the Next Wave of Electricity Demand 
Data centres’ significance in U.S. power systems 
continues to grow as AI workloads require 
increasingly dense and power-intensive computing 
capacity. The U.S. currently has approximately 50 
gigawatts (GW) of operating data-centre capacity, 
and several estimates suggest that this could double 
by 2030, denoting a meaningful inflection point for  
a power market that saw relatively limited growth  
over the past two decades.5 Future growth spans 
a wide range of credible scenarios, with the LBNL 
reference case, EIA counterfactual baseline,  
and EPRI medium scenario all helping frame the 
magnitude of incremental power demand and  
how it could evolve under different assumptions.

Which Commodities Stand to Benefit? 
The AI data centre buildout is expected to drive 
demand across a wide range of commodities 
and industrial materials, including steel, cement, 
concrete, aluminium, plastics, diesel, gasoline, 
construction aggregates, and electrical equipment 
inputs, while also reinforcing the broader investment 
case for commodities as real assets tied to physical 
economic activity. Commodities are relevant because 
they are direct inputs into building and powering  
AI infrastructure and because they have historically 
provided diversification and inflation-hedging 
benefits. Many sectors stand to benefit from this 
wave of investment; this paper focuses on the  
two commodities most directly exposed to both  
the construction and operation of data centres:  
copper and natural gas. 
From Data-Centre Growth to Commodity Demand 
The size of the AI and data centre buildout is the 
first part of the story. For commodity markets, what 
ultimately matters is how those projects translate  
into actual electricity and materials consumption.  
Not every announced data centre will be built. 
In 2026, a major data centre developer withdrew 
from a proposed $30 billion USD campus in Virginia 
after years of litigation, permitting disputes, and 
local opposition.6 Furthermore, facilities that are 
completed may take years to reach full utilization:  
A Pennsylvania data centre campus is 1.2 GW in total, 
but only 250-350 megawatts (MW) will be available 
in 2027 with a ramp up period of several years.7 In 
addition, electricity demand depends on factors such 
as server density, cooling requirements, operating 
intensity, and overall efficiency, meaning two 
facilities of similar size can have very different power 
needs. Higher power demand can increase the need 
for new generation, transmission infrastructure, 
transformers, and other electrical equipment, 
creating indirect demand for commodities such 
as natural gas and copper. At the same time, data 
centres also consume these materials directly 
through cabling, switchgear, cooling systems, and 
other electrical components. Understanding these 
links helps explain how growth in AI can influence 
commodity markets through both data centres 
construction and the broader infrastructure required 
to support ongoing operations.

Implications for Copper 
Copper is used throughout a data centre because 
these facilities are essentially dense electrical 
systems that need to move large amounts of power 
reliably and with minimal losses. It appears in utility 
connections, transformers, switchgears, busbars, 
power distribution units, cabling, grounding systems, 
and backup power infrastructure that deliver 
electricity from the grid to individual server racks.  
As racks become more power-dense, especially  
for AI workloads, copper becomes an essential 
component inside cooling systems, including 
cold plates, heat exchangers, tubing, and other 
components that help move heat away from 
high-performance chips. Copper is embedded  
across the electrical and thermal infrastructure  
that allows the facility to operate continuously,  
safely, and efficiently. 
A range of estimates exists for how much copper 
data centres require, but one useful benchmark 
comes from a $500 million USD data centre in 
Chicago, which consumed 2,177 tonnes of copper, 
equivalent to roughly 27 tonnes per MW. Applying 
that type of intensity across different buildout 
scenarios suggests incremental data-centre capacity 
could consume roughly 150 to 500 kilotonnes (kt)  
of copper per year between 2026 and 2030 – a little 
more than 1% of entire annual global refined copper 
demand.8 That is equivalent to the annual production 
of a small to medium-sized copper mine, making it 
material enough to matter for the demand narrative 
without becoming the dominant market driver.  
For perspective, the U.S. copper deficit is currently 
around 700 kt per year.9 The larger point is that  
data centres are only one part of a broader 
electrification story, with global electric grids, 
vehicles, and renewables together contributing  
an additional 600 kt to 1.1 million tonnes per year 
of copper demand10: AI infrastructure adds to an 
already constructive outlook for copper demand.

Implications for Natural Gas 
Natural gas supports data centres differently than 
copper: it is typically generated offsite and consumed 
through the electricity it produces, rather than being 
used directly within the facility. In a gas-fired power 
plant, natural gas is combusted to spin a turbine, 
which drives a generator and produces electricity 
that can then be delivered through the grid or, 
in some cases, directly to a data centre through 
dedicated generation. Combined-cycle plants can 
also capture waste heat from the first turbine to 
produce additional power through a steam turbine, 
improving efficiency and reducing the amount of gas 
needed per unit of electricity. 
For AI data centres, the appeal of natural gas is that 
it can provide reliable, dispatchable power that is 
available when renewable generation is intermittent 
or grid capacity is constrained. 
To estimate the impact of this demand, a reasonable 
working assumption is that roughly 50% of 
incremental data centre electricity demand will 
be supplied by new natural gas generation. Using 
conventional assumptions for utilization, heat rates, 
and the aforementioned incremental generation 
mix, this implies roughly 0.1 billion cubic feet per day 
(bcf/d) of gas demand for each 1 GW of additional 
data centre capacity installed.11 On that basis, AI data 
centre growth could add an estimated 0.5 to 2 bcf/d 
of incremental natural gas demand per year by 2030, 
which is meaningful against a U.S. market of roughly 
110 bcf/d, but still smaller than the more than 2 bcf/d 
of incremental demand expected from U.S. LNG.12

Conclusion 
The AI data centre buildout and increased need 
for AI infrastructure is not yet large enough to 
overwhelm commodity markets on its own, but 
it is momentous enough to matter at the margin, 
especially in markets where supply is constrained, 
infrastructure lead times are long, and exploitation 
cycles are already waning. The key takeaway 
is that the commodity impact will be driven by 
what ultimately gets built, how quickly facilities 
ramp utilization, and the mix of power required 
to fuel them. Copper appears most directly 
exposed through electrical equipment, power 
distribution, cooling systems, and the broader 
grid investment needed to connect new load, 
while natural gas may benefit where dispatchable 
generation is required to meet reliable power 
demand. At the same time, both commodities also 
sit within broader demand narratives that include 
electrification, LNG growth, renewable integration, 
permitting constraints, and substitution risk.  
The investment opportunity lies in identifying 
where incremental demand intersects 
with constrained supply and where market 
expectations have yet to fully adjust.
