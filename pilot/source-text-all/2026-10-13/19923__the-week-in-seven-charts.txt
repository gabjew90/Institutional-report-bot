Chart #5
Europe’s LNG supply is becoming 
overwhelmingly American
The United States’ share of EU LNG imports has risen 
from 28% in early 2021 to 63.2% today, and IEEFA 
expects it could reach 80% by 2028. Two shocks 
sped up this shift: Europe’s exit from Russian LNG and 
disruptions to supply from Qatar.
US cargoes provide flexibility, but they go to the highest 
bidder. When Asian buyers offer more, Europe must 
compete or pay a higher price. Europe has broken free 
of one energy dependence. Is it now creating another? 
With US LNG covering almost two-thirds of imports, does 
it strengthen Europe’s energy security or become its 
next point of vulnerability?
Source: Jack Prandelli on X
Chart #6
Europe has world-class talent. Can it 
build more world-class companies?
The size of America’s technology leaders raises a 
wider question: why does Europe find it so hard to turn 
scientific excellence into companies of similar scale?
Europe has no shortage of exceptional engineers, 
researchers and entrepreneurs. However, fragmented 
markets, complex regulation, slow permitting and 
limited access to growth capital can make it harder for 
companies to scale.
Robust consumer protection and fair taxation pursue 
legitimate aims. The task is to balance them with 
incentives to invest, innovate and take risks. Artificial 
intelligence makes this task more pressing.
Europe has the talent to play a part in shaping this 
revolution. Converting that potential into economic 
leadership will require a business environment in which 
ambitious companies can expand across borders.
The opportunity is clear, make it easier for European 
innovations to become global successes. What should 
Europe reform first?
Source: Michel A.Arouet
Chart #7
South America’s political shift now puts 
Brazil in the spotlight
The political realignment across South America is now 
turning attention to Brazil.
At the end of 2023, seven of the region’s 12 governments 
leaned to the left. Seven are now led by right-wing or 
centre-right leaders. Brazil’s runoff on 25 October could 
take this shift further. Flávio Bolsonaro finished ahead 
of Lula in the first round, with 47% against 44.9%. For 
investors, the implications extend well beyond politics.
Brazil is South America’s largest oil producer, and its 
output hit a record 4.62 million barrels per day in August. 
Petrobras is central to this energy story. Its ADRs rose by 
around 10% on the morning after the first round.
The next president could influence the company’s fuel 
pricing, dividend policy and investment priorities. In 
effect, Brazil’s election is also a vote on the future of its 
energy champion.
Source: The World in Maps
