Data Center Bonds vs. ABS/CMBS:
An Investor's Guide to Structural
Differences, Relative Value
Data center debt issuance has surged, with each market
providing financing in different forms with varying risks. We
review the structures, assessing strengths, weaknesses, and
relative value considerations.
With investors increasingly looking across financing markets to evaluate their exposure to AI
infrastructure, we discuss data center financing structures in the IG/HY bond and securitized
product (ABS/CMBS) markets. Issuance across each market has surged since late 2025 as issuers
have met their financing needs by tapping multiple markets, with IG/HY bonds providing
funding for construction at scale, and post lease commencement, ABS/CMBS have provided
financing for stabilized assets while typically offering higher leverage. ABS also provides
financing flexibility with the ability to add collateral/assets to the structure once developers
grow their data center portfolios.
Data center bonds front-load risk in construction, while ABS/CMBS face greater risk at maturity
given generally higher starting leverage and no amortization, resulting in higher refinancing and
re-leasing risk. Given limited visibility into data center market conditions several years out,
we generally prefer taking initial construction risk. While not dismissing construction as an
important consideration, we note most projects have already secured power agreements,
permits, and long lead time equipment. For higher-quality issuers with IG-rated tenants, at
similar spreads, we selectively prefer data center bonds, which offer potential spread
compression as projects advance through construction (see The Case For Data Center Bonds).
Relative Value Takeaways:
• Data center bonds vs. SASB CMBS: As with data center bonds, which generally feature a
single facility/tenant, single asset single borrower (SASB) CMBS assets are concentrated, with
cash flows typically generated from a single facility leased to one or a few tenants. We map
generic SASB CMBS AAA and BBB spreads to data center bonds trading at similar spreads: At
the top of the capital stack, SASB CMBS issues trade at ~155bp, in line with the highest-quality
data center bonds (eg, BLKPRL, WULF, and CIFR) which trade at 140-170bp; at ~310bp SASB
CMBS BBBs trade in line with mid-quality HY data center bonds (eg, CLSPRK, MERIDI, and
PFORGE) at 300-340bp. In each case, we prefer the data center bonds, although there may
be value in certain subordinate CMBS tranches at wider levels.

Data Center Investing Across IG/HY Bond and ABS/CMBS Structures
With data center financing surging across markets (IG/HY bond markets to finance data center
construction and ABS/CMBS markets to finance completed/stabilized data centers), we evaluate
the structural differences across IG/HY and ABS/CMBS securities and markets as well as relative
value considerations.
• Evolution of the data center debt markets: Data center securitization emerged in 2018 to
finance stabilized, largely traditional cloud data centers (supporting cloud workloads and
enterprise applications), and has grown to $67bn as operators recapitalized existing facilities
(accelerating more recently to fund AI infrastructure development). Securitization cannot
finance construction and lacks the depth to absorb multi-billion dollar campuses - average
deal sizes are $850mn for ABS and $1.2bn for CMBS, versus ~$2.3bn for HY data center bonds
and ~$7bn for IG (excluding the $27bn Beignet deal). This gave rise to the data center bond
market, now totaling $116bn in under a year, with the same size constraint that may limit
how much of the largest AI campuses can be refinanced in securitized form once stabilized.
ABS/CMBS transactions are increasingly shifting from more traditional data centers (backed
by shorter-term contracts) to larger, higher capacity data centers leased under long-term
contracts to hyperscalers, in some cases AI workloads. For issuers, the markets have so far
been largely complementary: IG/HY bonds fund construction at scale, and once leases
commence, sponsors can refinance at higher leverage in ABS/CMBS (QTS has used both), with
ABS master trusts allowing collateral to be added as portfolios grow.
• What assets are supporting the credits? In addition to financing assets at different phases of
the data center life cycle, IG/HY DC bonds are typically backed by a single facility leased to a
single, disclosed tenant, while ABS/CMBS tenants are often undisclosed. ABS also funds
multi-tenant colocation pools, offering diversification but less tenant transparency, while
CMBS SASBs securitize a mortgage on a single asset or small portfolio of assets.

resolved through special servicing. As a result, securitizations bear greater refinancing and re-
leasing risk, although both ABS and CMBS have mechanisms to amortize and/or extend
financing in the event of a maturity default.
• What happens in an event of default? Each market relies on different mechanisms to manage
defaults. IG/HY event-of-default lists are largely on corporate boilerplate terms and
enforcement is collective, with holders of 30% able to accelerate the debt and a majority
directs the trustee. Similarly, ABS relies on cash traps, hyper-amortization, and triggers that
effectively transfer greater control to noteholders as performance deteriorates. In contrast,
CMBS deals are structured with a named workout specialist at issuance, who exercises broad
control over loan modifications and strategies.
• How liquid is the market? The corporate bond market is significantly more liquid than the
securitization market due to its size, the high volume of secondary activity, and the ease in
obtaining spread levels. In contrast, the secondary trading in both the ABS and CMBS markets
is more limited, and TRACE data is not disseminated for CMBS at the CUSIP level.
• Ratings considerations: To rate data center IG/HY projects, the agencies use a combination of
project finance and digital infrastructure methodologies, while for securitizations, they rely
on commercial real estate valuations and structured finance methodologies. All HY DC bonds
are rated BB and IG DC bonds are mostly rated BBB, though ratings for both have shown low
correlation with perceived credit quality, with bonds trading well wide of ratings benchmarks
and high spread dispersion among issuers. Most ABS/CMBS debt by balance is rated in the A
to AAA range.

Relative Value Considerations
Data center bonds vs. SASB CMBS: With both classes of debt generally financing single data
center facilities or campuses with single tenants, the key structural difference between data
center bonds and SASB CMBS is that, in general, risk in the former is weighted to the front end
given bonds fund construction that is in progress, while SASB securitizations allow higher
spreads at increased leverage points with amortization following lease commencement and
usually lower starting leverage. Most IG DC bonds can be repaid in the initial lease term and
therefore face minimal or no re-leasing risk while HY issuers delever through maturity and are
mostly structured to enable full debt repayment over the lease term, providing fairly low re-
leasing risk. Conversely, data center SASB CMBS issues face no construction risk but tend to
come at higher leverage and do not amortize. Given the industry is in the midst of an
unprecedented buildout, significantly limiting visibility to market conditions several years from
now, we are more comfortable with construction risk than re-leasing risk (noting that we view
most construction plans at data center projects in the bond universe as usually well-conceived
and managed, with some idiosyncratic exceptions).
Comparing high-quality data center bonds (such asBLKPRL, WULF, and CIFR that have limited
remaining time to construction completion and high-IG rated tenants) with SASB CMBS that is
likewise leased to a high-IG tenant, we see value weighted to the highest-quality data center
bonds, currently trading in the 140-170bp range versus AAA and AA-rated SASB CMBS, trading at
~155bp and ~195bp, respectively. However, certain lower-rated SASB CMBS tranches trading
over 300bp may offer a sizable spread pickup, depending on collateral and sponsor quality, in
addition to tenant exposure.  For issues that comprise the mid-quality cohort of the high yield
universe (such as CLSPRK, MERIDI, and PFORGE with a combination of lower-IG tenants or
longer construction timelines), we prefer HY data center notes trading at 300-340bp versus SASB
CMBS BBBs which trade in a similar context (~310bp).
Data center bonds vs. ABS: In addition to sharing many of the structural differences between
data center bonds and SASB CMBS, data center ABS issues are generally backed by
fundamentally different assets than data center bonds. ABS is usually backed by multiple data
centers leased to multiple tenants, providing diversification largely not present in the other
markets. Further, the significant majority of data center assets in ABS structures issued to date
are traditional retail or wholesale data centers supporting cloud workloads/enterprise
applications, which in our view may not be subject to the same oversupply risks as the AI data
center market. However, we expect AI workloads and facilities to increasingly make their way
into the ABS market in the coming years, as sponsors look to refinance project debt when assets
become stabilized post construction.
Generic spreads for single-A rated data center ABS are trading around 175bp for portfolios
primarily backed by hyperscaler tenants and 205bp area for colocation portfolios. ABS Master
Trust spreads are mostly tighter than other data center issuances (eg, 222bp for the IG data
center bond universe), but provide the best way to diversify data center exposure to multiple
assets/tenants. That said, we selectively favor IG and HY data center corporate bonds for spread
compression into construction completion along with better liquidity in corporate markets
compared to ABS.
Within the ABS market, we prefer portfolios that are concentrated in primary or secondary data
center markets and backed by operators with deep data center leasing experience. We view
tenant diversification at the investment-grade level as a positive. We recommend viewing ABS
transactions with more concentrated risk profiles through the lens of CMBS SASB and/or
corporate relative value.

Evolution of the Data Center Debt Market
Data Center Finance Before AI
Before AI, data centers were a specialized real estate business. Operators leased space, power
and cooling to cloud providers, enterprises and colocation customers on contracts generally
ranging from 1-3 years (retail colocation) to 7-10 years (wholesale). Starting in 2018,
securitization emerged as a funding source for data center operators with stabilized assets
supported by contracted rent. Most operators which have issued ABS and/or CMBS are owned
by private equity and infrastructure sponsors, (eg, Blackstone [QTS], DigitalBridge [Vantage,
DataBank, Switch]). Securitization offers a multipurpose financing tool. Certain sponsors used
securitization to fund take-private transactions, while others have tapped ABS/CMBS markets to
recapitalize stabilized assets and help fund new projects. Vantage priced the first data center
ABS in 2018, and QTS/Blackstone brought the first data center CMBS in 2021. The market grew
steadily from 2018-22, from $1.1bn outstanding to $15.4bn, during a period in which US data
center capex was materially lower than in recent years (roughly a quarter of 2025 levels and
about one eighth of expected capex in 2026).
AI Boom Transforms the Data Center Industry
ChatGPT's release in November 2022 set off an arms race for compute and created demand for a
new class of HPC data centers, with current leading facilities supporting 100kW+ rack densities
(versus 5-15kW for traditional cloud), liquid cooling and high-bandwidth networking. To fund AI
data center builds, operators increasingly turned to securitization to recapitalize their
stabilized, largely non-AI facilities and free up capital for new development. More recently, some
smaller HPC data centers have been securitized in the SASB CMBS market. The data center
securitization market reached $29bn by mid-2024, and issuance hit a record $27bn in 2025,
more than double the prior year. Nearly 70% of the market is now leased to hyperscalers, but
mainly for cloud rather than AI workloads. The securitization market has indirectly funded some
of the AI buildout, with assets currently represented in ABS/CMBS still mainly comprised of
traditional cloud and colocation data centers.
The Limits of Securitization
The most fundamental constraint is that the ABS and CMBS markets cannot finance assets
under construction: securitization depends on stabilized, contracted cash flow, so it can only
refinance a data center once it has been built and leased, leaving out the construction phase of
the AI buildout that requires high upfront capex.
Separately, the securitization markets are simply too small for the task at hand. We estimate US
data center capacity will triple from 47GW at the end of 2025 to 132GW by the end of 2030, and
at an assumed $14.5bn/GW in construction costs, the 88GW of additions in 2026-30 implies
~$1.3trn of powered shell capex. Following a number of assumptions (outlined in our August 3
HY data center initiation), we estimate $682bn of data center construction debt funding needs
from 2026-30. Against that, the entire data center securitization market is $67bn built over
roughly eight years, and the ABS and CMBS markets overall, at roughly $967bn and $737bn
outstanding, are a fraction of the size of the US IG ($10.2trn) and HY ($1.5trn) corporate bond
market.
Individual projects have also outgrown what a securitization may be able to absorb: facilities
average roughly $13bn/GW in construction costs across data center bond deals to date, single
campuses now reach multiple gigawatts (Meta's 2.06GW Richland Parish site cost $28.8bn), and
the largest data center ABS/CMBS deal to date is $3.5bn, although the largest ABS master trust
has $4.2bn in debt outstanding across five series. This compares to IG data center bond deals of
up to $27bn.

The Emergence of the Data Center Bond Market
Starting in October 2025 with Beignet's $27bn IG deal (a Blue Owl/Meta JV) and WULF
Compute's $3.2bn HY deal, a new class of data center bonds emerged, issued as broadly
syndicated secured notes out of SPVs that own the ring-fenced facility and its lease. Unlike
securitizations, these bonds finance assets still under construction, typically 12-18 months from
delivery, with investors underwriting that construction risk against leases signed in advance,
largely with hyperscalers or hyperscaler-backed tenants (though noting several are leased to
CoreWeave, rated Ba3/B+/BB-), and against structural protections for cost overruns and delivery
delays.
The issuer base has also differed from the securitized market, where sponsors were established
operators refinancing stabilized portfolios; the bond market has instead drawn in a new group
of developers building HPC data centers at scale, spanning former crypto miners with
preexisting access to powered land, certain private hyperscale data center developers, newer
entrants with large financial backers and hyperscaler off-balance-sheet JVs, with QTS the
exception, having now issued two IG-rated data center bonds. Deal sizes have scaled with the
projects, averaging ~$7bn in IG (excluding Beignet) and ~$2.3bn in HY against $850mn for ABS
and $1.2bn for CMBS, and data center notes have accounted for 16% of HY issuance and 30% of
BB issuance year to date.
Where the Markets Stand Now
In under a year, the bond market has grown to $116bn ($70bn across seven IG issues and $46bn
across 20 HY issues), ~1.7x the $67bn total data center securitization market. While remaining
robust, securitization issuance growth has modestly slowed this year, up 4% y/y to $20bn year
to date, with $67bn securitized data center debt now outstanding including $45bn ABS and
$22bn CMBS. 
We think CMBS issuance could decline further due to recent SEC guidance1 related to data
center securitizations issued in ABS format. The SEC staff's position may ease execution costs
for ABS transactions and therefore may lead issuers to rely even more heavily on the ABS format
compared to CMBS.

Issuer Case Study: QTS
Blackstone-owned QTS, the largest sponsor of data center securitizations (~27% of total
outstanding as of September 2026), illustrates how established operators are using multiple
markets to fund the AI buildout. Since 2023, QTS has issued ~$18bn of ABS/CMBS, primarily to
recapitalize stabilized hyperscale and colocation portfolios. In August 2025, it also raised a
reported $1.65bn through a private placement of high-grade bonds (5-, 7- and 10-year
maturities) to fund US data center projects. In 2026, it became the first established
securitization sponsor to enter the data center bond market, issuing $8.5bn across two IG deals
for its Fayetteville, Georgia campus leased to Microsoft: the $4.6bn QTSQST 10y bullet in April
and the $3.9bn QTSODY 5NC2 in August. Notably, the QTSQST data center assets were already
completed at the time of issuance (unlike the QTSODY issuance, which funded ongoing
construction), the only data center bond issuance to date backed by fully delivered assets. 
While QTS is privately held and discloses little about its funding strategy, its activity suggests a
balanced and evolving approach. Securitization remains the vehicle for recycling capital from
stabilized assets, while project-level bonds fund construction and campus-scale projects
beyond the scale of existing issues in the data center ABS/CMBS markets; QTSQST alone
exceeds the largest data center securitization issuance by ~$1bn. The private placement
appears to have been a broader holdco financing, giving QTS a third source of development
capital not tied to a single asset.

contractual rent escalators: 3% a year on the QTSODY lease and 2.75% on the BX lease. QTSODY
also amortizes from mid-2029 (~5.5% of original principal), reducing estimated leverage to 9.8x

leverage and a shorter maturity, in exchange for construction risk that should decline over the
next ~18 months. That said, tranches C-E offer a pickup to the IG bond, and we see relative value

compensation for the increased refinancing risk and structural differences.

workloads with 100kW+ rack densities and liquid cooling, versus 5-15kW and air cooling at
traditional cloud facilities) leased to a single tenant, with the notes secured by the SPV's assets
including the real estate, equipment and the lease itself. Facility size varies widely, with HY

projects typically financing 75-250MW of critical IT load (median ~175MW) and IG projects
skewing larger, ranging from ~200MW to Meta's 2,064MW Richland Parish campus.
These bonds fund projects that are generally pre-revenue at issuance, with construction
timelines generally in the 12-18 month range. Investors underwrite a development asset that
converts to a stabilized, contracted one during the initial note term. Construction risk mitigants
vary by deal but include secured power agreements, advance procurement of long lead-time
equipment (typically 85-99% of budget locked in), GMP contracts, contingency reserves, parent
completion guarantees, and in several cases tenant cost-reimbursement or yield-on-cost
mechanics that shift cost overruns to the tenant.
Nearly all deals fund facilities leased to a single tenant, which is always disclosed: Of the 20 HY
issuers, nine are leased to or backstopped by high IG (~AA) tenants while CoreWeave is tenant at
seven; the IG cohort features only IG-rated tenants, spanning Meta, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Oracle
and Fluidstack (tenant)/Google (backstop). Lease tenor is typically 15 years with extension
options, matched to or exceeding the amortization profile. Roughly half the leases are triple-net
(all of IG are triple net); the balance are modified gross, though power (the largest operating
expense) is passed through regardless, supporting NOI margins of 85-100%, and the structures
benefit from annual lease rate escalation following stabilization (typically 3%).
ABS/CMBS: Unlike IG/HY data center bonds, the ABS/CMBS market funds both single-tenant
facilities as well as multi-tenant colocation data centers and are not exposed to construction
completion risk. For single-tenant transactions, the underlying lease terms tend to be longer
(10-15 years) with sliding scale termination fees. The colocation universe consists both of
wholesale (large scale computing needs, leasing entire data halls) and retail (small scale, tenant
may rent only a rack). Wholesale colocation transactions  are typically backed by a mix of IG
enterprise and hyperscale tenants on intermediate lease terms (4-8 years), whereas retail
transactions are backed by thousands of customer contracts on short term leases (WALT ~2-
years). Hyperscale leases tend to skew toward triple-net lease structures where retail colocation
skew toward gross lease; although many transactions are backed by modified gross leases.
Long-term triple net lease offers the most stability since the tenant is responsible for covering
all expenses. The shorter the lease term, the more exposed the transaction is to market changes
in rental rates.

How do the Structures Differ?
IG/HY DC Bonds: All DC bonds are secured by the assets of the issuing entity including the data
center itself (real estate, buildings, equipment, etc) and the lease agreement. The issuing
entities are ring-fenced SPVs, but the sponsors sitting above them vary, including public and
private data center developers and JV structures. Unlike ABS/CMBS, there is no tranching; each
transaction is a single class of secured debt, with credit enhancement provided by sponsor
equity beneath the notes rather than by subordination. The equity contribution varies
meaningfully, with HY projects 56-98% debt financed (typically ~80%) and IG projects ~90-97%.
All issues include a lock box repayment structure with cash collections from the lease first
allocated to opex/maintenance (for modified gross leases; triple net deals pass these costs to
the tenant), then interest and mandatory amortization, then funding of a debt service reserve
account generally sized to three to six months of post-construction debt service, followed by (in
several cases) excess cash flow sweep amortization, with residual cash flow to the issuer.
The bonds have fixed coupons and the maturity format differs between high yield and
investment grade. In high yield, all bonds to date have been issued as 5NC2s with some
amortization requirements prior to maturity. In investment grade, even though the set is
smaller (seven bonds compared to 20 in high yield), the structures are more varied. Most are
fully amortizing bonds (15- to 20-year stated maturities, 10- to 12-year weighted average lives),
but there is a 10-year bullet bond as well as a HY-style 5NC2 partially amortizing bond.
Amortization profiles differ accordingly: HY issues partially amortize, ranging from 0% at
SECMOS to 45% of original principal at WULF, while most IG issues amortize fully, with the two
QTS issues the exceptions (0% at QTSQST, 5.5% at QTSODY).
Covenants among structures vary (many provide flexibility) but typically include debt
incurrence limitations based on loan-to-cost ratios or minimum DSCR tests. Permitted loan-to-
cost ranges widely, from ~67% at APLD to the full construction cost amount at several issuers,
and in a number of structures, this flexibility could allow re-levering to a degree that the debt
would no longer be fully repayable over the initial lease term. Gross leverage at issuance spans
roughly 5-10x across the universe, delevering through the term of the notes.
ABS/CMBS: Both data center ABS and CMBS offer tranching of risk (senior and subordinate
debt) to stabilized real estate assets and contracted tenants lease cashflows, although the
structures differ between the two markets. Cashflow stability is a function of both the
underlying lease terms and the securitization structural waterfall. Noteholders receive principal
and interest based on the priority of payments in the prospectus. 
• Data center ABS are  issued through bankruptcy remote special-purpose entities that
indirectly own and pledge the underlying data center assets. ABS are structured as master
trusts which gives the issuer the ability to add additional collateral (data centers/MW
capacity) to the trust and issue additional series of notes over time (ie, a dynamic pool). ABS
notes are almost all fixed rate with interest-only payments unless certain performance
triggers are breached (eg, minimum DSCR). Principal is expected (but not required) to be
repaid at the anticipated repayment date (ARD) which is typically five years. Structures also
include floating-rate VFNs that can be drawn and repaid as needed, providing additional
financing flexibility. 
The ABS transaction manager (the data center operator) has operating duties enumerated in
the management agreement including: marketing the data center to new tenants,
negotiating and executing new tenant leases and renewals, and maintaining the facilities
(depending on the lease type). In addition, the manager has certain performance obligations
including minimum uptime requirements.

ABS noteholders are generally only entitled to interest payments prior to the ARD, unless
certain performance triggers are breached (eg, cash trap when DSCR is <1.4x, rapid
amortization when DSCR <1.2x). Also, if an issuer does not refinance a series at the specified
ARD, the series will begin to rapid amortize with a deferred step-up coupon. For both
performance-based triggers and post-ARD amortization, cash flow diversion and payment
priority often become more punitive to subordinate classes and residual interest (ie, the
sponsor/operator) because available cash is directed to repay senior notes. Data center ABS
transactions generally permit optional prepayments before ARD, but those made before a
specified call date (typically two years prior to ARD) are subject to a make-whole payment.
• Data center CMBS are structured as typical Single Asset/Single Borrow (SASB) Real Estate
Mortgage Investment Conduits (REMICS), and the collateral for the trust consists of a non-
recourse mortgage loan. Tenant lease payments are funneled to an account that is used to
make loan payments, which are then distributed to bondholders according to the payment
priority that is detailed in the documents for the trust. Like all CMBS, the trust structure
includes a mortgage loan servicer that manages various loan payments and reporting
requirements, and monitors the borrower's and property's performance under the loan
agreement. The trust also designates a special servicer (a designated workout-specialist) that
may step in if necessary. 
Because a SASB CMBS substantially wraps around a loan, the deal structure is largely
determined by terms of the single underlying mortgage loan, with the exception of bond
rated final dates, which are typically set several years past the underlying loan maturity or
expected final repayment date. Because SASB deals securitize a single mortgage, there is no
option for the transaction to issue additional series of notes from the same trust, as may be
done in ABS. However, the underlying loans may be collateralized by either one property or a
portfolio of properties. 
By balance, around two thirds of the current data center CMBS outstanding deals are floating
rate (structured with a two-year term with three, one-year extension options) with minimal
call protection. The remaining deals are fixed-rate with a mix of five- and ten- year terms that
typically include a prepayment penalty for most of the loan term. The market has shifted
toward fixed-rate product, which accounts for four of the five deals issued this year. To date,
all loans have been interest-only, with principal expected to be repaid in full at maturity.
Some include an ARD that requires a coupon step-up and hyper-amortization if the loan
remains outstanding past that date. 
CMBS bonds have very stable cash flows until loan maturity, ARD, or prepayment, barring
loan default and subsequent workout. Master servicers advance interest and principal
payments during the loan term (to the extent they are recoverable from final proceeds), but
not final balloon payments. CMBS typically do not include deal-level triggers such as those
found in ABS which could result in amortizing or step-up coupon payments. While the
underlying loan may contain performance-based triggers, the loan servicer typically holds
additional collected funds in reserve, rather than releasing these to the bondholders.
CMBS repayments typically occur near loan maturity, after the loan make-whole period has
ended. The loans are structured with a penalty period that deters early repayment and
compensate the bondholders for missed interest payments. These penalty periods typically
last only a few months for floating rate loans but may last for several years for fixed-rate
loans. For loans that are structured with an ARD, excess cash flow that is collected past the
ARD is directed first to bond principal and then to repay a stepped-up interest rate,
incentivizing the borrower to seek refinancing.

What Happens at Maturity? 
IG/HY DC Bonds: Amortization materially mitigates, and in most IG cases eliminates,
refinancing risk. Most IG issues fully amortize over the note term, such that bondholders are
repaid entirely from contracted lease cash flow with no dependence on a refinancing, a re-
leasing, or the residual value of the asset. The QTS issues are the exceptions, amortizing 0%
(QTSQST) and partly to maturity (5.5%, QTSODY), the latter due to its 5NC2 HY-like profile.
HY issues are 5NC2s that partially amortize, ranging from zero at SECMOS to 45% at WULF,
leaving a balance outstanding at the five-year maturity. At maturity, the issuer is refinancing a
stabilized, contracted, delevered asset generally with a decade of remaining lease term, a
materially stronger credit than at issuance when the asset was under construction. All HY
structures other than SECMOS are engineered such that the debt would fully amortize by the
end of the initial lease term following a hypothetical refinancing at the same initial terms, in
theory eliminating residual value risk. Two conditions must hold for that to play out: the tenant
must still be paying, since the refinancing is effectively underwritten against the remaining
lease, and leverage must have actually come down (covenant flexibility in a number of
structures permits re-levering such that the debt would no longer be fully repayable over the
initial lease term). Where amortization is lightest and covenants loosest, more of the outcome
rests on market access at the refinancing date.
ABS/CMBS: Both ABS and CMBS structures are designed to be fully refinanced at the ARD or
maturity date. While ABS typically includes penalties for the borrower if the debt is not repaid,
CMBS loans (and therefore deals) offer more leniency. ABS structures are generally designed to
self-amortize after ARD using operating cash flow and property sale proceeds, whereas CMBS
relies more heavily on a special-servicing framework that negotiates extensions, restructurings,
property sales, or foreclosures.
• ABS: Data center ABS are structured with an anticipated repayment date (ARD), which is the
expected refinance date but not a legal maturity. If a series is not refinanced at its ARD, the
transaction enters a workout-like phase. Collections that would otherwise flow to the
sponsor are redirected to principal repayment, post-ARD coupon step-ups begin to accrue,
and note balances start amortizing.  ABS notes are generally rated to a legal final maturity
approximately 25-30 years after issuance, and rating agency cash flow models typically
assume full extension through that legal final date under stressed scenarios. Therefore,
refinancing risk is primarily an extension-risk question rather than a maturity-default
question. 
That said, sponsors have incentives to refinance, because once a series extends beyond ARD,
cash that would otherwise flow to the sponsor is redirected toward note repayment.
Although post-ARD coupon step-ups generally accrue, those amounts are often subordinate
in the waterfall and may be deferred. As a result, the more significant and immediate
economic penalty is usually the loss of residual cash flow and funding flexibility rather than
the coupon step-up itself.
• CMBS: Because CMBS deals securitize a mortgage loan with a large balloon payment,
performance at maturity depends on the borrower securing new financing. In the event that
this is not possible, the special servicer steps in to resolve the loan (and therefore the trust).
The special servicer has broad discretion in its actions, with only certain bondholders
retaining consultation rights (typically those owning the bottom-most classes).
Workout strategies depend primarily on the borrower's willingness and ability to continue
owning the underlying hard asset(s) and eventually repaying the loan. These strategies can
include extending the loan under substantially the same terms as the original mortgage, in

What Happens in an Event of Default?
IG/HY DC Bonds: Recourse is ring-fenced. The notes are obligations of the issuer (and, in most
structures, its project-level subsidiary guarantors), secured solely by the collateral, with no
recourse to the sponsor, JV partners, or the tenant's parent absent an explicit guaranty (sponsor
completion guarantees and tenant-parent lease guarantees support construction and rent,
respectively, but do not guarantee the notes). The event of default lists are largely standard
corporate boilerplate (payment default, covenant breach, cross-acceleration, judgments, loss of
lien, bankruptcy), with thresholds and cure periods sized to each project, and enforcement is
collective (holders of 30% can accelerate and a majority directs the trustee, while foreclosure on
shared collateral runs through the controlling collateral agent under the intercreditor
agreement).
The differentiation sits in the project-specific triggers, primarily lease termination, which in
most structures is not an immediate event of default but opens a six-month re-leasing window
(extendable to 12 months subject to an LOI/MOU with a qualifying tenant and reserve/liquidity
tests) before transitioning into one. Notably, however, CIFR, BLKPRL, FLASHC, and WULF make
lease termination an immediate event of default, and termination of other material project
documents (eg, ground leases, tenant-parent guarantees, and the Google backstop
agreements) is generally treated more harshly, typically triggering an immediate event of
default or a short (45-60 day) replacement period. In most cases, acceleration brings due
principal and accrued interest only (the make-whole applies to optional redemptions), except in
certain IG-rated transactions (RPLDCI and TXEDCI) which make the make-whole payable on
acceleration and foreclosure, a departure from standard language. 
ABS/CMBS: Both CMBS and data center ABS are designed to maximize recoveries for investors
when collateral performance deteriorates. CMBS relies on special servicers to work through loan
defaults, whereas ABS relies more heavily on structural protections including manager
termination rights.
• ABS: Data center ABS transactions are structured so that noteholders have recourse only to
the pledged collateral and transaction cash flows, rather than the sponsor or related
corporate entities. If collateral performance weakens, transaction provisions can trap cash,
accelerate debt repayment, replace the manager (ie, the data center operator), or facilitate
asset sales, effectively transferring greater control to noteholders as performance
deteriorates. Formal events of default are generally limited to missed payments, insolvency
events, and material breaches of transaction documents. Upon an event of default,
noteholders may accelerate the notes and exercise remedies against the collateral through
the servicer and indenture trustee to maximize recoveries.
• CMBS: Because a CMBS deal securitizes a mortgage, there are separate triggers for bond and
loan defaults. Said differently, a loan default does not trigger a bond default, and most
bondholders have little input into loan workout strategies. At the time of issuance, CMBS
deals have negotiated agreements with special servicers, who are workout specialists that
are equipped to resolve any loan defaults, typically through loan modification, loan sale, or
foreclosure and REO property sales. Special servicers are tasked with maximizing recovery to

How Liquid is the Market?
IG/HY DC Bonds are a highly liquid market relative to securitization market. These bonds
finance multibillion dollar construction projects, and their tranches (averaging ~$2.3bn in HY
and ~$10bn in IG, noting the IG average is inflated by the very large $27.3bn Beignet Meta JV
issuance) are in many cases larger than the average IG or HY deal, which supports an active
secondary market. Even the club deals that weren't broadly syndicated (RPLDCI, RDMICH)
retain sizable floats with active two-way flow. Each issue is also a single class of debt, so
liquidity isn't split across tranches as it is in ABS/CMBS, and the bonds trade with observable
prices and spreads at the security level, a level of transparency the securitized market lacks.
The holder base includes both traditional buy-and-hold accounts as well as active
participation/trading by hedge funds, and the paper is 144A, but secondary trading activity has
been healthy. From January-September 2026, per TRACE, HY data center bond trades total
~$92bn, double the $46bn now outstanding, compared with just over $6bn traded year to
date against $44bn outstanding in data center ABS. We expect liquidity to continue to build
as the market grows and investors gain familiarity with these structures.
ABS/CMBS: The securitization markets are generally much less liquid than the corporate
market. Transaction sizes tend to be smaller in the securitization market relative to corporates.
Specifically, the average deal size is $850mn for ABS and $1.2bn for CMBS. For the ABS master
trust format, which allows for a dynamic pool of collateral, the largest outstanding trust is
$4.2bn issued across five series. Liquidity is further subdivided at the tranche level due to
differences in size and ratings.
For the ABS side, we estimate over $6bn has traded YTD through August 30th compared to
about $200bn for the overall ABS market based on TRACE data2 . Data center ABS secondary
volumes are up ~50% y/y, although there is significant dispersion in turnover by platform.
TRACE data is not disseminated for CMBS at the CUSIP level. 
Ratings Considerations
HY/IG: In rating data center projects, the agencies broadly use a combination of project finance
and digital infrastructure methodologies.  While tenant credit quality is an important
determinant, the agencies consider other factors including construction risk (complexity and
timing), other specific lease terms including termination provisions, other structural protections
(ie, GMP agreements), operating risk (ie, experience/track record of sponsors, contractors), and
financial risk (ie, leverage, amortization, covenants). 
All HY-rated issuers to date are rated in the BB category, with most having upgrade potential
once construction is completed and leases commence.  However, due to structural
considerations, despite many having IG-rated tenants, few are expected to be upgraded to IG.

With respect to the IG-rated issuers (mostly BBB rated), key factors distinguishing issues from
BB rated issuers include, first, that the debt is fully amortized over the lease term and then
various other factors including construction and cost mitigation, limitations on termination
rights, and provisions of IG-rated tenant backstops. 
Noting the above rating criteria, ratings are not closely tied to the market’s perceived
credit quality of the issuers (both HY and IG) or bond trading levels which are highly
dispersed.  This is due to a combination of tenant risk and other deal specific
considerations (including construction risk, time to RFS, lease specific attributes,
regulatory developments, etc.)
ABS/CMBS: In rating data center securitizations, the agencies broadly rely on commercial real
estate valuation and structured finance methodologies, supplemented by data-center-specific
analysis. While tenant credit quality and contractual lease revenue are important determinants,
the agencies also consider sustainable net cash flow, asset utility and long-term
competitiveness (eg, location, power availability, redundancy, connectivity, age and
obsolescence), as well as lease roll, operating and capital costs, leverage, diversification, and
transaction structure.   
For ABS, the agencies generally treat the debt as a long-dated operating cash-flow
securitization. Cash flows are stressed for tenant defaults, downtime, replacement tenants,
lease expirations and rent changes through the legal final, and then applied through the
transaction waterfall. Structural protections (eg, bankruptcy remoteness, cash traps, hyper-
amortization) can support the rating, while terminal-value credit varies materially by agency
and ownership structure and may be limited for site leases or shorter leaseholds. For CMBS, the
analysis is more directly focused on sustainable property NCF, stressed value, leverage and
recovery or refinancing of the balloon balance, with LTV, debt service coverage or debt yield and
mortgage protections driving proceeds and credit enhancement.
Ratings are not simply tied to tenant credit quality or whether a transaction is labeled ABS or
CMBS. Instead, the analytical distinction is driven by the cash-flow framework, structural
protections, and the nature of the underlying collateral. In particular, agencies often apply (but
not always) a common property-valuation lens across both ABS and CMBS formats, with
differences arising primarily from transaction structure and sources of repayment.

For CMBS, 55% of total tranches are rated AAA with A, AA, BBB, and BB ratings accounting for
nearly all remaining ratings.
