Bigger positions, bigger consequences
When considering risks in portfolios, managers often focus on mitigating “downside 
risk”—the risk of losing money. But managers must also consider “upside risk”—
having little or no exposure to stocks of fast-growing companies that achieve 
meaningful benchmark weights. 
Faster information flow, intense investor attention, and momentum around 
transformational technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) can quickly push a stock 
from a modest index weight to a major driver of returns. For an active manager, position 
sizing decisions can have an increasingly meaningful effect on portfolio outcomes—
especially as small cap indexes have become more concentrated (see chart).

As benchmark weights rise, so does relative risk
Excluding larger benchmark constituents on valuation grounds alone can increase the risk 
of meaningful underperformance. For example, in the momentum-driven market of 2025, 
the average small cap growth manager underperformed the Russell 2000 Growth Index 
by 5.22%. 
The culprit? Many of the strongest performers that year initially screened as expensive, but 
they appreciated rapidly through a combination of AI exposure and accelerating revenue 
and earnings growth. 
The importance of position sizing decisions extends beyond a single market cycle. Small 
caps have historically generated higher earnings growth than large caps over long periods 
of time, yet they currently trade at a substantial valuation discount. Missing participation 
in the companies driving that growth can therefore carry meaningful opportunity cost 
for investors.
Case study: Bloom Energy (BE)
In mid-2025, Bloom Energy’s fundamentals were improving rapidly due to the 
company’s emergence as a mission-critical power infrastructure partner for AI 
hyperscalers, and we believed its growing benchmark weight warranted attention. 
However, at first glance, its valuation did not fully fit our traditional growth-at-a-
reasonable-price discipline. Rather than ignore the opportunity, we scrutinized the 
fundamentals, formed a favorable opinion of the revenue growth prospects for the 
company, and realized valuation was not as stretched as the initial screen would 
have indicated. 
Therefore, we established a slightly overweight position that provided exposure 
to the opportunity in line with our level of conviction. This was an active decision 
grounded in research, not a reaction to price momentum alone.

To capture increasingly dynamic opportunities in small caps, we believe managers can’t 
assume that yesterday’s position sizing conventions will remain sufficient as benchmark 
weights change more rapidly. Fully capturing the long-term opportunity in small caps 
increasingly depends on the ability to assess both upside and downside risk and adjust 
position sizes accordingly.
