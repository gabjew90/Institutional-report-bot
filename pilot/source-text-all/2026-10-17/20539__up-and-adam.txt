Until yesterday’s 1.1% drop, the S&P 500 had gone 52 consecutive days without a decline of 1% or more—the longest streak in more

Thursday, October 15, 2026
Starting next year, recipients of Social Security benefits—more than 75 million Americans—will see a 3.6% increase. The cost-of-living 
adjustment is the largest in four years and exceeds the average of 2.5% since 2010. Retired workers will receive ~$75 more per month—
an adjustment that offsets inflation rather than boosting purchasing power in real terms. Later this month, the IRS is set to announce its 
own inflationary adjustments, which encompass 2027 tax brackets and contribution limits to IRA and 401(k) accounts.

September’s CPI report was hotter than expected: headline inflation rose 0.6% MoM (+3.7% YoY), with core up 0.3% MoM (+2.6% YoY). 
Higher gasoline prices accounted for roughly half of the headline increase, but the more important signal was the broadening in price 
pressures. Most notably, shelter and services excluding energy rose 0.4% MoM, alongside higher transportation and medical costs. This 
broader inflation backdrop is concerning for the Fed. Markets took notice, pushing the probability an Oct. 28 hike to 33%, from 22%.

Source: FactSet
Chart of the Day: A Social Boost
With 10-year Treasury yields above 5%, the equity market is increasingly focused on fundamentals, i.e. corporate earnings. Small-cap 
companies—whose earnings are more rate-sensitive than large-caps—provide a useful case study. The Russell 2000 is down 11.0% from 
its all-time high set in August, marking a correction. Small-caps with negative EPS are underperforming: their average drop is ~21% vs. 
just ~8% for those with positive EPS. We expect fundamentals—rather than sentiment—to remain top-of-mind for investors.
Tech sector jitters are spilling into corporate credit markets as investors continue to scrutinize the surge in AI-related borrowing. These 
concerns have weighed heavily on select tech stocks, prompting investors to demand greater compensation for credit risk. As a result, 
credit default swap (CDS) spreads—the cost of insuring against default—have widened to YTD highs across many names, with some 
reaching distressed levels. For now, credit stress remains largely company-specific, with only a modest impact on overall spreads.
