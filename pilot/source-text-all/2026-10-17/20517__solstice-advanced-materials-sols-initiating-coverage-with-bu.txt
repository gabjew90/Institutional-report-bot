Solstice Advanced Materials, Inc.
(Nasdaq: SOLS)
Initiating coverage of unique advanced materials secular growth story
with Buy rating, $80 PT
Summary:
We are initiating coverage of SOLS, a leading supplier of high-performance specialty
materials with exposure to a range of attractive end markets, with a Buy rating and
$80 price target. We believe the regulatory push for lower-global-warming-potential
refrigerants, growth in advanced computing and data center applications, and position
as the only US-based supplier of uranium conversion services to the growing nuclear
energy industry provide a strong base for secular growth. SOLS brings deep customer
relationships, technical know-how (particularly around fluorine chemistry), and leading
market positions to capture growth and develop innovative value-added solutions. SOLS
is investing in key growth platforms (refrigerants, electronics, nuclear, metered-dose
inhalers), and has a strong balance sheet to support bolt-on acquisitions as well as share
repurchases. SOLS tends to participate in markets with a limited number of competitors,
and products are truly specialty, in that they make up a small but essential part of the
customer's end product. Near-term, we are encouraged by visibility on growth across
multiple markets, and note SOLS should experience only limited raw material vs. pricing
impact on margins. Our Buy rating is based more on our positive view of longer-term
growth and what we perceive as a discount to fair value (SOLS trading at 10.0x our
FTM EBITDA estimate vs. sum-of-parts analysis supporting a low-mid teens multiple),
rather than a strong view around near-term earnings. We note our Q3 EBITDA estimate
is within 1% of current consensus.

Highlights
●SOLS serves attractive markets with secular growth drivers, including high-
performance computing, refrigerants and other fluorinated compounds that have
regulatory drivers, nuclear fuel conversion with tightening supply/demand dynamics,
and data center thermal management. We believe this can support mid-SD organic
revenue growth, with some potential for margin expansion. SOLS tends to play in
oligoploy markets, and we view the company's fluorine capabilities, Refrigerants, and
Nuclear assets as fairly unique.
●Balance sheet leverage was 1.3x net debt/EBITDA at the end of Q2:26, supporting
optionality around capital allocation. Recent organic investments include Refrigerants
capacity to serve regulatory-driven demand growth, copper sputtering targets
expansion in Electronics, and scale-up of next-generation medical inhaler propellants.
SOLS also recently announced a $500MM share repurchase program, which we
expect will be put to use at current valuation.
●The balance sheet should also support growth through M&A, though we think
shareholders made clear that a transformative deal like the canceled $14.5B cash-
and-stock acquisition of ESI should be off the table. As such, we anticipate SOLS to
focus on bolt-on opportunities within core markets, particularly Electronics.
●We note the recent settlement of litigation related to the Metropolis facility should have
little impact, given expected reimbursement from the DoE and insurance.

Solstice Advanced Materials is a global leader in high-performance specialty materials that enable

Investment Thesis
●Attractive end markets with secular growth trends: We view many of SOLS' end markets as
defensive in nature, and note regulatory tailwind related to lower global warming potential is a
driver in refrigerants, blowing agents, and healthcare applications. The nuclear business has a
strong competitive position in a growing market. Advanced computing is driving increasing need
for technology-enabling materials and thermal management. Healthcare, life science research, and
ballistic protection tend to be resilient markets.
●Oligopoly market structure: SOLS has a unique set of assets and capabilities, particularly around
the fluorine chemicals value chain, that lead to high barriers to entry. We view key markets including
Refrigerants, Alternative Energy Services, Building Solutions, Electronic Materials, Research
Chemicals, and Safety & Defense as all having an oligopoly market structure with a limited number
of competitors.
●Truly specialty product lines: SOLS materials tend to make up a small but essential part of the
customer's end product. Examples include refrigerants (without which the entire HVAC system won't
function), ballistic protection (where failure could mean serious injury or death), electronic materials
(where quality issues could lead to chip failure and impact customer yield), and uranium conversion
(which accounts for ~5% of customer fuel cost, but is the most critical and complex step to fueling
nuclear reactors). Customer relationships are deep, marked by heavy technical service and co-
development of innovative solutions.
●Balance sheet supports “all of the above” capital deployment strategy: Balance sheet
leverage finished Q2:26 at 1.3x net debt/EBITDA, where many companies in our coverage target
2.5x-3x, suggesting SOLS has $1B-$2B in “dry powder.” SOLS is investing in capacity to support
organic growth in Refrigerants, Electronic Materials, and Healthcare Packaging, and is exploring
further expansion of the Nuclear business. Remaining dry powder can support bolt-on M&A
while also repurchasing shares under an inaugural $500MM authorization. We also believe future
divestitures could focus the portfolio and provide additional optionality.
●Attractive valuation: Relative to the trading range over its short history (roughly 8x-15x EBITDA)
and our sum-of-parts estimate of a fair value range of 11.7x-14.6x EBITDA, we view SOLS as
attractively valued, currently trading at 10.0x our FTM EBITDA estimate.
Risks
●Refrigerant regulation and low-GWP transition: Growth in Refrigerants depends on the shift
to next-generation low-GWP refrigerants. Compliance may require elevated capex and product
redesign. Benefits could be delayed if rules are weakened or not adequately enforced, and illegal/
counterfeit products can undercut sales in regulated markets. Recent US moves to de-emphasize
climate change rules (including the EPA's draft rule to rescind its greenhouse gas endangerment
finding) could reduce LGWP refrigerant demand.
●Nuclear (AES) exposure: The Nuclear business relies on public and political support for nuclear
power. Opposition can delay licensing and construction of new plants. An expansion at the
Metropolis facility funded by the US DOE could trigger liquidated damages if timing milestones are
missed. SOLS recently announced a settlement of all personal injury and environmental litigation
related to the Metropolis facility, though we note any future decommissioning of this facility could
result in substantial costs.
●Raw materials and supply chain: Input costs are a key driver of margins, and wet fluorspar is
a particularly important raw material. Risks include failing to pass through inflation to customers,
supplier financial distress or delivery disruption, and capital project delays or cost overruns. Demand
spikes could also lead to capacity constraints.
●International FX and tariff risks: About 43% of 2025 sales were outside the US, and another
14% of sales were exported from the US. Risks include currency swings, tariffs and retaliation,
economic sanctions, and geopolitical conflict. We also note IP infringement has been an issue
around refrigerants in China.
●Cyclicality: SOLS has a number of businesses that can be impacted by investment cycles, as well
as exposure to industrial and construction-related cyclicality. This can cause swings in profitability
and cash flow.

●Legacy environmental liabilities and operational risks: As is the case with many specialty

History and Company Strategy
The business that we know today as Solstice Advanced Materials can trace roots back to the 1800s,
and became part of Allied Chemical & Dye with the 1920 merger of five companies (General Chemical,
Solvay Process Co., Semet-Solvay, Barrett, and National Anilene & Chemical). The merger was a
response to shortages of key chemicals during World War I, and brought together makers of key
basic materials of the time: acids, alkalies, coal tar, and nitrogen. Allied Chemical was the largest
US chemical company in the 1920s, specializing in fertilizers, explosives, and other basic chemicals.
The chemicals market grew in the following decades (particularly during World War II), but Allied fell
behind on innovation and allowed its plants to become obsolete, leading to a fall from first to sixth
in the chemicals industry.
Beginning in the late 1960s, Allied began to expand into intermediate and specialty materials. Several
transactions in the late 1970s and early 1980s accelerated this transition away from commodity/
basic chemicals and toward aerospace, electronics, automotive, and engineered materials, and
also included a foray into oil & gas. A critical deal was the 1985 merger with Signal Companies
to create AlliedSignal. The AlliedSignal business was streamlined in the 1990s, selling off less-
profitable businesses, reducing the size of the workforce, and reducing the debt load. By 1996,
following additional acquisitions, specialty chemicals accounted for $1B in annual revenue, including
refrigerants, fluorochemicals, and specialty materials that make up most of Solstice today.
AlliedSignal and Honeywell merged in 1999. Honeywell had grown from an early focus on hot water
heating systems and industrial controls to become a major defense manufacturer during World War
II. Through the late 1990s, the company expanded its presence in industrial automation, aerospace,
process controls, and electronics/computing. The merger brought AlliedSignal's $15.1B in annual
revenue together with Honeywell's $8.4B, creating an industrial conglomerate with strong positions
in aerospace, chemicals, automotive parts, and building controls.
Honeywell itself became a takeover target in late 2000, when General Electric attempted to acquire
it, though the deal was blocked by European Commission antitrust authorities. Honeywell continued
on an acquisition and divestiture spree (roughly 140 total transactions since 2002), and by 2017 (as
conglomerates came out of vogue), had reached a decision to spin off the transportation business
and consumer business into separate public companies. In 2024, the company announced plans
to spin off its advanced materials business, a transaction that created Solstice as an independent
company in October 2025.

Brief history of key SOLS product lines:
●Refrigerants: This business is rooted in the predecessor companies to Allied Chemical. Early
refrigeration systems used natural substances like ammonia, sulfur dioxide, methyl chloride, or
carbon dioxide. CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) were invented in the 1930s, and Allied Chemical's
Genetron brand of refrigerants was introduced in 1949. CFCs were the industry standard until
scientists discovered these chemicals destroyed the ozone layer, leading to a global phase-
out beginning with the 1987 Montreal Protocol. More recently, Solstice launched a line of
HFO (hydrofluoroolefin) refrigerants in 2010 to serve increasing demand for low-global-warming-
potential refrigerants.
●Building Solutions: The advent of CFCs as refrigerants led chemical makers like Allied to explore
other applications for fluorine chemicals, and trichlorofluoromethane became the dominant blowing
agent for polyurethane and phenolic foams in the mid-1900s. Similar to refrigerants, blowing agents
have migrated to more environmentally friendly alternatives including HFOs over time.
●Nuclear: This business dates back to 1957, when Allied Chemical won a contract to convert
uranium ore into uranium hexafluoride (UF6) for the US Atomic Energy Commission. Construction
began on its Metropolis Works facility in Illinois in 1958, and production continued apace through the
Cold War. As the Megatons to Megawatts program converted Soviet weapons-grade uranium into
reactor fuel, competitor General Atomics in 1992 responded by closing its facility in Oklahoma and
creating the CoverDyn joint marketing partnership with Allied. As a result, Solstice is the only UF6
converter in the US, and has maintained decades of relationships with blue-chip utility customers.
●Research & Performance Chemicals: Honeywell had a small position in research chemicals
through its Riedel-de Haën and Burdick & Jackson acquisitions in the mid-1990s. In 2015,
Honeywell acquired the Research Chemicals business of Sigma-Aldrich, which brought Fluka,
Hydranal, and Chromasolv brands. The Fluka laboratory chemicals and reagents business was
founded in the 1950s, and Hydranal reagents for Karl Fischer titration were invented in 1979.
Hydranal was revolutionary because it eliminated pyridine, a toxic and foul-smelling substance,
replacing it with imidazole, which was safer, less smelly, and provided better performance.
●Electronics: The core sputtering targets business traces back to metallurgical roots within the
AlliedSignal business, and sputtering as a deposition technique began to see more mainstream use
with the rise of the semiconductor industry in the 1960s and 1970s. Sputtering and physical vapor
deposition became the industry standard for depositing metal interconnect layers in the 1980s. In
the early 2000s, Honeywell acquired Mitsubishi Chemical's wet process chemicals business (and
related facilities in Texas) and created Honeywell Electronic Materials as a formal business entity.
●Safety & Defense Solutions: Ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene was developed in the
1960s, and it would be 1978 before a process to produce UHMWPE as a high-strength fiber
was developed by DSM. AlliedSignal developed similar technology and ended up licensing DSM's
technology and launched the Spectra brand in the mid-1980s. The DSM business is now known
as Dyneema, part of AVNT.
Go-forward strategy for SOLS:
In many ways, we view SOLS as a collection of specialty material businesses that were left to
their own devices as part of Honeywell. Now as a separate public company, we anticipate some
portfolio moves that could increase exposure to faster-growing, higher-value markets. While the
planned acquisition of Element Solutions (which would have strengthened the Electronics business)
was scrapped in September 2026, SOLS made clear that it was prepared to execute a meaningful
transaction. Although SOLS has seven separate product lines, we note fluorine chemistry is an
important throughline, suggesting the company would need to evaluate potential dissynergies if
it were to explore divestitures. In addition to leveraging its strong expertise in fluorine chemistry,
we believe SOLS wants to tap into secular growth opportunities around advanced computing,
environmental & energy evolution, life science, and safety/defense, supporting what SOLS views as
a right to win in core Refrigerants, Electronics, and Nuclear businesses.

Price Target & Valuation
SOLS is a collection of high-value advanced material businesses, with strong competitive positions
and exposure to secular growth drivers that should support mid SD organic growth, mid-20's EBITDA
margin with potential for expansion, and strong ROIC. We struggle to find appropriate comps, given
what we view as a unique set of end markets and product lines. As such, we think it makes sense
to consider SOLS valuation on a sum-of-parts basis.
●Refrigerants: Chemours (CC) would be the closest peer in terms of refrigerants overlap (SOLS and
CC are the only producers of HFOs), though CC also has an unrelated commodity titanium dioxide
business and an unusual set of legal liabilities that we think make it inappropriate as a valuation
comp. Other players like Daikin, Arkema, and Orbia have refrigerant businesses that are part of
larger companies. We view Refrigerants as a high-quality, high-margin business with regulatory
drivers that should support high-SD growth, and SOLS has a strong market and technology position.
We estimate this product line should be valued at a low-mid teens multiple of EBITDA.
●Building Solutions: While this is a high-margin business within SOLS, we view the end markets
as cyclical. Peers include CC and Arkema. In our view, this business deserves an EBITDA multiple
more typical of the average specialty chemical company, in the 8x-10x range.
●Nuclear: SOLS is the only US provider of nuclear conversion services, and other western players
in this space (Cameco, Orano) are integrated into larger businesses. Because of its position as the
sole US-based supplier in a heavily regulated industry (US Nuclear Regulatory Commission license
through 2060), attractive growth characteristics, long-term contracts with firm volume commitments,
and view on tightening supply/demand supporting strong pricing, we believe the Nuclear business
is deserving of a premium EBITDA multiple in the 20x-25x range.
●Healthcare Packaging: We believe AptarGroup (ATR) is an appropriate comp, trading in the
10x-11x range. We also point to the 2025 sale of Sonoco's Thermosafe business to private equity
for ~13x EBITDA as a transaction comp. We think this supports a valuation in the 10x-12x range
as a multiple of EBITDA.
●Research & Performance Chemicals: We view Avantor (AVTR) as an interesting comp, trading
at ~14x EBITDA, though we think SOLS' business is more diverse vs. AVTR's focus on life science
tools. As such, we think a low-teens multiple of EBITDA is appropriate.
●Electronic Materials: While peers like Entegris (ENTG), Element Solutions (ESI), and Qnity
Electronics (Q) all have slightly different product lines, all are focused on technology-enabling
materials into the semiconductor space. Based on current peer valuation, we think a mid-high teens
EBITDA multiple is appropriate for this business.
●Safety & Defense: The best peer would be the Dyneema business, which was acquired by Avient
(AVNT) for ~11.5x EBITDA in 2022. Given the oligopoly market structure and what we view as
positive secular tailwinds around defense spending, we think a low-mid teens EV/EBITDA multiple
makes sense.

the stock did trade into the mid-teens EBITDA multiple range when market sentiment was (overly?)
positive in mid-2026, we think a logical fair value multiple would be in the 11x-12x EBITDA range,
well above current valuation but still discounting the upside case given geopolitical and macro

Cyclicality, seasonality, and customers:
We view the SOLS business as broadly resilient, with Refrigerants (half is aftermarket), Nuclear,
Research & Performance Chemicals, Healthcare Packaging, and Safety & Defense Solutions
generally defensive in a range of macro environments. Refrigerant sales into OEM can be more
cyclical, though regulatory transition should help to dampen this impact. Electronics can be marked
by semiconductor industry cyclicality, and Building Solutions can be impacted by cycles in the
construction market.
RAS sees the highest sales in 1H, with Refrigerants peaking in Q2 as inventory builds ahead of
warmer summer weather. Building Solutions sales tend to peak in Q4 as a result of weather conditions
and higher construction activity. Healthcare Packaging sales are highest mid-year, ahead of winter
(when more medication is consumed). Seasonality in the ESM segment is more limited.
SOLS serves a global base of more than 3,000 customers, with the top 20 customers accounting for
less than 20% of total sales. Average customer tenure is 10 years, and SOLS has some customer
relationships that have lasted several decades.
Raw materials:
Raw materials include chlor-alkali-based materials, ethylene, polyethylene, wet fluorspar, and copper.
We note that as a result of the Iran war in 2026, SOLS has seen about 10% of its raw material spend
impacted, including olefins and sulfuric acid, as well as diesel/freight costs. The Nuclear business
can be viewed as a tolling arrangement, with SOLS not exposed to uranium cost volatility. SOLS is
able to put surcharges or structural pricing in place, and generally expects to quickly mitigate the
effects of input cost inflation.
Some notes on fluorine chemistry:
Fluorine is a highly reactive element that is a necessary component of many pharmaceuticals, fine
chemicals, and polymers. Fluorine is a powerful oxidizer, and has the highest electronegativity of any
element, meaning it attracts shared electrons toward itself to create strong chemical bonds. Fluorine
forms compounds with nearly every other element.
Fluorination reactions are typically accomplished with hydrogen fluoride (HF), a highly corrosive and
toxic gas that is produced by reacting the mineral fluorspar (calcium fluoride) with sulfuric acid. HF is
a key precursor to organofluorine compounds that are used in refrigerants (both hydrofluorocarbons
and more environmentally-friendly hydrofluoroolefins), blowing agents, polymers, and as reagents.
Fluorine chemistry is also critical to the uranium conversion process, in which both HF and fluorine gas
are used to produce gaseous uranium hexafluoride (UF6). UF6 is then enriched through an isotope
separation process, which increases the percentage of U-235 isotope needed for nuclear reactions
(vs. naturally occurring U-238, which cannot sustain a chain reaction).

Refrigerants:
Refrigerants sits at the intersection of several attractive end markets: stationary HVAC/R, automotive
HVAC/R, and data centers. It is composed of the proprietary hydrofluoroolefin (HFO) platforms
(Solstice yf, zd, and ze) as well as the legacy Genetron hydrofluorocarbon (HFC) brand. The business
is benefiting from durable secular demand, strong intellectual property, and long-standing customer
relationships concentrated in the US and Europe, with a strategic tilt toward recurring aftermarket
sales that provide revenue stability. Refrigerants tend to generate the highest EBITDA margins within
the RAS segment, into the upper 30% range.
The single biggest tailwind for Refrigerants is the regulatory-driven phasedown of high-GWP HFC
refrigerants in favor of low global warming potential (LGWP) alternatives. CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons)
and HCFCs (hydrochlorofluorocarbons) that have been phased out as refrigerants have been found
to contribute to atmospheric ozone depletion and a greenhouse effect much worse than CO2. HFCs
that replaced CFCs are less harmful to the ozone layer, but still have many times the global warming
potential of CO2. HFOs are viewed as having zero impact on ozone, and less than 1% of the global
warming potential of the HFCs they replace. Exhibit 6 shows some examples of different global
warming potential of different types of refrigerants (CFC/HCFC, HFC, and HFO).

As Exhibit 7 shows, frameworks like the EU F-Gas Revision and the US AIM Act are mandating steep
reductions in HFC use: EU HFC allowances are set to fall from 100% of baseline to just 3% by 2040,
while the US schedule calls for a decline to roughly 15% by the mid-2030s. The Kigali Amendment
calls for a parallel phasedown across developing markets. Because SOLS' HFOs are the primary
lower-GWP substitute for legacy HFCs, this regulatory wall drives demand toward its proprietary
chemistries. SOLS expects growing HFO mix over time, from 27% of its stationary refrigerants sales
in 2022 to ~60% in 2025 and ~80% expected longer-term. HFO volumes in North America and Europe
are projected to grow at roughly 8-9% CAGR through 2035, more than offsetting expected declines
in HFC volumes. This should provide SOLS a structural, policy-backed growth runway that is largely
insulated from ordinary cyclicality, since customers have little choice but to transition as compliance
deadlines approach. Phase-down reduces supply and increases cost of legacy refrigerants, further
incentivizing the changeover.

A more recent and fast-growing demand vector is data center cooling. As AI and cloud computing
drive denser server racks and higher chip power, thermal loads are outstripping what conventional
air cooling can handle, pushing the industry toward liquid-based approaches. SOLS sees multiple

chip level up through the rack and facility level. We view data centers as an important contributor to

Building Solutions & Intermediates:
The BS&I business serves two primary end markets: high-performance insulation and consumer
& industrial aerosols. The product platform is built around SOLS expertise in fluorine derivatives,
and includes liquid and gas blowing agents, cleaning solvents and flushing agents, propellants, and
hydrogen fluoride (HF), an upstream intermediate. Applications span residential and commercial
insulation, appliance insulation, vapor degreasing, precision cleaning, and consumer/industrial
aerosols. SOLS derives competitive advantage from technology leadership, a strong global footprint,
fluorine chemistry expertise, superior insulation performance that translates into better energy
efficiency, deep technical service support, and customer intimacy that informs product development.
Growth in BS&I can be cyclical, but also includes some secular drivers around sustainability and
energy efficiency. Green building initiatives aimed at reducing carbon intensity are driving adoption
of higher-performance insulation, which in turn favors SOLS' low-GWP blowing agent chemistries. In
some ways, BS&I benefits from the same LGWP regulatory transition as the Refrigerants business,
since blowing agents face similar pressure to migrate away from higher-GWP legacy formulations
toward SOLS' proprietary alternatives.
Nuclear (Alternative Energy Services):
The Nuclear business centers on uranium hexafluoride (UF6) conversion, which is the critical step
in the nuclear fuel cycle that chemically transforms mined uranium concentrate (uranium oxide, also
known as "yellowcake," U3O8) into gaseous UF6, the form required for enrichment. SOLS holds a
unique market position as the sole US provider of this service, operating through two entities: the
100%-owned Metropolis Works conversion facility (with 10+ kt/yr of capacity and an NRC license
valid through 2060) and ConverDyn, a 50/50 joint venture with General Atomics that serves as the
exclusive purchaser and marketer of Metropolis's UF6 conversion output. (Note General Atomics'
share of ConverDyn is backed out as a non-controlling interest in the SOLS P&L). This structure gives
SOLS roughly 30% of global conversion supply (excluding Russia and China), a competitive position
reinforced by the upcoming full enforcement of the US ban on Russian uranium imports at the end of
2027. We think new entrants into the US market would face high regulatory and operational barriers,
with start-up of any new conversion assets likely to take ~5 years.
Metropolis was idled from 2017 to 2023 amid weak pricing, which helped work off a large overhang
of excess secondary supply left from the Cold War era. Since restarting, SOLS has invested more
than $100MM in modernization and efficiency upgrades, increasing capacity from 8,000 tons/year
to 10,000 tons/year and positioning the facility to capture growth in a market that has tightened
considerably. Conversion pricing has climbed from roughly $10/kgU as recently as 2018 to more
than $30/kgU currently, driven by constrained new supply, shrinking secondary supply, and rising
demand. Contracts are typically bilateral, 3-5 years long, with ~90% firm volume commitments
and a mix of fixed and inflation-adjusted pricing, which provides SOLS with good visibility. SOLS
points to a backlog of ~$2.2B (as of March 2026), with capacity largely spoken for through 2030
and some contracts extending into the 2030s, which should support a double-digit EBITDA CAGR
through 2030. SOLS also generates $40MM-$80MM in revenue annually from non-conversion,
nuclear-adjacent activities, including uranium storage, sampling, U3O8 buy/sell sourcing, and product
swapping between locations with other industry players.
Looking forward, SOLS is positioning the nuclear segment to benefit from a number of industry
drivers: rising U.S. electricity demand (partly driven by AI data centers), a US Department of Energy
goal to roughly quadruple U.S. nuclear capacity from ~100GW to 400GW by 2050, reactor restarts

Healthcare Packaging:
This business has two core platforms: high-barrier packaging materials for pharmaceuticals and
next-generation propellants for inhalers. Within pharma packaging, SOLS provides high-moisture-

propellants are expected to be defensive, GDP-plus growth markets.

Electronic Materials: This business has four main product lines: sputtering targets, electronic
polymers, thermal solutions, and high-purity etchants and solvents. SOLS has positioned this
business to address growing demand for smaller, higher-performing, and more energy- and thermally-
efficient electronic devices. In addition to growing material use per chip as semiconductor architecture
becomes more complex, SOLS has exposure to back-end advanced packaging materials that are of
growing importance as chips are integrated into interconnected systems to enhance performance for
AI and other high-performance computing applications. SOLS serves semiconductor and data center
end markets, in addition to automotive applications including EVs and advanced driver assistance
systems (ADAS). SOLS has been a key supplier for 50+ years, with strong relationships (including
co-development) with semiconductor manufacturers and equipment manufacturers.
●Sputtering targets: SOLS has a strong position in copper, copper alloy, and titanium sputtering
targets, which are used in physical vapor deposition (PVD). PVD is a front-end process by which a
thin film of material can be deposited onto a semiconductor wafer or other substrate. The process
takes place in a vacuum chamber, where material from a target is transformed into a vapor and

condenses to form a thin layer on the substrate. We believe increasing chip complexity is driving a
need for more advanced deposition processes, higher-purity materials, and increased application
expertise. SOLS brings expertise in precisely controlling the microstructure to ensure low particulate
counts. Other target materials include aluminum, tungsten, tantalum, molybdenum, and ruthenium,
and a range of alloys. SOLS is investigating new metals for use in next-generation targets, and
is investing $200MM in its Spokane, WA, facility to enhance capacity and increase automation.
Competitors in this product line include Linde, Materion, JX Metals, Konfoong, and Plansee.
●Electronic polymers: These materials serve advanced packaging and display applications.
Product lines include anti-reflective coatings, lithographic and etch-patterning materials, dopants,
and organic fill and planarizing materials. SOLS was a pioneer in spin-on glass (SOG) technology
that uses centrifugal force to spread material into a thin, uniform film on a wafer for planarization,
dielectric formation, or doping. We believe this is a relatively small piece of the EM segment.
●Thermal solutions: Thermal management is becoming more critical to chip performance and
reliability, particularly as more chips are packed into smaller spaces. This primarily back-end product
line includes thermal interface materials (TIMs) for chip packaging and final board assembly,
including phase-change materials that are particularly well-suited for cooling graphic processing
units (GPUs) and EV battery inverters. TIMs help to fill gaps between two surfaces (like a chip and
a heat sink). Phase-change TIMs are typically solid or wax-like at room temperature, but melt at
operating temperatures to better fill all the microscopic voids between two surfaces, creating a low-
thermal-resistance path from the heat source (the chip) to whatever is designed to carry the heat
away (the heat sink). Competitors include Henkel, 3M, ESI, and Dow.
●High-purity etchants and solvents: These high-purity materials are used for etching and
cleaning in multiple front-end steps of the semiconductor production process, including lithography,
deposition, and CMP. As the size of chip features shrink, the sensitivity to impurities increases
exponentially, so purity becomes critical to preserving/improving yields. We believe SOLS is
particularly strong in hydrogen fluoride etchants (for oxide removal as well as cleaning steps), wash
solvents (which remove organic residues, photoresist, or water from the wafer), and BF3 (boron
trifluoride, a gas used for ion implantation and doping). SOLS also supplies some precursors for
NF3 (nitrogen trifluoride), an important etchant and chamber-cleaning gas. We believe custom-
blended products can bring higher margins. Competitors include BASF, Fujifilm, Kanto Chemical,
Avantor, and Technic.
Research & Performance Chemicals:
●Research chemicals: Includes the Fluka brand, a leading supplier of reagents for DNA and RNA
assays, as well as the Hydranal brand of Karl Fischer titration reagents (which help to measure
moisture content in lab samples). Also includes a range of solvents for chromatographic and
spectroscopic applications. We believe these products are primarily used by research institutions.
●Fine chemicals: Produces and refines a range of high-purity inorganic halides, phosphorous
derivatives, sulfur derivatives, oxides, and other inorganic and organic compounds. These
products serve a diverse set of end markets including pharmaceuticals, metal surface treatment,
electroplating, personal care, healthcare, nutrition, adhesives & sealants, catalysts, and energy.
Purity and consistency are key, and SOLS has a strong position in Europe.
●Specialty additives: Includes plastic and wax blends for a range of end uses, including processing
aids for vinyl and rubber, matting agents for coatings, asphalt additives, wax additives for adhesives,
color dispersants for polymer masterbatch, and performance additives for inks.
Safety & Defense Solutions: This product line produces ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene
(UHMWPE) fibers that are incredibly strong but light-weight, used primarily in ballistic protection
applications, as well as in some industrial applications (ropes and fishing lines), medical device, and
commercial applications. The Spectra brand serves manufacturers of military body armor and other
safety applications, and SOLS maintains a strong relationship with the US government in setting
quality standards. Growth tends to be driven by defense and law-enforcement spending, though we
also believe Spectra has an opportunity to gain market share over time. Spectra fibers have a lighter
weight and in most cases better performance than competing body armor products: Compared to
Kevlar (a para-aramid fiber), Spectra is more durable and abrasion-resistant, breathable/cooler in
hot conditions, doesn't absorb moisture (which would make it heavier), and is more UV-resistant. In
addition to Kevlar (made by DuPont), Dyneema (another UHMWPE fiber made by Avient) is a more
direct competitor.
