 
Another One Bites The Dust: Blackstone's AirTrunk 
Pushes $1.5 Billion Data Center IPO To 2027 Days 
After Firmus Faceplant 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 14, 2026 - 05:35 PM 
Last week it was a 42-megawatt former Bitcoin miner asking for a $30 billion valuation. This 
week it's the region's biggest, most established data center operator, owned by Blackstone, 
asking for... a REIT yield. Public markets said no to both. 
According to Bloomberg, AirTrunk is delaying the planned initial public offering of a Singapore 
real estate investment trust until next year "as higher interest rates and market volatility temper 
investor demand." The Blackstone and CPP Investments-backed operator had planned to start 
formally gauging investor interest for the $1.5 billion REIT IPO at the start of October; the WSJ 
reports the deal could now come in the first or second quarter of 2027, "depending on 
conditions." 
The kicker: before hitting pause, AirTrunk had already considered sweetening the deal, offering 
a roughly 6% yield instead of the previously envisaged 5.5%, per Bloomberg. That still wasn't 
enough. 

 
 
Below we look at why AirTrunk is not Firmus, why that makes its delay arguably more telling, 
and what Goldman's new ANZ data center work says about who funds the next A$50-65 billion. 
Three Shelved In A Week 
AirTrunk is the third Asia-Pacific data center listing to be pulled or pushed in roughly a week. 
Nvidia-backed Firmus withdrew its ASX float (Oct 8) after slashing the A$11 marketed price as 
much as 25% during bookbuild, and PLDT's data center unit Vitro deferred its Philippine REIT 
IPO to 2027, citing "current market conditions and rising interest rates." Which is also roughly 
what we predicted when SoftBank's SB Energy pulled its own IPO marketing (Sep 22):

Singapore, Malaysia, Hong Kong and India, bought by Blackstone and CPP Investments for 
A$24 billion in 2024 in the largest data center deal on record at the time. Its REIT was meant to

purchases from AirTrunk and refinance existing debt, which Bloomberg called "a key test for 
data centre financing in Asia, where banks are hitting their sector limits." 

 
The test came back negative, and the reason was rates. Per Bloomberg, the US rate increases 
in September kept eroding investor appetite, and Singapore's main REIT index has tumbled 
12% since late July (The Star/Bloomberg). Nor is the Fed done: Cleveland Fed's Hammack 
recently told the WSJ that rates "must go higher" from the current 3.75%-4% range, as 
Goldman's Sydney futures desk flagged in Sunday's morning comment. 
Which brings us to the napkin math. The US 10-year closed at 5.22% on Tuesday, per 
Goldman's FINS & REITs desk. At the original 5.5% target yield, AirTrunk's REIT would have 
offered a ~28bp pickup over Treasurys; at the "sweetened" 6%, roughly 78bp, for an illiquid, 
single-sector vehicle with currency, tenant and construction risk (the comparison is approximate 
and not like-for-like by currency, but the direction is the point). In other words, investors were 
being asked to take equity risk on AI infrastructure for a few dozen basis points over the 
risk-free rate. They declined. 
 
Goldman's economists put the backdrop bluntly in this week's Global Economics Analyst: 
"Global interest rates have reached their highest levels since 2007." A yield product priced off 
the 2024 rate regime was always going to struggle in the 2026 one. 
Same Message, Different Messenger 
So the mechanics differ, but the message is the same: investors want more attractive pricing for 
the risks they are being asked to fund. For Firmus, that meant a lower valuation. For AirTrunk, it 

 
means a higher yield, which mathematically is also a lower valuation for Blackstone's assets. 
Either way, the gap between what private marks say AI infrastructure is worth and what public 
buyers will pay has just been put on display twice in one week.

Goldman: Demand Is Real, Funding Is The Handbrake 
None of this means the demand isn't there. Goldman's sales desk flagged a new GIR initiation 
on ANZ digital infrastructure(available to pro subs), and the numbers are big. As GS' Jerry Shen 
summarized on Oct 13: 
"As AI adoption accelerates, ANZ faces a 0.7GW supply shortfall by 2030, sparking an 
enormous A$50-65bn capex supercycle." (emphasis ours) 

 
 
But here's the part that matters for AirTrunk. On listed peer NEXTDC (NXT AU), GIR stayed at 
Neutral precisely because of funding: 
"While the street loves the broad demand story, GIR Chris cautions that funding is the actual 
handbrake. He models FY28/29 EBITDA 3-5% below consensus, flagging near-term margin 
dilution from hyperscale transitions and a looming A$1bn+ funding shortfall." (emphasis ours) 
Translation: the A$50-65 billion has to come from somewhere, and the two obvious 
public-market pipes (an ASX growth IPO and an SGX yield vehicle) both just jammed in the 
same week. What's left is what Firmus already said it would do: "pursue capital from the private 
markets." Private credit, insurance balance sheets and SPVs, at a price. 
Bottom Line 
Bloomberg says AirTrunk's "deliberations over details such as the size and timing of the 
potential IPO are ongoing." That is banker-speak for "we'll be back when the price is right," and 
the price that's right for buyers is not the one that's right for Blackstone. 
We'd argue AirTrunk's delay is more telling than Firmus'. Firmus was easy to dismiss as a frothy 
outlier with a made-up multiple. AirTrunk is the blue-chip version of the trade: real assets, real 
tenants, a Blackstone sponsor and a yield sweetener. If that can't clear at 6% with the 10-year at 
5.2%, then the problem isn't one overreaching issuer, it's the cost of capital for the entire AI 

 
build-out. The boom was underwritten at 2024 rates; it is being refinanced at 2007 
rates.Someone eats that gap, and so far the public markets are making it clear it won't be them.
