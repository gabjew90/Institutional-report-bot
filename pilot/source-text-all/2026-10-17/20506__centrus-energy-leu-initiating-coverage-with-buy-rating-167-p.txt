LEU: Initiating Coverage With A Buy Rating And $167
Price Target
Key Message: We are initiating coverage of Centrus Energy Corp. (LEU) with a Buy
rating and $167 price target.
• Initiating Research Coverage With a Buy Rating and $167 Price Target: We are
initiating research coverage of LEU with a Buy recommendation and $167 price target.
LEU is the only company in the United States with a pathway to providing high-assay
low enriched uranium (HALEU) fuel to commercial customers. The company faces a
long, expensive road to HALEU enrichment operations, but we believe that LEU’s unique
competitive position merits a positive stance on the stock. Our valuation approach is
centered on a target multiple of 11x our 2032 EBITDA estimate. We have chosen 2032
as LEU’s HALEU enrichment efforts are expected to be at scale by then.
• The Strongest Positioned Company In US Nuclear Fuel Enrichment: The long decline
of the US commercial nuclear power business has left the US with no fully US-owned
enrichment capabilities. The two western-aligned suppliers, France’s Orano and the
German/Dutch/UK owned Urenco, are insufficient to meet expected demand. Russia and
China jointly control more than 60% of global enrichment supply. There are other US-
owned companies looking to set up enrichment processes in the US, but none of them
have yet operated centrifuges to our knowledge. LEU’s greater operational maturity and
financial resources make the company unique, in our view.
• Centrus Has Demonstrated That It Is Technically Capable: Uranium enrichment raises
the concentration of the more fissionable U-235 uranium isotope relative to the much
more common U-238 isotope, in order to create fuel that can be used to sustain fission in
a nuclear reactor. It is a well-understood process, but complex and expensive. Building
and operating the high-speed gas centrifuges used in the enrichment process is at the
core of the business. LEU has demonstrated, with its operations in Piketon, Ohio, that
the company is technically capable of meeting the operational challenge. The question
facing the company now is simply how to scale.
• The Supportive Policy Environment Is Critical: We believe that the public opposition
and unsupportive policy that drove the US nuclear decline has shifted fundamentally, and
that the new, more supportive environment should survive the current administration. This
is good news for LEU, which has significantly benefited from its relationship with the US
Department of Energy. The future of demand for HALEU, and the prospects for LEU’s
customers, also depend significantly on DOE and Department of War involvement.

LEU: Stock Price Performance
Weak Stock Performance Reflects Market Recalibration Of Nuclear Renaissance Risk
Source: FactSet, Guggenheim Securities, LLC
•
Over the trailing 3 years ending October 7, 2027, LEU stock returned ~167% vs. the S&P 500’s return of ~81%. LEU's 
appreciation from Q4 2023 to a peak near $436 in October 2025 was driven by the nuclear energy renaissance underpinned by 
AI data center power demand, the U.S. ban on Russian enriched uranium imports, and Centrus’ unique position as the only 
private U.S. producer of HALEU fuel for next-generation reactors; a legitimate thesis that the market was right to recognize, in 
our view.
•
The subsequent decline to $147 by October 2026 reflects a more recent market recalibration of the risk associated with the US 
nuclear renaissance generally, and the additional challenges associated with HALEU production more specifically. The stock 
price decline does not, in our view, indicate any changes in the fundamental prospects for LEU’s business. As the company 
advances towards its critical milestones, notably the deployment of initial HALEU enrichment, we expect the stock to react 
positively – which is reflected in our current Buy recommendation.

LEU: Company Description
A Supplier Of Enriched Uranium Fuel For Nuclear Power Plants
The Leading 
US Supplier Of 
Nuclear Fuel
• Centrus Energy is a leading U.S. supplier of nuclear fuel and services for the commercial nuclear 
power industry, delivering enriched uranium to utilities in the United States and abroad to help meet 
the growing demand for energy.
• The company operates through two segments: the Low-Enriched Uranium (LEU) segment, which 
sells enrichment work as separative work units (SWU), natural uranium, and enriched uranium 
products to nuclear power plant operators. The Technical Solutions segment provides uranium 
enrichment (including HALEU), engineering, manufacturing, and other technical services.
• Centrus has embarked on a multi-billion-dollar expansion of its uranium enrichment capabilities to 
supply LEU for existing reactors and HALEU for next-generation advanced reactors, with commercial 
LEU enrichment activities launched in December 2025.
• Centrus is leading the effort to restore America's domestic uranium enrichment capabilities at scale, 
addressing critical clean energy, energy security, and national security priorities amid heightened 
geopolitical concerns around foreign fuel supply dependence.
America's Sole 
HALEU Producer
• Centrus is the only NRC-licensed company in the United States producing High-Assay Low-Enriched 
Uranium (HALEU), which is uranium enriched between 5% and 20% U-235, a critical fuel required by 
most advanced reactor designs under development.
• The company has a significant partnership with the U.S. Department of Energy, including a $900 
million DOE investment to install additional centrifuge cascades at the company’s Piketon, Ohio 
facility.
Based In Bethesda, 
MD, LEU Should Be 
A ~$1.2B Business 
In 2032E
• Centrus Energy originated from the U.S. government's decades-old uranium enrichment complex, 
which was privatized through the Energy Policy Act of 1992 as the United States Enrichment 
Corporation, sold via IPO in 1998 as USEC Inc., before undergoing financial restructuring in 2014 
and re-emerging as Centrus Energy Corp.
• LEU is led by President and CEO Amir Vexler, and is headquartered in Bethesda, MD. Based on our 
estimates, LEU could be a ~$1.2B company in FY2032.

LEU: Valuation Analysis
An Attractive Entry Into A Supply-Chain-Critical Asset
Note: SMR, XE, OKLO, and GEV metrics are Guggenheim estimates; all others are Visible Alpha consensus
Source: Company reports, Visible Alpha, and Guggenheim Securities, LLC estimates 
•
We value LEU on our 2032 outlook, consistent with our approach to the pure-play SMRs in our coverage. We choose 2032 over 
earlier periods because the company’s enrichment business and its key customers should be reaching reasonable scale by then. 
•
LEU trades at a warranted discount to services peers, but at a steep premium to SMRs. We view the recent sector-wide risk-off 
move as somewhat overdone, creating an attractive entry into a supply-chain-critical asset like LEU that continues to build 
significant commercial momentum across its customer base. 
•
We believe a premium to the SMR pure-plays is warranted given that Centrus has operated an AC100M centrifuge for two years 
now, executing DOE deliveries on time, and continuing to receive strong government support. Uncertainty around the velocity of 
the commercial ramp and a steep investment period lay ahead, but LEU’s position as the only US supplier of commercial 
enrichment services for both standard enrichment levels and HALEU is unique in our view, which supports our valuation case.
•
Compared to services peers, we think that a target multiple on our 2032 estimates that places LEU at a slight discount to rapidly 
growing, well-established companies like BE and CW makes sense. We note that our target multiple of 11x 2032E EBITDA also 
is roughly comparable with valuation levels for GEV and BWXT. The analysis also works nicely based on our 2030 estimates.
•
Adjusting the implied enterprise valuation stemming from our analysis for current capital structure yields a market capitalization 
of $167 per share, which supports our Buy rating.

Nuclear Power Offers A Unique Value Proposition For Decarbonization
Small Physical Footprint, High Capacity Factor Allow Nuclear To Complement Solar And Wind
• Despite early efforts to transition away from fossil fuels, ever-increasing power needs and limitations of alternative assets have 
resulted in natural gas remaining the largest source of power generation in the US. Globally, natural gas comes in second to coal 
as the largest source of generation.
• Nuclear power generation offers a carbon-free, firm-power source that complements renewables. Given the low land use and 
lower transmission buildout requirements, nuclear is well-positioned against alternative sources that may be distributed or site-
constrained.
• Nuclear power is one of the few firm clean power sources, and one of the only options proven at scale given its operating history of 
over 70 years. Carbon capture and long-duration energy storage are compelling alternatives but remain in early stages of 
commercialization.
• The challenge for policymakers is balancing the need to meet electricity demand growth with the mandate to continue 
decarbonizing electricity generation. As grid stability concerns intensify, regulators are increasingly focused on grid reliability, 
resilience and affordability alongside decarbonization goals.

Reducing The Cost Of Decarbonization With Nuclear & Other Clean Firm Power
Adding Zero-Carbon Power When Solar Is Not Contributing Is Especially Valuable
• Variable renewables offer low capital and operating costs, but overall system costs increase substantially when accounting for the 
additional generation capacity and storage needed to achieve adequate reliability levels.
• Nuclear power reduces overall system costs by ~37% in high power price markets like California relative to renewable-plus-
storage alternatives. Firm clean power also generates electricity during the most expensive hours when renewables are unable to 
produce, strengthening the economic value proposition in markets with high renewable penetration.

There Is A Range Of Proven And New Technologies On The Market Today
Light Water Reactors Comprise The Vast Majority Of The World’s Operating Reactors
Source: DOE’s “Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Advanced Nuclear” Report, Guggenheim Securities, LLC
• Today's nuclear fleet in the US consists of Gen III and III+ reactors, which use water as coolant and low-enriched uranium (LEU) 
as fuel. The vast majority of operating commercial reactors use a pressurized-water (PWR) design. For the intermediate term, we 
expect PWR and boiling-water (BWR) reactors to dominate new deployments, while the industry works to advance Gen IV 
designs.
• Gen IV reactors offer the potential to deploy nuclear power in novel ways, notably including much smaller reactor sizes. These 
newer reactors will incorporate novel coolants, in some cases new fuel form factors, and will also utilize more highly enriched fuel 
(HALEU) in many cases. These advanced designs are still in development but represent the next evolution in nuclear technology.
• Natural uranium contains 0.7% of the U-235 isotope, with the remaining 99.3% predominantly U-238, which does not directly 
contribute to the fission process. Today’s LEU-based light water reactors require uranium to be enriched to 3-5%. HALEU is 
enriched to greater than 5% and less than 20% of the U-235 isotope. While applications for HALEU are currently limited to 
research reactors and medical isotope production, many new reactor designs incorporate more highly enriched fuel per the table 
below.

SMRs Favor HALEU Given Its Ability To Meet Critical Operational Needs
We Expect Many New Designs To Embrace Higher Enrichment Levels Despite Challenges 
Enables Compact
Core Design
• Many of the announced SMR designs feature compact core designs. This requires higher uranium 
enrichment to achieve and sustain critical configurations.
• There are workarounds, of course, but the most efficient way to support a smaller core design is with 
more highly enriched fuel. It is not surprising that the majority of new SMR designs are built around 
HALEU fuel. 
• HALEU is a viable alternative to plutonium for initial cores in fast reactors, which is particularly 
valuable for countries without existing plutonium stocks. 
• This expands the potential deployment pathways for advanced reactor designs in markets where fuel 
supply considerations have historically been a constraint.
• Even in cases where plutonium might be available – OKLO, for example – companies typically seek 
to transition away from surplus weapons fuel over time.
Higher Outlet
Temperatures For
Industrial Applications
• SMRs are well-suited to several industrial applications, including hydrogen production, desalination, 
and district heating, which rely on HALEU-based fuel designs that can deliver the necessary high 
outlet temperatures. 
• This positions HALEU-fueled SMRs well for a range of high-temperature applications, greatly 
expanding their use cases beyond traditional electricity generation.
Higher Burn-Up &
Longer Operational
Cycles
• HALEU enables SMRs to achieve higher burn-up rates, which drives more efficient fuel use and 
longer periods between refuelings. This can be particularly important for remote or off-grid 
applications where refueling logistics can be expensive and complex.
• Fewer refuelings means less operational downtime and cost while also lowering the risk associated 
with frequent fuel handling. The operational advantages become more pronounced in applications 
where accessibility is limited or where continuous operation is critical.

HALEU or Halt: Bottleneck Standing Between Today And Tomorrow’s Reactors
Building Sufficient Enrichment Capacity Is The Most Substantial Barrier
Mining & 
Milling
Conversion
Enrichment
Fabrication
The greatest risk concentrates around enrichment and conversion, given the high level of Russian control, long 
development timelines, and large capital requirements. The transition to SMRs will hinge on solving the HALEU 
bottleneck, which relies on Centrus expanding operations, establishing additional Western HALEU capacity, and 
building new fabrication infrastructure licensed for higher enrichment levels. 
Russia and China Control 44% of global conversion capacity and 59% of enrichment services, creating severe Western 
dependency at the two most capital-intensive and technically complex stages of the fuel cycle. 
HALEU chokepoint - until Centrus began operations, only Russia and China had commercial HALEU production capability —
the fuel required by nearly all Gen IV and SMR designs.
U.S. sources only 5% of uranium domestically, with significant exposure to Kazakhstan (~22% of deliveries), which 
maintains close ties to Moscow.
No U.S. owned commercial enrichment capacity since the last facility closed in 2013, creating complete foreign dependency.
Single-point-of-failure at conversion – Metropolis Works (Illinois), owned by Solstice Advanced Materials and marketed 
through ConverDyn, is the only U.S. UF6 conversion plant. Licensed to 15,000 tU/yr but running at 50–70% of that capacity, it

Conversion Capacity: A Western Choke Point With Russian Exposure
Backlog For Non-Russian Producers Is High
Source: World Nuclear Association, Nuclear Fuel Report (2023 edition)
• Global conversion capacity is highly concentrated with only a few major suppliers worldwide, creating a critical chokepoint in 
the nuclear fuel supply chain and strategic vulnerability. China's 15,000 tU is effectively captive, it is assumed the CNNC 
develops capacity for only its domestic fleet. Meanwhile Russia's 12,500 tU faces escalating Western sanctions and import 
bans. Excluding both, leaves ~34,500 tU of licensed, available capacity for Western buyers. 
• Against that supply base, ~70,000 tU per year of conversion is required to fuel the 2025 reactor fleet — more than total global 
licensed capacity of 62,000 tU, and approximately double available Western capacity. Actual UF6 output is lower, still at 
~42,000 tU, leaving very little room for error. WNA expects the market to remain tight and estimates conversion capacity must 
increase more than threefold to support the goal of tripling nuclear capacity. That imbalance is what shows up commercially in 
Metropolis Works’ (formerly ConverDyn) $2B+ backlog. It is important to understand that this is not cyclical tightness, but 
rather a structural deficit that will take time to resolve.

Nuclear Fuel Fabrication: Demand Drivers & Capacity Dynamics
Capacity Challenges, Yes, But Less Concentration Than Other Parts Of The Fuel Supply Chain
• Demand for fuel fabrication services should grow roughly in line with growth in nuclear generating capacity, with typical reload 
requirements of 16-20 tonnes per year per GW of installed capacity. First cores for new reactors create temporary peak demand, 
requiring approximately three to four times the volume of a standard reload batch at lower average enrichment levels (~2.8%).
• Fabrication demand is also influenced by changes in utilities' reactor operating and fuel management strategies, which are partly 
driven by technical improvements in fuel fabrication itself. LWR discharge burn-ups have increased steadily as improvements in 
fuel design have made this possible, and this has tended to reduce fabrication demand as fuel remains in the reactor for longer 
periods. There is a limit to how far burn-ups can be pushed, however, without addressing the 5% enrichment limit currently in place 
for criticality safety margins at fuel fabrication plants.
• An industry-wide focus on increasing fuel performance and reliability has also decreased the demand for fuel to replace defective 
assemblies. We note that these efficiency gains partially offset the impact of new reactor builds on overall fabrication demand 
growth.
• Global fuel fabrication capacity is moderately concentrated, with key players including Westinghouse, Framatome, and TVEL. The 
primary supply chain risk lies in asset specificity—fuel assemblies are designed for specific reactor types, creating high switching 
costs and lock-in effects. Western efforts to develop alternative VVER fuel through programs like Westinghouse's APIS initiative 
are underway, but fuel qualification timelines of 5-10 years limit the ability to rapidly diversify away from incumbent suppliers.

TRISO Fuel: Emerging Segment In Advanced Nuclear Fuel Fabrication 
•
TRISO (Tri-structural Isotropic) fuel is designed specifically for advanced reactors including high-temperature gas-cooled reactors, 
molten salt reactors, and microreactors. Unlike conventional uranium dioxide pellets, TRISO consists of poppy seed-sized particles 
with uranium kernels encapsulated in three layers of carbon and silicon carbide ceramic coatings, enabling "walk-away safe" 
operation at temperatures up to 1,600°C. The fuel requires High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU) feedstock enriched to 
~20%, compared to 3-5% for conventional LWR fuel, adding complexity and cost to the supply chain.
•
The competitive landscape is nascent with three key players: BWXT is the established incumbent with 20+ years of experience and 
the only U.S. company with production-scale TRISO manufacturing capability; X-Energy/TRISO-X is building the largest planned 
facility (700,000 pebbles/year) in Oak Ridge with production expected in 2028; and Standard Nuclear emerged as a fast-mover after 
acquiring Ultra Safe Nuclear's assets, becoming the first to produce commercial HALEU TRISO fuel in January 2026.
•
Key supply chain risks center on HALEU availability—the U.S. currently lacks commercial enrichment capacity, leaving producers

The global uranium enrichment market remains dominated by foreign and state-

is significantly smaller by capacity, but its positioning is unique: it is the only 
U.S.-owned enrichment company and the sole domestic producer of HALEU.
•
That positioning rests on demonstrated technology and federal backing. The 
American Centrifuge Plant in Piketon has operated since 2023, completed its 
demonstration obligation in June 2026 — two weeks early — and has produced 
more than 1,900 kg of HALEU. A $900M DOE task order funds the commercial 
pivot, with first new capacity expected in 2029. Contracts with X-energy (XE) 
and Antares (not listed), plus an Oklo (OKLO) framework, underpin a multi-
billion-dollar buildout. We think the combination of demonstrated technology, 
federal funding, and signed commercial agreements gives Centrus a head start 
that is measured in years, not months.
•

$900M DOE HALEU task order and is building at Paducah with Thiel-backed 
private capital. It has no licensed capacity and no operating track record, so 
Centrus has no comparable domestic HALEU competitor today. The gap is real, 
but it is more attributable to execution, not exclusivity.
•
Industry tailwinds are supportive. The Russian import ban runs through 2040 
with waivers ending January 1, 2028. AI-driven power demand continues to 
surge, and $2.7B of DOE funding is committed. Orano and Global Laser 
Enrichment (GLE) are the only other funded domestic entrants. Centrus' lead is 
real, in our view, but not necessarily permanent.

LEU: Investment Risks
Execution, Financing, And Counterparty Concentration Are The Binding Constraints
• Domestic centrifuge manufacturing is effectively being restarted from ground zero. Centrus holds a 
meaningful competitive position, but its current capacity produces only a small fraction of projected 
advanced-reactor demand will require. The $900M DOE contract funds a transition that has not yet been 
executed at scale. Slippage in manufacturing throughput, delays in cascade commissioning, or 
complications with NRC licensing amendments would each push commercial operations to the right, and 
we would note that these risks are not independent of one another. Fluor and Geiger Brothers are the 
named EPC contractors for the scale-up, and their ability to deliver on schedule will be an important 
variable to monitor. 
• Centrus is currently operating majority as a SWU broker dealer rather than producer — and will continue to 
do so until legacy supply contracts with TENEX and Orano phase out and Piketon ramps to meaningful 
production. Centrus is sourcing SWU primarily from Urenco (not listed) and Orano (not listed), both of 
which are eventual direct competitors in the enrichment market. We're not sure why either would have 
incentive to price favorably to a future rival, and we regard expectations of sustained margin stability under 
this arrangement as somewhat unrealistic. As a reseller, Centrus is effectively a price-taker on its primary 
input, which leaves the company exposed to margin compression that management has limited ability to 
offset with it being a function of contract mix and prevailing market SWU pricing. 
• Centrus's HALEU customer base: Oklo (OKLO), TerraPower (not listed), X-Energy (XE), Radiant (not 
listed), and Antares (not listed) — consists largely of pre-revenue developers with unproven reactor 
technologies and deployment schedules that remain, in our view, speculative. We are not ruling out that 
Centrus HALEU production capacity could precede the paying customer base depending on the rate to 
which customers execute against deployment timelines. We also note the DOE is simultaneously the 
anchor customer and regulator; the January 2026 award of $2.7bn across multiple enrichers, including 
General Matter, demonstrates volumes can be redirected.
• Centrus is in the early innings of a multi-billion-dollar capex cycle as it progresses towards transitioning to 
commercial operations and we think the funding path deserves careful attention. Management raised ~$1B 
via ATM last year and ~$500M in September, while this is meaningful capital, the funding path to 
commercialization still does not seem fully de-risked – the OKLO December equity offering is a reminder of 
this. We find it difficult to underwrite a free-cash-flow inflection while construction timing, licensing 
milestones, and the manufacturing ramp execution all remain unresolved. Incremental capital requirements 
will be a watch item as things progress.

Uranium Conversion, Enrichment, and Fuel Fabrication
Centrus Energy — The only U.S.-owned uranium enrichment company, operating the American Centrifuge Plant in Ohio and holding

Orano — Beyond mining, Orano operates integrated conversion and enrichment facilities in France, serving as a key supplier of

Rosatom / TENEX — Russia's state nuclear corporation and its fuel export subsidiary, historically the dominant global supplier of

Urenco — A European consortium-owned company and one of the world's leading uranium enrichment providers, operating centrifuge

Westinghouse Electric — A leading nuclear fuel fabricator supplying fuel assemblies for pressurized water reactors globally. 
Westinghouse's fuel business complements its reactor technology and services operations, providing an integrated offering to utility 
customers.
Converdyn — A 50/50 joint venture between Solstice Advanced Materials and General Atomics, operating the only uranium 
hexafluoride (UF6) conversion facility in the United States at Metropolis, Illinois. Converdyn represents approximately 20% of worldwide

technology to produce HALEU for advanced reactors. General Matter has received a $900 million DOE contract and is building a $1.5

Reactor Technology – SMR & Advanced
Aalo Atomics — An early-stage advanced reactor developer focused on compact, portable reactor designs for distributed power 
applications and remote locations.
Antares — A U.S. developer of factory-produced fission microreactors for defense, space, and remote applications. Its R1 design 
delivers 100 kWe to 1 MWe using TRISO fuel in a prismatic graphite core with sodium heat pipes and a closed nitrogen Brayton cycle. 
ARC Clean Technology — A U.S.-based developer of the ARC-100, a 100 MWe sodium-cooled fast reactor based on 30 years of 
proven EBR-II operating experience. ARC is targeting a 2029 deployment at New Brunswick Power's Point Lepreau site and has 
completed Phase 2 of the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission's Vendor Design Review.
Blykalla — A global based developer of factory built, 55 MWe lead-cooled reactor, the SEALER, an ideal baseload solution for AI data 
centers and industrial application. Blykalla is advancing regulatory review in Sweden and pre-application engagement with the U.S. 
NRC for Western expansion. 
Deep Fission — An early-stage company developing novel deep borehole reactor concepts, exploring unconventional approaches to 
nuclear deployment and waste management.
Holtec — A US-based established nuclear technology company developing a dual-unit Gen III+ SMR-300 at decommissioned nuclear 
power plants featuring a two-loop pressurized light-water design producing ~340 MWe using conventional LEU fuel. 
Kairos Power — A developer of fluoride salt-cooled high-temperature reactors, combining molten salt coolant with TRISO fuel. Kairos 
is advancing its Hermes demonstration reactor in Tennessee with DOE support.
Last Energy — A U.S.-based SMR developer focused on a turnkey, standardized micro-reactor offering for European industrial and 
data center customers, emphasizing rapid deployment and simplified financing structures.
Moltex Energy — A nuclear technology company developing the Stable Salt Reactor (SSR), a hybrid design that contains molten salt 
fuel within fuel assemblies submerged in coolant salt. Moltex is advancing its first SSR-W reactor at Point Lepreau, New Brunswick, 
with a focus on dramatically lower capital costs and the ability to consume nuclear waste as fuel.
NANO Nuclear Energy — The first publicly traded nuclear microreactor company in the U.S., developing two complementary designs: 
ZEUS (a solid-core "nuclear battery" with no coolant system) and ODIN (a molten salt-cooled design using off-the-shelf components). 
NANO is pursuing vertical integration across reactors, HALEU fuel fabrication, and fuel transportation.
Natura Resources — A Texas-based developer of liquid-fueled molten salt reactors, on track to deploy America's first Generation IV 
reactor in 2026 at Abilene Christian University. Natura holds the only NRC-licensed liquid-fuel reactor design and has secured over 
$240 million in committed capital.
Newcleo — A UK-based advanced reactor company developing lead-cooled fast reactors with a closed fuel cycle strategy, targeting 
European markets with backing from private investors.
NuCube Energy — An advanced-nuclear technology company developing factory-built, TRISO fuel, microreactors targeting FOAK 
deployment in 2029. NuCube plans to deliver firm, carbon-free electricity and high-temperature process heat at the point of use 
serving microgrids, industrial process heat, and behind the meter data center power.
