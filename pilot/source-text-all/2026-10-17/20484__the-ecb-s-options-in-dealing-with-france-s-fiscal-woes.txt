The ECB’s options in dealing with
France’s fiscal woes
The bar for activating the ECB's bond-buying programme remains high.
However, a further deterioration in France's situation could eventually
force the ECB to demonstrate whether its 'whatever it
takes' commitment still holds
ECB President Christine Lagarde
The ECB’s most powerful tools are the ones it has never used. They are the tools that the ECB
developed after Mario Draghi’s ‘whatever it takes’ speech. Tools that showed ‘whatever it takes’
was something markets could believe in. The Outright Monetary Transactions programme
during the euro crisis and the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI) after the pandemic
both shared the same objective: to enable potentially unlimited purchases of government
bonds if needed.
Political and fiscal woes in France and the widening of the French spread have now raised the
question of whether the ECB could intervene through TPI, which allows it to purchase securities
issued by member states whose financing conditions have deteriorated for reasons not
justified by country-specific fundamentals. However, before activating it, the Governing Council
considers a cumulative set of criteria to determine whether the countries concerned are
pursuing sound and sustainable fiscal and macroeconomic policies. In practice, this includes

compliance with the EU fiscal framework, which would, at a minimum, require the proposed
French budget to be fully adopted.
The ECB has nevertheless left itself some discretion, stating that the intervention criteria “will
be dynamically adjusted to the unfolding risks and conditions to be addressed”. This brings
back memories of the famous “because it’s France” by former European Commission President
Jean-Claude Juncker. Generally speaking, the ECB will be in a difficult position: intervening too
quickly in support of France may be viewed unfavourably by other member states and
interpreted as monetary financing of the debt of a country unwilling to follow the rules. That
argues strongly against early intervention. Also, don’t forget that the leadership changes at
the ECB over the next months (Schnabel, Lane, Lagarde) could also affect how the ECB views
TPI.
Given the challenges of justifying the activation of TPI at present, we expect the ECB to adopt a
three-pronged approach to address the French crisis.
The first line of defence is a more dovish approach to policy rates. As bond markets are
currently taking over the ECB’s job of inserting a more restrictive monetary stance on the
economy, there is less need for the ECB to hike rates aggressively, or to hike rates at all. In fact,
the ECB has already started to prepare this option, as ECB president Christine Lagarde recently
noted that “while growth has been resilient, since our last meeting long-term interest rates
have risen notably, which will slow growth and reduce pass-through by more than projected in
our September exercise".
The second line of defence would be a covert return to bond purchases. The ECB could instead
pause quantitative tightening temporarily and reinvest maturing bonds in its portfolio
“flexibly”, sending a positive signal to bond markets. This had already happened during the
Covid period.
Last but not least, and in our view, the ultimate option in the case of more contagion in
eurozone bond markets, the ECB would eventually trigger TPI. As long as France remains non-
compliant with the European fiscal rules, the ECB might even consider purchasing bonds from
almost all eurozone countries, except for France.
All in all, the current French situation is simply not enough for the ECB to trigger TPI. There are
other ways to tackle any unwarranted impact from higher bond yields on the broader
eurozone economy. However, a further escalation could eventually force the ECB to
prove whether its 'whatever it takes' commitment still holds.
