When Income became an Outcome 
Investment Strategy 
   
 
 
Income, re-invented and growing fast 
Income investing is no longer just coupons and dividends. What was mostly a defensive 
investing style tailored for retirees has matured into a multi-asset discipline that 
embraces derivatives, engineered payoffs and a broad spectrum of risk profiles. It spans 
a global retail structured products market measured in USD trillions and is rapidly 
adopting one of the most successful financial innovations in history: the exchange 
traded fund (ETF). In 2026, derivatives ETFs drew their strongest inflows in at least a 
decade, growing to $550bn globally. Over 40% of this AuM sits in income-focused 
strategies such as covered calls, autocallables and put-writing funds, a segment that has 
grown 5x faster than the entire ETF market in recent years. 
Higher rates are back, so why option income? Five reasons 
Despite the return of higher yields from traditional sources, demand for derivatives 
income has grown almost 3x since 2021, resulting in over $80bn of global equity-linked, 
income-focussed structured product issuance every quarter. In North America, a region 
known for its culture of equity participation, the yield enhancement style is now ~50% 
of all equity-linked issuance. What’s driving this? Structural trends like (1) a broadening 
demand for cash flow beyond retirees, (2) investors disillusioned with traditional fixed 
income after 2022’s inflation shock, (3) higher volatility boosting option derived payouts, 
(4) regulation and product innovation expanding investor choice, and (5) equity options 
usage moving mainstream. In our view, these forces are redefining income as a desired 
outcome and not just an investing style. 
Building diversified, multi-asset income portfolios 
Higher income can come at a cost. Investors need to be mindful of and navigate a trade-
off between yield and capital preservation, particularly if income is extracted for cash 
flow rather than reinvested. Furthermore, history shows that higher yielding strategies 
also tend to be more volatile. Fortunately, a portfolio approach can help manage these 
risks and trade-offs. Indeed, our simple, hypothetical backtests suggest that asset 
allocation across traditional income assets and derivatives-based strategies can deliver 
an attractive mix of yield, capital preservation, risk control and diversification versus 
riskier growth strategies. Moreover, our analysis suggests that derivatives-based income 
can complement traditional fixed income, with autocallables, for example, receiving a 
positive allocation in stylized portfolios that seek to maximize real yield per unit of risk.

 Not your grandfather’s income 
Investing for income holds an enduring appeal: the prospect of a steady, tangible return 
in a world where capital gains aren’t assured. While the concept is as old as civilisation 
itself, 20th century finance largely framed it as a pursuit of coupons and dividends to 
serve as a central pillar of lifecycle pension provision, particularly for older cohorts 
transitioning away from riskier, growth-oriented strategies. Today, income investing has 
matured into a sophisticated, multi-asset discipline which is at ease with engineered, 
derivatives-based payoffs and a spectrum of risk profiles. It not only spans a retail 
structured product‡ market measured in USD trillions but is fast embracing one of the 
most successful financial innovations in history, the exchange-traded fund (ETF).  
In what follows, we (i) set out the current landscape for income-focused strategies 
across retail structured products and the fast-growing universe of derivatives ETFs, (ii) 
examine why income from option selling has retained its appeal even as ‘safer’ yields 
have returned with interest rates no longer anchored near zero, and (iii) consider how 
derivatives income strategies can be integrated into broader portfolios. 
The rise of income: From structured products to ETFs 
Retail structured products have been a mainstay of financial markets since the start of 
this century, offering end investors risk asset participation, capital protection and yield 
enhancement solutions across asset classes, with a large variety of defined ‘payoffs’ and 
risk profiles. Exhibit 1 shows that nearly $1trn of these products were issued globally in 
2025 alone, with the majority (70%) being equity-linked.  
How does income investing fit into this picture? Exhibit 2 shows that in North America, a 
region known for its active retail investor base and strong culture of equity participation, 
yield enhancement as a product style has grown to nearly half of all equity-linked 
issuance. While this was understandable in the era of financial repression and ultra-low 
interest rates (lower availability of traditional yield), the trend towards structured income 
generation has remained intact despite a material rise in interest rates since 2022.

Five forces feed the growing derivatives income trend  
We see five forces fueling the rapid growth in derivatives-based income investing:  
1. 
Income has broadened beyond retirees to younger investors seeking cash flow 
2. 
Bonds have lost their monopoly over portfolio income after the 2022 inflation shock 
3. 
Higher equity volatility has increased the income available from selling options  
4. 
Regulation and product innovation have made derivatives payoffs easier to access  
5. 
Options have gone mainstream beyond institutional investors and vol professionals 
1. Income has broadened beyond retirees 
Income investing itself has broadened beyond its traditional retiree base.  Younger 
investors increasingly frame portfolio cash flow as “passive income” or a side hustle, 
with the FIRE ("Financial Independence, Retire Early") movement, crypto-era focus on 
“yield” and app-based investing all helping familiarize a new generation with the idea of 
generating recurring income from financial assets. Exhibit 11 makes the case that the 
majority (55%) of U.S adults think of generating regular income or supplementing 
regular income as their key purpose for investing, with Younger Millennials (31-37yrs) 
leading across the age cohorts. Meanwhile, Gen Z (18-30yrs) are at least twice as likely 
to learn about options trading than the broader population.

Derivatives-income strategies are one expression of this shift.  Just as buffer products‡ 
trade some upside for downside protection, income strategies can monetize future 
upside or option premium in exchange for current cash flow.

3. Higher equity volatility has boosted derivatives-income payouts  
Derivatives-income strategies seek to turn option premiums into recurring income, with 
the amount available from option selling heavily influenced by implied volatility.  Higher 
implied volatility generally increases the premium available from selling options, 
supporting larger potential coupons and distributions. 
The post-pandemic environment has marked a clear departure from the exceptionally 
low vol regime of the 2010s (Exhibit 14).  Cross-asset volatility fell to historic lows in 
2014, driven by low macroeconomic vol and investor confidence that central banks 
would act to alleviate market stress (see our report The death of volatility: why and are 
we at a turning point?).  The VIX and US single stock volatility subsequently reached all-
time lows in 2017.  Since Covid, however, equity implied volatility has reset structurally 
higher: our composite measure across major US indices and large cap stocks has risen 
from a median of 17.7% in 2013-19 to 22.9% in 2020-26 (Exhibit 14). 
The increase has been particularly pronounced in individual stocks amid a more 
idiosyncratic risk environment, with AI-driven disruption and uncertainty driving a record 
number of stock-specific outlier moves (see our report Stock vs index vol at 90s Dotcom 
extremes).  This has translated into higher option premiums: for example, annualized 
premiums from selling 1m 105% single stock calls have more than doubled from a 
median of 8% pre-Covid to 19% post-Covid (Exhibit 15).  Premiums are also higher 
across index calls and longer-dated puts, showing that the increase in income potential 
is not confined to one corner of the market (Exhibit 15).

4. Innovation broadened access and choice 
Product and regulatory innovation have made derivatives strategies easier to access in a 
wide range of formats.  Strategies once delivered mainly though structured products or 
institutional portfolios are now also available in exchange-traded vehicles.  In ETFs, the 
SEC’s 2019 Rule 6c-11 standardized the framework for launching funds, reducing the 
cost and regulatory burden of bringing ETFs to market and giving qualifying ETFs 
greater flexibility in how they manage creation and redemption baskets.  Alongside the 
subsequent growth of active ETFs, this has helped broaden the range of strategies that 
can be delivered through the ETF wrapper. 
Innovation has also broadened the menu of payoffs available to investors.  Across 
structured products and ETFs, investors can now access autocallables, covered call and 
put writing strategies, buffers and other option-linked structures across a wider range of 
underlyings, maturities and payout profiles.  The scale of this expansion is already visible 
in the ETF market, where derivatives-linked ETFs now hold more than $550bn globally, 
including over $230bn in income-focused strategies (Exhibits 5-8). Payoffs that once 
required bespoke derivatives can increasingly be accessed through packaged products.  
Investors can choose the objective, whether income, downside protection or enhanced 
participation, without having to trade the options themselves.  
5. Options went mainstream 
Listed US options activity across single stocks, ETFs, and equity indices has continued to 
set records in recent years.  The options market has also grown materially relative to US 
cash equities.  Indeed, daily options notional volume has risen from roughly half of US 
cash equity notional volume in 2011 to nearly 2.5x today, while options open interest 
relative to US equity market capitalization is also near historical highs (Exhibit 16). 
Importantly, this growth has extended well beyond institutional investors and volatility 
professionals.  Commission-free trading, digital brokerage platforms, stay-at-home 
investing during the pandemic and the 2020-21 meme stock era helped accelerate retail

The shift is also visible at brokerages like Schwab and Robinhood, where options

Options have also become more familiar to investors who do not trade them directly, 
with options-related content increasingly visible across forums such as Reddit, 
traditional financial media and digital brokerage apps.  At the same time, structured 
products and ETFs allow investors to access professionally implemented options 
strategies without selecting strikes, managing positions or rolling contracts themselves.  
Together, greater familiarity and easier access have helped move option-based income 
strategies further into the investing mainstream.

A trifecta of trade-offs: Yield, Capital Preservation & Risk 
Given income strategies span a broad risk spectrum, asset allocation involves trade-offs, 
as we showed for derivatives income ETFs in Exhibit 9 and Exhibit 10. Specifically, there 
is an important tradeoff between seeking higher income (yield) and preserving capital, 
which is important if the end investor decides to consistently extract income for 
cashflow needs rather than re-invest into the strategy. Adjacent to this is also a need to 
control strategy risk or drawdowns, an imperative for many portfolios.  
To visualise that these are competing objectives in practice, Exhibit 19 shows the return 
and risk profiles of multi-asset income sources outlined in Exhibit 18. While strategies 
like high dividend stocks or REITs can offer significantly positive price returns, they also 
exhibit high tail risk and offer among the lowest yields. Similarly, while Overwriting and 
Autocall strategies offer higher yields, they are at risk of long run capital erosion 
(negative price return) and can experience material tail risk. While classic fixed income 
inhabits the middle ground here, it too is at the mercy of the same tradeoffs, which 
Exhibit 20 summarises. Indeed, no single strategy dominates all 3 objectives of high 
income, capital preservation and low tail risk. 
Important note: In our analysis, we use gross yields for different income 
strategies and (later) gross total returns so as not to take a view on taxation, 
which is dependent on a variety of factors including tax jurisdiction, investor type, 
nature of distributions and other factors that are beyond the scope of this report. 
Furthermore, we do not include management fees which can vary across asset 
classes, derivatives securities and strategies. We acknowledge that the inclusion 
of tax considerations and fees may alter the relative attractiveness of different 
income sources for any given investor.
