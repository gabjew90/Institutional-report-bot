36 States elect Governors but only a handful move stocks 
We think the 2026 cycle matters for utility equities in far fewer states than the headline 
count suggests. Where it does matter, the impact runs through commission 
appointments and data center policy rather than through partisan control. We would lean 
into names where we think the market is overpricing election risk: CMS and DTE in 
Michigan, EVRG in Kansas, LNT in Iowa and Wisconsin, and PNW in Arizona, all Buy rated. 
We also favor names in states where the large load framework was built ahead of the 
load: BKH in Wyoming, XEL in Minnesota and Colorado, and IDA in Idaho. On Texas 
exposure (SRE through Oncor, VST, CNP, AEP), we would note risks of wider outcome 
ranges than the market consensus. 
Five States matter most 
GA, OH, OR, PA, and TX. Texas is the most consequential. The incumbent has ordered a 
load audit, ERCOT has paused Batch Zero studies, and TCEQ has halted data center 
permits, against a 474 GW interconnection queue that is roughly 90% data centers. We 
think the Texas backdrop is decided in 1H27, not on November 3. In Georgia, a statewide 
pause is the largest single candidate risk to SO via potential Democratic victories for 
Governor and at the PSC. In Ohio, both candidates back conditional moratoriums state-
wide and there are 21 data center ballot measures at the jurisdiction level. In 
Pennsylvania, the regulatory construct review matters more than the ballot, unless the 
state Senate flips. In Oregon, wildfire policy drives POR and AVA, and both candidates 
are calling for moratorium. 
Economic development pitch to campaign liability 
Pause and moratorium proposals now appear in 20 of the 36 races. We read most of 
them as sequencing rather than opposition. After the election, we expect capital to sort 
rather than stop, flowing toward Wyoming, Minnesota, Iowa, Wisconsin, Kansas, Nevada, 
South Carolina and Tennessee, pending electoral outcomes.  Outside of the TCEQ review 
in Texas, we expect current projects to be grandfathered even if pauses or moratoriums 
are enacted. 
Low probability surprises would create tail risks 
Upsets would be low probability surprises in AL (Doug Jones victory), AR (Fred Love 
victory) and OK (Cyndi Munson victory). In each of these states the expected outcome 
preserves current policy, so an upset would be a real policy shift. Alabama carries the 
most potential exposure to an upset, because HB 475 gives the governor a Secretary of 
Energy who directs the PSC agenda. 
Three dates to watch in TX in addition to November 3 
November 9: Texas prefiling opens for the 90th Legislature. We expect at least 60 data 
center bills, with the $3.3bn exemption under review. December 10: ERCOT reports its 
Batch Zero verification audit covering 66.4 GW of conditional base load and 127.9 GW of 
studied load. Q1/Q2 2027: Final Batch Zero allocation is nominally due.

 Midterms Preview: Executive Summary 
Our view is that the 2026 gubernatorial cycle is a story about regulators and data center 
rules, not party control. Thirty-six states elect governors. In most of them the stock 
consequence runs through three channels: who the winner appoints to the commission 
and when, whether the large load framework is already written, and how far campaign 
positions on data centers can travel once they meet a Legislature, a statute or an 
elected commission. We sort the states into four groups below. 
States that matter most: GA, OH, OR, PA, TX Texas is the single most consequential 
election for utilities and power this cycle in our opinion. The next governor inherits a 474 
GW interconnection queue, roughly 90% of it data centers and more than five times the 
all-time system peak. The incumbent's posture has already shifted. Since August 3, he 
has directed the PUCT and ERCOT to audit every data center in interconnection, ERCOT 
has paused Batch Zero and halted energization approvals for loads of 75 MW or more, 
and on September 21 TCEQ was ordered to halt all data center permits.  
The PUCT has approved Import Paths 1 and 3 of the $33bn Permian Basin Reliability 
Plan but remanded Import Path 2, and legislative leadership has called for overhauling 
the CCN process. The $3.3bn data center tax exemption, including already authorized 
projects, is under review heading into the 90th session. We think these processes now 
carry momentum independent of the winner, and we would carry wider volatility margins 
than the market for SRE (Oncor), CNP, AEP and VST.  
Georgia pairs the most restrictive major candidate position in a leading data center 
state, a temporary statewide pause, with two of five PSC seats on the ballot that could 
flip commission control. That combination is the key risk to SO in our view. In Ohio, both 
candidates back conditional moratoriums and 21 local data center measures are on the 
ballot. We would not underwrite growth for AEP and FE until rules settle, and would tie 
fundamentals to reshoring and transmission. In Pennsylvania, we think the PUC review of 
ROEs, the DSIC and large load ratemaking matters more than the election for PPL, EXC 
and FE. In Oregon, both candidates support a pause, and wildfire mitigation and recovery 
remain the defining valuation issue for POR and AVA, in our opinion. 
States where we think the market may be overpricing election or moratorium risk: AZ, 
FL, IA, KS, MI, WI. In these states we see durable frameworks that limit how far 
campaign rhetoric can travel. Michigan's candidates both call for a pause, but legislative 
work on rules is advanced, and we expect any moratorium to be brief and less damaging 
than feared for CMS and DTE. In Kansas, a Holscher moratorium would face a Republican 
supermajority, and HB 2527 and executed ESAs protect EVRG. In Iowa, the Democratic 
eight-point plan largely codifies what large customers already mostly meet, and the cost 
allocation model remains constructive for LNT. In Wisconsin, neither candidate supports 
a moratorium, and existing large sites likely already satisfy a lot of what is proposed for 
WEC, LNT and MGEE. In Florida, an ROE cap would require legislation, and commission 
turnover is slow, which limits risk to NEE and DUK. In Arizona, Republicans retain ACC 
control regardless of outcome for PNW. 
States with little election risk and solid constructs: CO, ID, MN, OK, SC, WY; plus LA, MO, 
and KY (which don’t have Gubernatorial elections this cycle).  Wyoming requires large 
load customers to fund their infrastructure, pairs that with a 9.75% ROE and 52% equity 
ratio, and supports our model of roughly 3.1 GW online by 2031 for BKH. Minnesota's 
2025 law already sets a very large customer class with no cost shift for XEL. Colorado is 
resolving the tariff at the commission in Proceeding 26AL-0137E. Idaho remains a very 
constructive backdrop for IDA. South Carolina's legislature, not its governor, elects the 
PSC, and the binding constraint is supply rather than siting for DUK. Oklahoma's 2026 
Ratepayer Protection Act requires large users to fund infrastructure and commit to ten 
years of service. Among states without a governor's race, Louisiana has two of five PSC 
seats on the ballot with Republicans holding a 3-2 majority (ETR), and we view Missouri

(AEE, EVRG) and Kentucky (PPL) as stable. West Virginia has no election catalyst, and 
there could be meaningful progress toward GenCo structures. 
Where an upset would be a genuine surprise: AL, AR, OK. In all three states the market 
assumes continuity, and a different result, while the probabilities are low, would be a 
significant policy shift. Alabama has the most exposure. HB 475 expands the PSC to 
seven members and creates a Secretary of Energy who serves at the governor's 
pleasure, sets the commission agenda and can open rate hearings. Jones backs a one-

the base case carries risk for SO. In Arkansas, an appointed APSC with one seat open in 
2027 and Act 548 incentives in statute would slow any change for ETR. In Oklahoma, an 
elected OCC and the Ratepayer Protection Act would confine a Munson moratorium to 
siting and pace rather than ratemaking for OGE and AEP. 
In most other states we would underwrite continuity, or an investment case driven by 
grid modernization, electrification and reliability rather than large load. That includes NY, 
where a moratorium is already in place by executive order, and NH, VT, RI, MA, MD, CT, 
ME and HI. Nevada stands out as a state where the next governor appoints all three 
PUCN seats by mid 2028, and there is a constructive data center and large load 
backdrop. 
There are also 35 ballot initiatives regarding data centers to watch on November 3. 
There are no statewide data center measures on the ballot this November. All 35 
initiatives, which we list in Exhibit 1 are municipal, township or county measures, and 
three states account for nearly all of them: 21 in Ohio, 7 in Illinois and 3 in Kansas. 
About half would prohibit or cap facilities, often at 25 MW, while several Illinois advisory 
questions point the other way, as do six in Ohio. We read the outcomes as a gauge of 
local acceptance rather than a change in state policy, and we think they reinforce our 
view that siting risk sits mainly at the county and municipal level.

We think the 2026 Alabama gubernatorial election matters for utilities because the next 
governor will have unusually direct influence over the state's regulatory framework 
beginning in January 2027. HB 475 expands the Alabama Public Service Commission 
from three members to seven members, creating substantial turnover and a materially 
different commission structure. Governor Kay Ivey has already appointed four new 
commissioners effective January 18, 2027, who will join Commission President Cynthia 
Almond and the two commissioners elected in November. At the same time, HB 475 
creates a cabinet level Secretary of Energy effective January 19, 2027. The secretary will 
be appointed by and serve at the pleasure of the governor, direct commission activities, 
control personnel and administrative functions, set meeting agendas, and may initiate 
formal rate hearings and determine their scope.  
As a result, investors should view the gubernatorial election not simply as a race for the 
executive branch but as a contest that will shape both PSC composition and the 
regulatory agenda through the governor's choice of energy secretary. The available 
campaign material also indicates utility affordability could become a focal issue. 
Republican nominee Tommy Tuberville has publicly called for a four-year freeze on 
electricity rates, however, Southern Company subsidiary Alabama Power already has an 
existing rate freeze in place. 
The clearest policy divide between the candidates is on data centers. (see:  Tommy 
Tuberville outlines new data center approach as opposition to facilities mounts | 
Alabama Reflector, Tommy Tuberville calls for 4-year freeze on electricity rates in 
Alabama - al.com, Doug Jones addresses data centers, healthcare in central Alabama 
swing | Alabama Reflector). Democratic nominee Doug Jones has called for a one-year 
moratorium on data center development, arguing the state needs guardrails, 
transparency, and community protections before additional projects move forward. Jones 
has repeatedly highlighted concerns surrounding Project Red Clay in Lowndes County, 
criticized what he characterized as a lack of public transparency, and stated that 
communities should know what is being proposed before projects are effectively 
finalized.  
Republican nominee Tommy Tuberville has moved toward a more conditional support 
framework rather than unconditional promotion of data centers. Tuberville has said local 
communities should have power to reject projects, that data centers should pay the full 
cost of substations, transmission lines, water infrastructure and replacement capacity 
required to serve them, and that they should be placed in Alabama's highest property tax 
bracket. He has framed the issue around ensuring that data centers cover the full cost of 
the resources they consume while preserving local control over siting decisions.  
For utilities, the election therefore represents a choice between a candidate seeking a 
temporary pause to establish statewide rules and a candidate seeking continued 
development subject to stronger cost allocation, local approval, and tax requirements. 
That distinction could shape the pace, location, and economics of future hyperscale load 
growth in Alabama.

We think the 2026 Arizona election matters for utilities because it will occur alongside 
two Arizona Corporation Commission seats that are on the ballot, with affordability, 
resource planning and large load growth emerging as central issues. Republicans will 
retain control of the commission regardless of the November outcome, although two

The election also carries a potential appointment dimension. Ralph Heap remains on the 
ballot while campaign finance complaints and Clean Elections scrutiny remain 
unresolved. The allegations do not themselves establish ineligibility, but if Heap were 
elected and subsequently unable to take or retain office, Arizona law provides that the 
resulting vacancy would be filled by the governor with a Republican appointee. As a 
result, investors should focus not only on the election outcome itself, but also on how 
the resulting commission approaches affordability, integrated resource planning, and the 
allocation of costs associated with large new loads. 
Data centers (see: Attorney General Kris Mayes, Warren Petersen sharply disagree on

would watch the Attorney General’s race more than the Governor’s race particularly 
given Attorney General Kris Mayes prior experience as a utility commissioner on the 
ACC. In a debate over Arizona's data center strategy, the Attorney General argued for a 
temporary pause on new data center development, citing concerns about Colorado River 
water supplies, pending utility rate increases, and the need for a more formal approval

should determine whether projects are appropriate and that data centers should be 
responsible for their own power and water needs.

bears the associated infrastructure, power supply, water, and transmission costs.

R.26-04-009 would establish advanced electric rate design policies to update residential

accurately reflect the cost of providing service, send price signals that support efficient 
use of grid infrastructure, and allocate costs fairly across all customers. This rulemaking

Demand Flexibility Rulemaking. Senate Bill 57 requires the Commission to provide an 
assessment by January 1, 2027, on the potential cost impacts from new, large 
transmission-connected loads from data centers on electrical corporations and their 
customers. In coordination with the Senate Bill 57 assessment, this proceeding will 
consider rate design issues for data centers and other large load customers.  The January 
1, 2027 report will be the new Governor’s first look at large load impacts in the state. 
A second rulemaking opened September 3 will consider updated requirements for

The audit now has its own clock, and those dates matter more to 2027 load than the 
election result. ERCOT issued conditional Batch Zero classifications on September 3, 
with 204 projects totaling 66.4 GW conditionally classified as base load and 158 projects 
totaling 127.9 GW as studied load. Base load locks the requested MW and exempts the 
project from system wide reallocation, while studied load remains subject to allocation 
and may be approved for less than requested. Those classifications are conditional on 
clearing the verification audit, which ERCOT must report by December 10, 2026, with 
developer information due October 12.  
Final study results, meaning actual megawatt allocation to studied loads, carry a nominal 
timeline of spring 2027, though ERCOT has indicated the long-term load forecast and 
reliability study will slip past that date. We would treat December 10 and Q1/Q2 2027 as 
the real catalysts. Bloomberg has estimated roughly 20% of the national 253 GW data 
center pipeline, close to 50 GW, is at risk from the Texas pause, with up to $8bn in 
revenue implications by the first quarter of 2027.

become genuinely unsettled. The PUCT approved the first two lines on August 28, 
roughly 424 miles at a combined cost near $3.95bn, forming Import Path 1 of the $33bn

We think the combination of a contested transmission framework, an active load audit, a

Consensus holds that Abbott reverts to a pro data center stance after a win, and that 
may prove correct on rhetoric, but the audit, the CCN remands, and the legislative docket

clearest secular demand growth story in the sector, but we would carry materially wider

Our view is that Wyoming is one of the more interesting utility states in the 2026 cycle 
because the governor appoints the Public Service Commission and will oversee the 
state's response to rapidly changing electricity markets, transmission development, and 
evolving resource policy. Consistency is the buzzword.  
Our Wyoming investor trip found unusually strong alignment across the governor's 
office, legislators, local officials, and the state energy authority, with the debate 
centered not on whether to accommodate large loads but on building the regulatory, 
legislative, and ratepayer protection framework to advance development quickly. On data 
centers, current outgoing Governor Mark Gordon signed an Executive Order “Data 
Centers the Wyoming Way” (see: News Release: 06-03-2026 | Governor Mark Gordon).   
The outgoing governor also emphasized continued structural reform alongside an all of 
the above strategy spanning coal, nuclear, natural gas, and renewables, and we think 
preserving that continuity through the transition matters more to the investment case 
than the campaign itself. We would watch the legislature for any potential action and 
whether the incoming administration carries that work forward on the same timeline. 
Republican Candidate Eric Barlow noted that data centers aren’t coming they’re already 
here (in the state).  We are positive and think the opportunity in Wyoming is larger than 
it looks. Cheyenne LEADS identifies 10 facilities operating, 5 under construction, and 10 
planned, and our site visits showed active construction, campuses nearing completion, 
and substantial acreage for additional phases.  
We would caution against extrapolating localized opposition around Cheyenne to the 
entire state. Wyoming runs roughly six people per square mile against a national average 
near 93, Cheyenne itself covers 32 square miles of a 97,089 square mile state, and the 
state's 96 municipalities occupy just 0.3% of the land mass, leaving roughly 93,000 
square miles outside the city municipalities and the state and national park land.  Of this 
approximately 14,000 square miles in National Forest, and about 27,000 square miles of 
this is Federal land under the Bureau of Land Management. It is important to note in this 
context that Federal Executive Order 14318 directs agencies to use federal land for data 
centers of 100 MW and up. At least 12 proposals covering more than 17,500 BLM acres 
have been filed in six western states, and Wyoming is one of them.  For BKH, the LPCS 
tariff was built around customer generation, so on-site supply that remains grid 
connected is part of the earnings construct rather than a bypass risk. 
Water, the usual constraint elsewhere, has been addressed on the facts rather than the 
rhetoric: the state encourages and pushes for closed loop cooling rather than once 
through or evaporative designs, which recirculate the initial charge for years, and 
Cheyenne's overall water use has been declining. We think that fact-based approach has 
neutralized the single most common source of opposition nationally and is a meaningful 
part of why development continues to move. 
The investable consequence is that the tariff construct, not the load itself, is what 
converts demand into durable earnings. Wyoming requires large load customers to fund 
the infrastructure needed to serve them, insulating existing ratepayers from incremental 
cost, and pairs that with a 9.75% allowed ROE and a 52% equity layer. Our framework 
models roughly 3.1 GW online by 2031, which we view as only a fraction of the ultimate 
opportunity given the development footprint we observed. The binding constraint is 
transmission, fiber, and generation adequate to serve very large loads rather than 
political appetite, and given the small starting base even a modest number of projects 
would have an outsized effect on statewide demand.  
For investors, Wyoming is the clearest example in this cycle of a state where the 
regulatory framework was built ahead of the load rather than in reaction to it, and we 
would expect that sequencing to hold under the next administration.
