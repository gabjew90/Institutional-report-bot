Thought of the day
European government bonds stabilized on Friday after a volatile week amid 
concern over inflation and public finances in the region. French 10-year 
borrowing costs reached their highest level since 2002 on Thursday, while the 
yield spread over German government bonds widened to above 150 basis 
points on Friday, its highest level since late 2011. The widening gap between 
French debt and German Bunds, traditionally viewed as among the region’s 
safest sovereign assets, reflected investors’ increasing focus on France’s fiscal 
outlook. Eurozone headline inflation also rose to 3.8% year over year in 
September from 3.2% in August, with higher energy prices accounting for 
much of the increase. Against this backdrop, the euro extended losses and 
fell to its lowest levels against the US dollar since May 2025.
The combination of inflation above the European Central Bank’s target, 
high public borrowing, and widening sovereign spreads points to continued 
market volatility. But we do not believe these developments signal an 
imminent funding crisis or remove the investment opportunities in Europe.
The latest inflation data call for ECB vigilance, rather than an 
aggressive or extended tightening cycle. Although Eurozone headline 
inflation rose more than expected in September, core inflation increased only 
modestly, to 2.5% from 2.4%. Energy inflation accelerated to 18.8% from 
14.3%, confirming that the headline increase was driven primarily by higher 
energy prices. There is still limited evidence that these costs are becoming 
embedded in wages or generating broad second-round effects. ECB President 
Christine Lagarde said that second-round effects remained absent and that 
a “measured response” is appropriate. We expect one further 25-basis-point 
rate increase in December, taking the deposit rate to 2.75%, rather than the

Fiscal risks are not pointing to an imminent crisis, and higher yields 
increase emerging opportunities in fixed income. The concerns on 
borrowing are real. France faces a structural deficit, a rising debt burden, 
and a growing interest bill. Debt-service costs are projected to increase from 
around 4.3% of government revenue currently to more than 6% by 2030, 
making fiscal consolidation progressively harder. Italy’s prospective borrowing 
path also deserves attention, with the government expecting the deficit to 
rise to 3.4% of GDP in 2027 after falling to 2.9% in 2026. However, we think 
these pressures are likely to build gradually rather than produce an imminent 
loss of market access. France’s latest debt sale attracted solid demand, with 
around EUR 12 billion of securities between two and three times subscribed, 
while the region’s institutional framework provides tools to limit disorderly 
fragmentation. We therefore see a case for selectivity within European fixed 
income, not abandonment of the asset class.
The region’s growth outlook remains supportive despite higher 
energy and borrowing costs. Bundesbank President Joachim Nagel said 
Germany could grow by around 1% this year, roughly twice the central bank’s 
June forecast of 0.5%, supported by robust export demand and government 
investment. France expects growth of 1% in 2027, while recent upgrades 
to Italy’s forecasts add to evidence that activity across the region is proving 
more resilient than anticipated. Our latest forecasts call for Eurozone GDP 
growth of 0.8% in 2026 and 1.2% in 2027. For equities, what matters is why 
rates are rising: When tighter policy accompanies resilient activity, earnings 
growth can offset the effect of higher discount rates. We continue to expect 
Eurozone earnings growth of 15% in both 2026 and 2027.
So, we remain Attractive on fixed income and see the rise in European 
yields as creating selective opportunities in high-quality bonds. We continue 
to prefer short- to medium-term maturities and stronger investment grade 
issuers, while remaining cautious on the longest maturities and keeping 
higher-risk credit relatively short-dated. Within France, we see attractive risk-
reward in select agency, covered, and corporate bonds. We also remain 
positive on Eurozone equities, supported by an improving earnings cycle, 
resilient economic activity, structural growth drivers, and still-reasonable 
valuations. On the euro, the decline in EURUSD in recent weeks has now 
reached a point at which the risks appear increasingly skewed toward a 
recovery. With the US dollar trading near the upper end of recent ranges 
and euro risk premiums elevated, we believe current levels offer an attractive 
opportunity to gradually build euro exposure.
Caught our attention
US payrolls cool. US nonfarm payrolls rose by 29,000 in September, below 
expectations for a 90,000 increase, while gains for July and August were 
revised down by a combined 60,000. But the three-month average increase 
at 51,000 remains broadly in line with estimates of the pace needed to 
keep unemployment stable. The unemployment rate edged up to 4.2% as 
more people entered the workforce, with the participation rate increased 
to 61.8%. Average hourly earnings growth moderated to 3.0% year over 
year, although aggregate private wage income remained relatively healthy 
at 4.2%. In response, market expectations for further tightening from the 
Federal Reserve declined, leaving 25 basis points of tightening priced by year-
end.
Our view: The latest data point to a labor market that is keeping pace 
with population growth, but is not so strong as to generate significant

Brazil first round shifts fiscal reform expectations. Senator Flávio 
Bolsonaro outperformed expectations in the first round of Brazil's presidential 
election, winning around 47.2% of the vote versus 44.9% for incumbent 
President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. As expected, neither candidate secured 
an outright majority, sending the race to a runoff on 25 October. Still, 
the challenger performed significantly better than pre-election polls had 
indicated, and right- and center-right parties strengthened their position in 
Congress, improving the prospects for fiscal and structural reforms should 
Bolsonaro ultimately win the presidency. The vote comes as Brazil faces 
elevated public debt, slowing growth, and ongoing debate over the country's 
fiscal framework.
Our view: We expect a positive market reaction when Brazilian 
markets reopen, reflecting Bolsonaro's stronger-than-expected first-round 
performance. The election focus will now shift to voters backing smaller 
candidates, who collectively captured nearly 8% of valid ballots, and could 
play a decisive role in the second round. On balance, the result increases 
the likelihood of a more market-friendly fiscal outcome, including a credible 
spending framework, reforms to mandatory expenditures, and measures to 
stabilize public debt. Beyond an initial risk-on rally, we believe domestically 
driven equities, local bonds, and the Brazilian real should continue to benefit 
as markets price a higher probability of fiscal consolidation. While Brazilian 
equities have risen in recent days, valuations remain below historical averages 
and investor positioning remains relatively light, leaving scope for further 
gains if reform expectations strengthen. Lower fiscal risk premiums could also 
support a gradual decline in local bond yields. We continue to rate emerging 
market equities as Attractive, and focus on select Brazil and broader Latin 
America equities that can offer exposure to commodities, infrastructure, and 
domestic cyclicals alongside still attractive valuations.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
