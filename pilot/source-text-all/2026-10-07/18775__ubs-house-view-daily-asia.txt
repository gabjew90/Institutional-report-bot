Thought of the day
US government bonds ended a volatile week close to recent highs as 
investors weighed competing signals on growth, inflation, and monetary 
policy. On Thursday, the 10-year Treasury yield climbed to 5.34%, its 
highest intraday level since 2002, after manufacturing activity remained 
firmly in expansion and initial unemployment claims fell to a 10-week low. 
The yield subsequently reversed course as senior Fed officials indicated that 
policymakers were not in a hurry to raise rates again. Treasury markets 
remained volatile on Friday following a weaker-than-expected employment 
report.
The swings illustrate the difficulty investors face when judging the 
outlook for rates. Resilient growth and renewed input-cost pressure could 
keep yields volatile. But we think the broader employment picture, the 
underlying inflation trend, and a more patient Fed should enable equities to 
withstand moderately higher rates.
The broader body of employment evidence remains consistent with 
a solid labor market despite considerable monthly fluctuations.
Nonfarm payrolls rose by 29,000 in September, below consensus 
expectations for a 90,000 increase, while the gains for July and August 
were revised down by a combined 60,000. But payroll growth has been 
particularly volatile recently, and the three-month average increase 
stands at 51,000, broadly in line with estimates of the pace needed to 
keep unemployment stable as the working-age population expands. 
The unemployment rate edged up to 4.2% as more people entered 
the workforce, with the participation rate increased to 61.8%. Initial 
unemployment claims also recently fell to a 10-week low, with no broad 
rise in layoffs. Overall, the data remain consistent with a resilient labor 
market that is keeping pace with population growth, but is not so strong as 
to generate significant wage pressure.

Recent data and Fed commentary point to a more patient approach 
to further tightening over the past week. Vice Chair Philip Jefferson 
said policymakers would need to reach their own judgment on the 
appropriate path for interest rates, “which may take more time,” while 
Governor Michelle Bowman said she did not “currently see an urgent 
need for further action.” John Williams offered a similar message, saying 
that “there is no need for urgency, and we have time to gather more 
information.” The softer employment report further reduced market 
expectations for an October rate hike, although investors continued to see 
a strong likelihood of an increase by December. Our base case remains for 
the Fed to raise rates once more in December and then keep them steady 
in 2027, rather than delivering the extended tightening cycle previously 
reflected in market pricing.
So, we expect resilient economic growth and robust earnings to support 
further equity gains over the next six to 12 months. Higher yields become 
more concerning for stocks when economic growth begins to falter; for 
now, the broader activity and employment evidence remains consistent 
with an economy on solid footing. We forecast S&P 500 earnings growth 
of 25% this year and 14% in 2027, which should help equities absorb 
moderately higher interest rates. We recommend positioning for further 
equity gains through broad sector and regional diversification, while 
managing concentration risk.
Caught our attention
Debt and inflation worries keep European fixed income volatile.
European government bonds stabilized on Friday after a volatile week 
marked by concerns over inflation and public finances. French 10-year 
borrowing costs reached their highest level since 2002 on Thursday, while 
the yield spread over German government bonds widened above 150 basis 
points on Friday, its widest since late 2011. Eurozone headline inflation also 
rose more than expected, reaching 3.8% year over year in September from 
3.2% in August, driven primarily by higher energy costs. Core inflation 
increased more modestly, to 2.5% from 2.4%.
Our view: Fiscal pressures and elevated inflation are likely to keep European 
bond markets volatile, reinforcing the need for selectivity. France faces 
a structural deficit, rising debt, and growing interest costs, while Italy’s 
large debt stock leaves its public finances sensitive to higher yields. But 
orderly financing conditions do not point to an imminent funding crisis. 
France’s latest EUR 12 billion debt sale attracted solid demand, and higher 
yields are already tightening financial conditions. Meanwhile, the modest 
increase in core inflation suggests that higher energy costs have not yet 
generated broad second-round effects, supporting a measured rather 
than extended European Central Bank (ECB) tightening cycle. We remain

Euro declines. The single currency has fallen to its weakest level since May 
2025, with EURUSD trading as low as 1.1214 on Friday amid broad US 
dollar strength. EURUSD fell around 1% last week and more than 3% since 
the start of September. French fiscal concerns (see text above) have added 
to pressure on the euro, alongside elevated US Treasury yields, higher 
energy prices, and market fears that the US could limit diesel exports. 
Notably, EURUSD has weakened even as markets have eased back on 
expectations for additional near-term Fed tightening, suggesting investors 
are increasingly focused on risks within Europe. Currency markets will next 
look to US payrolls data for further direction.
Our view: The decline in EURUSD in recent weeks has now reached a point 
at which the risks appear increasingly skewed toward a recovery. With 
the US dollar trading near the upper end of recent ranges and euro risk 
premiums elevated, we believe current levels offer an attractive opportunity 
to gradually build euro exposure. France's fiscal challenges could continue 
to weigh on sentiment in the near term, but we see limited evidence 
of an imminent funding crisis and believe a credible fiscal consolidation 
path could help restore confidence. We continue to expect EURUSD to 
recover toward 1.15-1.20 over the medium term and favor selling EURUSD 
downside below 1.12 over a one-month horizon.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
