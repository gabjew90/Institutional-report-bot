Global Daily 
Market comment 
Q3 went out with a bang. US Treasury 2-year yields were little changed on the day at 4.89% but 
10s spiked 5bps to 5.30% and 30s +6bps to 5.63%, the highest since June 2002. French 30s were 
+9bps to 5.46% with Le Spread over Bunds now 152bps. As the Financial Times talks of a “vicious 
loop” of selling, Bill Gross states, ‘Don't own bonds and be cautious with stocks’, while the Wall 
Street Journal counters ‘Money Managers are asking clients to give the bond market another 
chance’. That’ll arguably be down to how Cold and Hot War things get: another phase shift higher 
in US 30s and we’d be in the 7.25-8.00% range of the late Cold War period. 
Q4 also starts with a bang: Israel’s own 9-11 was stopped by heroic passengers and the pilot 
preventing the Omani co-pilot from crashing the plane. PM Netanyahu has said, “We’ll find out” 
whether Iran was involved. The UK PM and Trump also believe Iran was involved in the recent RAF 
Fairford security incident, the latter stating, “We are seriously considering a response.” Yet as the 
US closed its final Iraqi base built after long yields were last this high, Iran is seen eyeing that 
power vacuum. Elsewhere, the Saudis have said they won’t compromise on security against 
Houthi “chaos”, implying escalation, and there are reports that Eritrean forces may have crossed 
into Ethiopia, who is already accusing Egypt and Sudan of working against it.   
NATO Secretary General Rutte reassured “There is no immediate threat to NATO territory,” but 
Ukraine reports new Russian steel-cutting drones that destroy pylons, causing power outages in 
three regions. Politico also reports ‘Ukraine’s war chest is running low at the worst possible 
moment’, with a massive funding gap as European governments look at soaring bond yields. 
The UK PM is suggesting a second Brexit referendum, where rejoining would have to be from 
scratch on far less favourable terms; yet Macron is at 18% in the polls and Merz just 13%, leaving 
it unclear whom the UK might one day be talking to and about what. Reflecting how fluid lines on 
maps are --and, as warned, so are lines on screens-- could the UK join Canada given they already 
share the same head of state? The picture gets messier with Parti Quebecois currently tipped to 
win the 5 October Quebec election on the promise of another independence bid and Alberta 
holding a secession vote referendum on 19 October. For now, Canada is settling for a new 
partnership with the EU over firefighting, which seems appropriate, and critical minerals. 
Where will those minerals be (filthily) processed, what will the common subsidy and external 
tariff be to ensure they remain competitive vs. China’s cheaper imports? Don’t tariffs need to be 
downstream too to prevent finished goods using Chinese rare earths pushing out EU and 
Canadian goods with local rare earths, making the whole exercise pointless? That’s to say nothing 
of Dutch intelligence warning no secret conversations should be held around an EV as everything 
is recorded. 
Doesn’t this sound like what Trump is doing but without military muscle to support it? 
Relatedly, the US ambassador to the EU just attacked its ‘Buy European’ weapons push as “self-
interest” (by definition it is on both fronts) that could disrupt the transatlantic defence industrial 
base and benefit Europe’s enemies. Canada’s former defence chief says the country should hold 
on to the military relationship with the US. Yet PM Carney, who just flagged the world’s longest 
peaceful open border seeing fighting like the Taliban, is whispered by some to be in the 
running for the Nobel Peace Prize and Time Magazine’s Man of the Year.

Meanwhile, Secretary of War Hegseth extolled the US military, declared ‘No beardos, no 
weirdos, no wimps,” invited governors to bid for “next great American base”, and called in 
Musk and Luckey to put cutting-edge tech in US weapons, including ‘AutoWarCom' for AI and 
drone capabilities by October 2027 – a date geostrategists know carries potential significance. 
The US is also using that kind of pledged muscle to get economic benefits. Trump just unveiled 
$200bn of South Korean FDI into the US as part of his new trade deal. Seoul is hedging on the 
$53bn Alaska LNG pipeline but will build 8 nuclear power plants, with the first in Texas. Yet that’s 
as a Congressional Republican committee chief urged “heightened vigilance” over South Korea-
China ties. Sides are going to have to be taken. 
The EU is also now playing the trade game differently: it’s reported Brussels is linking greater EU 
market access for Thai canned tuna to the fish being caught by EU, not Thai, fleets. What about 
China though? How long until the trade war or peace call is made? 
Central banks don’t get to sit this out. They are in the hottest of hot seats: 
In Europe, PM Meloni is reported to be imploring EC President von der Leyen to let countries 
overspend to battle the current energy crunch, which the ECB will likely watch closely for its 
inflation impact, if so. 
Former Fed Chair and current FOMC voter Powell was cleared of criminal misconduct over 
$2.5bn renovations of the Fed building, but the report found “deficiencies” in his project 
leadership: is that OK for such a key role and large budget? Apparently, it is in the financial press, 
but Trump is calling on him to resign as a result.  
The BOJ doesn’t want to keep raising rates rapidly, the September meeting notes showing some 
hawks but others pointing to weak private consumption and warning against hasty action, as 
government representatives urged weighing the cumulative impact of past rate increases. 
However, with the latest Tankan survey the most upbeat for large manufacturers since 2018 the 
Bank may not have a choice – assuming it is the one choosing, not Bessent. 
Former UK PM Truss gave an interview again claiming she was forced out of office by a liberal 
deep state that includes the Bank of England, which she’d threatened to reform. One can 
support that view, deny it, or ignore it because ‘this is the UK’ not a banana republic - and the UK 
certainly doesn’t grow that fruit or have a president. However, the banana skin for default thinking 
is that central banks are the key pillar of the political economy. If the populace desires higher 
growth, or ‘fairer’ growth, or enough growth to provide national security then the current role of 
central banks would logically need to change. Yet central banks, like any bureaucratic institution, 
would resist that from self-interest or ideology, both wrapped in technocratic speech and actions 
such as the timing of rate or QE/QT decisions, etc. 
Elsewhere, Joe Rogan interviewed a guest who claimed he’d seen many kinds of alien 
specimens held by the US government, including “Greek gods”, “Grays,” and “Reptillians.” He 
stated the US is covering this up not due to concerns about public panic but rather about federal 
contract fraud and sole-source awards to those who reverse-engineered alien tech and sold it to 
the Pentagon(!)  
Monopolies, corruption, and bureaucratic cover-ups taking precedence over the existential and 
theological revelation of non-human intelligence sounds believable and Trump has kept 
posting hints around this topic. If true, it changes what we understand about ourselves and the 
world. Whether true or not, hints of alien technologies in US hands should be taken within the 
Cold War context detailed above as attempts to intimidate others into a deal. How long do we 
have to wait for the financial press to pivot from the unthinkable idea that all-too human 
central banks might be political(!) and instead focus on how aliens are “good for markets”?
