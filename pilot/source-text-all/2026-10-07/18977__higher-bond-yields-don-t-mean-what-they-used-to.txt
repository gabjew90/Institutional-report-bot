 
Higher Bond Yields Don't Mean What They Used To 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 05, 2026 - 05:25 PM 
Authored by Vassilis Karamanis, Bloomberg FX and Rates strategist, 
The historic surge in global bond yields isn’t merely about price discovery, but also about 
correlation discovery. That makes yesterday’s playbook increasingly unreliable. 
The norm is for investors to build models around relationships that appear theoretically and 
empirically dependable. For instance, stronger US growth means higher relative yields and a 
stronger dollar, while being generally bad for non-yielding assets. Risk-on means one thing for 
FX, risk-off another. 
A look at the euro is telling. A model of daily moves versus the dollar based on changes in 
US-German yields, French sovereign spreads, relative US-European equity performance, bond 
volatility and oil explained about 18% of the pair’s variation in the 2010. Apply those same 
coefficients unchanged to 2025 and 2026 and the out-of-sample r-squared falls sharply 
negative. 
In other words, the old equation performs worse than a simple benchmark. But the model 
worked in 2023 and 2024 before breaking down, which suggests markets may be switching 
between correlation regimes rather than simply abandoning the old ones. 

 
 
Part of the explanation lies in why yields are rising. Higher yields would normally offer a 
currency some support if they reflect stronger growth, inflation or tighter monetary policy. But the 
latest rise in French and Italian borrowing costs increasingly reflects fiscal and sovereign-risk 
concerns. That can therefore generate the opposite FX response: instead of attracting capital, it 
raises the compensation investors demand to hold European assets and can push money 
toward havens. 
The UK learned a version of this during the 2022 gilt crisis and could eventually face the same 
distinction once again. Japan offers another example of how unstable the old playbook can 
become: higher domestic yields do not necessarily translate mechanically into a stronger yen 
when capital flows, policy credibility and global risk appetite are pulling in different directions. 
The US Treasury market may be undergoing a similar shift. Higher yields have traditionally been 
associated with stronger growth, tighter Federal Reserve policy and attractive returns on dollar 
assets. But yields can also rise because supply is overwhelming demand, offshore reserve 
managers are less willing to absorb issuance, or investors are demanding a greater fiscal and 
term premium. 

 
 
This is crucial because the Treasury market increasingly relies on price-sensitive and, in some 
cases, leveraged buyers. If repo funding becomes scarcer or more expensive, hedge funds may 
reduce basis and relative-value trades just as the government needs buyers most. Instead of 
higher yields automatically stabilizing demand, a selloff can raise volatility and funding costs, 
forcing some marginal buyers to retreat and requiring still higher yields to clear the market. 
That in turn creates an unusual policy risk. If rising yields eventually threaten Treasury-market 
functioning, the Fed could be forced to provide liquidity or expand its balance sheet even while 
inflation prevents it from cutting interest rates aggressively. Balance-sheet expansion would not 
necessarily mean traditional QE, but the possibility of renewed intervention changes what a rise 
in long-term yields tells investors. 
Yet fiscal risk is only half the dollar story. The debate around US exceptionalism has mostly 
focused on relative economic growth: if growth outside the US catches up, one of the dollar’s 
major advantages fades. But what if growth exceptionalism is being replaced by AI 
exceptionalism? 
Global capital does not necessarily need superior US GDP growth to keep flowing into America. 
If the US keeps capturing a disproportionate share of AI investment, corporate capex, 
productivity gains and equity returns, foreign demand for US assets — dollars included — can 
remain strong even as traditional growth differentials narrow. 

 
In that regime, the dollar may trade increasingly off US technology earnings, capital expenditure 
and equity inflows rather than simply rate spreads. That would be a different relationship, not 
merely a different coefficient. 
None of this means history or quantitative models are useless. It means models built on stable 
coefficients become less reliable when the underlying transmission mechanism changes. In 
practice, that means conditioning on the driver. For the euro, that argues for watching sovereign 
spreads as closely as the level of Bund yields. 
The task for investors therefore changes. In a structural transition, the first job is no longer 
simply to estimate the coefficient, but to identify what the relationship has actually become. 
Decades of data on horses would have been of little use in predicting the economic impact of 
cars.
