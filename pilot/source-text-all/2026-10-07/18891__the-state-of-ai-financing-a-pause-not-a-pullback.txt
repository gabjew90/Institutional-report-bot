The State of AI Financing: A 
Pause, Not a Pullback
September marked the second-lowest month of AI-related 
supply this year, with only  ~$23bn globally. In our view, this 
slowdown is temporary and driven by a combination of 
significant front-loading of issuance in the year and building 
political/regulatory constraints. We expect a busier fall and 4Q, 
as the focus shifts to 2027 capex.

Key Takeaways
AI issuance underwhelmed in September, in line with our expectations. However, 
this does not reflect weakening fundamentals or shortage of capital to fund the 
buildout. In our view, September's supply slowdown is due to a combination of 1) 
significant front-loading of issuance that happened in previous months, especially 
over the summer, 2) emerging signs of regulatory/political challenges to new 
data center construction, and 3) the rates sell-off possibly requiring project-level 
renegotiations for data-center deals.
In US IG, we think the macro backdrop - and not elevated supply - is the biggest 
test between now and year-end, with outflows expected on the back of a sharp 
drawdown in returns. We expect at least one more deal in the US market, and 
note that HQ hyperscalers continue to tap non-USD currencies in between their 
larger US deals earlier this year. HQ hyperscalers should remain largely 
insensitive to higher rates, and we expect another wave of volatility into the fall, 
especially around 3Q26 earnings and likely further upward capex revisions. 
Lev Fin was active in September, but we expect 4Q26 to look more like 1Q/3Q, 
with a slower pace of deals given higher rates and regulatory/political limitations. 
Lower-quality issuers and project-level debt are more sensitive to the current 
rate environment, in our view, but issuers might be able to negotiate better lease 
economics with tenants to improve their returns.  
A handful of new securitized products deals came to market in September after a 
pause in August, bringing with them wider spreads and structural innovations. A 
floating-rate ABS deal and partial amortization in CMBS reflect the evolution the 
market is undergoing to attract incremental demand as the cost of debt for 
sponsors surges. We expect spreads to continue to leak wider from here, and 
reiterate our preference for colocation over hyperscaler data center ABS. Raising 
interest rate volatility could adversely affect the economics of data center 
securitization.  
For Single Name, despite lighter September issuance, off-cycle earnings and 
headline flow continue to drive elevated investor inbound. Both ORCL and NVDA, 
the latter a new addition to our coverage, remain central to these discussions, for 
their evolving funding strategies and their contribution to heightened lease 
documentation scrutiny.

supply in the upcoming quarter. 
Collateral-backed paper still has better risk-reward than unsecured corporates, 
in our view. Within the universe of collateralized assets (~30% of AI-related debt), 
construction risk on data center paper in the IG/HY markets has been a significant 
driver of spread volatility on the back of  headlines about  permit and power delays, 
as well as the risk of lease reassignment and contract uncertainties (ORCL's Force 
Majeure episode). Stabilized assets in ABS/CMBS are therefore more insulated from 
such negative volatility. As for chip financing, we think the nature of the assets 
allows for faster cash flow generation and amortization, but there is still only a 
handful of public deals that would allow investors to express this preference. 
Despite better performance last month from the hyperscalers, we expect near-
term volatility amid rising rates/outflows going into the midterm elections and 
likely another leg of upward capex revision at 3Q26 earnings. Looking past 4Q26, 
however, we think much of the hyperscaler decompression trade may have played 
out: issuance cadence is becoming more (although not completely) predictable, 
capex  is shifting to shorter-dated assets (like chips), and continued evidence of 
strong returns on AI capex underscore that the fundamental story behind the 
spending remains robust.

US Investment Grade Credit: Macro Poses a Bigger Test 
Than Elevated Supply  
There was no AI-related issuance in the US IG market during the month of September. 
Following  a busy summer, we were expecting a slowdown in near-term supply, considering 
how much had been done already and issuers' efforts to  better establish cadence. 
Importantly, we think it's now fairly well understood that higher capex next year will likely 
come with higher issuance. On the other hand, the macro backdrop has become more 
challenging, with rates selling off sharply and a more hawkish Fed path priced in. 
Therefore, thinking through the upcoming weeks,  the macro environment will likely 
be the bigger test for corporate credit markets, while issuance might take a backseat. 
In our view, the credit market at large should be able to handle the sell-off in yields. 
With nominal growth running at an impressive 6%+ and earnings growth even better, the 
rate move has a strong fundamental underpinning and as such, we expect credit quality to 
hold up really well. Importantly, AI-related capex has been a large anchor for US growth, 
and our economists see it being relatively insensitive to the level of yields, at least locally. 
Notably, our economists' rate path is more dovish than consensus, calling for two more 
rate hikes (vs. market pricing that is closer to four). 
Technical factors will likely dominate in the near term, especially around investor 
outflows. With IG down 3% in total return on the year and 4% on the quarter, the 
drawdown has reached a level where mutual funds/ETFs could see meaningful 
redemptions. But it is worth remembering that over the past five years, the credit market 
has seen multiple bouts of rate repricing, as the 10Y has gone from 1% to over 5%. While 
the rate-hiking cycle of 2021/2022 created a sustained drag on spreads and outflows, that 
was a case of much higher inflation and the Fed being behind the curve. Today it appears 
to be a case of inflation not coming down fast enough, coupled with robust growth driving 
a modest reset higher in policy rates. 
When it comes to debt-financing future capex, we expect high-quality hyperscalers to 
remain largely insensitive to higher rates, given strong visibility into attractive returns 
on invested capital  (for those interested in returns math, we recommend slides 22-29 in 
this deck) and debt still proving to be a cheaper funding source relative to equity. While 
the equity market is not rewarding the 'capex factor' to the same extent as earlier in the 
year, our equity analysts expect strong capex growth next year, supported by continued 
demand for compute, incrementally expanding inference workloads, and enterprise 
adoption.
Near term we expect continued choppiness  going into the midterm elections and 3Q26 
earnings, with arguably another leg of upward capex revision (consensus is still at ~$1-
1.1tn for 2027 combined hyperscaler capex, MSe is at ~$1.4tn). As for the lower-rated 
cohorts (see also Lev Fin section), we  expect more sensitivity to higher cost of capital, and 
continue to see more spread volatility on headlines around construction delays and 
political pushback to build-outs. Finally, we think relative value will play a part for the BBB 
cohort given TMT-related supply outside of the AI players (~$35bn of BBB TMT debt has 
been issued since August).

Looking ahead beyond 4Q26, however, we think that much of the hyperscaler 
decompression trade has played out.  While a persistent basis to the broader index is 
warranted on elevated absolute and relative supply, spreads have widened a fair amount 
(similar to banks in the past). There is more (albeit not perfect) clarity on the cadence and 
frequency of issuance. Our analysts' estimates for ROIC also remain robust, with more 
evidence of that coming through over the next year.
We also think that in 2027, capex will shift toward components such as chips, where 
financing is more tailored to short duration/asset-level/private capital. That doesn’t 
mean less IG issuance per se, but potentially a  smaller  y/y increase than we saw this year. 
Additionally, as rates stabilize, yield-based investors will likely continue to remain an 
anchor (despite low spreads) due to the moves on the Treasury benchmark. The story is 
arguably different for the big semiconductor names, in our view, as we've gotten little 
clarity so far on the magnitude and format of chip financing structures. A lot of the chip 
financing remains ahead of us, and what form it will take/which markets and investor 
pockets will be involved are all unknowns for now (see the recent credit initiation on 
NVDA for more thoughts).

derivatives market. Liquidity across hyperscaler CDS have improved meaningfully since

hedging  to manage counterparty exposure (even in a benign scenario in which AI 
investments are profitable and therefore investments continue). Key risks to this trade: 
lower issuance vs. estimates, as well as faster-than-expected monetization results in 
stronger fundamentals, could result in CDS spreads tightening back.

Collateral-Backed Risk in The IG Market
We reiterate our preference for collateral-backed risk  over unsecured corporate risk. 
For data center-backed paper, this is driven by our view that, in addition to the long-term 
demand-supply imbalance for compute,  near-term asset value might also benefit from 
incremental challenges to start new construction and secure power (please refer to our 
thematic strategists' work on the 3 Ps: people, power, and politics). Once construction is 
completed, we expect the differential between data center paper and tenants' unsecured 
bonds to compress. We favor deals with high-quality tenants, residual value 
guarantees, full amortization through the life of the notes/lease, and documents that 
1) require rent commencement once shells are delivered (regardless of power 
availability), and 2) offer adequate protections or bondholder participation in case of a 
lease reassignment.

Investment Grade Reverse Yankee: Hyperscalers Continue to 
Tap Non-USD Markets  
Hyperscalers Diversified Their Funding Base And Tapped 
Numerous Currencies This Year
While September saw a lull in AI-debt supply overall, non-USD hyperscaler issuance has 
continued to broaden. This month saw a £4.25bn four-part GBP deal from AMZN, only a 
few weeks after GOOGL's debut A$5.5bn six-parter in August. The recent deals bring 
total non-USD hyperscaler supply to $72bn: nearly a third of global hyperscaler 
supply this year. We maintain our expectations for further non-USD issuance from 
hyperscalers, with recent media reporting also pointing to potential EUR deals in the 
pipeline for 4Q. In contrast to 2025 when hyperscalers only tapped USD and EUR 
markets, less than 40% of non-USD supply this year has come in EUR. So far, issuers have 
diversified their funding towards five other currencies, including CAD, GBP, CHF, AUD, and 
JPY ( Exhibit 43 ).
Given their relatively large deal sizes ( Exhibit 45 and Exhibit 46 ), these issuers have 
already become some of the largest issuers in many of the local IG benchmarks ( Exhibit 
44 ). However, since only two issuers (GOOGL and AMZN) have utilised the non-USD 
markets so far this year, sector concentration remains less pronounced in other markets, 
particularly EUR, AUD and GBP.

Reverse Yankee Spread Performance
The performance of hyperscaler new issues this year has been mixed, with many deals 
seeing spread widening in the first 20 days of trading ( Exhibit 47 ). Since July, hyperscaler 
new issue performance has been relatively more benign, mirroring the better relative 
performance of these issuers' secondary bonds. Even so, hyperscaler EUR bonds still trade 
wide to the AA benchmark, with AMZN trading closer to the A-rated index ( Exhibit 48 ). 
The underperformance remains particularly pronounced in the long-end, with these 
issuers' curves screening steep compared to the overall market ( Exhibit 49 ).
In the APAC LC space, GOOGL's AUD bonds spreads were tighter post issuance in August, 
outperforming the AUD LC corp bond market ( Exhibit 51 ). In contrast, GOOGL's JPY 
bonds saw spread widening in the past two months, significantly underperforming the JPY 
LC corp bond market ( Exhibit 52 ). 
On a cross-currency adjusted spread basis, EUR hyperscaler bonds still trade modestly 
tight to their USD bonds, though the differential has narrowed since late August ( Exhibit 
50 ).

Leveraged Finance: Corporate Issuance Drove Supply Last 
Month
AI-related leveraged finance issuance continues to grow and is now at ~$63bn, with 
HY issuance exceeding $50bn YTD and leveraged loans reaching over $10bn. Loan 
issuance slowed in September following a busier July and August, with just one $509mn 
GPU financing reaching the market. Within HY, AI exposure rose from 3% to 4% of the HY 
index during September; we saw two data center-backed deals and one unsecured 
corporate deal in the form of  a record $10bn USD corporate HY offering, alongside $1.14bn 
equivalent in EUR. Despite this jumbo unsecured issuance, AI-related debt in leveraged 
finance remains predominantly secured/project-finance-style (~70%).

temporary challenge to supply (on top of increasing political pushback and challenges 
related to power). Therefore, we  expect 4Q supply to be more similar to 1Q26/3Q26 than 
to 2Q26 ( Exhibit 57 ). 
Collateral-backed paper in the HY market trades inside of unsecured paper: there is a 
ratings differential given the benefit of asset value for recovery, and investors can 
now choose between chips and shells as collateral for certain HY issuers ( Exhibit 54 ). 
Given the different nature of the assets, chips offer a faster path to monetization vs. 
powered shells. We expect more clarity over the next few months on how credit support 
from higher-quality semiconductor names could play a part in accelerating chip financing 
supply in the HY and leveraged loan markets. With regard to data center bonds, we favor  
deals with high-quality tenants or IG credit support, lower LTC, and some deleveraging 
through amortization before refi, and  documents that 1) require rent commencement once 
shells are delivered (regardless of power availability), and 2) offer adequate protections or 
bondholder participation in case of a lease reassignment. Headline risk around 
construction delays could continue to drive bouts of volatility.
Similar to what we wrote earlier for IG, the HY market is also facing headwinds from the 
magnitude and velocity of the rate sell-off. HY yields are now at +8.3%, and total returns 
are approaching negative territory. Demand has been choppy, with HY seeing outflows in 
four out of the past five weeks. Macro uncertainty has played out alongside a heavy

Securitized Credit: Issuance into a Higher Rate Environment
After a relatively quiet summer, issuance is starting to pick up again in securitized 
credit, albeit slowly. Two ABS deals and one CMBS deal priced in September ($1.5bn in 
ABS and $375mm in CMBS). This comes as the 5y Treasury crossed 5%, leaving cost of 
debt more than 100bps higher than at the start of the year for data center ABS. 
Spreads are weaker coming out of the summer months even as the pace of supply 
remains slightly low, moving in sympathy at last with the broader weakness in AI and 
data-center related financings in other pockets of the market. We continue to think risks 
skew to the downside for securitized credit data centers, particularly given our 
expectation that the market will continue to need to absorb the elevated supply through 
the end of the decade. 
Two notable structural developments in the securitized credit space last month are also 
worth highlighting: in ABS, the first floating-rate ABS tranche was issued on September 
30, a departure from the otherwise fixed-only asset class. In CMBS, partial amortization 
was proposed in an upcoming deal.  Moreover, Vantage priced the lowest-LTV AAA tranche 
that we have seen thus far in ABS, at ~25%. We think these modifications reflect both the 
sponsor's need to crowd in more demand to contain spreads and alter the structure to 
accommodate the higher rate environment.
We recently modified our original supply forecasts to account for the higher rate 
environment, while taking into account expectations for significant new leased 
capacity additions through 2029. We brought our gross issuance expectation for 2026 
down to $25-30bn with ~$17bn in ABS and ~$10bn in CMBS (down from $30-35bn). For 
2027, we lowered our forecast to $40-50bn, down from our previous estimate of ~$55bn. 
For 2028, we hold our forecast steady at $55-65bn. In total, that means we expect $120-
$145bn of gross issuance across ABS and CMBS through 2028 (inclusive of 2026).
See our latest note for more thoughts on how the higher rate environment affects supply 
and the financing of stabilized assets: 
Securitized Products & US Credit Strategy: Data Center Financing: Funding Stabilized 
Assets in a Higher Rate Environment (24 Sep 2026)

financing structures, ecosystem support, and supply-demand dynamics. Despite lighter 
September issuance, off-cycle earnings and headline flow continue to drive elevated 
investor inbound. NVDA and ORCL, both within our current coverage, remain central to 
these discussions, from a funding and lease documentation perspective:
• NVDA – funding update: We initiated NVDA credit coverage with a neutral view. 
Exceptional growth has turned the balance sheet into a strategic financing tool, 
but the potential tail risks of what we call "balance sheet-as-a-service" remain 
early-stage, opaque, and significant. The FY2Q27 print reinforced an expanding 
support toolkit that now includes shell lease guarantees, revenue-share/contract 
price-floor support, and potential RVS structures tied to the announced $500bn 
partnership.  A key debate remains how markets absorb ~$1tn guided GPU/XPU-
related financing, including the structures, ratings treatment, and investor bases.
• NVDA – lease documentation development: With its FY2Q27 earnings, NVDA's 
disclosure of plans to reassign ~$20bn of future shell leases intensified investor 
scrutiny of powered-shell documentation.
• ORCL – funding update: ORCL completed its $20bn equity ATM program in 
FY1Q27. We forecast sizable bond issuance in early CY27 and CY28. Customer 
prepays have emerged as a meaningful funding source, albeit with limited 
disclosure and associated rollover sensitivity. Future equity needs likely depend on 
prepay success and cost inflation. Lease disclosures imply  a ~1GW shell lease 
signing during the quarter. We look for greater clarity on long-term funding, capex, 
and build-out plans at the October Analyst Day.
• ORCL – lease documentation development: Bloomberg reported on September

though Oracle stated on X the project remains on schedule, and such notices are 
commonplace tools used to preserve contractual rights. The surprising 
development  prompted this response across a few Research teams.
• ORCL – construction financing link: Given heightened attention on Stargate sites, 
we highlight ~$65bn of short-dated bank and private-credit construction loans 
funding shells to be leased by ORCL, based on company disclosures and  
Bloomberg news articles including one dated 4/24/26. The ~$65bn figure sits 
outside the public IG and HY construction debt discussed elsewhere in this note by 
our Strategy colleagues and, accordingly, excludes RDMICH.

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)
Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including 
standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages 
in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, 
as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment 
speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the 
MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even 
though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any 
premium paid.
