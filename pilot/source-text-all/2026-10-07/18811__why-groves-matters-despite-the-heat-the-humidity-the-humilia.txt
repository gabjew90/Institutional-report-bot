Why Groves matters despite the heat, the humidity,
the humiliation
It was hot - thick with that humidity that leaves you instantly thirsty - yet we endured.
And naturally, I managed the sticky situation with characteristic grace, taking a tumble
down a set of stairs, thoroughly humbling myself before the gathered management
team. Steps 1, pride 0.
So why brave the heat, the humidity, the humiliation to see Oklo’s new isotope test
reactor humming outside Austin? Was it truly worth it?
It was.
(And yes, there they were: golden Cybercabs gliding past like shiny trophies).
Groves is proof of something essential to the Oklo story: that management can actually
build the thing – quickly and differently. It broke ground on the proof that Oklo can defy
an industry long paralyzed by glacial timelines and eye-watering budgets.
We live, so many of us, in the abstractions of spreadsheets and digital models. But
pouring concrete and bending steel has a way of teaching lessons no simulation can
offer; reality is a tough, indispensable teacher. Groves matters there as well; Oklo is
leaving the whiteboard behind- and looking to leverage lessons into future isotope,
recycling and power builds.
It is also the opening chapter of leveraging the neutron. By harnessing fission neutrons
to produce critical isotopes, Oklo isn't looking to just generating power - it is supplying
modern medicine and shoring up national security.
And Groves serves as an initial signpost that management can walk into a town, meet
a community whose reflexive instinct is to say no to nuclear, look them in the eye, and
bring them along to yes.
What's in a name? First, why the name Groves? Because Lieutenant General Leslie
R. Groves Jr. made the nuclear age a reality. As director of the Manhattan Project, he
marshaled the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to build America’s first industrial-scale
nuclear footprint at breakneck speed - giving rise to Hanford and Oak Ridge.
Proving that they could build - quickly and differently. The US nuclear industry
has long accepted a painful premise. Projects take a decade. Budgets inevitably balloon.
In Lockhart, Texas, Oklo ran a direct counter-experiment. On August 12, 2025, the
Department of Energy (DOE) selected the team for its Reactor Pilot Program. Just 229
days later, the Groves-1 Isotope Test Reactor achieved substantial completion. The core
reached first criticality on August 5, 2026.
Other pilot developers chose fabric tents or leased old government test cells at Idaho
National Laboratory (INL). Oklo chose permanent civil infrastructure on private land.
Oklo crews drove steel pilings directly into open pasture. They poured perimeter
concrete walls before excavating the center. This novel technique carved out a 60-foot-
deep subterranean vault in mere weeks.

Proving they can launch the isotopes segment - and why that matters. A
pilot-scale test facility, the Groves Isotope Test Reactor provides the critical data and
technical groundwork needed to advance commercial isotope reactors.
At their core, isotopes are distinct variants of a single element, existing in either stable
or unstable forms. Oklo's commercial focus centers on the unstable radioisotopes that
release energy as they decay, These isotopes drive four vital sectors: healthcare, space
and defense, industrial applications, and semiconductors, with space and industrial
currently driving the highest volume.
In healthcare, isotopes bind to targeting molecules to either map disease through
diagnostic imaging or destroy it through targeted radiotherapy - pairing these
complementary isotopes on the same targeting platform forms the basis of precision
theranostics. In space and defense, decaying radioisotopes provide indispensable heat
and power where solar energy falls short, from harsh lunar environments to deep-sea
naval operations in critical regions like the Indo-Pacific. Industrially, sealed radiation
sources enable non-destructive testing to expose hidden structural flaws in metal.
Finally, within semiconductors, tailored isotopes power essential processes like neutron
transmutation doping and silicon enrichment for high-performance EVs.
Groves targets a critical bottleneck in modern oncology. Targeted radioligand therapies,
including Novartis’s Pluvicto and Lutathera, link precision-targeting molecules to
unstable radioisotope payloads. These complexes bind to cancer cell receptors and
enter the cell. Once inside, localized beta or alpha emissions shred tumor DNA while
sparing distant healthy tissue.
The core challenge is radioactive decay. Therapeutic isotopes like Lutetium-177 and
Actinium-225 decay continuously. They lose roughly one percent of their radioactivity
every two to three hours. Unlike conventional biologics, their shelf lives cannot be
extended. Clinicians have only a few days to administer a dose before it becomes
subtherapeutic.
Today's supply chain is fragile. Raw materials are irradiated in power or research
reactors. Next, trucks haul the material across borders for extraction. Cargo flights
then carry isotopes internationally for chemical purification. Finally, additional flights
return the purified materials to regional compounding hubs. A single flight delay or
reactor outage ruins the product. Patients often arrive at infusion clinics only to find
their treatments spoiled in transit.
Oklo is attempting to vertically integrating the radioisotope supply chain into a single
facility, slashing the handoffs, decay losses, and logistical friction that plague traditional
four-site models.
Rather than shuffling materials across reactors, processing plants, and finishing sites
via complex road and air transit, Oklo plans to consolidate target irradiation, hot-
cell extraction, and chemical purification under one roof. This single-site approach
eliminates international transit for precursor materials, preserving isotope potency
and protecting patient access. By anchoring its efforts in core nuclear capabilities,
Oklo plans to produce customer-grade isotopes directly, partnering downstream with
established providers only for final sterile manufacturing and clinical distribution.

What is enrichment, irradiation, and separation? Isotopic enrichment changes an
element’s isotopic composition, increasing the percentage of a desired isotope within a
target material. This target can then be irradiated and transmuted into the final isotope
of interest. However, at this stage, the desired isotope may be intermixed with residual
target material and other unwanted isotopes. Various chemical separation techniques
are then used to isolate the desired radioisotope at high purity.
• Enrichment is often the first step in isotope production. Many radioisotopes
require an enriched target material before irradiation. The target isotope is enriched
to increase production yield and purity, after which it is transmuted.
• Transmutation occurs through neutron irradiation to create a new isotope
or element. Transmutation is a process in which the number of protons in the
nucleus of an atom changes, creating a different isotope or, in some cases, a new
element. Transmutation occurs when an atom is exposed to neutrons from a nuclear
reactor or particle accelerator. In isotope production, this process is commonly
referred to as irradiation. Irradiation uses neutron capture reactions to transform a
target material into a desired radioisotope.
• Radiochemical separation techniques are often required to isolate useful
radioisotopes from irradiated targets. Following irradiation, the desired
radioisotope may remain mixed with residual target material, impurities, and other
activated isotopes. Chemical separation techniques are therefore required to isolate
the final product.

How do we quantify isotope production performance? The production rate of
isotopes in a neutron reaction is determined by the flux of the neutrons hitting the
target, the capture cross-section of the target nucleus, and the quantity of raw target
material present. Higher neutron flux, larger capture cross-sections, and greater
quantities of target material enable faster and more abundant radioisotopic production.
• What is neutron flux?(Φ) Neutron flux, according to the NRC, is the rate at which
neutrons pass through an area, usually measured in neutrons per square centimeter
per second. It helps determine how many neutron interactions can occur, which
affects isotope production in nuclear systems. The higher the neutron flux, the faster
radioisotopic production is.
• What is capture cross-section?(σ) It is the target size to an incoming neutron.
Cross-sections depend on neutron energy and are a lot larger at low energies than at
high energies.
Fission creates slow neutrons that have bigger capture cross-sections. The slow,
dense neutron cloud created by fission allows targeted materials to bake and absorb
neutrons with an enhanced capture cross-section.
• Target atoms (N). The amount of target materials you are trying to bombard
with neutrons, the more target atoms absorbing neutrons, the higher the isotopic
production rate. Fission reactors have large cores where you can pack big batches of
target material which is better for mass production of radioisotopes.
Source: Canaccord Genuity, NIH, IAEA
Fission vs. fusion isotope production
Why is radioisotope production different between fusion and fission? Beyond
neutron energy levels and flux, there are many factors to consider in producing
radioisotopes. Energy levels still effect the processes described below and contribute to
why fusion and fission vary in isotope production. Reasons for the variances and other
factors to consider are:
• Capture cross-section: As we previously discussed,how big does the target
materials nuclei “target size” look to an incoming neutron at that specific energy?
• Resonance peaks: Nuclei within the target material need to absorb neutrons to
become a useful radioisotope. These nuclei do not absorb neutrons at all energy
levels, in fact, every nuclei has multiple distinct energy level ranges where its
willingness to absorb a neutron spikes exponentially. This energy range is called the
nuclei’s resonance peak. If a neutron hits the target at its precise resonance energy,
capture is more likely.
• Scatter vs. absorption: When a neutron hits a nucleus, it either gets absorbed
(transmuting the atom) or bounces off (scattering). High scattering means neutrons
waste energy bouncing, generating excess heat rather than creating the desired
radioisotopes.
• Half-life stability : Once a neutron is absorbed, the new radioisotope begins to
decay. Every radioisotope has a different half-life and thus a different amount of time
it is usable.
Fission reactors excel at thermal capture by slowing neutrons down to ideal
absorbing speeds.Fission produces neutrons that can be slowed, using a moderator
to low thermal energies where target nuclei exhibit their largest target size(cross-
section). The larger cross-section maximizes absorption. When neutrons match these
specific speeds or hit narrow resonance peaks, capture is more likely. However, because
thermal capture often leaves the product as the same chemical element as the target,
separating the product from the target requires complex physical isotopic enrichment.

• Fission reactors create high-flux, slow neutron clouds to bake targeted
isotopes. When U-235 splits, it immediately ejects 2-3 neutrons. These neutrons
are often moderated by water or other materials to increase the likelihood of neutron
capture by target materials.
• Slower neutrons spend more time near the target elements nucleus,

activating target materials with dense, high flux neutron clouds. Generally, fission
isotopes are used for medical imaging, industrial radiography, and sterilization.

Fusion reactors release ultra-high-energy neutrons that unlock unique nuclear
reactions. Instead of slow thermal capture, fusion releases monoenergetic 14.1 MeV
neutrons that have enough energy to trigger reactions that fission neutrons cannot
reach. Because these high-energy impacts alter the nucleus by ejecting protons, the
target transmutes into an entirely different chemical element, allowing for easy, carrier-
free extraction of high-purity radioisotopes.
• Fusion reactions yield ultra-fast, high-flux neutrons to bombard targeted
atoms. D-T fusion produces neutrons with 14.1 MeV of energy, ~7x more than
fission neutrons. Because fusion neutrons are not typically moderated, they travel
at ~17% the speed of light and interact differently with target materials. As neutron
energy increases, the cross-section shrinks, making some nuclei more difficult to hit.
Fusion systems compensate for this by producing extremely intense neutron fluxes,
increasing the total number of nuclear interactions.
• High-energy fusion neutrons can also knock neutrons or protons out of
target nuclei. This can change both the isotope and—in some cases—the element
itself. These additional reaction pathways may enable production of specialized
medical and industrial isotopes, support targeted cancer therapies, and potentially
contribute to future nuclear waste transmutation applications.
Source: Canaccord Genuity, IAEA, Avalanche Energy, Shine Technologies, CANDU

Risks to achieving Target Price / Valuation:
Oklo - OKLO
• Regulatory/sentiment risk. Regulatory changes/ application denials could materially impact deployments and subsequent power
sales. Oklo currently does not have NRC approval and therefore is not permitted to operate a commercial Aurora Powerhouse plant.
• Costs. FOAK plants could face cost overruns and NOAK plant cost savings may not materialize to the degree expected.
• HALEU fuel. HALEU fuel is currently not permitted for commercial use in the US. If indefinitely prohibited, Oklo will have to
redesign the reactor core – if possible – to burn LEU fuel, sacrificing reactor core efficiencies.
• Reprocessing may not materialize. Oklo’s business model economics are substantially exposed to HALEU fuel costs. Over the long-
term management plans to substantially reduce costs by reprocessing spent fuel – a process that is currently prohibited in the US
on a commercial scale. If reprocessing does not materialize and laser-based enrichment does not proliferate, HALEU costs may not
decrease as anticipated, and Oklo’s economic viability could be threatened.
• Technology. Oklo’s technology leverages previous reactor designs, promising enhanced safety. Despite operating history, the NRC
may not approve Oklo’s streamlined design. Moreover, Oklo’s technology and licensing regime could fail to deliver on its promises
at scale.
• AI driver may not materialize. Nuclear’s rapid resurgence has been driven – at least in part – by onerous AI power demands amidst
an already vulnerable US power grid. If AI power demand wanes, or commits to an alternative power source, Oklo’s sales could be
materially limited.
