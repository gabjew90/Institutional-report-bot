War-er for longer? 
 
Global Daily 
Market comment 
Monday saw Treasuries slump again, 10s up to 5.31% and 30s to 5.67%, the highest since early 
2002, despite Trump easing red diesel usage limits and deferring related taxes to try to cushion 
the logistics and agri sectors. If we keep going like we have since February, we’ll soon have 20th 
century yields again, a shock for the generation who weren’t born in it and for elders who kept 
telling themselves “Lower for longer” would hold after the geopolitical consensus couldn’t. That’s 
obviously a huge problem for the US but, as usual, it’s proving a bigger problem for others.  
France’s central bank governor warned his country is at risk of being “strangled by interest 
rates” if nothing is done about its public finances. Hundreds of schools closed or burned and 
scores of teachers injured in violent student protests points to what some fear lies ahead if seven 
months from now --an eternity for bond markets-- a new French government embraces austerity. 
While France doesn’t qualify for the ECB’s Term Premium Instrument bond buying for a variety of 
reasons, the Financial Times today runs an op-ed from former ECB member Smaghi arguing that 
at the least ‘Bond turbulence means it’s time for the ECB to put QT on hold’. 
Meanwhile, the US Nasdaq just hit a new record high, again taking us back to the turn of the 
century and its dotcom bubble. That’s another reason we can’t expect yields to fall back easily – at 
least not until the miraculous deflationary impact of AI and SI (also referred to as AGI). Either way, 
SI is now the national security equivalent of the 20th century Manhattan Project and the 19th 
century investment surge into railways and steel. It won’t stop easily. 
Neither will national security spending in general as wars rage on. Trump's top national security 
aides met secretly at Camp David on Friday to discuss Yemen and Iran, following which the 
Yemeni government and Saudis launched a new war to remove the Houthis starting with freeing 
the Bab-el-Mandeb chokepoint. Encouragingly, it’s reported that they have already retaken the 
key port city of Mocha.  
Türkiye and Pakistan also told the Saudis their military Mecca Pact will defend it ahead. If true, 
Iran would be attacking a NATO member and a nuclear power/talks negotiator if it strikes Saudi 
again, as the US approved $2bn in weapons sales to the UAE, Kuwait, and Egypt, who is rumoured 
to be interested in joining the Mecca Pact too. Nearby, Ethiopia’s PM, who just pushed Eritrean-
backed separatist forces out of Tigray province, insists his landlocked country must gain access to 
the Red Sea, via breakaway Somaliland, despite the risk of a broader Horn of Africa conflict 
unfolding. The US is now trying to calm things in neighbouring Sudan before they get worse.  
Meanwhile, Iran insists it still controls Hormuz and has hit 11 tankers in a week, Trump believes 
Iran was responsible for the flydubai attack, which demands a response, and the US pulled its B-1 
bombers from a UK airbase on fears of Ukraine-style Iranian drone attacks the UK authorities 
cannot mitigate (as the opposition Tories talk of a £10bn ‘Britannia Shield’ inspired by Israel’s Iron 
Dome). On the non-kinetic side, the US has again warned banks not to do business with Tehran. 
Ukraine just struck Moscow again with drones but has hit no refineries yet today, though they 
are clearly a target – something Trump is not complaining about again. Moreover, a Turkish grain 
ship sank in the Black Sea after being hit by a drone. Hungary is meanwhile poised to lift its veto 
on Ukraine’s EU membership bid, seeing the bloc move a step closer embroiled in a vicious, 
existential war with a nuclear power. To think that doesn’t have market implications is naïve.

In the Americas, the US Army is testing counter-drone tech at the Mexican border as drug 
cartels’ drone use rises - another DM facing expensive security problems from EM use of cheap 
military tech. Elsewhere, it’s reported that Cuba and the US maintain “channels of communication” 
but there are no negotiations underway, and suspicions remain that the US will move when timing 
is propitious. 
In the face of military fiscal pressure that lifts bond yields, using one’s military to free key 
supply chains is how bond yields can now come down again. That’s very uncomfortable for 
central bankers and rate strategists who need to be talking lines on maps to talk lines on screens 
with any authority. However, it is what it is, as are the messy geopolitics around us. 
Even if one sticks to commerce over conflict the signals point to higher bond yields, albeit as a 
path towards lowering them again for some in a new world order. The New York Times notes 
what was said here yesterday, ‘A Bolsonaro Win Would Nearly Complete Trump’s Latin America 
Map.’  Indeed, the Donroe Doctrine would be close to being Don. Only Canada would remain a 
major hold-out, and there Quebec just voted for a separatist government pledged to a new 
independence referendum, as ‘water-is-wet’ news from Alberta points to Republican aid to 
separatists offering the US access to its water and energy resources.  
In Europe, France’s Macron and Germany’s Merz wrote an open letter to the EC’s von der Leyen 
arguing the EU needs to adopt a trade “kill switch” mechanism vs. imports from China (if not 
named directly) as well as: a new trade diversification instrument; faster, broader trade defence 
probes; sector-wide use of safeguards; urgent targeted measures; rewriting anti-dumping and 
subsidy rules to tackle circumvention; prioritising investigations strategically; push countries with 
undervalued currencies to appreciate them; preparing for retaliation with an inventory of possible 
retaliatory measures; quantifying the impact of EU retaliation on member states and holding 
regular EU coordination sessions, including at EUCO level if necessary; work with FTA partners to 
stop circumvention of EU measures; and targeted “European preference” in strategic sectors and 
tech. 
Let’s be clear here: 
1. This echoes what the US has been doing for a decade and Europe ignored. Indeed, the rise 
in the EU-China bilateral trade deficit is, as predicted, because the US raised tariffs on China. 
2. Will other EU member states agree or back off? Note Spain, which had been moving much 
closer to China, will hold a snap election on November that could return a new coalition more 
open to a confrontational trade stance vs. China.  
3. This is now the trade statecraft equivalent of Europe’s shift from pacifism to rearmament. 
Yet just as questions linger about how much practical rearmament Europe is doing, how well 
coordinated it is, and how it can work without the US, so they on trade given Airbus is 
currently expanding its production facilities in China and it’s unclear how this EU approach 
might work without support from the US, for example vs. a “No US or China” stance. 
4. China will be livid ahead of looming EU-China trade talks – does full-blown trade war now 
loom, and who will help Europe if it is cut off from rare earths, etc? This could prove a ‘with us 
or against us’ geoeconomic moment.  
In short, we are still in a global environment that is ‘war-er for longer’. 
Day ahead 
Today sees second order releases with German factory orders, Eurozone retail sales, and Canadian 
and US trade data.
