Headlines 
 
 
 
 
Odds of an October fed funds rate hike fell throughout the week, as a soft 
September jobs report and a relatively benign inflation reading gave the Fed room 
to be more patient. The economy added just 29k jobs in September versus 
expectations for 90k, while the Fed's preferred inflation gauge has increased at just 
a 2.0% annualized pace over the past three months. 
 
 
A number of speeches from Fed officials last week reinforced this view; they 
generally supported the idea that additional rate hikes could be necessary "later 
this year," but that the Fed would need more time to make that determination. More 
Fed officials will have an opportunity to weigh in this week. 
 
 
Growth appears to have accelerated through the middle part of 2026. Real GDP 
growth in the second quarter was revised up to 2.2%, and it now seems more likely 
than not that growth in the third quarter will exceed 3%. That's impressive given 
that the economy is still facing headwinds from higher interest rates and rising 
energy prices. 
 
 
It's a relatively light data week, with the ISM services report and the University of 
Michigan Consumer Sentiment survey among the highlights. Focus will also be on 
the release of the September FOMC meeting minutes on Wednesday, which could 
provide helpful context around the Fed’s near-term reaction function. Our baseline 
remains for two additional 25bp hikes, one in December and another next March.

Weak September jobs report with 
downward revisions 
The September jobs report capped a busy data week, 
showing that the U.S. economy added just 29k jobs 
versus expectations for 90k, accompanied by 
downward revisions to the prior two months. This 
brought the three-month moving average of job gains 
to 51k, right around where we think it needs to be to 
keep the unemployment rate stable. Private-sector 
job growth was somewhat stronger at 46k in 
September, as weakness in local government hiring 
weighed on overall job gains.

Figure 2: September job growth ranked by sector 
 
 
The unemployment rate was little changed at 4.18% in 
September, up just slightly from 4.14% the month prior. 
We view this as roughly in line with what the Fed 
considers "full employment," and it's worth noting that 
a rebound in labor force participation, rather than 
weaker hiring or an increase in layoffs, primarily drove 
the increase. 
 
Figure 3: Unemployment rate (%) 
 
The more dovish wing of the FOMC will likely take last 
Friday's jobs report as further evidence that they can 
be patient with additional rate hikes; we agree with 
that view. It's clear that the labor market itself is not 
currently a major source of inflationary pressure, even 
if we continue to believe it is heating up after stalling 
out through much of 2025. Wage growth, as 
measured by average hourly earnings, slowed in 
September and remains firmly below pre-pandemic 
trends. 
 
Strong consumer numbers in August, 
upward revisions to income 
The September Personal Income and Outlays report 
offered some encouraging news on both inflation and 
household finances. The Fed's preferred inflation 
gauge, the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) 
price index, increased 0.3% in August, while core PCE 
inflation rose a modest 0.2%. Although both measures 
remain above the Fed's 2% target on a year-over-year 
basis, methodological revisions incorporated into this 
release lowered previously reported inflation readings 
and made the inflation outlook appear somewhat less 
daunting.

At the same time, consumer spending remained 
remarkably strong, rising 0.9% in August, before 
adjustment for inflation, the largest monthly increase 
since March. Just as importantly, substantial upward 
revisions to personal income significantly improved 
the outlook for household finances and savings. The 
stronger income data suggest consumers are on a 
firmer footing than previously believed, supporting 
continued spending growth even in the face of 
elevated interest rates, higher energy prices, and

Fed officials pour cold water on 
October hike 
Market-implied odds of an October fed funds rate 
hike declined throughout last week as several key Fed 
officials implied that, while they expect further hiking 
this year, they don’t plan to rush the decision. NY Fed’s 
John Williams speech was particularly influential after 
he said it may be appropriate to adjust the Fed funds 
rate upward just once “late this year.” We maintain 
our expectation that the committee will deliver only 
one 25 basis point hike this year, in December, 
followed by one more in March. 
 
“If the economy evolves in a manner broadly 
consistent with my forecast, one further upward 
adjustment of the federal funds target range may be 
appropriate late this year to support a timelier return 
of inflation to target.”            
- NY Fed President John Williams 
 
Multiple regional presidents also explicitly described 
the current policy stance as only being “somewhat 
accommodative” or requiring only “modest further 
tightening,” which reinforced the sense that an 
October hike seems unlikely. Both Vice Chairs 
emphasized patience, with Bowman saying she 
doesn’t see an “urgent need for further action” and 
Jefferson saying it “may take more time” for officials 
to make judgement on trends in the data. 
 
More Fed officials will have an opportunity to weigh in 
this week, with Bowman, Collins, Hammack, Kashkari, 
Musalem, Schmid, and Waller all scheduled to make 
public comments or speeches. We’ll be paying 
particular attention to whether officials adopt a more 
cautious tone following the weak September jobs 
report. 
 
Data preview 
The economic data calendar is light this week. The ISM 
Services index, released this morning at 10 a.m., edged 
down from 55.4 to 54.9, but the September reading still 
indicates that the nonmanufacturing sector is 
expanding. 
Markets will pay extra attention to the September 
FOMC meeting minutes, which follow the Fed’s first 
policy rate increase in more than three years and 
policymakers’ indication that an additional hike may

The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index 
is due Friday following three consecutive monthly 
declines, as consumers contend with the ongoing 
conflict in Iran and an extended period of elevated

continue given the lack of a resolution to the Middle

 
-$106.2b. The U.S. total trade deficit likely widened significantly in August, reflecting a significant deterioration from the prior 
month. A sharp increase in the goods trade deficit was the primary driver, while the services surplus likely offered only limited
