Two-Faced 
Trades
Often the label on a risk and the risk itself are not the same thing. So, 
it’s critical that investors avoid confusing form with substance. This 
is especially true when one encounters transactions that have been 
financially engineered to appear one way to certain stakeholders but 
another way to different stakeholders. 
 →
An entire category of transactions occurring across corporate credit, asset-based 
finance and infrastructure have been designed to be simultaneously labeled debt 
and equity depending on which party, and through which lens, one is looking at 
them. Like Janus, the mythical two-faced Roman god, these deals manifest a 
different face depending on the direction from which they are viewed. 
Many naturally recoil from such creative efforts, accusing Wall Street of getting 
“too cute” once again, planting the seeds (and keeping them well fertilized!) for 
the next cycle. As always, both angels and devils are found in the details. Careful 
investigation can either reveal legitimate and compelling economic and relative 
value or the opposite: misalignment of risk, incentives and economics. 
LESSON LEARNED #4: ALIGNMENT IS A TOOL
For us, after answering the questions, "Why is a transaction designed the way it 
is?" and "Who stands to benefit (or lose) from that design?," our focus then shifts 
to determining whether the financial engineering has introduced unintended 
consequences – e.g., new risks and vulnerabilities when evaluated from a different 
axis, perspective or set of scenarios – and most importantly, whether investors 
are being appropriately compensated for such risk. 
What follows is an analysis of a certain genre of these two-faced transactions 
that we’ve seen recently; they are sometimes referred to as “high grade alpha” 
transactions because they offer a yield premium compared to traditional investment 
grade debt. The details of each transaction necessarily vary from one another 
based on the particulars, but those details don’t ultimately change the nature of 
what financial engineering accomplished. 
What we tend to find in these chimera transactions is that each presents resiliency 
and quality, but typically only along a particular axis of risk. Each can also manifest 
vulnerability to orthogonal risks where few (if any) are looking. They illustrate the 
critical importance of discerning substance over form.

IS IT DEBT OR EQUITY? YES.
One version has become especially common: the corporate 
joint venture marketed as an investment grade solution. It is 
an arbitrage of the gap that can exist between (a) accounting 
conventions and (b) ratings criteria.
The approach begins by forming a JV. The operating company 
(the "corporate") contributes hard assets to it: plants, data 
centers, oil-and-gas facilities, or the AI chips now driving data 
center capex. Some of these assets may already be cash-
generating; others may require significant future capex. 
The corporate keeps 20–51% of the JV. The rest goes to a 
sponsor, typically an asset manager with affiliated insurance 
capital. The corporate signs an agreement with the JV 
that functions as an economic “guarantee” or backstop: 
take-or-pay agreements, residual value guarantees, power-
purchase agreements, true-up payments, a call option at a 
predetermined IRR. The critical part is that it is built to act like 
a guarantee without actually being one. An actual guarantee 
would be deemed debt… a designation that must be avoided.
With the backstop in place, the sponsor can now borrow 
against its stake in the JV, typically funding 80–100% (usually 
90%+) of its investment with debt. This debt is typically 
held by its affiliated insurer, serviced by the JV's dividends. 
Because of the corporate guarantee, the debt can be rated 
at, or a notch below (often higher than), the corporate itself. 
From the corporate’s perspective, this structure is an elegant 
capital solution. The JV is treated as equity by its accountants 
and, largely or wholly, also by the rating agencies. Its cost 
of capital lands just above its cost of debt and well below its 
cost of equity. Thus, it has raised equity capital that is priced 
more like debt.
The sponsor sees a mirror image. It has funded most of an 
"equity" investment with investment grade debt (principal, 
interest, maturity, covenants) held by an affiliated insurer. 
In return, it owns the JV's asset risk and a claim on the 
corporate that is effectively debt, but not quite, unsecured, 
typically yielding more than the corporate's bonds.
Notice the symmetry. From a corporate perspective, the 
underlying instrument is engineered to behave like equity, while 
from a sponsor perspective the instrument is engineered to 
behave like debt. Same transaction; opposite labels.

THE ARBITRAGE: RATING CRITERIA
The rating agencies sit on both ends. One set of criteria 
governs when the corporate's obligation counts as equity 
rather than debt. Another governs when the sponsor's back 
leverage can be rated by looking through to the corporate.
The financial engineering satisfies both criteria at once — 
sometimes within the same rating agency — so the corporate 
achieves equity treatment while that same equity is funded 
80–100% with investment grade debt. Even where the JV 
isn't called debt, the agency may still haircut the corporate's 
EBITDA to account for the contractual payments, a subtle 
nod that the "equity" carries a coupon.
For the sponsor's debt to deserve its rating and get repaid, 
four conditions must hold. 
(1)  The corporate must make its contractual payments
(2)  The JV must generate enough cash flow to cover the 
sponsor's principal and interest
(3)  The plumbing (including covenants) in between must keep 
the cash flow unimpeded
(4)  No adverse change in ratings criteria or accounting 
treatment (for either side)
RESIDUAL VALUE: WHEN THE BILL COMES DUE
Alas, any chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Which link 
that is depends on which way the wind is blowing. Take the 
residual value guarantee version where that guarantee is rock 
solid. Assets go into the JV; the corporate leases them back 
and backstops their value: if they must be sold for less than 
an agreed floor, the corporate pays the shortfall. 
That backstop is what lets the sponsor issue its investment 
grade debt up to 100% LTV. For the lenders, that is a 
genuinely good outcome — the secured debt was solid, and 
the credit support held. But that protection is not free, and 
the corporate's own stakeholders pay for it. 
At closing, the guarantee sits off the corporate's balance 
sheet; it is deemed a contingent liability, out-of-the-money, 
and not yet "probable." So corporate leverage ratios and the 
rating are untouched. Its existing lenders and shareholders 
enjoy the benefits of an equity label.
Moody's counts roughly $662 billion of aggregate data 
center leases that Amazon, Meta, Alphabet, Microsoft and 
Oracle have signed but not yet commenced as commitments 
that do not yet appear as debt because the service has not 
been delivered to trigger the liability. That liability is larger 
than the five firms' entire adjusted debt, and their total future 
lease commitments run to nearly a trillion.
The now-familiar structures do the rest. A hyperscaler 
contributes land to a joint venture, keeps a minority stake, 
leases the whole thing back, and books the arrangement off 
its balance sheet. The bonds the venture issues get rated a 
notch or two off the parent because everyone pricing them 
knows who is really standing behind the lease. 
Meta's Hyperion venture with Blue Owl is the cleanest 
example of this: deconsolidated for accounting, wrapped 
with a residual value guarantee that keeps Meta on the 
hook, and financed with single-A rated paper that priced 
accordingly. If the lease is genuinely off the balance sheet, 
why does the bond trade like parent recourse? Because it is... 
or it is supposed to be.

These are risks akin to the winds blowing from a different 
angle. They tend to not be in any conventional model or 
underwriting approach, yet they present a certain loss 
vulnerability that is often either undervalued, ignored or 
missed entirely. 
Under normal, base case scenarios, both of the investments 
(corporate and JV) can appear as perfectly sound 
investments. Each is engineered to be resilient along the risk 
axis for which it was designed: the corporate's leverage or the 
sponsor's credit rating. Each, however, possesses a critically 
important risk factor that sits quietly off to the side.
When that risk triggers, equity becomes debt, or debt 
becomes equity… and the consequences can be very 
material. For insurance balance sheets which are typically 
levered 12:1 (or more), the designation of debt (vs. equity) 
has major consequences from a capital perspective. This is 
especially true where one finds transactions whose primary 
aim is achieving a certain accounting or rating treatment.

fertilizer. You can

We recently ran across this pithy quip from credit investor, 
Simon Mikhailovich: Securitization is like fertilizer. You can 
grow tomatoes or blow up buildings. Despite the graphic 
imagery, he is not wrong. As we wrote in the last edition: If for 
some reason one cannot lift the lid on an investment's risk, 
one simply moves on; paranoia and humility demand it.
