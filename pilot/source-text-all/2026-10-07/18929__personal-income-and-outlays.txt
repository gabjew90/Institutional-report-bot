Strong Growth in Consumer Spending 
in August; Better Numbers on Saving
Headlines 
 
Nominal personal income rose 0.2% in August, with a 0.3% increase in after-
tax income. 
 
Nominal consumer spending rose a strong 0.9% over the month. 
 
The personal consumption expenditures price index rose 0.3% in August, 
with the core PCE price index up 0.2%. 
 
Real after-tax income was flat in August, with real consumer spending up 
0.6%. 
 
The personal saving rate was 4.1% in August, but there was a substantial 
upward revision to the saving rate in prior months. 
 
Overall PCE inflation was 3.4% year-over-year in August, with core PCE 
inflation of 3.0%. There were methodological changes incorporated into 
the PCE price index with this release that reduced measured inflation, but 
it remains well above the Fed’s target. 
 
Real GDP growth was 2.2% in the second quarter, an upward revision from 
1.5%. 
 
PNC’s forecast is for fed funds rate hikes in December and March.
Details
Personal income rose 0.2% in August from July, 
before adjustment for inflation, according to the 
Personal Income and Outlays report from the 
Bureau of Economic Analysis. Nominal disposable 
(after-tax) personal income (DPI) rose 0.3% over the 
month. Wages and salary income, which is 
important for consumer spending, was also up 0.3% 
in August. Nominal after-tax personal income rose 
0.4% in July (revised down from a 0.5% increase).  
Nominal consumer spending rose 0.9% in August, the 
biggest gain since March. Spending on energy 
goods and services jumped 2.5% over the month on 
much higher prices for gasoline and other types of 
energy. But even after taking out energy, consumer 
spending rose 0.8% in August. 
With spending rising much faster than income, the 
personal saving rate—saving as a share of after-tax 
income—was 4.1% in August, down from 4.6% in July.

However, there was a substantial upward revision to 
personal income in 2026 in this release. For example, 
disposable income growth in July 2026 from a year 
earlier was revised up from 4.2% to 4.9%. With a 
much higher level of income, the personal saving 
rate in July was revised up to 4.6% from 3.0%. This 
large upward revision to the saving rate paints a 
much better picture of household finances, reducing 
concerns about overstretched consumers. 
The personal 
consumption 
expenditures price 
index—the Federal Reserve’s preferred inflation 
measure—rose 0.3% in August from July. The core 
index, excluding volatile food and energy prices, 
rose 0.2% over the month. On a year-ago basis 
overall PCE inflation was 3.4% in August, with core 
inflation of 3.0%. Both of these are well above the 
Fed’s 2% objective, and inflation using these 
measures has been above the objective since early 
2021. 
However, there were methodological changes to 
the PCE price index incorporated in this release that 
reduced the measured rate of inflation. Year-over-
year overall PCE inflation in July was 3.4% after the 
revisions, compared to 3.7% before the revisions. 
Similarly core inflation was 3.0% post-revision in July, 
compared to 3.3% pre-revision. Inflation remains 
above the central bank’s target, and the revisions 
don’t mitigate the need for fed funds rate hikes, but 
the Fed’s task isn’t quite as daunting as it previously 
appeared. 
The BEA also released the third estimate for second 
quarter real GDP. Annualized growth in the second 
quarter was revised notably higher, to 2.2%, from 
1.5% in the second estimate. There were upward 
revisions 
to 
business 
investment, 
consumer 
spending, and government spending in the third 
estimate. On a year-over-year basis real GDP 
growth was a solid 2.2% in the second quarter. 
Core GDP—consumer spending, business fixed 
investment, and residential investment—rose a very 
strong 4.7% annualized in the second quarter, 
thanks to big gains in consumer spending and 
business investment.  
The release also included the annual updates to 
GDP. GDP growth in the first quarter of 2026 was 
revised up to 2.5%, from 2.1%. The revisions indicate 
that economic growth in the first half of the year 
was much stronger than previously thought. Solid 
growth 
in 
consumer 
spending 
and 
surging 
investment in AI and other types of tech drove 
economic growth in the first part of 2026, and those 
drivers appear to remain in place in the second half 
of this year. 
Real gross domestic income, an alternative measure 
of the size of the economy based on the income 
going to households and businesses from economic 
activity, increased 2.6% annualized in the second 
quarter, revised higher from 2.2%. Real gross 
domestic income growth was also revised higher in 
the first quarter, to 2.5% from 1.2%. On a year-ago 
basis growth in real gross domestic income was a 
strong 2.7%. 
The economy is in solid shape in the fall of 2026. GDP 
growth was strong in the first half of the year, 
especially after the recent data revisions, and 
consumer spending has continued to expand in the 
third quarter. The upward revision to personal 
income is a positive for near-term growth in 
consumer spending, and higher-income households 
are benefiting from the strong stock market. 
Tech spending has been driving the growth in 
business investment and was a huge contributor to 
second quarter growth. However, higher interest 
rates and concerns about returns on this investment 
are significant downside risks to growth in late 2026 
and 2027. 
The FOMC increased the fed funds rate by 25 basis 
points in mid-September. Given solid economic 
growth and inflation that continues to run well 
above the Fed’s 2% objective, PNC expects 
additional 25 basis point rate hikes at the FOMC’s 
December and March meetings. This would take the 
fed funds rate to a range of 4.25% to 4.50% by early 
spring.
