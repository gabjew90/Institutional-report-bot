Why the US rare earth supply chain
remains vulnerable
The US has significant rare earth resources, but its supply chain remains
heavily reliant on China. The biggest gaps do not sit in the mine, but
rather in processing, heavy rare earth separation and magnet
manufacturing
A rare earths mine in Mountain Pass, California
The US supply chain remains incomplete
Last year we wrote about how China could use its dominance of rare earth supply chains as
political leverage. Although supply pressures eased after the US and China reached a
framework agreement in June 2025, Beijing's subsequent expansion of rare earth export
controls in October highlighted the persistent vulnerability of downstream supply chains.
Headline import dependence tells only part of the story. The US is producing more rare earths,
but it still cannot process enough of that output into the specialised materials and magnets
needed by domestic industry. The main weakness emerges after the ore leaves the mine.

Mining is only the first step
Once ore is mined, it must be concentrated and separated into individual oxides. These are
then converted into metals and alloys before being manufactured into magnets. China
dominates these value-added stages.

Mountain Pass shows both the strength and weakness of the US position. MP Materials
produced a record 50,692 tonnes of rare earth oxide in concentrate in 2025 and expanded
production of separated neodymium-praseodymium oxide. The company also began
manufacturing neodymium-iron-boron magnets in Texas in December 2025.
These are important steps towards a domestic mine-to-magnet supply chain. But one mine,
increasing light rare earth separation and limited magnet production, cannot yet supply the
full range of products needed by US industry.
The US rare earth capacity weakens further down the supply
chain
Source: ING Research
The real choke points are downstream
US vulnerability increases further down the chain. According to the International Energy
Agency (IEA), China accounts for 60% of mined magnet rare earths, 91% of refined output and
94% of permanent magnet production. Two decades ago, it produced only about half of the
world’s permanent magnets.
Mining capacity is growing in the US, but China retains an overwhelming advantage where
most of the value is added. That position was built over decades through state support,
integrated supply chains, technical expertise and proximity to downstream manufacturers.
Together, these factors created economies of scale that are difficult for new entrants to
replicate.
Separating individual rare earths is difficult because their chemical properties are so similar.
Plants are expensive, technically complex and often tailored to a particular ore body and
product mix. Processing can also generate toxic and, in some cases, radioactive waste.

Heavy rare earths are the weakest link
The US gap is widest in heavy rare earths. Dysprosium and terbium are added in small
quantities to neodymium-iron-boron magnets to help them perform at high temperatures.
This makes them particularly important for electric-vehicle motors, aerospace systems and
defence equipment.
US output is concentrated in light rare earths such as neodymium and praseodymium.
Commercial-scale domestic separation of dysprosium and terbium is only beginning.
MP Materials is developing a heavy rare earth separation line at Mountain Pass designed to
produce around 200 tonnes of dysprosium and terbium a year. This would be an important
domestic step, but it will not close the gap on its own. Domestic metallisation and magnet-
making capacity also remain limited.
New separation facilities will also need reliable feedstock. Heavy rare earth production is
concentrated in China and Myanmar, leaving US processors exposed even if domestic
separation expands. Alternative deposits exist, but developing them and connecting them to
metals, alloys and magnet production will take time.
Magnet dependence is the most immediate risk
For many strategic applications, the critical import is not an oxide but a qualified magnet or a
component containing one. High-performance magnets must meet precise requirements for
strength, heat resistance and durability. New suppliers must be tested and certified, making it
difficult to switch during a disruption.
US magnet capacity remains small compared with domestic demand and China’s industrial
base. The IEA estimates that, even after announced projects, diversified magnet output
outside China would meet well below 20% of demand outside China in 2035. The project
pipeline is strongest in mining and weakest in magnets.
Demand is also rising across electric vehicles, wind turbines, robotics, data centres and
defence. This increases the risk that diversified capacity will struggle to keep pace, even if
announced projects proceed.
The defence deadline adds urgency
Rare earths are used in small quantities, but they are difficult to replace in advanced defence
systems. The US Department of Defence estimates an F-35 requires more than 400kg of rare
earth elements, an Arleigh Burke-class destroyer around 2.4 tonnes and a Virginia-class
submarine around 4.2 tonnes.
