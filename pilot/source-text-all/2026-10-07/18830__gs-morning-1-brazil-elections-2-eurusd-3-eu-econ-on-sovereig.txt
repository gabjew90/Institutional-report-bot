1) BRAZIL ECON (Alberto Ramos) - Brazil Elections: Flavio Bolsonaro Outperforms – LINK
BOTTOM LINE: With 99.8% counted from Sunday's (Oct 4) vote — which renewed all 513 Lower House
seats, 54 of 81 Senate seats, 27 governorships and state legislatures — right-wing Senator Flávio
Bolsonaro (PL) took 47.1% of valid votes, more than 4pp above the poll-of-polls, against incumbent Lula
(PT) at 45.1%, sending the presidency to an October 25 runoff. Bolsonaro swept the 3 biggest
battlegrounds: São Paulo 51.9% vs 38.2%, bellwether Minas Gerais 48.3% vs 43.2%, and Rio 53.0% vs
39.4%.
The runoff campaign starts Monday, Oct 5, with free TV/radio airtime running Oct 9–23 — now split
equally, erasing Lula's first-round coalition-based advantage — plus four televised debates. History favors
the first-round leader, leaving the race to hinge on execution, debate performance, mobilization, and
courting the 21.1% who stayed home.

2) FX STRATEGY (Mike Cahill) – Thoughts on EUR.
EUR: Reminds me of the bad old days. The Euro's recent slide on rising fiscal pressures echoed its
behaviour around similar events over the past ~15 years, and three principles explain when and how it
reacts.
First, contagion is the trigger — idiosyncratic stress, especially in smaller member states, barely moves
FX until it infects regional risk sentiment (best proxied by GDP- or market-weighted spreads, or testing
whether an unaffected sovereign is moving with its typical beta). This episode, the move in BTPs explains
why OAT pressure finally started biting the Euro on Thursday.
Second, the beta jumps when credit is in focus: the Euro depreciates roughly 4-5% per 100bp of spread
widening on average, but in practice the response is near zero most of the time and spikes only in acute
stress — spreads don't matter for the currency until they're the only thing that matters.

Third, the EUR/USD hit is biggest when a clear risk element drives Bunds lower and EGBs higher,
layering a weaker credit story onto a dovish repricing of the policy path; note the broad Euro effect is far
smaller — often net positive — as EUR appreciates against the Scandis and CEE satellites.
The key distinction this time: the ECB is actively trying to tighten financial conditions, unlike past fiscal
episodes. Still, credit tightening episodes are particularly currency-negative, since they both curtail
monetary tightening and deter foreign investment. This week's moves were, for the most part, consistent
with all three principles.

3) Updates On Euro Sovereigns from GS Econ.
On Italy’s Fiscal (Filippo Taddei): The Italian government has just revised up its fiscal target following the
cabinet meeting that just ended. No press conferene is announced. Let's not understate the size of the
surprise . The almost unanimous consensus among clients was that 3% not to be breached from 2027
onward. They used the entirety of national escape clauses (NEC) (for energy 0.6% and defence 0.6% of
GDP) for ADDITIONAL deficits rather than slower fiscal consolidation. With these numbers deficits are
revised up to 3,5% in 27, 3,3% in 2028 to fall to 2,4% is 2029 when national escape clauses (NECs) are
discontinued. on a first approximation the debt-to-GDP ratio grows to almost 138% of GDP in 2028. Not
assuming any more bond selloff, spread widening. Italian debt ratio will be above Greece by early 2027
almost surely with these fiscal targets.
On France: (Alex Stott): We downgraded our forecast for French growth following the significant
tightening in financial conditions: we forecast growth of 0.4% in 2026 (unchanged) and 0.6% in 2027 (vs.
0.7% previously) on an annual average basis, below most other forecasters. We also published a note
discussing the implications of rising sovereign stress for the ECB.
Fiscal news. The presentation of the budget bill last Thursday was largely in line with expectations, and
we continue to forecast a deficit of 5.4% of GDP in 2026 and 5.3% in 2027. Marine Le Pen outlined her

proposal for a constitutional “golden rule”, which would be approved by referendum and would require
the deficit to fall by 0.5pp/year until the end of the next presidential term.
Polls and elections model. Le Pen remains well ahead in first-round polls at 33% of votes (unchanged over
the week), with Edouard Philippe second at 18% (-1pp) and Mélenchon third at 17% (unchanged). Our
poll-based model sees a 68% (+3pp) probability that Le Pen wins the presidency, compared to 16% (-2pp)
for Philippe and 6% (unchanged) for Mélenchon.

4) EU ECON - ECB—Potential Implications of Rising Sovereign Stress - LINK
European sovereign risk is back in focus, with French 10-year spreads at their widest since 2012 and early
signs of spillover into other bond markets.
The key question is the ECB reaction function? (1) on rates: tighter financial conditions do part of the
ECB's work. Our Euro area FCI has tightened nearly 40bp since the start of July, roughly substituting for
one 25bp hike. We keep our call for a third and final hike in December, but the tightening reduces any
urgency to go further — reinforcing the view that the Governing Council will not accelerate the pace of
hikes or take rates significantly restrictive.
(2) the balance sheet is the more likely first lever. The Council could lean against sovereign stress by
slowing QT — either returning to partial (and flexibly deployed) reinvestments or starting new purchases
to build the planned structural bond portfolio. Helpful against fragmentation risk, but controversial and
contingent on clearer evidence of bond market stress.
(3) anti-fragmentation tools are the last resort, not the next step. OMT requires a macro adjustment
programme (potentially unlimited size); the TPI targets "unwarranted, disorderly market dynamics"
without a programme requirement, subject to criteria that leave significant Governing Council discretion.
But activation would need far more intense and broad-based stress — a high bar with inflation well above
target. Critically, the bar is much lower for protecting "innocent bystanders" from contagion (e.g. Spain)
than for intervening where current fiscal policy is inconsistent with debt stabilisation (e.g. France). The
TPI could conceivably be deployed temporarily under disorderly conditions to buy time for a policy
adjustment — a parallel to the UK mini-budget episode — but fundamental sovereign risk needs a fiscal
solution, not an ECB one.
