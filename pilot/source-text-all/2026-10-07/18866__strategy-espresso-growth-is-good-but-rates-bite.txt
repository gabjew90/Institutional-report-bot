We look at some key questions on Europe equities from recent conversations 
n
with both investors and corporates. 
European equities have underperformed other regions since early August with 
n
mega-cap Tech being a large driver of indices in US and Asia. That said, STOXX 
Europe is only 4.5% from peak and we find investors remain surprised by the 
resilience of European equities. We argue this resilience is earnings driven. EPS 
has been upgraded for most sectors (barring consumer discretionary) and EPS 
revisions explain close to 70% of relative sector return year-to-date. 
Also, while S&P 500 performance has been exceptionally narrow that is not true 
n
in Europe where breadth has been close to normal; put another way more 
sectors/stocks have participated in Europe. Moreover, the median stock in 
Europe has outperformed the median in either US or Asia this year. 
But, nonetheless, the rise in bond yields is becoming increasingly difficult to 
n
digest. The 6-month correlation — based on weekly changes — between STOXX 
600 and the Germany 10y bund yield is -0.7. Outside 2022 we haven’t been this 
negative since the late 1990s. We find each 10bp rise in yields corresponds to 
roughly a 1-1.2% fall in STOXX 600.  
It’s not just yields rising but spreads too, European equities’ valuation has 
n
historically tracked sovereign spreads and these have widened dramatically. Each 
10bp widening in the average spread of OATs and BTPs to Bunds takes 0.9% from 
Europe’s PE. 
The good news is that the near-term prospects for earnings remain strong, EPS 
n
momentum is positive, and Industrials, Technology, Energy and Financials (all 
large sectors in Europe) have strong structural tailwinds. The consensus expects 
EPS growth of 22% yoy in 2H, 15% ex energy.  
Moreover, Europe has a FCF yield of 5.5% vs. 3.5% for the US. Companies 
n
continue to buyback shares and equity supply remains net negative. Europe is 
increasingly ‘being bought’ with M&A deals at highs. Domestic flows into Europe 
remain weak, but foreign investors are allocating as they seek to diversify.  
We continue to expect earnings to be the key driver of returns, and given 
n
earnings growth is likely to slow in 2027 we prefer sectors where we see 
continued robust earnings prospects, we remain overweight in Technology, 
Banks, Renewables (GSSBRNEW), Defense (GSSBDEFE) and Capital intensive 
(GSSTCAPI).

Growth is good, but Rates bite 
 
 
The rise in bond yields is hitting European stocks: STOXX Europe is down around 4.5% 
since the peak in early August. We see the sell-off as a function of the continued energy 
shock combined with the sharp rates re-pricing, the German 10-year yield is up around 
35bp since early August and France is up 100bp and at 4.9%. The equity market is not 
immune to higher rates, with correlation between rates moves and the STOXX Europe 
having turned sharply negative (Exhibit 1). Since the start of the latest Middle East 
conflict, a 10bp rise in US 10-year yields has corresponded to roughly a 1.0% fall in the 
STOXX 600, while a similar move in German Bund yields has been associated with around 
a 1.2% decline.  
That said, as a small counter earnings revisions continue to be positive (albeit the change 
is small in front of the start of the Q3 earnings season) (Exhibit 2), and the economic 
data has been resilient and strong in the case of Germany and the UK. 
 
The link between earnings and performance has rarely been stronger. Over the past 
year, rising interest rates, the energy shock and uncertainty around the impact of AI have 
weighed on valuations across sectors (with Banks and Utilities the notable exceptions). 
As a result, sector performance has been driven entirely by earnings growth rather than 
multiple expansion (Exhibit 3).

Of course this longer period of low Persian Gulf energy exports means persistent high

a hit to company margins, Exhibit 14 shows the current gap between output prices (what

impact of higher energy and raw material prices on wages given that labour markets are 
looser than they were in 2022-23 and this will limit the margin impact this time, (ii) the 
change in margins is more closely correlated to the change in output or GDP, not to costs 
(Exhibit 15). It’s demand that largely dictates pricing power and this in turn dictates 
margins, we see flat margins next year rather than a sharp fall. 
 
Q: What about France? French sovereign spreads have widened sharply in recent weeks, 
a function of a wide fiscal deficit, a large accumulated debt burden, and no signal or 
political will for fiscal consolidation. Our economists recently raised their forecast for 
public debt to reach 125% of GDP by the start of the next decade (vs. 123% before). 
They now estimate that France will eventually need to run a 1% primary surplus to 
stabilise its public debt-to-GDP ratio, a feat rarely achieved in recent history and 
certainly not one being espoused by the main candidates on the left or right for France’s 
2027 presidential election. Sovereign spread widening matters both for European stocks 
more broadly (as a signal of sovereign risk and political and policy uncertainty) (Exhibit 
16) — and for French equities specifically. Each 10bp widening in the average spread of 
OATs and BTPs to Bunds takes 0.9% from Europe’s PE. 
That said, while we expect the run-up to the 2027 election, and before that the annual 
budget debates, to create further volatility and keep French risk premia elevated, we do

Are Industrials/HALO too expensive? We are frequently asked this question and 
industrials are trading in Europe c.90th percentile of their historical valuation range on a 
P/E. Industrials have performed in line with the market over the last year, and we remain 
selective within this group preferring exposure to Aerospace and Defense (GSSBDEFE), 
or getting exposure via our Capital Intensive basket (GSSTCAPI). The growth drivers are 
well understood; our analysts Capex tracker has seen sizable upgrades in medium-term 
forecasts (2026E Capex 4.6pp up since May), pointing to a robust investment 
environment driven primarily by Technology (Data Centres and Semiconductors). Data 
Centres, Semis and Utilities, represent c.38% of total Capex in 2026E, compared to only 
22% just four years ago in 2022 — by 2029, these are expected to be 53%, according to 
our analysts. 
There is no doubt that positioning in the broad Industrials and Infrastructure area has 
moved significantly higher, looking at sector flows these have attracted the most inflows 
globally (Exhibit 19). Industrials valuations now appear stretched relative to growth 
expectations.

Q: How about banks, any value there now? Banks trade at a slight discount to the 
market on a growth-adjusted basis. And as we have highlighted before European banks 
have consistently outperformed US mega cap tech (Exhibit 20), a function of the low 
starting point for valuation but mainly a function of banks earnings growth and 
improved ROE. Relative to ROE banks valuation has moved up, but the sector remains 
one of the cheapest in Europe and recent valuation moves do not suggest the sector is 
over-pricing the current growth environment. Our analysts see double-digit % EPS 
growth and high-teens % ROTE, and strategic excess capital deployment into 
shareholder return and M&A, with banks under their coverage currently trading at c.10x 
12-month forward P/E they continue to see an attractive entry point (Exhibit 21). The 
largest risk remains growth weakness and/or a sharp further widening in sovereign risks. 
We remain OW European Banks.
