Introduction
As November 3 midterm elections draw closer, investors are increasingly starting to think about 
what the potential outcomes might mean for financial market performance. In this report, we dis-
cuss the major issues at stake with the latest elections and include contributions from some of our 
sector analysts on what the implications might be for companies under their research coverage.
The Electoral Math
As in every midterm, all 435 seats in the House are up for reelection. So too are 35 of the 100 
Senate seats (33 regular seats plus special elections in Ohio and Florida), 36 governorships, and 
thousands of state and local offices.
History shows that it’s rare that the president’s party gains seats in the midterm elections. In the 
past century, it has picked up House seats only three times: 1934, 1998, and 2002. Most Americans 
have historically liked Congressional gridlock as a check on the president’s power, and this elec-
tion cycle looks no different. While the House will almost certainly flip into Democrats’ control, the 
Senate still looks too close to call.
Republicans hold the Senate 53-47. Democrats need a net gain of four seats for a majority, since 
Vice President Vance would break a 50-50 tie. They also must hold competitive seats in New Hamp-
shire, Georgia, and Michigan. Runoffs in some states could leave control of the chamber undecided 
for weeks after November 3. Recall that in 2020, both of Georgia’s Senate races went to runoffs 
after no candidate won more than 50% of the vote. Those elections took place in January 2021 and 
swung the Senate to the Democrats with 50 seats, with Vice President Harris’s tie-breaking vote 
giving them the majority.
Democrats Maintain a Wide Generic Ballot Lead
Democrats lead the generic congressional ballot, a measure of House voting intentions, by 
8.5 percentage points in the RealClearPolitics average of national polls—nearly double their 
4.6-point lead in January (exhibit 1). That lead is wider than the 7.4 points Democrats held at 
the same point in 2018, when they gained 41 House seats. In 2010, 2014, and 2022, Republicans 
led at this stage of the cycle and gained seats each time. The polls have been a fair guide. In 2022, 
for example, RCP’s final average had Republicans ahead by 2.5 points, and they won the House 
popular vote by 2.8.

Greater Reliance on Executive Orders
A Democratic House would stall the president’s legislative agenda but would not halt his power 
to act outside of Congress. Executive orders give him plenty of room to maneuver, and he has used 
them heavily even with Republicans controlling both chambers. He has signed 285 executive or-

a divided Congress, that pace could hold or rise.

Outside of executive orders, the president still retains plenty of leeway over foreign policy, includ-
ing tariffs, sanctions, investment restrictions, and the military. This administration views China 
as the principal strategic challenge facing the U.S. and argues that it must be countered through 
greater preparedness, secure access to raw materials, and domestic production of chips, medi-
cines, and other goods critical to national security. If anything, some China hawks in the president’s 
own party contend that he has been too soft on China when it comes to making difficult trade and 
security decisions. Much of this remains under presidential control, as do the executive agencies 
carrying out policy, and a Democratic Congress would have little ability to alter the course.
The conflict with Iran may also become more consequential after the election. Trump has said he 
expects the war to end immediately after the midterms, arguing that Tehran is delaying a settle-
ment to hurt Republicans. At the same time, speculation that the administration could escalate 
military operations after November 3 adds another source of event risk for oil, inflation, and 
financial markets.

Personnel Is Policy
Washington has a saying: “personnel is policy.” Because the president appoints the heads of the ex-
ecutive agencies, he can do more than block new regulations. He can also decide how existing rules 
are interpreted and enforced. Measured by the number of pages in the Federal Register, regulation 
has fallen sharply under President Trump (exhibit 5). We expect deregulation to slow over the 
next two years as congressional oversight increases or gridlock sets in, but a Democratic Congress 
would have little power to undo many of those changes.

Policy Narrative Shifts
Entering the Lame Duck Period 
In a sense, a second-term president is a lame duck from the day they are inaugurated. After the 
midterms it gets worse. With members of Congress no longer beholden to the president for their 
own reelection, party discipline weakens. There will also be more noise, as members try to raise 
their profiles or align themselves with whoever they see as the party’s next leaders. A weak ap-
proval rating only speeds that process up: the president’s job approval fell to 38.7% on September 
28, just off the 37% low of his first term (exhibit 6).

From Legislation to Congressional Oversight, Why These Midterms Matter More
While midterm elections matter far less than presidential elections, two issues make this year’s 
vote more important than most.
First, they are important in deciding whether policy in Washington shifts back from legislation 
on tax and spending priorities and toward greater Congressional oversight, where the narrative 
becomes more about regulation and slowing the pace of any political initiatives, as opposed to 
driving ahead with new ones.  
If the Democrats regain control of Congress, Washington will undoubtedly start to be weighed 
down with more Congressional hearings, investigations, subpoenas for communications, docu-
ments, and testimony. And, as the president himself said in January: “You got to win the midterms, 
because if we don’t win the midterms, it’s just going to be—I mean, they’ll find a reason to impeach 
me … I’ll get impeached.”
In practice, impeachment has become largely symbolic. The House can impeach with a simple ma-
jority, but conviction requires a two-thirds majority (67 votes) in the Senate, which neither party is 
close to holding. President Trump was impeached twice in his first term. Only two other presidents 
have been impeached: Andrew Johnson and Bill Clinton. Richard Nixon resigned before the House 
could vote on impeachment.

Republicans losing either chamber would bring legislative gridlock. Without bipartisan support, 
the president and Republicans would find it hard to pass new tax or spending measures. It would 
also make another budget reconciliation bill much harder to pass. Reconciliation allows qualifying 
tax and spending legislation to clear the Senate with a simple majority, but the House and Senate 
would still have to agree on the bill. Under divided government, that would require a degree of 
bipartisan cooperation that has been largely absent from recent fiscal debates.
The low-probability exception would be a Republican sweep. President Trump has promised $5,000 
checks to every adult American if Republicans retain both chambers. Taken literally, the proposal 
would cost about $1.2 trillion, although no legislation or funding mechanism has been announced. 
Coming at a time when the economy is already operating near capacity, stimulus on that scale would 
raise the risk of stronger inflation, a higher term premium, and fewer Fed rate cuts.
A divided government could also bring back market volatility from government shutdowns and 
debt-ceiling standoffs. The debt limit is expected to be reached in mid- to late 2027. Democrats 
would press for concessions on healthcare and ACA programs, the environment, and social care. 
Republicans would have less room for their own priorities: further deregulation, tighter border 
and immigration controls, and extending the Trump tax provisions that are due to expire.
Campaign ad spending shows where those fights are likely to be. In battleground Senate races, 
healthcare is by far the Democrats’ top issue, at roughly $65 million. That is about twice what 
they have spent on any other topic (exhibit 7). Republicans are focused on taxation, at a little over 
$60 million, followed by candidate character, crime, and immigration. The House picture is similar 
(exhibit 8). There, taxation is also Republicans’ biggest issue or campaign promise, at about $45 
million, while Democrats lead with healthcare, special interests, and inflation.

The issues each party avoids are just as telling. Democrats spend almost nothing on immigration, 
and Republicans devote almost none of their advertising to messages centered on Donald Trump. 
Democrats, meanwhile, have spent roughly $18 million in House races and $27 million in Senate 
races attacking him. In other words, Democrats are running on affordability, healthcare, and the

outcome of these elections, a Democratic Congress would focus its oversight on healthcare costs 
and the administration itself. A Republican Senate would focus on defending the tax cuts.
Republicans and their allies, armed with a much larger war chest, are expected to spend about 
$888 million more on advertising between now and Election Day, compared with $666 million for 
Democrats, according to AdImpact, which tracks political advertising.
From Data Center Buildout to AI Backlash
The second reason markets are watching these midterms is what they could mean for the data 
center buildout that underpins AI development. Energy costs are already a campaign issue. The 
previous exhibits show Democrats spend roughly three times as much as Republicans on energy-
themed ads in the more contentious Senate races. Voters’ frustration with rising electricity bills is 
increasingly aimed at data centers.
If there is one race to see this play out, it is what’s happening in Texas. The state has welcomed data 
centers with fast permitting, cheap land, and generous tax breaks, and it now has the second-most 
in the country after Virginia. That welcome is starting to wear thin. A growing number of residents 
say the buildout has gone too far and is raising electricity prices, straining water supplies, and has 
been generally detrimental to the local environment. Many also argue that the 20-year tax incen-
tives used to attract the hyperscalers were too generous.
That backlash has overflowed into the governor’s race, where the popular Republican incumbent 
Greg Abbott, who has been a strong champion of the centers, is up against Democrat Gina Hinojosa, 
who is proposing a moratorium on them.

Investment Implication: Politics Matter, but the Economy 
and Inflation Matter More
Gridlock Should Help Ease Pressure on Bond Yields
Historically, midterm elections have not been a major driving factor for Treasury bond market 
performance. The far bigger drivers continue to be changes to Fed policy and the pace of real 
economic growth, capital investment, and inflation. However, the term premium has been creep-
ing higher over the last few years on the back of geopolitical concerns, greater policy uncertainty 
in Washington, and larger deficits and debt. Against that backdrop, the potential return of policy 
gridlock should help provide some support for the Treasury bond market and moderate upward 
pressure on yields.
The average total Treasury bond market return has been relatively consistent across each year of 
the presidential cycle (exhibit 10). Differences across the cycle are modest, suggesting that interest-
rate dynamics and Federal Reserve policy have been more important drivers of bond returns than 
the electoral calendar itself.

Analyst Sector Implications
We asked several William Blair analysts how the midterm results could affect their coverage. The 
common thread is that the election is more likely to shift sentiment, valuations, and timing than 
underlying demand. In defense, a Democratic sweep would weigh on sentiment, but the bipartisan 
need to rebuild military inventories means a continuing resolution would delay rather than elimi-
nate spending. For government consultants, a “blue wave” could lift compressed valuations even as 
federal contracting stays sluggish. For AI infrastructure, the moratorium debate plays out mostly in 
governor races, affecting when and where data centers are built rather than whether they are. In 
fintech, a divided Congress would limit the risk of disruptive policy, and in housing, proposed bills 
would help only at the margins while rising input costs remain the main constraint.
Energy and Power Technologies
Neal Dingmann
Oil and gas
We believe there would be little immediate impact to oil or natural gas prices if the Democrats win 
the House and/or the Senate during the midterms, as the same geopolitical pressures are likely to 
continue to influence oil prices and abundant near-term supply is likely to continue to influence 
natural gas prices. While we see little that could impact natural gas prices in the coming months, 
various war resolutions would be key to lowering oil prices. While the Majors and other domestic 
oil producers may drill more wells if oil prices remains high, Middle East and Russian oil produc-
tion could return sooner than later. However, no domestic company has spare fuel refinery capac-
ity to replace the facilities destroyed in Russia or that are trapped on the wrong side of the Strait 
of Hormuz.
Rare earth
We believe Democrats could use various oversight tools to scrutinize the Trump administration’s 
critical minerals agreements and equity stakes in various rare earth/mining companies if they win 
back the House or Senate in this fall’s U.S. midterm elections. While it may be difficult to walk back 
previous government funding, there could be a hiatus of any near-term rare earth/critical mineral 
company funding, which could impact newer pre-revenue entrants. However, both sides of the 
congressional aisle have stated the importance of non-Chinese rare earth supply, and this indicates 
the continued development of the overall group. 
Financial Services and Technology
Cristopher Kennedy
Fintech
Policy and headline risk has been an overhang for fintech/payments stocks, in our view, and it is 
unclear whether that dynamic will materially improve following the midterms. For example, while 
legislative action would be required to become law, credit card stocks materially underperformed 
after a Truth Social post proposing a 10% cap on credit card interest rates. Nonetheless, a divided 
Congress could help reduce the likelihood of more disruptive policy proposals ultimately becom-
ing law and help alleviate some investor concerns.

Healthcare
Brandon Vazquez and Steven Lichtman
Medtech
In the medtech sector, government subsidies for programs like the Affordable Care Act (ACA) and 
Medicaid remain a key focus into the midterm elections. Some key ACA subsidy cuts went into effect 
in 2026, and the Kaiser Family Foundation (KFF) estimates anywhere from 3.8 million to 5.8 million 
individuals will lose insurance coverage in 2026. The impact of a lower number of covered lives, and 
to some degree more patients with higher deductible plans, is already being realized as a headwind 
to a select few medical device companies that saw lower-than-expected procedural volumes in the 
second quarter. Further, while 2026 saw $17 billion in Medicaid subsidy reductions, that amount is 
expected to increase more than twofold in 2027 to $47 billion, according to the Congressional Bud-
get Office. The lingering question is if ACA headwinds, especially when combined with a step-up in 
Medicaid cuts, will act as material headwinds to procedure volumes.
The impact of reduced participation in ACA and Medicaid could also pressure the hospital system, 
driving up uncompensated care and bad debt. In addition, proposed payment cuts to 340B drugs 
would be an incremental margin impact to hospitals (not-for-profit) participating in the program 
(more than 2,600 hospitals did so to varying degrees as of early 2023, according to KFF). The cuts 
were introduced in the proposed 2027 Hospital Outpatient Prospective Payment System (OPPS) 
rule in July. The hospital lobbying groups have pushed back meaningfully, and we will see if/what 
is finalized in the final OPPS rule this fall. 
For medtech, the financial health of hospitals is key as it is a factor in their willingness to spend, 
particularly on larger capex items. While there are other potential cost offsets for hospitals, the 
incremental expense headwinds above bear watching. As we look to the midterms, investors have 
increasingly asked if a Democratic-led Congress might reduce some of the expected cuts. It is un-
clear, though, what legislation could pass with likely thin party margins and what would be signed. 
Regardless, we believe that investors will be focused on messaging from Democrats should they 
take both houses of Congress on potential offsets to the cuts underway or proposed.

A Democratic midterm sweep would likely be viewed as a modest negative for defense stocks given 
the party’s historically less aggressive posture toward defense spending. However, we would cau-

combination of depleted inventories, elevated geopolitical tensions, and increasing investment in

multiyear demand outlook.

ramp funding at a time when the Pentagon and industry are working to meaningfully increase mis-

supercycle and push revenue recognition to the right for certain suppliers. However, a CR would not 
address the underlying inventory shortfalls driving demand, suggesting that any disruption would 
primarily defer rather than eliminate the opportunity.

Stephen Sheldon
Real Estate
There are multiple housing-related bills and proposals in progress that could be impacted by 
midterm elections. Some are meant to help unlock home inventory and reduce some of the fric-
tion driving gridlock for current homeowners, especially the More Homes on the Market Act, 
which would increase the exclusion for capital gains tax on a primary home in the U.S. (doubling 
the exclusion to $1 million for married filers and $500,000 for single filers). There is significant-
ly more proposed legislation trying to alleviate affordability issues for first-time homebuyers. 
Examples include proposed legislation that would create a federal tax credit for first-time home-
buyers of up to 10% of the home purchase price (capped at a $15,000 credit) and legislation 
that would increase penalty-free IRA withdrawals for first-time homebuyers to $50,000 (from 
$10,000 currently). There are also proposals that could spur more development (especially for 
affordable housing) and that could provide tax incentives for conversions of existing properties 
(such as outdated office space) to residential housing. Overall, these bills could certainly help 
on the fringes to support a better housing backdrop, but are unlikely, in our view, to change the 
narrative drastically on the biggest issue, which is rising input costs that make it even tougher 
for home developers to pencil out starting new projects, adding to the structural undersupply 
(and rising prices) of housing.

Conclusion
November looks set to produce divided government. With Democrats favored to retake the House 
and control of the Senate still uncertain, Washington may struggle to pass major legislation after 
the election. That would make another reconciliation bill more difficult, though not impossible. It 
would also shift the House’s focus toward oversight and investigations, and raise the prospect of a 
debt-limit confrontation before the next deadline.
Gridlock in Congress does not mean policy stands still. The executive branch retains broad au-
thority through executive orders, trade actions, and agency rulemaking. In healthcare, for ex-
ample, the proposed 2027 Hospital Outpatient Prospective Payment System (OPPS) rule would 
cut payments for 340B drugs. Those cuts would add to pressure on hospitals already absorbing 
reductions to ACA subsidies and Medicaid, and in turn on medtech companies that depend on 
hospital capital spending and procedure volumes. Investors have increasingly asked whether 
a Democratic-led Congress could soften some of these cuts, but with likely thin party margins, 
it is unclear what could pass and be signed. The Senate would also continue to shape policy 
through the confirmation process, including a replacement for former Chair Powell, whose term 
as governor expires in January 2028. Some issues with direct implications for investors, includ-
ing permitting, power infrastructure, and data-center development, are also being decided in 
state capitals rather than Washington.
For investors, however, the midterms should not displace the larger forces driving markets. The 
fundamentals that have shaped returns this year will not change on November 3. Divided govern-
ment would make large fiscal initiatives harder to pass, which could ease some concerns about 
future Treasury issuance, even if a debt-limit standoff would bring its own volatility. The unlikely 
alternative of a Republican sweep would create the opposite risk: renewed fiscal expansion, in-
cluding the proposed $5,000 checks, could add to inflation and upward pressure on bond yields. 
Investors hoping for a calmer political backdrop may be disappointed. Legislative activity may 
slow, but political conflict probably will not. Uncertainty over the next phase of the conflict with 
Iran could also keep geopolitical risk elevated after the election. In many ways, the midterms are 
less a verdict on the past two years than the opening round of the 2028 contest, and markets could 
start trading on that sooner than many expect.
