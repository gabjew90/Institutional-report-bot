 
"No One's Liability": Bundesbank Boss Defend 
Gold's Reserve Role As Debt Risk Crowds Out Bond 
Yields 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 06, 2026 - 10:15 AM 
When the president of the Bundesbank tells a room full of bullion bankers that rising 
government debt strengthens the case for more gold in official reserves, you listen. Especially 
when he says it while global yields are climbing, which is supposed to be the one thing that 
makes non-yielding metal look dumb. 
 
Speaking Monday in Sorrento at the LBMA's Global Precious Metals Conference, Joachim 
Nagel walked straight through the textbook objection and then kicked it aside. Per Bloomberg's 
Alexander Weber and Mark Burton (Oct 5): 
"The recent rise in global government bond yields has boosted the relative attractiveness of 
debt securities again," he said. "At the same time, rising debt levels have increased concerns 

 
about the credit risk of these assets. Furthermore, geopolitical risks are likely to continue to 
shape reserve management decisions." (emphasis ours) 
His punchline, again via Bloomberg (and confirmed in the Bundesbank's published keynote): 
"Taken together, the case for further diversification into gold remains significant" for central 
banks. (emphasis ours) 
In other words: higher yields make bonds look better on a carry screen. Higher debt and 
'frozen-reserves' geopolitics make those same bonds look worse on a survival screen. Gold 
wins the second argument. 
Below we walk through Nagel's three-chapter history of official gold, the Bank of Italy's matching 
"safe haven" pitch from the same stage, Goldman's still-structural CB bid, and why Germany's 
flat holdings are the exception that proves the rule. 
Anchor, Retreat, Return: Nagel's Three Chapters 
Nagel did not just drop a gold soundbite and leave. He gave the room a compressed 
monetary-history lecture, and the arc is the point. 
Per the Bundesbank text: gold was the anchor of Bretton Woods — almost 70% of global CB 
reserves at market value in 1950, still close to 60% in 1979 after the dollar-gold link broke. Then 
came the retreat: from ~60% to around 10% by 2009, as globalization, liquid bond markets and 
(relative) geopolitical calm made US paper look safer and more useful than metal that pays no 
coupon. Then the return: after the GFC, China and Russia led official buying; gold's share 
crawled from 10% in 2009 to ~14% in 2023. From 2023 to 2025 it jumped to almost 25% — 
mostly price, Nagel admits. Hold prices at 2023 levels and the share would have slipped to 
~12%. 

 
 
The mechanism he flagged for the post-2010s rebound is the one ZH has been pounding since 
2022: foreign securities and deposits can be frozen; physical gold held domestically is not 
subject to the same risk. Regular readers will recall we covered exactly that calculus when the 
Dutch moved nearly 90 tonnes out of US/Canadian vaults toward London (Sep 3) under the 
banner of "crisis preparedness," with Goldman Sachs' Lina Thomas warning that geographic 
concentration risk is now "increasingly top of mind" for reserve managers. 
As we put it when the Dutch gold trucks started rolling: 
Germany, for its part, still sits on the world's second-largest gold pile — and has kept holdings 
broadly steady while other monetary institutions have bought aggressively, Bloomberg notes. 
Diversification for thee, not for me. Classic Bundesbank. 
"Probably The Safe Haven Asset": Italy's Altimari Piles On 
Nagel was not alone on the Sorrento stage. Bank of Italy deputy governor Sergio Nicoletti 
Altimari, in his welcome address, went further on branding: 
"Gold is a safe-haven asset, probably the safe haven asset, as proven by its performance over 
time and across a broad range of crises," he told the conference, per Reuters (Polina Devitt). 
"This role is particularly relevant today in an environment of high geopolitical risk and concerns 
about economic fragmentation." (emphasis ours) 

 
Altimari's own text adds the line that should be taped to every reserve-manager desk: gold is 
"no one's liability." The absence of credit and default risk, he said, "distinguishes gold from 
virtually every other major reserve asset." 
He also called the post-2022 surge in emerging-market official buying a structural shift, and 
pointed to the so-called "debasement trade" — worries about high public debt and fiscal 
expansion — as the reason gold's traditional inverse link to real yields "weakened significantly" 
in 2025 and early 2026. Reuters adds Metals Focus's June forecast that CB buying slows 15% 
YoY to 720 tonnes in 2026, still well above pre-2022 levels. A slowdown from record, not a 
retreat to normal. Put differently: the floor is higher even if the ceiling is less frantic. 
As we noted when Bloomberg's Garfield Reynolds argued central banks put a strong floor under 
gold (Aug 4), official holdings dwarf ETF stock — and China was already on a multi-month 
buying streak through the summer. 
The Buyers Are Still Coming: WGC's Record 45% 
Nagel's "significant" case is not a lonely German opinion. It is what the buyers themselves tell 
the World Gold Council. 
In the WGC's 2026 Central Bank Gold Reserves Survey — fielded Feb 5 to May 19, published 
in June, and covered here when a record percentage of central banks expected to add (Jun 16) 
— 45% of respondents said they plan to increase their own gold holdings over the next 12 
months, the biggest share since the survey began in 2018. Bloomberg's Weber/Burton note the 
same punchline from a 74-bank cut of the survey: just one said it planned to cut. 
The rest of the survey is even less subtle. 89% expect global official gold reserves to rise over 
the next year. 74% expect the dollar's share of global reserves to be lower five years out. 84% 
think gold's share of total reserves will be higher in five years. Translation: the institutions that 
actually hold the world's reserves are telling you, on the record, that they intend to keep moving 
away from dollar paper and toward metal. 

 
 
And the official numbers still understate how hard China is leaning into the metal: 
We flagged the same price-insensitive bid when China bought the dip hard enough that central 
banks looked like they loved a gold sell-off (Jul 15): 1,090 tonnes of official buying in 2024, still 
863 tonnes in 2025, then 244 tonnes in Q1 2026 as the metal cheapened from its January high. 
Savers, not momentum chasers. 
Goldman: Still Structural, Just Slower Near Term 
Sell-side is not fighting the official bid. Goldman's Lina Thomas and Daan Struyven, in Precious 
Analyst: Fed Hikes to Slow, Rather than Derail, the Gold Rally (Sep 18) (available to pro subs), 
kept a $5,400/toz end-2027 forecast even after a Fed hike and an expected October follow-up. 
They cut year-end fair value to $4,650 from a prior $4,900 (spot then ~$4,350) — slower path, 
not a broken thesis. 
The structural driver, in their words: 
"Continued central bank diversification remains the main structural driver of our constructive 
gold view, contributing nearly all of our expected 23% appreciation through end-2027. Our 
central bank nowcast points to purchases running at ~91 tonnes/month on a three-month 
seasonally adjusted basis, well above the pre-2022 average of 17 tonnes/month. Reflecting this 
acceleration, we raise our central bank demand assumption to 60 tonnes/month on average 

 
through 2026-27, versus 50 tonnes/month in 2026 and 40 tonnes/month in 2027 previously." 
(emphasis ours) 
 
GS desk color this week rhymes. Thomas Evans' Weekly Commodity Thoughts (Oct 4) called 
gold "rates are holding it back, but still long-term constructive," with ETF/futures positioning still 
constructive even as options interest cooled. GS is hosting an LBMA conference feedback 
webcast Tuesday — which is a polite way of saying the Street expects more of what Nagel just 
said out loud. 
Having pounded the table on Goldman's "structurally constructive" framing since the bank's 
mid-year tactical trim (Jun 20), we'll allow ourselves a modest victory lap: the Bundesbank just 
endorsed the structural half of the call from a podium in Campania.

Bottom Line 
Nagel's closer is the whole story in one sentence: yields make bonds more attractive again; debt 
and geopolitics make them riskier again; "the case for further diversification into gold remains 
significant." 
ZH's take: the significant case wins. Germany can sit on its second-largest stockpile and look 
prudent while EM (and increasingly some DM) reserve managers keep converting credit risk into 

 
vault risk. Higher yields may clip ETF demand and slow the path — Goldman's own fair-value 
cut says as much — but they do not reverse a bid that is still running at several times the 
pre-sanctions pace, with 45% of surveyed central banks still planning to buy and precisely one 
planning to sell. Especially with US fiscal math still pointing one way and the 2022 freeze of 
Russia's reserves still burned into every reserve manager's memory, a coupon on a Treasury is 
a poor substitute for an asset that is no one's liability. 
Then again, if the Bundesbank ever decides its own "steady" holdings need a little diversification 
into more gold, we will know the last holdout has blinked.
