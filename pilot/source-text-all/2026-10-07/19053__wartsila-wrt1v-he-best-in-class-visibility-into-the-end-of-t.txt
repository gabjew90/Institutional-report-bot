PM summary 
 
 
We upgrade power and marine engine maker Wartsila to Buy (from Neutral) with an 
increased price target of €35, implying 19% upside. The stock is down c.5% YTD (vs 
Multis up c.10%) – despite material increases in Wartsila’s backlog visibility – given 
investor concerns on the sustainability of datacenter demand and the supply/demand 
balance for turbines and gas engines after 2028 when much of the OEM capacity 
increases come to market (our S/D model points towards tightness at least until 2030). 
We estimate Wartsila’s current share price implies 12% adj. EBIT margins in Energy 
beyond 2027 (Exhibit 25), a level not seen since pre-2023 when the business was more 
skewed to lower margin EPC projects. We think these worries are overdone and see 
three reasons to buy the shares: 
We expect consensus estimate upgrades over the next three years as Wartsila 
1.
executes on an all-time high backlog and benefits from improved industry 
pricing while harvesting the portfolio pruning of recent years. Wartsila has higher 
visibility on its backlog (Exhibit 11) than it has had historically with capacity fully 
loaded to mid-2029 in Power and c.2.5 years of visibility in Marine. This drives 
Wartsila to have the second fastest organic sales growth rate across our coverage 
over the next three years as well as the second highest ROIC on our forecasts. On 
Energy, we expect a significant uptick on margins as the company delivers on the 
500bps of gross margin expansion seen in its backlog over the last 12 months, while 
across Marine margins should be structurally higher than long-run history as the 
portfolio has been significantly pruned of underperforming assets. 
Noting the concerns about the sustainability of datacenter capex and orders 
o

visibility post 2029 underpinned by a pick-up in Services. Given the recent 
uptick in datacenter orders (which come with >80% attachment rates), we see 
a strong first maintenance period in 2029 (Exhibit 49) supported by renewal 
rates >90% and potential upside from the replacement of newer, less proven 
technology currently entering the market which could disappoint customers 
on quality relative to Warstila. This should still hold even if obstacles to 
datacenter build out were to increase as existing assets are likely to be run at 
higher utilisation levels. Also, with datacenter demand more likely to be 
fulfilled by hyperscalers deploying off-grid generation rather than wholly 
supplied by conventional utilities (and in the US we note emerging legislation 
on the state level which would require requestors of permits to bring their own 
power), we see less bias for the deployment of turbines (which utilities 
technicians are more familiar with maintaining) and higher likelihood of 
engines gaining share in the mix. Lastly, Wartsila’s focus on service agreements 
(currently only c.30% of Service sales) should support margin expansion given 
their 2-5x spend ratio (EUR/kW) relative to transactional services.

We conduct in-depth analysis of the nuances in each end market (e.g., technology shifts, regional

correlation).

What is currently priced in? 
 
 
Given our current discussions with investors mainly center on the Energy outlook, we assess what the 
shares are pricing in for Energy margins.  
From the current EV, we back out a market-implied EV for the Energy segment using the average 12m 
forward EV/EBIT of Marine peers (c.19x) on Visible Alpha Consensus 12m forward Marine adj. EBIT 
forecasts which gives c.€10bn Marine EV, putting the Energy EV at c.€6bn. On the consensus average 12m 
forward EV/EBIT of Energy peers (c.21x), this implies an Energy 12m forward adj. EBIT of €280mn, which 
compares to our forecast of €445mn. 
This implies the market is discounting Wartsila’s Energy adj. EBIT by c.35% versus our expectations. 
On our 12m forward Energy adj. EBIT of €445mn, Wartsila is trading on c.14x EV/EBIT, 8 turns lower 
than the peer multiple sitting at c.22x.

Energy: Datacenter demand drives tight capacity 
 
 
We are -5%/18% below/above VA consensus on Energy sales in 2027/28. We see 3 
levers of growth for Wartsila’s Energy top line: (1) Datacenter behind-the-meter demand 
remaining strong at least until 2030 (our Europe O&G team recently raised their BTM 
outlook from 40GW to 67GW by 2030 here), supported by healthy momentum in the US 
(where Wartsila has had a presence for over half a century) and increasing penetration 
of the EU market, as illustrated by our Capex Tracker’s c.43% CAGR over 2025-29E for 
DC investment (DC orders now account for >50% of Wartsila’s mix and we see room for 
this to grow given the planned 65% increase in manufacturing capacity); (2) We see the 
S/D imbalance sustaining until 2029 based on our S/D model; and (3) Greater need for 
backup power as the grid increasingly relies on renewable sources of energy.  
We are 1%/22% above consensus on Energy adj. EBIT with +1p.p margin difference. 
We note 3 main tailwinds to Wartsila’s Energy margins: (1) Operating leverage, as 
capacity utilisation rises from c.75% in 2025 to >90% in the next few years on our 
forecasts; (2) Improving margin profile of datacenter orders, with better pricing 
momentum (see Innio’s most recent DC 450MW order here); and (3) Step up of 
datacenter-related service revenues should drive higher margins starting in 2029, in our 
view. Lastly, Wartsila’s stable Service business along with the technical advantages of 
engines over turbines should limit the S/D downside risk, in our view, in the event of 
power generation overcapacity.

Gas Power Generation S/D model 
 
 
Strong demand to continue, with moderation of S/D only expected post 
2029E 
Our Gas Power Generation S/D model indicates strong demand trends for gas power 
until 2029 after which demand could moderate. Despite this slowdown, we see c.115GW 
of cumulative excess demand during 2026-28 making up for c.115GW overcapacity in 
2029-30, if no projects ever get cancelled. We view Wartsila well positioned to benefit 
from this sustained period of S/D imbalance, supported by the rise in BTM and modular 
solutions, requiring a fast ramp-up time, low water usage and flexibility, areas in which 
reciprocating engines outperform their gas turbine counterpart. Additionally, Wartsila’s 
engines lead times still remain well below that of CCGT’s current timelines, with delivery 
times anywhere between 10-25 months faster. On our estimates, global capacity of 
engines and gas turbines should increase to c.170GW/year by 2030, over 2x the 2025 
level of 80GW. On the flip side, demand is expected to average c.125GW per year over 
2026-30, which is c.4x the average seen over the past five years. 
Gas power market environment 
The gas power market currently faces acute capacity constraints both on the turbine 
(limited capacity until 2030+) and engine (limited capacity until 2028+) sides. As a result, 
gas turbine and engine manufacturers have steeply increased their capacity expansion 
plans. To contextualise, if all planned plant capacity began operations in 2026, additions 
would surpass the record 100GW added in 2002 (source: Global Energy Monitor). 
This is primarily driven by surging demand from datacenters, struggling for connections 
to an aging grid and generation infrastructure. We believe aging infrastructure should 
sustain demand over the next 10 years, as >160GW of gas generation assets (excl. 
engines) are older than 30 years (or c.10% of total gas demand in 2025 Exhibit 53), 
forcing decisions on closures or life extensions. The US and the Middle East present the 
largest pool of replacement demand. Further, the grid across the world is saturated, with 
wait times for connections to the US grid extending into 2031, while connection to the 
UK, Netherlands and Ireland grids could take 9+ years (Exhibit 52). The Global Energy 
Monitor highlights that two-thirds of that capacity and more than half of projects tied to 
data centers do not currently have a named turbine or engine manufacturer, 
exacerbating the need for additional manufacturing capacity. The need for balancing 
power, as the grid shifts to renewable fuels, is also a key driver of the current capacity 
constraints.

Conclusion 
Our Gas Power Generation S/D model indicates strong demand trends for gas power 
until 2029 after which demand softens. Despite the slowdown, we see c.115GW of 
cumulative excess demand during 2026-28 making up for c.115GW overcapacity in 
2029-30. We view Wartsila as well positioned to benefit from the sustained period of 
S/D imbalance, supported by the rise in BTM and modular solutions requiring a fast 
ramp-up time, low water usage and flexibility, areas in which reciprocating engines 
outperform their gas turbine counterpart. Further, Wartsila’s engine lead times still 
remain well below that of CCGT’s current timelines, with delivery times anywhere 
between 10-25 months faster. 
 
Limitations 
We see 3 key limitations to our S/D model: (1) and (2) would ease 2029/30 overcapacity 
risk, while (3) would exacerbate it.

1.
engines with power rating >20MW. For instance, Cummins mostly sells engines in the 
range of 1-4MW, therefore it is less likely to face the vertical demand trends some of 
its peers are seeing. 
Our Demand assumptions rely on the IEA WEO and Global Energy Monitor forecasts

Our capacity modelling assumes that the diesel and gas mix of additions remains in line

Risks 
We see a few risks which could reduce the S/D imbalance seen today, namely: softer 
AI-driven power demand, slipping construction readiness, loosening of China 
nickel-based superalloys restrictions or competition from cheaper batteries, and 
phasing of Backlog Conversion based on whether customers pull forward or push 
backward. Furthermore, should fuel cells become more efficient, there is risk of eroding 
the share of gas capacity on the demand side for turbines and engines. Similarly, if coal 
becomes easier to permit, this could lower the demand for gas solutions. Lastly, there 
could be demand erosion in the scenario where SMRs develop faster than expected.  
We note investors have expressed concerns for Wartsila specifically that some players, 
such as Cummins, could still have some capacity in 2028, with more modular solutions 
(relative to Wartsila’s longer installation process) and using high-speed (HS) engines, 
which are intrinsically better for backup generation than Wartsila’s medium speed (MS) 
engines. While it is true that HS engines have seen a surge in demand for backup power 
needs, these should work hand in hand with MS engines, which remain the preferred 
choice for baseload medium-sized BTM solutions and grid balancing operations. 
Additionally, while Wartsila’s solution might be less plug-and-play than containerised 
solutions for instance, it does offer an advantage: once S/D starts to loosen, customers 
could easily replace their arrays of maintenance-heavy HS engines with Wartsila’s more 
efficient medium-speed engines.

Marine: Providing resilience to Energy fluctuations 
 
 
We are c.0%/4% above VA consensus Marine sales in 2027/28. We see 4 levers for

IMO implementation uncertainty might continue to privilege dual fuel, which favours

difference. We note 3 main tailwinds to Wartsila’s Marine margins: (1) Dominant market 
share in 4-stroke medium speed engines should support pricing; (2) Steadily increasing 
service penetration anchored by >90% renewal rates; and (3) Manufacturing capacity 
shift to power generation engines at the expense of marine could drive tightness in 
supply, driving higher pricing.

Valuation and key risks  
 
 
We are Buy rated on Wartsila. Our 12-month price target remains based on our 24m 
forward EV/IC to ROIC/WACC valuation methodology and rises to €35 (previously €34) 
reflecting the roll-forward of our valuation period to 6m27/6m28 estimates (from 
9m27/3m28 estimates) and, to a lesser extent, decrease in estimates. Our new 
12-month price target implies roughly 19% upside. 
Key downside risks:  
(1) Supply/Demand imbalance: While some gas turbines players said they expect the 
market capacity to be constrained until 2030, large investments in manufacturing and 
weaker-than-expected demand could drive overcapacity. Beyond 2030, connections to 
the grid coming online following modernisation could also further reduce demand. If 
buyers perceive this earlier, they might start pushing back on order pricing sooner than 
expected. Lastly, we note SpaceX recently announced the development of gas turbines 
blades for AI datacenters, expecting to speed up turbine deployment by as much as 18 
months. 
(2) Worse-than-expected pricing in new Energy orders: Market share loss in 
reciprocating engines could drive weaker pricing along with faster-than-anticipated 
capacity expansion from gas turbine players, particularly as grid connections improve, 
alternative off-grid technologies are introduced (e.g. fuell cells or battery storage) or 
peers expand capacity excessively. 
(3) Lower cash distribution to shareholders: Further capacity expansions or M&A 
could limit shorter-term returns to shareholders. 
(4) US tariffs and regulation: Geopolitical headwinds from tariffs and regulatory 
changes could exacerbate the drag on the US as Wartsila produces most of its 
equipment in Finland. The US executive order prohibiting certain foreign-produced 
bulk-power system electric equipment from being purchases or installed in the US has 
added pressure to Wartsila. This puts the company  at a disadvantage to other local 
power generation players as most orders have been in the US. We see this reversing as 
datacenters expand in Europe (Exhibit 37).  
(5) Contract execution issues: Execution issues on current large contracts could delay 
revenue recognition and lower customer confidence for future orders (e.g. in 2008-09 
following the financial crisis, a combined c.€750mn of order were canceled, equivalent 
to c.15% of the 2007 Ship Power orderbook, and also some cancellations in 2022 after 
exiting Russian projects). 
(6) IMO delays: Further delays in stricter IMO requirements for carbon intensity could 
result in lower adoption of duel fuel solutions in Marine.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
