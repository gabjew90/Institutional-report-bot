How Much More Can China’s Exports Grow? 
 
 
The rise in Chinese exports and the trade surplus over the past few decades has been 
extraordinary. From 2000 to 2025, China’s share of global goods exports rose from less 
than 4% to nearly 15%—above Germany’s peak of 13% in the late 1970s and Japan’s 
peak of 10% in the 1980s, and surpassed only by the US before 1970 during the 
post-WWII period (left panel of Exhibit 1). Even as Chinese exports have continued to 
rise in recent years, imports have stalled amid weak domestic demand and the pursuit of 
self-reliance. From 2021 to 2025, China Customs’ official data show that export volume 
increased by around 25%, whereas import volume was largely unchanged. This pattern is 
highly unusual: in the early 1960s, when US exports accounted for 19% of the global 
total, the US trade surplus was only 0.3% of global GDP, far below China’s 1% in 2025 
(right panel of Exhibit 1). 
The natural question is: “How much more can China’s exports grow?” This is especially 
relevant because Chinese imports are unlikely to rise much from here, given the strategic 
shift away from fossil fuels and toward technological self-sufficiency (crude oil, natural 
gas, and semiconductors accounted for 30% of Chinese imports in 2025). In this note, 
we examine the profitability of Chinese exports and trading partners’ 
balance-of-payments constraints to assess the sustainability of China’s export growth. 
Our analysis suggests that, at least over the next few years, there is still room for Chinese 
exports to maintain their strong momentum. Over the longer term, however, stronger 
economic growth in importing economies will be required for continued Chinese export 
gains. 
 
Sustainability Through the Profitability Lens 
One way to assess export sustainability is to ask whether rising exports coincide with 
rising profits. If so, the export boom is likely demand-driven and more persistent. During 
“China Shock 1.0” in 2001-07, this was indeed the case: Exhibit 2 shows that China’s 
industrial profits as a share of total industrial assets rose alongside the export share of 
manufacturing value-added in the early 2000s. In recent years, however, this 
relationship has reversed: the export share of manufacturing value-added has climbed 
again, while industrial profits as a share of assets have declined. In other words, unlike

Pressures Are Increasing…  
On the surface, China Shock 1.0 and China Shock 2.0 look similar in terms of shifting 
export destinations. In both 2001-07 and 2021-25, emerging markets—including 
low-income and other EM countries—accounted for rising shares of Chinese exports.2 By 
contrast, developed markets—including the US and other DM countries—accounted for 
falling shares (Exhibit 4). Our previous research has documented the growing 
importance of EM countries to Chinese exports (see here and here, for example). 
 
Beneath the surface, however, bilateral trade balances between EM countries—
especially low-income countries—and China have deteriorated notably over the past 
two decades. For example, in 2005, only 24 countries had trade deficits with China 
exceeding 3% of their GDP, and these countries received 24% of total Chinese exports. 
In 2025, however, the number of countries with bilateral trade deficits above 3% of GDP 
rose to 82, and collectively they accounted for 44% of total Chinese exports (Exhibit 5).

… But There Is Still Room to Import More from China Over the Next Few 
Years  
One offsetting factor is trading partners’ substitution away from imports from other 
countries and toward imports from China, limiting the impact on their overall trade 
deficits even as their bilateral deficits with China rise. To gauge the extent of this 
substitution, we estimate a panel regression model using annual data for more than 170 
countries. Exhibit 6 presents the results separately for 2001-07 and 2021-25, from 
which we draw two conclusions. 
First, there is meaningful substitution between imports from China and those from 
elsewhere. Second, this effect has strengthened slightly in recent years. We estimate that 
each 1pp-of-GDP increase in the deficit with China was associated with a 0.7pp decline 
in the deficit with non-China in 2021-25, compared with 0.6pp in 2001-07. This pattern 
suggests that the overall pressure on trading partners from higher imports from China 
may be smaller than bilateral trade deficits alone imply. That said, our estimates still 
point to weaker balance-of-payments positions, as the offset is less than one-for-one.

Benchmarking the Upside for Chinese Exports 
To quantify how much further Chinese exports can grow, we perform a simple thought 
experiment. We examine 98 ex-China countries whose total imports were at least 
US$10bn in 2024, the latest year for which HS-2-level trade data are available for all 
sample countries at the time of writing. We exclude mineral imports, mostly crude oil, 
because China does not export oil and only non-mineral trade is relevant to gauging the 
potential increase in China’s market share.3 The left panel of Exhibit 8 shows that China 
accounted for 15.2% of total imports and 17.6% of total non-mineral imports by the 98 
countries in 2024. 
Together, our sample of 98 countries represents roughly 90% of global ex-China trade. 
In 2024, China was the largest source of imports for 54 of them. These countries, 
however, tend to be smaller and accounted for 44% of global ex-China non-mineral 
imports (right panel of Exhibit 8). China was the second-largest supplier for another 31 
countries, which accounted for 46% of global ex-China non-mineral imports. The 
remaining 13 countries accounted for the final 10%.

In a benchmarking exercise, we set China’s share of each country’s non-mineral imports 
equal to the highest bilateral share that country recorded with any trading partner 
during 2000-24. For example, Germany accounted for 16.3% of the UK’s non-mineral 
imports in 2014, the highest share for any single country during the 2000-24 period. We 
therefore replace China’s actual 2024 share of 13.4% of UK non-mineral imports with 
16.3%. This approach may overstate China’s upside in some markets: Mexico’s highest 
import share was 67%, supplied by the US in 2000. China is unlikely to reach 67% of 
Mexico’s non-mineral imports because of Mexico’s geographic proximity to the US and 
the two countries’ free-trade agreement. Conversely, our methodology may understate 
China’s upside in some markets because it does not allow out-of-sample extrapolation. 
China accounted for 24% of Saudi Arabia’s imports in 2024, and we leave that share 
unchanged because it was Saudi Arabia’s highest bilateral share during 2000-24. In 
practice, however, China’s share is likely to rise further if the recent trend continues. 
Exhibit 9 shows that, in this thought experiment, China’s share of global non-mineral 
imports could rise from 17.6% to 27.6%, an increase equivalent to 9.5pp of China’s GDP 
and 1.9pp of global GDP. If we hold China’s shares of US, Canadian, and Mexican 
non-mineral imports unchanged from their 2024 levels, the estimate falls to 23%. Under 
more conservative assumptions—holding China’s shares unchanged for the US, Canada, 
Mexico, and all other DM countries—the estimate falls further to around 19.2%, still 
noticeably above the actual 17.6% recorded in 2024.

For every 1pp increase in China’s share of global non-mineral imports, Chinese exports 
would grow by 5.5%. Combined with annual trend growth of 2.5% in global trade, this 
implies 8% annual growth in Chinese real exports in the near term, even under the most 
conservative scenario shown in Exhibit 9. Under similar assumptions but using the less 
conservative Scenario 2, China could see 8% annual real export growth through 2031. 
Taken together, our analysis suggests that Chinese export growth still has some runway 
over the next few years, despite the historically high trade surplus. However, pockets of 
vulnerability remain, particularly among low-income countries whose trade deficits 
exceed 50% of FX reserves. Chinese exporters may temporarily ease these 
balance-of-payments constraints through vendor financing, but extending the runway 
sustainably will require stronger economic growth in importing countries.
