Trade tensions notwithstanding, China has continued to deepen 
its presence in global supply chains by capturing value-added 
content in global imports and increasing exports  to EMs. We see 
this trend continuing, allowing China's global exports market 
share to rise to 16.5% by 2030, up from 15% now.  
China’s presence in global supply chains has been deepening: Despite the rise of 
protectionist measures, China has continued to deepen its presence in global supply 
chains. Its market share in global exports has risen by 2pp since 2017 and we 
estimate that its share of value-added content in global ex China imports has also 
risen by 2pp since 2017 in a broad-based manner across most manufacturing sectors. 
Leading with innovation and indigenous technologies: China is at the forefront of 
emerging sectors, leading with innovation and indigenous technologies. It plays a 
critical role in supplying low-cost components and high-end capital goods, 
supported ably by a domestic supply chain that is fully integrated. As we have 
highlighted before, China’s policymakers have already been planning for the next era 
of the industrial cycle. As we move into the embodied AI phase, China has already 
built up a dominant role in robotics and humanoids, and is working on applying 
embodied AI in an industrial setting (see China's Emerging Frontiers: China's Next 
Industrial Revolution, Sep. 9, 2026).
The US is still importing just as much value-added content from China, just not 
directly: The US’s tariff and non-tariff-related measures have reduced China’s share 
in US imports by 14pp since 2017, but we estimate China’s value-added content in US 
imports has actually held steady. In other words, the US is in effect importing 
indirectly from China. Having said that, the US has at least stabilized the share of 
Chinese content in its imports against a backdrop where China’s value-added 
content in the rest of the world’s imports has risen by 2pp in the same period. 
EMs are increasing their reliance on China: For the rest of the world, especially for 
EMs, their trade relationship with China continues to deepen. They are increasing 
their reliance on China to feed their domestic demand and are also relying on China 
to supply them with the parts and components needed to grow their exports basket. 
For instance, while India has made significant headway in electronics manufacturing, 
it still imports a large part of its intermediate and capital goods from China. 
Why China will continue to widen its lead in global exports share: We believe the 
combination of targeting high growth sectors, executing with an integrated supply 
chain, having a financial system aligned with strategic goals, focusing on R&D and 
innovation, and a large skilled talent pool mean that China remains well-positioned 
to deepen its presence in global supply chains. We continue to expect China will 
raise its market share in global exports from 15% currently to 16.5% by 2030.

Executive summary  
Efforts to reduce dependence on China have increased in recent years: The transition 
towards a multipolar world has intensified the focus on economic, national and tech 
security. As policymakers increasingly take up efforts to diversify supply chains and de-risk 
in strategic sectors, there is increasing focus on the bilateral trade balances (see quotes 
below). Faced with rising trade deficits with China, global trade partners – led by the US – 
have increasingly pursued ways to address this via tariffs and non-tariff barriers. But 
despite these measures, China’s trade surplus with the rest of the world has continued to 
rise over the past six years, underpinned by rising competitiveness in key emerging new 
sectors as well as relatively subdued domestic demand.  
“The world cannot have a China with a US$1.2 trillion trade surplus… The rest of 
the world is going to have to examine their terms of trade with China” – US 
Treasury Bessent, Aug-26
“Our trade deficit with China is now €1 billion euro — a day. It has reached a tipping 
point… It leads to de-industrialisation in the industrial heartlands of Europe. This is 
unsustainable.” - European Commission President von der Leyen, Sep-26
Going beyond bilateral trade balances - A holistic approach to assess dependence on 
China: Bilateral trade flows provide a useful starting point, but they do not capture the 
full extent of how the rest of the world is dependent on China. In an increasingly 
multipolar world, tariffs and other trade barriers can redirect trade through third 
economies, whether through shipping or conducting final assembly in third economies. 
This can obscure an economy’s importance in another economy’s production and 
consumption. To provide a holistic view, we make use of the world input–output tables, 
tracing the origins of the value added content in each economy’s imports and exports, 
including Chinese content that reaches its destination indirectly through other economies. 
This approach provides a more comprehensive assessment of China’s presence in global 
supply chains, helping to distinguish shifts in trade routes from underlying changes in 
dependence on China.
Our analysis complements the overarching conclusion from our global and China 
economics team’s work – that is, modern supply chains are geographically concentrated 
and technologically complex, requiring significant up-front investment to shift, with 
geopolitical distances between trade partners further complicating these shifts. In their 
analysis, our global economics team found that most low-hanging fruit from supply chain 
realignment opportunities has already been harvested. Instead, further rewiring of supply 
chains will take time to develop, introduce higher costs, and increase risk (see Global 
Economic Thematic: Multipolar World: Supply Chain Strain, Feb 17, 2025). For China, our 
findings also align with our China economics team’s view that China’s role in global trade 
is only changing form and not diminishing, from a “made in China” model towards a 
broader “made by China” model where products can be made elsewhere with increasing 
China value-added content (see Bluepaper: China's Next Industrial Revolution, Sep 9, 
2026).

We highlight 7 key conclusions: 
#1: Global reliance on Chinese value-added content continues to rise: China’s value 
added content share in global imports (excluding its own imports) has risen from 12.6% in 
2017 (before the onset of the first round of US-China trade tensions) to 14.3% in 2024. As 
importing destinations, the EU and the US have together accounted for ~80% of this 
increase, reflecting both their weight in global import demand and stable to rising Chinese 
content within their own imports. This is followed by Vietnam, India, Mexico and Thailand, 
reflecting greater use of Chinese inputs in these emerging manufacturing hubs.

#2 China’s value-added share gains have been felt across most manufacturing 
segments: Chinese value-added content share gains in global imports over the past 
decade have been broad-based, with gains in 10 out of the 13 broad manufacturing 
segments. Some of the largest gains have come in traditionally lower value-added 
segments in textiles, leather products, footwear and miscellaneous manufacturing, while 
China has also made further inroads into higher value-added segments. China now 
accounts for 33% value-added content share in electrical and optical equipment, 24% in 
machinery and equipment, and 10% in transport equipment. These patterns are consistent 
with China retaining higher-value production processes even as final assembly moves 
elsewhere, while China continues to supply the equipment and inputs needed by new 
manufacturing hubs.

#3: The US is still importing from China, just not directly: The US has actively diversified 
away from China as an import source, as evidenced by the decline in China's share in US 
imports from 22% in 2017 to 8% currently. However, the approach has been less effective 
when considering Chinese value-added content in US imports generally. While Chinese 
value-added content in US imports has not risen like it has in rest of the world's imports, it 
has also not declined over time. Instead, China’s share of value-added content in US 
imports has been broadly stable over the past few years, at 15.4% in 2024. 
This has also meant that 25% of Chinese content in US imports now first go through third 
economies (versus arriving bilaterally), up 10 percentage points since 2017. This increased 
use of indirect channels, particularly since 2022, is unique to US-China trade and is 
consistent with relocation of production serving the US market, while remaining reliant on 
Chinese inputs. We do not observe a similar shift towards indirect channels in imports of 
the rest of the world outside the US, nor for EU imports.

#5: More sectors in the US have increased rather than reduced their dependence on 
China: Since 2016, only five of the 13 manufacturing sectors in the US have recorded a 
material reduction in Chinese value-added content in their imports. Two of these, wood 
products and rubber & plastics, also saw declines in Chinese content outside the US, 
suggesting this is a broader global trend rather than a result of US policy actions. 
Electrical and optical equipment, chemicals, and leather products and footwear therefore 
stand out as the three sectors where US import shares fell while Chinese content 
increased elsewhere. In contrast, textiles, miscellaneous manufacturing, nonmetallic 
minerals, paper products and metals saw rising Chinese content in US imports, 
underscoring the uneven nature of the adjustment.

#7: Outlook: Diversification is achievable, but decoupling will be challenging: Our 
findings reaffirm our global economics team’s views that achieving actual supply chain 
realignment – especially in the context of decoupling from Chinese value-added content – 
will take time, introduce higher costs for corporates, and increase risks as well (see Global 
Economic Thematic: Multipolar World: Supply Chain Strain, Feb. 17, 2025). US reshoring 
efforts will likely remain narrow and require a front-loading of imports to establish 
modern capital stock. 
More importantly, China is now at the forefront of key emerging manufacturing sectors in 
terms of innovation and leads with indigenous technologies. China is increasingly playing a 
critical role in global supply chains, not just in low cost component supplies but also in 
high-end capital goods as well. Our China team also believes China’s advantage in global 
manufacturing will be strengthened as it enters a new phase of industrial development in 
the form of “Industry 5.0” (see China's Emerging Frontiers: China's Next Industrial 
Revolution, Sep. 9, 2026). These factors should ensure China’s value-added content share 
in not just US but also the rest of the world’s imports will continue to rise. 
Risks 
However, we must also acknowledge that part of the reason why China’s exports are 
competitive is because its companies appear to be comfortable with the trade-off 
between maintaining or gaining market share and the low expected returns that come 
with this approach. Policymakers expressed concern about excessive competition 
(involution) in July this year, which then coincided with a significant decline in 
manufacturing fixed asset investment. Yet the overall stance is still one of cutting back on 
older sectors, which face excess capacity, but continued investing in the new sectors that 
have been identified as future growth engines. 
In our view, the key risk is if China’s continued rise across its trade surplus, global export 
share and value-added content share trigger a significant escalation in protectionist 
measures, not just from the US, but also from other major trade partners. Policymakers 
are accelerating industrial policies globally, as perceived relative benefit of security over 
economic efficiency drives concerted efforts to derisk supply chains. 
The world input-output table – an important input for our analysis 
The world input-output table enables users to track transactions and inter-
dependencies across sectors and economies – to what extent the output of one 
sector in one economy becomes an input in another sector in another economy – 
and how these trends evolve over time. In this report, we utilize the version 
published by the Asian Development Bank (ADB), which covers 61 economies in 
the world (plus a rest of the world component), and 35 goods and services sectors. 
Importantly, the data is available up to 2024, giving us a more updated look of the 
map of the global production network. We also utilize a methodology as laid out 
by the Peterson Institute of International Economics (PIIE) in their report (linked 
here). 
One major advantage of input-output analysis is that it allows us to distinguish 
between gross exports and value-added content (gross exports less cost of 
intermediate goods and services bought). The input-output table allows us to

precisely tell what the value-added content from economy A is in in economy B’s 
imports, even if the value-added content has traveled through intermediate 
economies. Furthermore, it provides information on the end-use of inputs, i.e., for 
intermediate transactions that go into exporting, or for final use in domestic 
investment or consumption. 
For our analysis, its main advantage is that it allows us to estimate where the value 
embedded in trade was originally generated – not just which economy exported 
the finished product. By combining these tables with input-output calculations, we 
can estimate China’s contribution to another economy’s imports or exports, 
including Chinese value added that passes through several economies along the 
supply chain. For example, in a hypothetical case where Vietnam exports a $100 
product to the US containing $20 of Chinese value added, the analysis would 
attribute that $20 to China rather than treating the entire $100 as value generated 
in Vietnam.
Glossary for major indicators in this report:
1. Chinese value-added content in global ex-China imports: This is the value 
created in China contained in all goods and services that the rest of the 
world imports. It counts only the portion of those imports’ value created in 
China, and excludes any value created in other economies. This indicator 
captures both value created in China that is imported directly from China, 
as well as value created in China that is embedded in imports from other 
economies. 
2. Direct Chinese value-added content in US imports: The portion of value 
created in China contained in goods and services that the US imports 
directly from China. 
3. Indirect Chinese value-added content in US imports: The portion of value 
created in China contained in goods and services that the US imports 
indirectly from China, i.e., value created in China that is embedded in 
imports from other economies. 
4. Chinese value-added content in third economy exports to the US: The 
value created in China contained in the goods and services that another 
economy exports to the US. This captures China’s value contribution to 
that economy’s exports to the US, such as in intermediate inputs and 
services used.

#2 China’s value-added share gains has been felt across 
most manufacturing sectors  
China’s value-added share gains have been broad-based across both lower value-added 
sectors…: Since 2017, China has gained value-added content share in global imports 
across 10 of the 13 broad manufacturing sectors. Interestingly, the largest by sector share 
gains have come in lower-value added sectors such as textiles, leather products, footwear 
and miscellaneous manufacturing (such as toys). This contradicts the common thinking 
that China would lose competitiveness in lower value sectors to other emerging 
manufacturing hubs in ASEAN and India. Instead, China has moved to dominate higher

lower value-added processes, such as final assembly, to those economies. This has 
allowed it to increase its overall value-added shares in these sectors. 
…and higher value-added sectors: At the same time, building production capacity outside

intermediate inputs, which are increasingly sourced from China. China has also made 
significant inroads into these higher value-added sectors, and now holds 33% value added

in transport equipment.
This reaffirms our long-standing view that China’s strength in manufacturing is anchored 
by its industrial policy, which continually identifies new emerging high growth sectors in 
which to increase competitiveness, executed by a mature and integrated domestic 
manufacturing ecosystem, and backed a financial system aligned to these strategic goals.

#3: Reducing reliance on China: The US experience  of value-
added content
Value-added content share in US imports has not declined…: Our analysis on the flow 
in value-added content provides new evidence that confirms this conclusion. We find that 
despite the drastic changes at the bilateral trade level, China's share of value-added 
content in US imports in 2024 is actually more or less the same as it was in 2018, at 15.4%. 
In other words, the bilateral trade data not only overstates the degree of US-China 
decoupling, but that the US’s reliance on Chinese value-added content has not declined. 
Having said that, the US has at least stabilized the share of Chinese value-added content 
in its imports against a backdrop where China’s content in the rest of the world ex-US’s 
imports has risen by 2pp over the same period. 
…But is increasingly arriving indirectly via other economies: The findings also mean a 
growing portion of China’s value-added content is arriving into the US indirectly – i.e., via 
third economies – instead of via direct US-China trade. To find out just how much, we 
subtract the Chinese value add in US bilateral imports from China (i.e., direct Chinese 
value add) from the Chinese value add in US total imports (i.e., indirect Chinese value add). 
We find that the share of Chinese value-added content arriving indirectly into the US, 
expressed as a percentage of total Chinese value add in US imports, has risen from about 
18% in 2016 to 25%. Multiplying 0.25 with 15.4%, we know that China indirect value-added 
content now accounts for 3.9% of value-added content in US imports, up from 2.7% in 
2017. In USD terms, this is equivalent to $157bn. 
More than pure trade rerouting, we see genuine production relocation while still being 
dependent on Chinese inputs: This increase in proportion of Chinese value-added content 
arriving into the US came in two distinct phases – once in 2018 during the first round of 
US-China tariffs, as share rose from 18% to 20%, and the second time being a persistent 
increase since 2022 to current levels. Since the second phase of increase is much more 
material and also predates the tariff actions in 2025, this suggest to us the second phase 
has been driven more by genuine relocation of production serving US demand to third 
economies by corporates, which are still reliant on Chinese inputs, rather than pure trade 
rerouting. 
A situation unique to US-China trade: Additionally, we do not observe this same 
phenomenon when it comes to indirect Chinese value-added content in global ex-US 
imports. Instead, the ratio of indirect to direct Chinese value-added content in global ex-
US imports has been relatively stable.

#5: Which US sectors have reduced dependence on China? 
Which sectors have not?  
Only a handful of sectors have reduced their Chinese content: Of the 13 broad 
manufacturing sectors, only five in the US have seen more than a 1pp decline in China

#7: Outlook: Diversification is achievable, but decoupling will 
be challenging  
Decoupling will be challenging, reaffirming our global base case: Our findings above 
reaffirm our global economics team’s views that even as global pressures to de-risk supply 
chains continue to rise under a multipolar world, achieving actual supply chain 
realignment – especially in the context of decoupling from Chinese value-added content – 
remains challenging (see Global Economic Thematic: Multipolar World: Supply Chain 
Strain, Feb. 17, 2025). Modern supply chains are geographically concentrated and 
technologically complex, which in turn take significant up-front investment to shift, with 
geopolitical distances between trade partners further complicating these shifts. As such, 
further rewiring of supply chains away from China will take time to develop, introduce 
higher costs for corporates, and increase risks as well. 
For the US, limited onshoring will constrain the ability to decouple from China value-
added content: On the US front, our global economics team argues that true reshoring 
requires sustained growth in domestic capital stock and manufacturing capability. Given 
the US has such a shortage of modern capital stock after years of under-investment and 
such high import dependency, any reshoring efforts are likely to be narrowly focused, 
highly technical, and in any case require a front-loading of imports to establish modern 
capital stock (see Global Economic Briefing: Reshoring? Not Quite Yet, Jun. 9, 2026). 
China’s manufacturing strength underpins this challenge: Importantly, we believe China 
will continue to consolidate its dominance in global manufacturing, complicating any 
efforts to reduce reliance on Chinese value-added content. As we have been highlighting in 
past reports, China’s industrial policy-driven growth model has been built on anticipating 
global demand trends and building capacity to meet future demand in new emerging 
sectors (see Asia Economics: Why China will widen its lead in global manufacturing 
exports, Dec. 7, 2025). 
In the next phase, our China economics team sees China entering a new phase of industrial 
development in the form of “Industry 5.0”, featuring AI- and robotics enabled 
manufacturing. Even as China +1 strategy and reshoring efforts intensify, China’s upstream 
ecosystem strength will mean it will increasingly become a case of 'Made by China' rather 
than 'Made in China', with China supplying the components, machinery, production 
systems, technology, and service networks for manufacturing overseas (see Bluepaper: 
China’s Next Industrial Revolution, Sep. 9, 2026). By 2030, we project China’s market 
share in global exports will reach 16.5%, up from the current 15%. These factors will likely 
ensure China’s value-added content share in not just US but also the rest of the world’s 
imports will continue to rise.
