With the introduction of several action-based consumer agents in 
the last few weeks (Spear Street Technology’s Instinct; Meta’s Muse 
AI; etc.), the consumer AI landscape has begun the process of 
undergoing a fundamental paradigm shift, transitioning rapidly 
from passive, conversational chatbots to proactive, autonomous 
agents capable of executing real-world tasks. We see the industry 
moving from simply answering questions to completing complex, 
high-value personal workflows such as travel booking, bill 
negotiation, calendar management, shopping, and administrative 
tasks. 
Muse is Meta’s full scale personal AI agent app/website. We’d 
highlight a few key details/takeaways from the launch: 
Interface and Access: Users interact with Muse through 
n
dedicated iOS and Android applications, the web (muse.ai), and 
WhatsApp. Meta designed the product around existing 
messaging behaviors to minimize the learning curve. 
Capabilities: Powered by the Muse Spark 1.3 model family, 
n
which is optimized for long-horizon agentic tasks, Muse can 
autonomously browse the web, manage workflows, fill out 
forms, make purchases, and coordinate long-term goals. 
Monetization: Meta has initially launched the Muse product as 
n
a paid subscription (Meta’s first major foray into paid consumer 
AI subscriptions). Current subscription tiers include: Free Tier 
(capped at 100 million weekly tokens); Power Tier ($20/month 
for 500 million weekly tokens and faster response times); and 
Maximum Tier ($100/month for 3 billion weekly tokens and 
priority access). We see the potential for additional 
monetization avenues over time, including commerce (agentic 
commerce capabilities with potential to charge a take rate over 
time) and/or advertising.

Infrastructure: Each user’s agent runs inside an isolated Linux container on a 
n
dedicated virtual machine (Muse Secure VM) in the cloud, allowing it to continue 
executing tasks in the background after the user closes the application. Muse also 
has a number of partnership integrations to facilitate safe & secure interactions with 
various 3P networks, including most prominently Stripe (integration with Link’s 
wallet for agents to facilitate agentic commerce). 
Security & Privacy: The transition from information retrieval to autonomous 
n
execution introduces several security, privacy, and transactional challenges. To 
mitigate risks, Meta utilizes its Sentinel system, a system-isolated process that acts 
as the sole permission authority for network access and connector actions (with 
credentials and authentication tokens stored outside of the agent’s runtime). Meta 
has confirmed that Muse data/content is fully encrypted (using the same technology 
featured in WhatsApp) and not shared with the company (excluded from its 
advertising systems, with sanitized interaction data used for model training by 
default unless users manually opt out).  
We see a key advantage for Meta being scale & distribution (i.e. the ability to leverage 
billions of daily active users across Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, and WhatsApp) - by 
integrating identity, commerce, payments, communications, and advertising 
infrastructure into a single platform, Meta has a powerful user acquisition advantage 
that is difficult for startups/independent AI companies to replicate. Based on past 
corporate priorities, it is unlikely that Meta overly emphasizes monetization with 
such an effort in the early adoption period & instead anchors its strategic approach to 
this effort building scale of adoption among users. Lastly on consumer trust - we see 
any questions or hesitancy from consumers around data privacy and security as likely 
the biggest potential headwind for broad based adoption of consumer AI agents. We 
expect consistency of performance (lack of errors in sourcing products and transaction 
completion) to be the biggest factor that must be executed consistently to retain and 
build transactional momentum among platform users. 
In our note post its settlement of social media litigation a few weeks ago (link), we 
framed Meta as poised to begin to move from a “wall of worry” stock (around 
scaling/building compute given less visible return profiles vs. some of the other US 
hyperscalers) to a company that stood positioned to both productize and/or monetize 
that compute in a manner that could increase investor confidence. We reiterate this 
view with increased conviction with a clearer catalyst path for the shares going 
forward post settlement & Muse launch (Connect event on 9/23; potential for further 
foundational model releases, e.g. Watermelon; other initiatives aimed at the intersection 
of consumer, SMB & enterprise compute shifts toward AI). We reiterate our Buy rating & 
$725 PT.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
