Executive Summary: Building a better mousetrap to 
estimate the contribution of AI spending on growth  
Understanding the outlook for the US economy increasingly requires understanding AI 
spending and how it permeates the National Income and Product Accounts. While we have 
long sought to understand AI and its economic effects, the evolution of the AI investment 
cycle has created an opportunity to refine how we track its contribution to US GDP 
growth. In particular, we enhance our framework along three dimensions: the treatment of 
imports, the separation of AI from non-AI spending, and the distinction between spending 
on AI infrastructure build-out and AI adoption.
Our refined methodology provides a more comprehensive view of how AI spending is 
flowing through the US economy. It extends beyond the technology-focused categories at 
the center of our original estimates, to capture the broader infrastructure required to 
build AI capacity. Under this broader definition, we estimate that AI-related investment has 
contributed 0.6pp to annualized real GDP growth on average since 2025. Narrowing the 
lens to isolate AI-only investment still produces a sizable 0.4pp contribution. The 
framework also allows us to distinguish between the infrastructure build-out and AI 
adoption, providing a way to track the AI cycle as it moves from capacity creation to 
broader diffusion across the economy. 
Translating AI spending into growth is difficult since capex does not translate one-for-
one into US GDP.  Corporate capex measures what firms spend on long-term assets.  GDP, 
on the other hand,  measures final output of domestically produced goods and services. 
Spending on intermediate goods is therefore not counted, while spending on imported 
final goods and services is subtracted. In the case of AI investment, spending on imported 
equipment used in data center construction is included in corporate capex plans but does 
not contribute to US GDP. 
First, we improve the accuracy of our import offset. We adjust imports for re-exports, 
so that only imported goods that remain in the US are netted out of spending. Second, we 
estimate the share of those imports purchased by businesses for private fixed investment, 
as imports can also be purchased by consumers or governments. Third, we expand the 
adjustment beyond imports of computers and peripherals to also include semiconductors 
and related devices. Although the supply of chips and memory, and the associated 
inflationary pressures, have been widely discussed this year, semiconductor imports were 
previously much smaller and slower-growing than imports of computers and peripherals. 
But they have accelerated sharply since 4Q25, making them increasingly critical  to 
account for. Together, these changes provide a more precise bridge between the capex 
associated with the AI boom and the domestic investment recorded in GDP. 
Second, we now compute the contribution from AI spending to growth in two ways:
1. Under a broad definition, "AI-related" investment contributed 0.8pp to annualized 
real GDP growth in 1H26 and 0.6pp on average since 2025. "AI-related" investment 
captures spending that could be used for AI, whether or not it actually is. This is necessary 
because the NIPA accounts classify investment by asset rather than by end use: the 
business investment categories most exposed to AI therefore contain investment

supporting both AI and non-AI activities, although AI spending is driving a majority of the 
change in recent quarters.  Its contribution to real GDP growth therefore be viewed as an 
upper-bound estimate and a broad measure of where the AI investment boom is appearing

2. Using a narrower approach that  attempts to isolate "AI-only" investment, we find AI 
contributed 0.5pp to GDP growth in 1H26 and 0.4pp on average since 2025. "AI-only"

the AI boom. To do this, for each AI-related investment category in the NIPA accounts, we 
estimate a counterfactual path for how investment would have evolved in the absence of 
the AI boom; the difference between actual investment and this counterfactual is our 
estimate of the incremental investment attributable to AI.  
Third, we now distinguish between AI infrastructure spending and AI adoption 
spending. Infrastructure investment, primarily hyperscaler build-out by the "merchants of 
compute", creates the computing capacity required to develop and run AI models and 
applications. In contrast, spending by AI adopters, mainly through software, provides a 
window into the diffusion of AI across US businesses. The distinction is economically 
useful: the infrastructure build-out tells us about the expansion of AI's supply-side 
capacity, while adoption spending tells us how rapidly businesses are putting that capacity 
to use. Presumably, adoption-related spending will become a larger share of the story 
over time.
AI spending to date has been more about the build-out than adoption:
• Of the 0.5pp contribution to 1H26 real GDP growth from domestic AI-only 
investment, we estimate that 0.4pp came from AI infrastructure spending and the 
remainder came from AI adopter spending.  
• In nominal terms, AI-only investment reached an annualized $450bn in 2Q26, 
including $391bn in infrastructure and $59bn in software. 
• Our revised import estimates also suggest that AI infrastructure build-out has 
become increasingly reliant on domestic inputs since 2025, although more data are 
needed to determine whether this represents a sustained shift.

Both our measures focus on direct AI investment and likely miss broader spillover effects. 
The AI build-out can induce additional investment by construction firms, equipment 
suppliers, and other businesses supporting it. It can also generate employment and 
income that support additional spending, creating broader multiplier effects that are not 
captured by our measures of direct AI investment.
At the same time, AI-related investment is not necessarily entirely incremental. Absent AI,

capture some of this displacement through its counterfactual, but cannot fully distinguish 
new investment from investment redirected toward AI. Estimating that counterfactual

Introduction: Tracking AI's trail through the US economy  
AI investment has been booming with few signs of slowing in 2026. Not only have 
hyperscalers repeatedly raised their capital expenditure  plans, with greater shares of this 
spending financed through equity and debt offerings, but token usage by the rest of the 
business sector has gradually moved higher, pointing to greater usage and penetration. AI 
infrastructure investment so far appears relatively insensitive to the business cycle, 
geopolitical risks, and even interest rates, at least among firms with stronger credit 
quality. Understanding the outlook for the US economy requires a thorough 
understanding of AI spending and how it permeates the National Income and Product 
Accounts.
To better understand AI and its effects  and account for the evolution of the AI investment 
cycle, we have revisited our approach. In particular, we refine our framework to account 
for AI's contribution to GDP growth along three dimensions. First, we include more 
investment categories and improve our treatment of imports, which increases the 
accuracy of our estimates. 
Second, we now compute AI's contribution to growth in two ways:  a broad approach and a 
narrow approach. The broad approach captures spending that could be used for AI, 
whether or not it actually is, while the narrow approach attempts to estimate the portion 
of that spending actually attributable to the AI boom. The advantage of the broad 
approach is that it relies on officially published data and is model-free, but it has the 
disadvantage of deriving contribution estimates from spending in categories that include 
both AI and non-AI activities. The advantage of the narrow approach is that it provides a 
better estimate of the contribution from investment attributable to AI, but with the 
disadvantage of requiring modeling of the official data. Modeling brings with it some 
uncertainty. 
Third, we separate the contribution to growth from AI infrastructure spending and AI 
adoption spending to account for the evolution of the AI investment cycle from the build-
out to diffusion. In the coming years, we expect the relative importance to shift from 
infrastructure-related spending to adoption-related spending, and we propose a way to 
account for the differences.
Our broad approach – what we call AI-related investment spending –  estimates that AI 
has contributed an average of 0.6pp to annualized real GDP growth since 2025. Our 
narrow approach, which attempts to isolate AI-only investment spending, points to a 
contribution of 0.4pp.
This work augments our broader AI and Economic Transition work, where we have been 
tracking the AI cycle as it moves from infrastructure build-out to adoption and, ultimately, 
leaves its imprint on the broader economy (see our List of Reports: AI and Economic 
Transition  ). This note focuses on where AI spending is showing up in GDP. Our AI 
disruption tracker follows how AI is reshaping labor demand across occupations and 
industries, while our labor productivity tracker looks for evidence that greater AI diffusion 
is translating into productivity gains. We also draw on evidence from past innovation 
cycles and a theoretical framework to assess how the US economy may manage the 
transition under different diffusion speeds. Together, this work provides both our longer-

separate the contribution from AI-related spending from that of AI-only spending. We 
then distinguish between the contribution from the infrastructure build-out to the 
adoption cycle.

A more accurate treatment  of imports  
As we have noted in previous research, GDP accounting is different than corporate 
accounting. Capex spending announcements by US corporations, which have been 
dominated by hyperscaler spending and other merchants of compute, include all relevant 
spending on AI from a corporate balance sheet perspective. That said, GDP is a different 
measurement: it captures final spending on domestically-produced goods and services 
over a period of time. Estimates of GDP exclude spending on intermediate goods and 
imports. Put differently, GDP excludes "circular" spending between hyperscalers and 
spending that goes to support output abroad. A large share of hyperscaler investment 
consists of imported capital goods, such as semiconductors, memory, and servers. In 
addition, their spending plans include intermediate inputs, which do not map directly into 
measured investment in the national accounts.
Our previous approach: Useful but limited
Our previous approach estimated AI's contribution to real GDP by focusing on four 
categories of nonresidential business fixed investment including information processing 
equipment in equipment spending, software in intellectual property products, and data 
centers and power centers, both of which reside in structures investment. When it came 
to offsetting this spending by imports, we netted out total imports of computers and 
peripherals. We then summed the net contribution of these components and used that as 
our estimate of AI's contribution to growth in real GDP.
Relative to our previous methodology, we make three refinements to the import 
adjustment that we believe more accurately account for import behavior. First, we adjust 
imports for re-exports so that only imports remaining in the United States are netted out 
of spending. Second, we estimate the share of imported computers and peripherals used 
for private fixed investment rather than total domestic demand. Third, we expand the 
import adjustment beyond computers and peripherals to include semiconductors and 
related devices.
A refined treatment of imports part 1: Accounting for re-exports
Beginning with re-exports, we note two aspects of US trade that should be well known. 
First, the import content of purchases of computers and peripherals by US businesses is 
high. Second, the US is also a distribution, warehousing, and logistics hub for electronics. 
A computer, server, or peripheral can enter the US, be recorded by the Census as an 
import, sit in inventory or a distribution center, and subsequently be shipped abroad 
without undergoing meaningful transformation. The Census Bureau explicitly classifies 
such shipments as re-exports, which refer to imported goods that leave the US without 
substantial transformation.
Consider, for example, a Taiwanese manufacturer that ships $100mn of servers to its US 
subsidiary or distributor. Although the full $100mn is initially recorded as imports, assume 
$60mn is sold to US-based data centers and the remaining $40mn shipped to customers 
in Canada and Mexico. Because those servers leave the US in essentially the same 
condition in which they arrived, the $40mn is recorded as US re-exports rather than 
domestic absorption. Similarly, semiconductors imported into the US could subsequently 


[Page 8]
M
 Foundation
8
be re-exported to Mexico, assembled into servers, and then imported back into the US as 
part of the finished equipment. In this case, the value of the semiconductors would be 
captured once when they are initially imported and again as part of the value of the 
servers when they return to the US. Not accounting for re-exports  means we are netting 
out too many imports when assessing the contribution of AI to GDP.
To account for re-exports, we estimate the share of imports that remain in the US using 
Census Bureau data on imports and re-exports over an annual window. Because goods 
may be imported and re-exported multiple times, using an annual window reduces short-
term noise and provides a more reliable estimate of the share of imports that ultimately 
remains in the United States. Our estimates indicate that re-exports account for a 
meaningful share of imports of both computers and peripherals and semiconductors and 
related devices, as shown in Exhibit 3 .  In 2025, we estimate that only about 86% of 
imported computers and peripherals remained in the United States, while the 
corresponding share for semiconductors and related devices was approximately 53%.

A refined treatment of imports part 2: Estimating what goes to private 
fixed investment
Even after adjusting for re-exports, we were likely subtracting too many imports since not 
every computer and peripheral piece of equipment left in the US  goes to private fixed 
investment. Some are purchased by households and counted in personal consumption 
expenditures; some are purchased by federal, state, or local governments; and some may 
be purchased by businesses in ways that do not qualify as fixed investment.
To estimate the portion of imported computers and peripherals used for business 
investment, we estimate the share of computers and peripherals used for private fixed 
investment relative to total final sales of computers and peripherals. The estimate is 
based on the BEA's final sales of domestic computers tables and assumes that 
households, businesses, and governments purchase imported and domestically produced 
computers in the same proportions. Our estimates suggest that businesses account for

A refined treatment of imports part 3: Including semiconductors and 
related devices
Finally, we broaden the import adjustment to include semiconductors and related devices, 
which are also an important input into AI investment. Although the supply of chips and 
memory, and the associated inflationary pressures, have been widely discussed this year, 
the dollar value of imported semiconductors and related devices remains much smaller 
than imports of computers and peripherals, both in nominal and real terms. Moreover, 
imports of semiconductors and related devices did not accelerate until 4Q25, two years 
later than imports of computers, peripherals and parts. Their subsequent growth, 
however, has been significant—averaging a 57% annual rate in 1H26—and, together with 
the discussion around "chipflation," makes accounting for them increasingly important.  As 
an approximation, we assume that these imports are used entirely for private fixed 
investment, as households and governments are more likely to purchase final computing 
devices than semiconductors themselves. This refinement better captures the imported 
content of building AI hyperscalers than relying on computers and peripherals alone.

Where our refined estimates of import offsets are still lacking
Although our refined approach to accounting for imports in hyperscaler 
investment provides a more accurate estimate, it is still not perfect. In principle, 
the import adjustment should be comprehensive: for every detailed equipment 
category included in hyperscaler investment, the corresponding imports should 
also be accounted for. In addition to semiconductors, computers and peripherals, 
hyperscaler investment includes other equipment that could be imported, such as 


[Page 10]
M
 Foundation
10
communication equipment, engines and turbines, and other specialized machinery.
The challenge is that the investment and trade data are reported using different 
classifications. BEA reports imports by type of product, whereas NIPA investment 
is reported by asset class. As a result, there is not always a direct import 
counterpart to a detailed NIPA investment category. For example, engines and 
turbines appear as a distinct asset within NIPA investment, but the corresponding 
import data are available only within a broader industrial equipment category that 
also includes products unrelated to hyperscaler investment. Using the broader 
import category could therefore overstate the import adjustment, so we do not 
make an adjustment for these categories at present.
The resulting omission is likely to be moderate. Computers and peripherals 
account for roughly 60–70% of hyperscaler equipment investment and are also 
likely among its most import-intensive components. Our refined approach 
therefore captures the largest source of imported content, even though it does not 
capture all imported equipment used in the hyperscaler build-out.

From AI-related spending to AI-only spending
One challenge in estimating the effects of AI on activity is that the starting point may be 
too broad.  To some degree, this was by necessity. Splitting investment into its AI and non-
AI components is difficult since the National Income and Product Accounts (NIPA) 
accounts report investment by asset class – spending on equipment, intellectual property 
products, and structures –  rather than by end use. 
Our prior published estimates began with four categories that we believe contain large 
amounts of AI spending, including information processing equipment in equipment 
spending, software in intellectual property products, and data centers and power centers 
in structures investment. These categories are where AI investment likely shows up, but 
reflect investment regardless of whether the underlying activities are AI-related.  Hence, 
even with a more refined accounting for imports, we still begin the process of estimating 
the contribution of AI investment to GDP growth  with spending categories that include 
both AI and non-AI spending. We call this "AI-related" spending since we know it comes 
from both AI and non-AI sources, with the understanding that the acceleration in spending 
in these components likely reflects the AI boom. AI may be the main driver of the period-
to-period change, but, at a minimum, the base period level of spending contains a non-
trivial amount of non-AI activity.
We propose two adjustments to our prior starting point to improve our understanding of 
AI's effect on activity and derive what we refer to as "AI-only" spending. Put simply, these 
changes are intended to better isolate the direct contribution of AI spending to GDP 
growth without contamination from changes in non-AI spending. First, we expand our 
definition from four spending categories to eight to better capture the broad range of 
inputs used by hyperscalers in building the AI infrastructure. Second, we propose a 
method to isolate AI spending from non-AI spending in these categories. 
From four to eight: Capturing the full range of AI spending
Our updated methodology keeps two of the four categories mentioned above – software 
(IPP), data centers (structures), takes the sub-items of the other two – computers and 
peripherals, communication equipment from the information processing equipment 
(equipment),  electric facilities from power centers (structures), and adds  engines and 
turbines; special industry machinery; and electrical transmission, distribution, and 
industrial apparatus spending; each of which falls under industrial equipment spending. 
We believe these detailed NIPA items better align with the capital inputs required to build 
AI hyperscalers. We will also argue later in this report that we can use these categories to 
distinguish between AI spending that goes to support the infrastructure build-out and AI 
spending that reflects the broader diffusion of AI to the economy.

Isolating AI-only spending
With these eight spending categories in hand, we now separate AI spending from non-AI 
spending and narrow our focus from "AI-related" spending to "AI-only" spending. To do so, 
we estimate counterfactual paths for how investment would have evolved in the absence 
of the AI boom. We apply a similar approach to imports to estimate how they would have 
evolved absent the AI boom. We define the difference between the actual data and these 
counterfactual trends as AI-only investment. We do not believe this method to be 
foolproof, nor entirely accurate, but we believe it is likely closer to the truth than our 
prior estimates. 
What would investment have looked like without the AI boom?
For each of the eight detailed investment categories identified in Exhibit 7 , we estimate 
how investment would have evolved in the absence of the AI boom. To do so, we first 
construct a non-AI-related investment benchmark for each broad asset class. We compute 
real non-AI-related investment in structures, equipment, and intellectual property 
products by aggregating all detailed categories within each asset class other than the 
eight AI-related categories identified above, using the Fisher chain-weighted aggregation 
method.
For example, we detail our approach using computers and peripherals to illustrate how we 
construct the counterfactual path and estimate the portion of investment attributable to 
AI. We begin with how real investment in computers and peripherals moved with broader 
non-AI equipment investment before the recent AI boom. Using quarterly data from 3Q11 
through 3Q22, we regress real investment in computers and peripherals on a constant, 
quarterly seasonal dummies, the price index of computers and peripherals, and real non-
AI-related equipment investment. 
We then assume that this pre-AI relationship would have persisted in the absence of the 
AI boom and extrapolate the estimated relationship beyond 3Q22 to construct a 
counterfactual path for computers and peripherals. This becomes our estimate of how 
investment would have evolved absent the AI boom. The difference between actual

The explanatory variables serve two different purposes. Non-AI-related equipment 
investment captures broader movements in business equipment spending associated with 
the economic cycle and other common factors. The price index of computers and 
peripherals captures the longer-run relationship between falling technology prices and 
investment demand. For categories such as computers and peripherals, communications 
equipment, and software, quality-adjusted prices have declined substantially over the past 
several decades as production has become more efficient and product quality has 
improved. These secular price declines can themselves support greater investment and 
therefore need to be accounted for before we attribute the post-2022 acceleration to AI. 
Persistent declines in the cost of a technology relative to business output can be an 
important driver of its diffusion.

AI intellectual property products for software). The resulting counterfactual paths provide 
our estimates of investment that would have occurred absent the AI boom, with the gaps 
between actual and counterfactual investment representing AI-attributable investment.

AI-only spending reflects the gap between the actual and counterfactual 
spend
Not surprisingly, most categories of investment and imports show a meaningful deviation 
from their counterfactual paths following the onset of the AI spending boom, with the 
exceptions of electrical transmission, distribution, and industrial apparatus and imports of 
semiconductors and related devices. There is also considerable variation in when these 
deviations emerge. For example, data center investment began to accelerate in late 2022, 
whereas investment in engines and turbines did not pick up until mid-2025.
With the estimated dollar values of AI-only investment and imports, we can compute their 
contributions to real GDP growth. We allow each category to enter our measure of AI-
only investment at a different point in time, based on when its acceleration relative to the 
counterfactual path begins. Once a category is included, however, it remains in the 
measure even if actual investment subsequently falls below its counterfactual path. This 
allows the estimated AI-only component to increase or decrease over time rather than 
mechanically dropping an item whenever its actual value falls below the counterfactual. 
We exclude electrical transmission, distribution, and industrial apparatus from the real 
estimates for now, as there is no clear sign of acceleration. We continue to include imports 
of semiconductors and related devices, however, because semiconductors are a key input 
into AI hyperscalers even though their imports do not show a clear acceleration relative to 
the estimated counterfactual path.

Two caveats about our measures of AI investment's contribution to GDP are worth noting 
here. First, both AI-related and AI-only investment focus on direct AI investment and likely 
miss broader spillover effects. The AI build-out involves a wide range of players, from 
construction firms to equipment suppliers. These supporting businesses are likely to 
invest more as they expand capacity to support the build-out. In addition, the build-out 
can generate employment and income that support additional spending, creating broader 
multiplier effects that are not captured by our measures of direct AI investment.


[Page 19]

Second, AI-related investment is not necessarily entirely incremental. Absent AI, firms 
would presumably still have invested in other technologies, and some of that spending 
may instead have been redirected toward AI.  Estimating this counterfactual precisely is 
inherently difficult. Our AI-related investment estimates capture the gross contribution 
from AI-related spending, but not the investment that may have been displaced by the AI 
boom. Our AI-only measure may capture some of this displacement through its 
counterfactual, but cannot fully distinguish new investment from investment redirected 
toward AI.

AI infrastructure and AI adoption spending  
The final dimension of our framework to understand the effects of AI on economic activity 
is to distinguish between spending by companies responsible for the build-out of AI 
infrastructure – the hyperscalers, or merchants of compute – and spending by companies 
adopting AI. This differentiation is relatively straightforward to implement; we argue that 
software spending will capture AI adoption and its diffusion throughout the broader 
economy. The remaining categories largely capture infrastructure spending during the AI 
supply-side build-out. While AI infrastructure spending dominates the landscape today, we 
expect software spending to become a larger part of the story over time.
AI infrastructure investment 
Building hyperscalers requires a broad range of capital inputs, including chips, memory, 
servers, racks, power infrastructure, and cooling systems. Data centers and electric  
facilities capture the physical construction of data centers and associated electric 
infrastructure. Note that data center structures represent only the construction of the 
data center buildings themselves. The hardware and equipment installed in them are 
recorded under equipment in NIPA. Within equipment, we include  computers and 
peripherals and communications equipment to capture computing and networking 
hardware more accurately. We also identify engines and turbines, special industry 
machinery n.e.c., and electrical transmission, distribution, and industrial apparatus within 
industrial equipment. These categories capture the supporting mechanical and electrical 
systems needed to power and operate hyperscalers. 
Together, these categories capture the principal structures and equipment required to 
construct and operate AI hyperscalers, which are spread across several categories of 
nonresidential private fixed investment in NIPA as shown in Exhibit 7 . They extend 
beyond computing hardware to include the physical facilities, electrical infrastructure, and 
specialized industrial equipment necessary to support large-scale AI computing. 
AI adoption investment
We have confirmed with the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA)  that firms' spending on AI 
LLMs and applications, whether through subscriptions or application programming 
interfaces (APIs), is recorded as investment in software under intellectual property 
products. The majority of this spending is classified as prepackaged software. It is possible 
that some spending is classified as custom software because of differences in company 
classification and self-reporting in Census surveys. Therefore, software investment 
provides a broad measure of business investment in AI adoption.
What software investment captures
BEA also includes expenditures on software development in software investment. 
Therefore, strictly speaking, software investment captures both investment by 
developers of AI LLMs and other AI applications and spending by firms adopting 
these technologies. Because the NIPA accounts do not classify software 
investment by end use, we have not found a reliable way to separate software 


[Page 21]
M
 Foundation
Morgan Stanley Research
21
development from adoption spending. However, as we move further into the AI 
investment cycle and AI diffuses more broadly across the economy, we expect 
software investment to become increasingly representative of AI adoption 
spending.

Is the import content of AI infrastructure spending 
falling?
What has been surprising, in our view, is that the direct contribution of AI 
infrastructure spending to growth has been fairly steady in the three half-yearly 
estimates beginning in 2025, with the average contribution to growth in the 
neighborhood of 0.5pp, while the offset from imports has been moderating. The 
import offset in 1H25 was close to 0.4pp, but fell below 0.2pp by 1H26. 
We do not have a clear view on why the import content of AI infrastructure 


[Page 23]
M
 Foundation
Morgan Stanley Research
23
spending is declining. About two-thirds of this spending is dedicated to computers 
and peripherals, which have high import content. The recent decline in the import 
content of AI infrastructure spending may be statistical and could reverse in 
subsequent quarters. However, we suspect it may also represent hoarding effects 
given supply chain constraints, or it may also represent front-loading behavior in 
response to increased trade policy restrictions. We simply don't know and raise the 
point as an unexplained stylized fact worth further research.
...while the AI-adoption contribution has been more volatile
The contribution to real GDP growth from software – what we refer to as AI adoption 
spending – has also picked up, though with some volatility ( Exhibit 24 ). As noted above, 
we acknowledge that a portion of spending on software is likely related to its own 
research and development and not solely spending by non-developers on AI adoption. 
That said, we expect the share of AI adoption spending in software to increase over time 
as the technology improves, firms learn how to apply AI efficiently, and diffusion spreads. 
In 2024, the contribution of software spending to real GDP growth averaged 0.21pp. In 
2025, the contribution slipped to 0.15pp on a weak second half which, in our view, may 
reflect volatility in software prices (see box below). In 2026, the contribution of software 
as a proxy for AI-adoption spending was 0.18pp. On net, this suggests that AI adoption 
spending has not accelerated markedly and has been contributing somewhat less to 
economic growth than  AI infrastructure spending. In our view, this should not come as a 
surprise given the early stages of the AI investment cycle. We think the growth rate in AI 
infrastructure spending is likely to decelerate in 2027 and 2028, while the growth rate of 
AI adoption spending should remain more consistent, if not pick up.
Rising software prices in 2025 dented the contribution of AI 
adoption spend
We note that movements in software prices may have played a role in increasing 
the volatility of the contribution of AI adoption spending to growth. The software 
price index was volatile during 2024 and 2025, which affected real software 
investment. In particular, software prices increased meaningfully in 2H25, weighing 
on the level of real software investment and therefore likely narrowing its 
estimated gap relative to the counterfactual path. So far in 2026, the software 
price index appears to have moved back toward its previous trend.

real dollars. Both approaches, however, make it more difficult to preserve a transparent 
decomposition across the individual investment components. For illustrative purposes, in 
Exhibit 26 we therefore stack the real values to show the relative importance of 
computers and peripherals in our estimates of AI infrastructure investment. Computers 
and peripherals account for roughly 60–70% of the total, underscoring their importance

A nominal cross-check 
The drawback of working with real values at the component level is that chain-weighted 
real spending cannot generally be added across categories, nor can meaningful 
component shares be calculated directly from those real values. In addition, corporate 
spending announcements and equity analyst estimates are usually reported in nominal 
terms. To facilitate comparisons, we therefore repeat the same exercise using nominal 
spending data, with the main difference being that price indices are no longer included in 
the regressions. 
The nominal results are broadly similar to those obtained using real values. The primary 
difference is electrical transmission, distribution, and industrial apparatus, for which 
nominal investment stays meaningfully above  its counterfactual path. We therefore 
include this category in the nominal estimates even though it is excluded from the real 
estimates. The divergence between the nominal and real results suggests that at least 
part of the recent increase in spending on this category reflects higher prices rather than

an increase in the volume of investment.

Based on 2Q26 data, we estimate that nominal AI-only investment reached an annualized 
rate of $450 billion. Of that total, $391 billion was attributable to AI infrastructure, while 
about $59 billion was attributable to AI adoption.
Because no official measure of AI investment exists, it is useful to benchmark our 
estimates against independent industry estimates. Our equity analysts publish annual 
hyperscaler capex estimates and forecasts, along with a bottom-up breakdown of 
spending by component. These estimates focus on hyperscaler capex and therefore 
correspond most closely to our measure of AI infrastructure investment rather than AI 
adoption investment. Because corporate capex estimates capture total spending

regardless of where the underlying capital goods are produced, we compare them with 
our gross AI infrastructure investment before subtracting imports.
Our equity analysts estimate that hyperscaler capex totaled about $426bn in 2025. While 
our estimates are somewhat lower in dollar terms, the two measures are not directly 
comparable. Corporate capex measures companies' total capital spending, whereas NIPA 
measures investment in final goods and services and excludes intermediate transactions 
that may occur between hyperscalers and their suppliers. Notwithstanding these 
conceptual differences, we think it is notable that the composition of spending in our 
NIPA-based estimates and the bottom-up hyperscaler capex estimates is remarkably 
similar. We estimate that computers and peripheral equipment accounted for 63% of 
nominal AI infrastructure investment in 2025, broadly in line with our equity analysts' 
estimate that roughly 60–70% of hyperscaler capex was spent on servers, racks, and 
memory.
