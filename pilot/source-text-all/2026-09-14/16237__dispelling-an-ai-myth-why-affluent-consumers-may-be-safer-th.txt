Key Takeaways
AI will affect consumers simultaneously through several channels, with labor 
markets, asset markets, and prices all playing key roles. Exposure to each of 
these varies widely across demographic groups. 
For college-educated, high-income, city-dwelling (“CHIC”) households, elevated 
equity wealth relative to labor income makes asset markets an important driver 
of spending. Relatively modest increases in asset markets can offset the spending 
effects of temporary declines in labor income. 
Career stage is critical to understanding overall risks. Established CHIC workers 
likely have greater exposure to augmentation and wealth offsets, while younger 
consumers may be more vulnerable to displacement. Given that this younger 
cohort accounts for less spending, the near-term drag on aggregate consumption 
is likely smaller. 
In our base case for AI and economic transition, task creation and wealth effects 
offset labor displacement, resulting in an increase in output and income. CHIC 
households reap the largest gains, and income and wealth inequality widen. 
In our bear case, rapid diffusion leads to higher unemployment, with more 
extreme outcomes when accompanied by asset market declines. CHIC 
households are first affected by displacement, but the resulting aggregate 
demand shock spreads the weakness across cohorts, and monetary and fiscal 
policy responses will be key.

Executive Summary  
 
Key takeaway: In our base case outlook for AI adoption and economic transition, AI 
boosts productivity without a large rise in unemployment, as task creation and 
wealth effects are offsets to labor market disruption. In this outcome, we expect AI 
to disproportionately benefit college-educated, high-income, city-dwelling ("CHIC") 
households over the longer run, a contrary view to the conventional wisdom that 
white-collar households are the most AI-vulnerable cohort. This is based on our 
findings across three key channels: 
Labor markets: CHIC workers have high occupational exposure to AI. This brings 
risk but also  opportunity; these workers are exposed to displacement, but also  to 
productivity gains and new jobs. 
Wealth: CHIC households, and particularly older CHIC households, have greater 
equity exposure, resulting in larger spending impacts for these households from AI-
driven gains in capital returns. 
Inflation: CHIC households are  more exposed to longer run AI-driven disinflation in 
services and less exposed to potential near term AI-related inflation pressures.
Importantly, among CHIC households, outcomes likely vary  by career stage. 
Established, older professionals  may be  well positioned to capture AI-related 
productivity and asset-price gains. Highly-educated younger professionals  may be 
more vulnerable, as AI may substitute for entry-level tasks with less offset from 
wealth. These distinctions matter for macroeconomic transmission: weakness 
among younger entrants would likely have a smaller  effect on aggregate  
consumption than a comparable disruption among established  households who 
account for a large share of spending. Overall, AI could weaken career formation 
without adversely affecting the CHIC cohort as a whole, widening income and 
wealth inequality over time, much as the  internet and digital innovation wave did.
Conventional discussions of the effects of AI  on different cohorts of consumers have 
focused primarily on occupational exposure, often culminating in a fear of white-collar job 
loss. This is only one part of the story. We broaden the analysis to three channels through 
which AI can affect consumer spending: labor markets, financial wealth, and inflation. 
Across demographic groups, consumer exposures to each of these will vary. 
AI "exposure" is not inherently positive or negative.  Greater occupational exposure, for 
example, could mean greater risk of displacement or larger income gains from 
productivity-driven wage growth. As this is the fifth note in our AI and Economic 
Transition series, we build off results of our prior work. In our last note, we used a general 
equilibrium model to form base and bear case outlooks around AI. Our base case assumes 
AI diffuses twice as fast as the internet and digital age. In this scenario, AI boosts 
productivity without triggering a downturn; a temporary rise in unemployment is offset by 
real wage gains and new job creation, resulting in output and income gains. Our bear case

The second transmission channel is through financial wealth: Higher corporate profits 
or valuations from AI could lead to wealth gains, but with the risk that AI-related 
overinvestment could bring a downturn.
CHIC households, and especially older CHIC households, have more equity exposure, both 
in absolute terms and as a share of their own net worth. The top 20% income cohort, the 
college-educated group, and  the 55+ age group all account for around 80+% of equity 
exposure among households. Among lower-income and less-educated households, a 
smaller share have any equity exposure at all. As a result, changes in equity valuations can 
more easily drive changes in net worth, and thus changes in spending, for CHIC 
households. 
The final transmission channel we consider is through inflation: Near-term demand 
pressures from AI could lead to higher prices, but in the longer-term, productivity gains 
could result in disinflation. 
We expect electricity, software, and chip-related goods to drive near-term AI-related price 
pressures, even if those pressures are somewhat limited. Given these categories 
(especially electricity) make up larger shares of spending for low-income, less-educated, 
and younger households, these groups are likely more exposed. Meanwhile, if longer-term 
disinflation first stems from industries with higher adoption rates, and thus materializes in 
services categories such as financial and communication services, higher-income 
households will benefit first.

Combining these findings, CHIC households are more exposed to labor displacement, but 
also to AI's potential upside:  productivity-driven wage growth, new job creation, wealth 
effects, and disinflation. Lower-income, less-educated consumers are at less risk of 
displacement, but face more near-term price pressures and stand to benefit less from AI-
related job and wealth creation. 
Net Effects: CHIC households benefit most in the base case 
as wealth effects and task creation offset labor displacement 
The next question is how these exposures will balance out. As described above, our base 
case is that AI will raise productivity, and positive effects from wage growth, task creation, 
and wealth effects will offset the rise in unemployment. In this scenario, we expect CHIC 
households to benefit most. Some high-income, highly-educated workers may face 
temporary periods of softer consumption, especially younger workers that likely have 
greater risk of displacement and lower accumulated equity wealth. That said, for this 
group's spending as a whole, substantial levels of equity wealth relative to labor income 
mean that relatively modest gains in asset prices can offset the spending effects of 
declines in labor income. In our base case, CHIC households are best off, reaping the 
largest gains from financial asset appreciation and also from eventual wage gains and 
disinflation.

 While income and wealth inequality widen in this scenario, it does not 
mean low-income or less-educated consumers are worse off in 
absolute terms. These consumers could benefit from eventual 
broadening out in wage gains and disinflation, or from monetary 
easing that may accompany any initial displacement. 
In our bear case, recession-like outcomes are possible as rapid AI 
diffusion means job creation cannot keep up with job displacement.  In 
this scenario, we expect CHIC households to experience the initial 
adverse effects because of their greater occupational exposure. If 
displacement is accompanied by asset market declines, the downturn 
would be much more extreme. Given these households control a large 
share of total spending, weaker demand would ultimately spread the 
shock across the broader economy. The initial intuition might be that 
white-collar workers would be most affected, but  the net effect, in 
our view, would be worse outcomes for all cohorts. The ultimate 
distributional effects would depend not only on the pace of AI adoption and asset market 
outcomes, but also on  monetary and fiscal policy responses.

Setting the Stage: Our Framework and Assumptions  
Grounding in our Prior Work: Our Base Case for AI
For this analysis, we build off  the results of our prior work on AI and Economic Transition. 
In our recent note, we used a general-equilibrium framework to assess potential labor 
market outcomes of AI. In doing so, we found the amount of AI disruption is largely 
dependent on three forces: 1) the speed of AI adoption, 2) the speed and quantity of new 
task creation, and 3) indirect feedback loops such as wealth effects. 
These results are intuitive. Slower diffusion gives workers more time to adapt, faster task 
creation creates new employment opportunities, and stronger asset markets support 
demand, and in turn, labor demand. As a result, assuming faster diffusion or excluding 
offsets from task creation and wealth effects leads to much more extreme conclusions. 
In our "base case", we assume a moderate speed of diffusion, which is still  2x that of the 
Internet (e.g. AI diffuses in about a decade versus two previously). We link the pace of task 
creation to the speed of diffusion and assume wealth effects that are smaller than those 
observed historically, to be conservative.
In this scenario, the outcome is relatively optimistic; AI boosts productivity without 
triggering a downturn. While there is some temporary labor displacement, with the 
unemployment rate rising by around 0.5pp at the peak, real wage growth accelerates, 
employment is rebalanced across occupations, productivity supports disinflation, and 
profits rise. The net outcome is faster output and income growth.  
Our "bear case" is based on a scenario of rapid AI diffusion, in which full adoption takes 
only six years, much faster than any historical precedent. Here, recession-like outcomes 
are possible as fast adoption means job displacement far outweighs job creation in the 
short term. Dependent on the size of wealth effects assumed, the unemployment rate 
rises by anywhere from 0.6pp - 4.1pp at its peak. The increase in unemployment is due to 
two drivers: 1) a mismatch in skills between workers and available jobs, and 2) demand-
side weakness after the initial employment shock.

While the above model helps us develop an aggregate base case scenario, the model

assumes a "representative household". It therefore does not capture the  behaviors and 
outcomes of different cohorts of consumers. 
Consumer subgroups differ in the types of work they do, their levels of income and 
wealth, their responses to changes in the economic backdrop, and the goods and services 
they purchase. As a result, the effects of AI  will not be uniform. In this note, we focus on 
differential effects across four demographic factors: income, education, region, and 
age. 
In each section, we show as much detail as the data allow, but we cannot break down 
every channel of exposure across all demographic subgroups.  That said, we find some 
common threads: the exposures and potential outcomes for  college-educated, high-
income, city-dwelling consumers, which we refer to as "CHIC" households, tend to be 
similar. Meanwhile, the effects for low-income, non-college-educated, and rural consumers 
tend to be similar. This makes sense given the overlap among these groups.  CHIC 
households tend to work white-collar jobs, are more sensitive to asset market moves, and 
spend relatively more on discretionary services and big-ticket items. Meanwhile, the 
opposite is true for the second group; these households are more dependent on labor 
market income and spend relatively more on essentials ( Exhibit 7 ). 
Further distinction comes when we include age.  Within our CHIC group, we expect 
different outcomes for older, more experienced workers versus younger workers. First, 
while the labor market exposures of two individuals in the same occupation will appear 
the same, in reality tasks likely differ by career stage. Wealth levels  also vary greatly by 
age, even for consumers with similar income and education levels, as do spending 
patterns.

Three Key Transmission Channels


[Page 10]
M
 Foundation
10
To organize our analysis, we focus on three key  transmission channels, each of which plays 
an important role in the general equilibrium model: 1
1) Labor markets. AI can affect labor markets in several ways. First, it could be possible 
that some tasks are cheaper to perform with AI rather than human labor, and if so, this 
could lead to labor displacement. Second, AI could enhance consumers' productivity, 
allowing them to produce more output with the same number of hours worked. If so, this 
could lead to stronger wage growth. Third, new tasks and jobs could be created related to 
AI, increasing employment in certain industries or creating new industries entirely. 
For consumers: The net effect of these three forces will determine  total labor income,  the 
main driver of spending. If the income lost due to labor displacement is larger than the 
income gained from productivity-driven wage growth and new jobs, then labor income will 
decline, but the opposite could be true as well. 
2) Wealth. If AI boosts productivity, firms may produce the same output at a lower cost, 
increasing profits. Even before profits rise, optimism around AI could lift valuations.  In a 
bear scenario, fears around AI or overinvestment could result in asset price declines. 
For consumers: If corporate profits or valuations rise, consumers with equity exposure 
(either directly or through retirement accounts) will experience increases in wealth. 
Studies have shown that when a consumer's  wealth rises, their spending typically does as 
well; it is estimated that for every dollar increase in permanent wealth, consumers 
typically spend 3-5 cents more.
3) Inflation.  AI's effect on inflation is likely  two-fold. In the near term, increased demand 
for electricity, software, and chip-related goods (phones, tv, household appliances, and 
cars) could result in larger price increases. In the medium to longer term, productivity 
gains could lower production costs, slowing inflation or even reducing prices. 
For consumers: Higher prices in the near term can weigh on consumer purchasing power, 
reducing the quantity of goods and services consumers can buy. In the longer term, 
disinflation could have the opposite effect. For consumption, we typically compare income 
growth vs. inflation: if income growth is outpacing inflation, consumers have more 
purchasing power.
The next three sections dive into each of these channels, and the exposures of each 
demographic group, in more detail.
1. These three  are not the only transmission channels. Changes in labor markets and inflation could lead to changes in monetary and fiscal policy, which can  further affect 
spending. Agentic commerce can change the way consumers shop, or which items they decide to buy. AI developments in mortgages or other consumer debt products can help 
facilitate debt consolidation and  personal finance decisions. All of these can affect consumer balance sheets and spending, but for this analysis we limit our focus to these three 
primary channels.

Occupational Exposures by Cohort
We first look at which consumers are most exposed to AI in their occupations. To do so, 
we define occupational exposure based on a framework developed by Felten et al. (2021). 
Felten and co-authors construct an AI exposure index by assessing how tasks and abilities 
within each occupation align with current and anticipated AI capabilities. For example, an 
accountant may perform tasks related to classification and document recognition, which 
AI is likely capable of. After mapping AI capabilities to the tasks within each occupation, 
the exposure score is calculated as a weighted average.

 We group occupations into three categories based on their exposure 
values: the bottom 25% of exposure scores are considered "low 
exposure", the middle 50% are "medium exposure", and the top 25% 
are "high exposure." Because occupation sizes differ, workers are not 
distributed 25/50/25 across the three groups: about 18% of workers 
are in "low exposure" occupations, 56% are "medium exposure", and 
26% are "high exposure" ( Exhibit 10 ). 
The occupations within each grouping are largely intuitive: high 
exposure occupations are typically white collar jobs involving 
information processing, quantitative analysis, and standardized 
decision making, while low exposure occupations are predominantly 
blue collar jobs that rely heavily on human presence, physical work, 
and dexterity.  Importantly, this indicates only measured exposure, 
which can lead to either automation or  augmentation; AI could either 
replace a task or enhance a worker's productivity in performing it.

group that had median income of $97k ( Exhibit 11 ). This implies  the median worker in low 
exposure occupations was around the 30th percentile of income, while the median worker 
in high exposure occupations was around the 60th percentile.

Occupational Exposure: Positive in our base case, with risks 
in scenarios of rapid diffusion 
Whether high occupational exposure to AI is a  net positive or net negative is not a 
foregone conclusion, as we see three potential channels through which AI could affect 
labor markets: 1) labor displacement  could lead to income loss, 2) faster wage growth 
from productivity gains could raise earnings, and 3) new job creation could create new 
income opportunities. 
Displacement
The first potential transmission channel of AI labor effects to consumers is through 
displacement. In our recent AI labor market tracker, we found evidence that 
unemployment is rising more in highly AI-exposed occupations. In this high exposure 
group, defined the same as we did above, unemployment is about 0.5pp above where 
cyclical trends say it should be. While some of this could be due to non-AI factors, we 
think there is some evidence that AI is contributing to more layoffs and slower rehiring in 
these occupations.
Given our analysis  above of which consumers are in these occupations, if displacement 
rises faster than these workers can be reabsorbed,  CHIC households will  be most at risk. 


[Page 16]
M
 Foundation
16
Within this CHIC group though, we find further distinction. So far, there has been more 
evidence that younger consumers within these occupations have seen larger displacement 
effects. We think this makes sense given entry-level tasks are likely more exposed to 
automation from AI, rather than augmentation. As a result, we would specifically expect 
young CHIC households to be most exposed to potential labor displacement. 
Productivity-Driven Wage Growth
 As described previously, in our base case AI leads to faster wage growth, driven by 
productivity gains. This is in line with historical data, as over the past 20 years, in any 
given quarter, industries with larger productivity gains also tended to have larger real 
wage gains ( Exhibit 19 ). 
We recently found that industries more exposed to AI have experienced faster 
productivity growth over the past year. We expect this to continue, and if so, workers  in 
these industries will likely benefit more from the productivity-wage growth channel.  So 
far, there is some evidence here, but it is not definitive; industries more exposed to AI have 
relatively strong absolute wage growth, but some less exposed industries have 
experienced larger recent accelerations  ( Exhibit 20 ). 
If wage gains accrue to those in high exposure occupations,  CHIC households  would 
benefit more (assuming these workers remain in highly exposed occupations throughout 
any reshuffling that occurs). The wage gains would likely accrue to those for which AI 
augments rather than automates their tasks, potentially leading  to more gains for 
experienced workers within this group.

What About Embodied AI/Robotics?
William Tackett 
The exposure analysis above covers agentic and software AI, which operates in the 
digital, non-physical world and skews toward higher-income, college-educated, 
urban coastal workers. Embodied AI, meaning robotics and physical automation, 
hits close to the mirror image of that group: the manual-labor occupations least 
exposed to agentic AI. These workers handle physical tasks like manipulation, 
movement, and operation in messy environments, exactly what robotics targets. 
Timing is an important caveat. Software AI tends to diffuse faster, as scaling an 
additional instance is generally cheaper than deploying physical hardware. We 


[Page 19]
M
 Foundation
Morgan Stanley Research
19
expect robotics to move slower, weighed down by hardware costs, safety 
requirements, heavy capital intensity, and a shortage of the real-world physical 
interaction data that robotics models need to train on. Our Global Robot Model 
projects just 35 million total robots in the US by 2030, up from 15 million in 2026, 
a notable increase but not enough to seriously dent the labor force, and these may 
well add to productivity rather than replace workers one for one. Humanoid-
specific robots do not reach 1 million in the US until 2035. That longer runway 
gives the manual-labor cohort time to adjust, and as with agentic AI, exposure can 
mean augmentation, think cobots and exoskeletons, not just automation.

Income and wealth gains will ultimately matter relative to the changes in prices 
consumers face. If AI leads to higher inflation, consumers will have less purchasing power. 
Meanwhile, if AI leads to price declines, the quantity of goods and services consumers can 
buy will increase.  
Near term demand side pressures
Stronger demand for goods and services related to the AI infrastructure build or AI 
adoption could lead to near-term inflationary pressures. If so, we would expect to see 
these price increases in  electricity, software, and categories of goods exposed to chips 
(phones, televisions, household appliances, and cars). Many of these  categories have 
relatively small weights in  both PCE and CPI prices. As a result, we are still optimistic 
about disinflation this year and next in our base case. We expect payback from tariff 


[Page 26]
M
 Foundation
26
inflation to more than offset these AI pressures, while other components, such as shelter, 
continue to soften. The AI pressures are therefore not large enough to change our base 
case expectation  that the Fed will remain on hold this year. 
That said, there is upside risk to these inflation forecasts. Software prices, for example, 
accelerated more than we expected earlier this year, boosting core inflation noticeably 
even despite its small weight. Upcoming methodology changes will likely put downward 
pressure on software inflation rates, but there could be similar larger-than-expected 
acceleration in other categories. We have also already seen more pressures in some AI-
related categories within PPI. These higher input costs will likely drive either margin 
pressure or further consumer price increases, which could be larger than we expect. While 
there is uncertainty around the magnitude, these near-term price increases  could weigh on 
consumer purchasing power and lead to softer demand. 
In the below exhibits, we combine electricity, vehicles, computers, telephones, computer 
software, game consoles, and major appliances to create an "AI-exposure" basket. We then 
look at what share of spending this basket, and different parts of the basket, makes up for 
different cohorts. If items with higher inflation rates make up a larger share of spending 
for a given consumer, then their experienced inflation rate will be higher. 
By income group, exposure is relatively even for the basket as a whole, with these AI-
related items making up around 10% of spending.  However, the composition differs. 
Telephones, computers, major appliances, software, and game consoles altogether make 
up right around 1% of spending across cohorts. Spending on vehicles is a larger share for 
upper-income cohorts (8% for upper versus 5% for lower) while spending on electricity is 
a larger share for lower-income cohorts  (2% for upper versus 4% for lower). 
So far, we see more evidence of AI-related pressures on electricity prices than on car 
prices. For vehicles,  higher chip costs have not yet been passed through to consumer 
prices and have instead been absorbed by OEM margins. We expect this to continue in the 
near term, though there is more uncertainty about future pass through. For electricity, we 
see evidence that in areas with higher data center concentration, electricity prices have 
risen more.  While we do not expect a sharp increase in electricity prices because of AI, we 
do expect electricity prices to remain sticky and above the pre-Covid trend. 
If our assumptions around vehicles and electricity are correct, in the near term, low-
income cohorts are likely more exposed to these AI price pressures. High-income 
consumers also have more ability to trade down in times of temporary price increases, 
consistent with the data which shows experienced inflation rates of low-income 
consumers tend to be higher when inflation is above target.

and lower rates of college-educated households as compared to the national average. 
Longer term disinflation

enhancements should not boost inflation longer term. In our base case, AI  productivity 
gains lead to disinflation, and this is likely led by industries with higher adoption rates. So 
far, AI adoption rates are highest in the information technology industry and in services 
industries such as financials and communication services ( Exhibit 45 ).

Bringing it All Together  
Overall, CHIC households are more exposed to labor displacement, but also more exposed 
to productivity-driven wage growth, new job creation, wealth effects, and longer-term 
disinflation. Lower-income, less-educated households are less exposed to displacement 
but also less exposed to the upside channels and more exposed to near-term inflationary 
pressures. We  now assess how these opposing forces balance for each cohort in our base 
and bear case  AI scenarios. 
Base Case: Task creation and wealth effects offset labor 
displacement, and CHIC households benefit most
As described previously, in our base case for AI, we expect a temporary increase in 
unemployment (+0.2-0.5pp at the peak), but this is more than offset by an acceleration in 
wage growth, employment gains from new occupations and tasks, disinflation, and 
positive wealth effects. Past innovation waves point to a similar outcome, but this is still a 
relatively optimistic scenario in which output and income ultimately increase. Below, we 
use data on income and wealth levels to  quantify this mechanism and assess how 
different cohorts will fare.  
Overall, we consider labor income the main driver of consumer spending, as the marginal 
propensity to consume (MPC) out of labor income is relatively high. That said, large 
enough changes in wealth can also drive meaningful changes in spending.  For different 
income groups, the importance of these two drivers will vary.

Of course, much larger increases in unemployment could lead to nonlinear effects, and 
aggregate spending outcomes do not capture potential welfare effects at the individual 
level. At the aggregate level, these data confirm the finding from our general equilibrium 
model; in terms of spending and overall economic outcomes, feedback effects from AI 
(including asset market moves) are just as important as initial productivity and 
displacement effects. While it might be intuitive that AI could lead to white-collar job loss 
and thus weaker high-income spending, these data point to the importance of considering 
all transmission channels in the outlook for this cohort.  
In the base case outcome described above, CHIC households benefit most. These 
consumers ultimately experience the largest increase in resources due to their higher 
exposures to equity prices, productivity gains, new tasks, and disinflation. Within this 
group, some consumers (potentially younger households, who are more exposed to 
displacement and less exposed to asset markets) might be worse-off in the short-term, 
but others will be better off. Overall, spending for this cohort rises most and income and 
wealth inequality widen, as was the case in prior innovation waves. 
Given these CHIC households account for a large portion of consumer spending, positive 
outcomes for this group as a whole mean that the overall drag on spending from 
displacement should be more modest. CHIC households also drive even larger shares of 
discretionary spending categories, including big-ticket items like furniture and vehicles, 
luxury goods, and recreational services such as entertainment. Our positive outlook for 
the high-end consumer means spending in these discretionary spending categories should 
remain robust.

This outlook does not mean low-income or less-educated cohorts will be worse off in 
absolute terms. Disinflation should still support consumption for these consumers, and 
wage gains may broaden across industries. In addition, any temporary increase in 
unemployment could potentially lead to rate cuts, benefitting these consumers who tend 
to have more short-term and floating rate debt. That said, in order to get a scenario where 
low-income or less-educated groups are relatively better off, it would likely need to be the 
case that new jobs created (that we cannot yet conceptualize) lean towards the skills of 
these cohorts, or certain fiscal policies are put in place that directly benefit these 
consumers. 
Bear Case: Rapid AI diffusion leads to more extreme labor 
displacement, followed by a demand shock that affects all 
cohorts 
In our bear case, there is a larger spike in unemployment, anywhere from 0.6pp-4.1pp 
dependent on indirect effects. This increase in unemployment is two-fold: the initial rise is 
due to a  mismatch in skills between workers and available jobs that occurs because of 
rapid AI diffusion, while a further increase is because of secondary effects of weak demand 
after the initial shock. 
Even in this rapid diffusion bear case scenario, asset market moves are important, as 
demonstrated by the large range of potential unemployment outcomes. The most severe 
downturn occurs when initial labor displacement is accompanied by asset market declines. 
It is also possible a large and persistent enough asset market decline could cause a 
downturn on its own, without a labor market trigger. 
In this bear case, we expect the initial rise in unemployment from displacement  would first 
affect CHIC households, as they are more exposed to AI occupationally. A decline in 
resources for this group would likely lead them to reduce their discretionary spend. Given 
the large share of spending these households control, weaker demand from this group 
would eventually mean the shock spreads beyond those directly exposed. Low exposure 
occupations tend to be more cyclical, so we expect weak demand would result in higher 
unemployment for these consumers as well. While the gap between these groups may 
narrow for a short period,  all groups would be left worse off, and monetary and fiscal 
policy responses will be critical.

relatively larger share of spending in that category, relative to other categories. Red cells

As we would expect, lower-income consumers make up relatively larger shares of 
essentials such as groceries, utilities, and shelter, while high-income consumers make up

spending. Finally, the breakdown by education level is similar to that of income cohort. We
