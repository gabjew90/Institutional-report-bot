INVESTMENT HIGHLIGHTS
■
KRONOS combines an established reactor technology family with an acquired development base, but UIUC is the critical
proof point. NNE's flagship KRONOS MMR is a ~15 MWe / 45 MWth high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) using helium
coolant, graphite moderation and TRISO fuel embedded in a Fully Ceramic Microencapsulated (FCM) matrix. The January 2025
acquisition of Ultra Safe Nuclear Corporation assets brought nearly a decade of prior engineering work and, according to NNE, more
than $120MM of historical development spending for $8.5MM of cash consideration. HTGR and TRISO technology have meaningful
historical operating precedent, but KRONOS itself remains unproven at full scale. We therefore view the University of Illinois Urbana-
Champaign (UIUC) project as the central technology-validation event: successful licensing, construction and operation would provide
the first project-specific evidence that NNE can translate inherited engineering into a deployable reactor platform.
■
Formal NRC review gives NNE a visible regulatory pathway, with the next major milestones concentrated in 2027. UIUC
submitted the KRONOS Construction Permit Application (CPA) on March 31, 2026, and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC)
accepted and docketed it for technical review on May 18, 2026. The NRC currently targets completion of its final Environmental
Assessment in May 2027 and final Safety Evaluation in September 2027, after which issuance of the construction permit would
represent the key regulatory gating event before regulated construction. NNE targets construction activities beginning in 2H27 and
initial operation in 2029–2030, with a separate operating license required before startup. We view adherence to the NRC schedule
as the most important near-term indicator for the reactor program because delays, requests for additional information or material
design changes would flow directly through to the broader commercialization timeline.
■
KRONOS's lower initial enrichment requirement could mitigate one of the advanced-reactor industry's largest supply
constraints. NNE's standard KRONOS specification uses uranium enriched to approximately 9.9% U-235, with the reactor
architecture contemplated to accommodate enrichment up to approximately 19.9%. Although 9.9% technically falls within the
Department of Energy's definition of high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU), it is materially below the ~19.75% assay required by
several competing advanced-reactor designs. That distinction is becoming commercially relevant: Urenco USA is authorized to enrich
below 10% and completed an initial 8.5% production run in December 2025, while commercial-scale Western supply approaching 20%
remains constrained. We therefore view KRONOS's enrichment flexibility as a potential sourcing advantage.
■
Multi-GW commercial interest provides substantial deployment optionality, but conversion into binding contracts is the
key demand-side inflection. NNE has assembled a pipeline spanning AI data centers, industrial facilities and defense applications,
led by the August 2026 Tillman Digital Gateway framework targeting 2 GW or more of potential nuclear capacity by the mid-2030s
and 6 GW or more by 2040. The contemplated Tillman structure is particularly relevant to our model because Tillman or its affiliates
would finance, develop and own qualifying project infrastructure while NNE would supply reactors and fuel and support development
and operations, providing support for our assumption that more than 80% of eventual deployments could use a customer-funded
structure. BaRupOn adds a potential Texas deployment of up to 1 GW, while Super Micro and AFWERX broaden NNE's exposure to
AI infrastructure and defense. None of these opportunities currently constitutes binding commercial reactor backlog, however. We
therefore assign substantially greater significance to future site-specific agreements, customer financing, deposits and binding reactor
purchases than to headline pipeline GW.
■
Vertical integration creates a differentiated fuel-and-services platform, while STS gives NNE an operating business before
KRONOS commercialization. NNE is building capabilities around the reactor rather than relying exclusively on third-party fuel
and logistics providers. HALEU Energy Fuel is pursuing fuel-cycle capabilities; NNE's $2MM investment in LIS Technologies provides
longer-dated laser-enrichment optionality; Advanced Fuel Transportation is developing a licensed HALEU transportation package;
and the May 2026 acquisition of Secured Transportation Services (STS) added an established nuclear-logistics operation. STS
generated approximately $7.1MM of audited calendar 2025 revenue and $1.3MM of net income and contributed approximately
$0.2MM of NNE consolidated revenue between the May 22 acquisition and June 30. We view this strategy as potentially reducing future
single-point supply dependencies and allowing NNE to retain selected lifecycle-service economics, but most of the fuel-cycle platform
remains developmental; we therefore value existing STS earnings separately and do not assume vertically integrated enrichment or
fabrication margins in our base case.

COMPANY DEEP DIVE
CORPORATE HISTORY & STRATEGIC EVOLUTION
NNE is an early-stage advanced nuclear technology company developing microreactors and complementary nuclear fuel-cycle
capabilities. Incorporated in Nevada in February 2022 and headquartered in New York, NNE went public on the Nasdaq Capital Market
in May 2024 at $4.00 per share. The company has since evolved from a portfolio of internally developed reactor concepts into a
broader nuclear platform centered on the KRONOS MMR Energy System, with additional activities spanning nuclear fuel supply, fuel
transportation, space applications and nuclear consulting.
The defining step in that evolution was NNE's January 2025 acquisition of assets from Ultra Safe Nuclear Corporation (USNC). NNE
acquired the USNC assets for $8.5MM in cash through the company's Chapter 11 process, gaining intellectual property, contracts and
development assets associated with USNC's Micro Modular Reactor (MMR) and Pylon programs. The transaction effectively repositioned
KRONOS as NNE's lead reactor platform and brought with it an established development history as well as the University of Illinois Urbana-
Champaign (UIUC) demonstration project. NNE subsequently acquired Global First Power, since renamed True North Nuclear, in October
2025, giving the company direct ownership of the Canadian licensing application associated with a proposed KRONOS demonstration
project at Chalk River Laboratories in Ontario.
NNE has increasingly concentrated capital and engineering resources around KRONOS. The company acquired a 2.75-acre property
and 23,537-square-foot building in Oak Brook, Illinois for $3.5MM in July 2025 to support engineering, component assembly, prototype
fabrication and non-nuclear testing. In March 2026, NNE entered into a definitive agreement to sell its ODIN reactor technology to
Cambridge Atomworks, further concentrating the reactor portfolio around its gas-cooled technologies; the transaction remained pending
as of the June 2026 quarter. The most significant regulatory milestone to date came when UIUC submitted its KRONOS Construction
Permit Application to the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) on March 31, 2026. The NRC formally docketed and accepted the
application for technical review on May 18, 2026, moving the planned UIUC deployment into formal construction-permit review.
NNE has also expanded beyond reactor development into nuclear logistics. In May 2026, the company acquired Secured Transportation
Services (STS), a specialized nuclear and radioactive-material transportation and logistics provider, for consideration of up to $13MM.
The acquisition moved NNE into an early-revenue stage: STS contributed approximately $0.2MM of consolidated revenue from the May
22 closing through June 30, 2026, while NNE's reactor programs remain pre-commercial. The transaction also adds an operating capability
that could ultimately support NNE's broader fuel-cycle and reactor-deployment strategy, although STS currently remains small relative
to the capital required to commercialize KRONOS.
NNE has funded this expansion primarily through equity issuance. Following its May 2024 IPO, the company completed several capital
raises, including a $400MM gross private placement in October 2025 at $47.11 per share. At June 30, 2026, NNE reported approximately
$580MM of liquidity, consisting of $298.5MM of cash and cash equivalents and $281.5MM of short-term U.S. Treasury securities. This
liquidity provides substantial funding for near-term engineering, licensing and development activities, although management continues
to expect that commercialization of its reactor portfolio will require significant additional capital over time.
The company's strategy is now increasingly centered on translating KRONOS from an acquired development program into a licensable
and ultimately commercial reactor platform while building adjacent capabilities around the nuclear fuel cycle. In August 2026, NNE
entered into a non-binding strategic commercial framework with Tillman Global Holdings and Tillman Digital Gateway that contemplates
potential deployment of 2 GW or more of NNE reactor capacity by the mid-2030s and 6 GW or more by 2040. We view the framework
as evidence of potential end-market interest rather than contracted backlog, as reactor purchases remain subject to future binding
commitments. Taken together, the USNC acquisition, UIUC licensing process, Oak Brook investment, STS acquisition and Tillman
framework mark NNE's transition from an internally focused reactor-development company toward a broader advanced-nuclear
development platform; however, the core value proposition remains dependent on successful licensing, demonstration and eventual
commercialization of KRONOS.

TECHNOLOGY OVERVIEW
KRONOS MMR — NNE's Lead Reactor Platform
KRONOS combines established high-temperature gas-cooled reactor architecture with a simplified, modular balance of plant.
KRONOS is NNE's lead reactor program and the principal technology underlying our valuation. The stationary high-temperature gas-
cooled reactor (HTGR) is designed to produce ~15 MWe and 45 MWth, using helium as the primary coolant, graphite as the moderator
and TRISO fuel embedded in a silicon-carbide Fully Ceramic Microencapsulated (FCM) matrix. NNE is targeting 14 reactors per plant
(~210MWe total) to optimize plant size and balance of plant cost share per unit. NNE's standard design specifies primary-system pressure
of ~6 MPa, coolant temperatures of 300°C–660°C and targeted electrical conversion efficiency of ~35–36%. Heat from the reactor is
transferred through an intermediate heat exchanger to a molten-salt secondary system incorporating thermal-energy storage, before
electricity is generated through a conventional steam cycle.
The architecture combines relatively mature HTGR principles with several design choices intended to simplify deployment. Helium is
chemically inert and remains gaseous throughout the operating cycle, avoiding the boiling and phase-change behavior associated with
water-cooled reactors. At ~6 MPa, KRONOS also operates at materially lower primary-system pressure than a conventional pressurized
water reactor (PWR), although it remains a pressurized system. Its 660°C maximum coolant temperature is substantially above the ~300°C
operating temperatures typical of the existing light-water-reactor (LWR) fleet, supporting somewhat higher targeted thermal efficiency
while also creating potential applications for industrial process heat.

The safety case is built around fuel integrity, passive heat removal and inherent reactor physics rather than extensive active
safety systems. KRONOS uses TRISO particles, in which the uranium-bearing fuel kernel is surrounded by multiple carbon and ceramic
containment layers, with those particles subsequently embedded in an additional silicon-carbide FCM matrix. The architecture is
designed to retain fission products at temperatures well above normal operating conditions. Combined with an inert helium coolant,
graphite moderator, negative temperature-reactivity feedback and passive decay-heat-removal characteristics, the design is intended to
shut down and remove residual heat without continuous reliance on powered safety systems or operator intervention. The NRC approved
the Fuel Qualification Methodology applicable to KRONOS's FCM fuel in April 2025, establishing an agreed methodology for the testing
and data required to qualify the fuel; however, fuel qualification and reactor licensing remain ongoing.

Fuel flexibility could reduce KRONOS's exposure to HALEU availability, although the commercial benefit remains to be
demonstrated. NNE's standard KRONOS design currently specifies fuel enriched to ~9.9% U-235 and management has indicated that
the same basic reactor architecture can accommodate higher enrichments, potentially up to ~19.9%, without a fundamental redesign
of the plant. This matters because domestic high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) enrichment and fabrication capacity remains
limited, creating a potential supply-chain bottleneck for advanced reactors designed exclusively around enrichments ~20%. KRONOS's
ability to begin at a lower enrichment could provide greater fuel-sourcing flexibility during early deployments, while higher enrichment
could eventually support longer core life and lower refueling frequency as HALEU supply expands. We do not currently assign a specific
economic benefit to higher enrichment in our base case, as achievable burnup, core life, fuel pricing and the licensing implications of
alternative enrichment levels remain subject to further engineering and qualification.
The balance-of-plant strategy is intended to shift equipment procurement toward established industrial supply chains. Rather
than developing proprietary power-conversion equipment, KRONOS is designed around commercially established technologies including
heat exchangers, molten-salt thermal storage, steam generators and turbines, drawing in part on equipment and operating experience
from concentrated-solar and other high-temperature industrial applications. To the extent regulatory classification permits use of
commercially derived equipment, this approach could reduce bespoke engineering requirements and improve supply-chain depth relative
to a reactor requiring purpose-built nuclear equipment throughout the plant. The ultimate procurement, quality-assurance and cost
advantages will depend on final design classification and NRC requirements.
Thermal-energy storage is also potentially important to the commercial proposition. By transferring reactor heat into a molten-salt
secondary system, KRONOS can conceptually operate the nuclear island at relatively steady thermal output while varying electrical
delivery from the downstream power block. This could allow the plant to serve variable customer loads without repeatedly maneuvering
the reactor itself and may be particularly relevant for data centers and industrial facilities with changing load profiles. NNE ultimately
envisions individual units serving smaller remote, industrial and defense loads and multi-unit installations scaling toward larger data-
center campuses.
LOKI and ZEUS — Longer-Dated Technology Optionality
NNE's remaining reactor portfolio provides potential longer-term optionality but is substantially earlier in development than KRONOS,
and we assign no value to either platform in our base-case valuation.
LOKI MMR is NNE's smaller transportable HTGR platform. Acquired alongside KRONOS through the January 2025 purchase of Ultra
Safe Nuclear Corporation assets, LOKI is designed around the same broad HTGR and TRISO-fuel technology family while targeting
materially smaller applications. NNE currently describes the reactor as capable of ~0.5–3 MWe and suitable for terrestrial, marine and
space applications, including remote infrastructure and potential extraterrestrial power systems. The common technology base could
allow LOKI to benefit from engineering, fuel-qualification and supply-chain work undertaken for KRONOS, although LOKI will still require
its own development and applicable licensing pathway. NNE is currently assessing demonstration, licensing and commercial-launch
timelines, and we therefore treat the platform as longer-dated optionality rather than a contributor to our forecast.
ZEUS is a portable solid-core nuclear-battery concept targeted at smaller and highly mobile applications. The design emphasizes
a sealed, factory-fabricated reactor with passive characteristics and limited operator requirements, targeting remote, military and other
infrastructure-constrained applications where diesel generation is often the incumbent power source. NNE is currently evaluating
modifications intended to make ZEUS smaller and more mobile. Given the evolving design and absence of a defined licensing or
commercialization schedule, we assign no value to ZEUS in our base case.
ODIN and ALIP — Non-Core Technologies
NNE is in the process of divesting ODIN, its low-pressure-coolant reactor concept, to Cambridge Atomworks. The parties signed a
definitive asset purchase agreement in March 2026 and amended the agreement in June 2026, with closing expected on or before November
30, 2026. The original September 2025 letter of intent contemplated $6.2MM of consideration, including a $250M non-refundable upfront
payment, plus potential low-single-digit royalties on future net sales. The transaction remained pending as of NNE's June 2026 quarter
filing.
NNE also owns annular linear induction pump (ALIP) technology intended for potential small-reactor cooling applications. We assign
no value to LOKI, ZEUS, ALIP or prospective ODIN proceeds in our base-case valuation. In our view, KRONOS's licensing, demonstration
and eventual commercial performance should overwhelmingly determine the economic value of NNE's reactor portfolio over our forecast
horizon.

COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE & TECHNOLOGY POSITIONINING
The advanced-reactor market spans several technology families with materially different operating histories, fuel requirements,
licensing pathways and deployment scales. KRONOS sits within the high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) family, giving
NNE access to a relatively established base of underlying reactor-physics, helium-coolant and coated-particle-fuel experience. However,
technology-family precedent should not be confused with project maturity: several competing developers are further advanced in
hardware demonstration, licensing or construction than NNE today.
High-temperature gas-cooled reactors — the closest technology comparison for KRONOS. NNE's approximately 15 MWe KRONOS
design is a helium-cooled, graphite-moderated high-temperature reactor using TRISO-based fuel. X-energy's 80 MWe Xe-100 is the most
relevant larger-scale commercial comparison, while StarCore is developing another smaller helium-cooled TRISO-fueled HTGR. The
technology family benefits from historical operating experience at Germany's AVR and THTR, Japan's HTTR and the U.S. Fort St. Vrain
plant. This history provides meaningful precedent for several of KRONOS's underlying technology choices, although the specific KRONOS
configuration, fuel form, manufacturing process and integrated power system remain subject to first-of-a-kind demonstration.
Sodium-cooled fast reactors — greater fuel flexibility in several designs, with substantial historical reactor precedent. Oklo's
Aurora Powerhouse and TerraPower's Natrium are prominent U.S. examples. Aurora is a compact sodium-cooled fast reactor designed
around metal fuel, while Natrium combines a 345 MWe sodium fast reactor with molten-salt energy storage capable of increasing electrical
output to approximately 500 MWe for several hours. Both draw on a broader U.S. history of sodium fast-reactor development, including
EBR-II and subsequent Argonne programs. Oklo is also pursuing the ability to use multiple qualified fuel sources, including HALEU and
eventually recycled material. We therefore view fast reactors as a distinct technology and fuel-cycle approach rather than categorically
more or less mature than HTGRs.
Fluoride-salt-cooled high-temperature reactors — Kairos is pursuing an iterative demonstration strategy. Kairos Power's KP-FHR
combines solid TRISO pebble fuel with low-pressure molten fluoride-salt coolant. This differs from molten-salt-fueled reactors because
the nuclear fuel remains in solid form rather than being dissolved in the coolant. Kairos has progressed through multiple non-nuclear
Engineering Test Units and is constructing its Hermes demonstration reactor in Oak Ridge, Tennessee after receiving an NRC construction
permit in December 2023. Hermes is supported through DOE's Advanced Reactor Demonstration Program. Kairos therefore provides an
important competitive benchmark for NNE because its reactor technology is novel but its physical demonstration and NRC licensing
program is currently further advanced.
Molten-salt-fueled reactors represent a separate technology pathway. Developers including Terrestrial Energy are pursuing designs
in which nuclear fuel is incorporated into circulating molten salt. These systems potentially offer different operating temperatures,
pressure characteristics and fuel-management approaches from both HTGRs and sodium fast reactors, but introduce their own materials,
chemistry, component and licensing challenges.
Light-water SMRs leverage the nuclear industry's deepest underlying commercial technology base. NuScale's VOYGR platform, GE
Vernova Hitachi's BWRX-300 and Holtec's SMR-300/SMR-160 lineage apply light-water-reactor technology at smaller unit sizes. NuScale
received NRC Standard Design Approval for its 77 MWe-per-module US460 design in May 2025. The BWRX-300 has also reached an
important regulatory milestone internationally, with the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission issuing Ontario Power Generation a
license to construct the first unit at Darlington in April 2025, while TVA is pursuing U.S. licensing at Clinch River. Light-water SMRs
consequently benefit from mature fuel and component supply chains, although individual designs retain first-of-a-kind construction and
commercialization risk.
KRONOS's lower initial enrichment requirement could provide sourcing flexibility relative to designs requiring enrichment
approaching 20%. NNE's standard KRONOS specification calls for approximately 9.9% enrichment, with the design contemplated to
accommodate higher enrichment as fuel supply develops. Although 9.9% material technically falls within DOE's HALEU definition,
its lower assay potentially expands the set of enrichment facilities capable of producing feed material relative to designs requiring
approximately 19.75%. We view this as a potential supply-chain advantage rather than a solved fuel constraint: KRONOS still requires
qualified TRISO/FCM fabrication, sufficient enrichment capacity and project-specific fuel procurement, and a future increase in
enrichment could require additional engineering, qualification and licensing work.
Overall, KRONOS's competitive positioning rests on a combination of established HTGR/TRISO precedent, a relatively low initial
enrichment requirement and inherited USNC engineering work, rather than a clear lead in regulatory or physical deployment

FOAK DEPLOYMENT: UNIVERSITY OF ILLINOIS
The University of Illinois project is NNE’s principal near-term opportunity to demonstrate KRONOS at full scale. NNE is developing
its first-of-a-kind (FOAK) KRONOS installation with the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (UIUC), with planned integration into
the campus’s Abbott Power Plant infrastructure. The proposed reactor is rated at 45 MWth, corresponding to ~15 MWe in NNE’s standard
configuration. UIUC is the NRC applicant and intended owner and operator, while NNE provides the proprietary reactor technology. We
view the project’s principal investment significance as validating licensing, construction and operating performance ahead of subsequent
commercial deployments.

FUEL STRATEGY
Fuel availability remains one of the principal constraints on advanced-reactor deployment, making KRONOS's fuel
flexibility strategically important. Management has repeatedly identified nuclear-fuel availability as one of the industry's largest
commercialization bottlenecks, and NNE's vertical-integration strategy is intended to reduce reliance on any single future supply source.
Rather than depend exclusively on higher-assay fuel availability, NNE is developing multiple potential pathways across conversion,
enrichment, fabrication and transportation. We view this strategy as a response to a sector-wide constraint rather than evidence that
NNE has already secured its long-term fuel requirements.
KRONOS's approximately 9.9% initial enrichment specification could broaden near-term sourcing options. NNE's standard
KRONOS design specifies approximately 9.9%-enriched uranium and management has indicated that the same basic reactor architecture
can accommodate higher enrichment without a fundamental redesign. Although material above 5% and below 20% technically falls within
DOE's HALEU definition, the 5–10% range is commonly described as LEU+ and can access emerging enrichment capability that does not
extend to 19.75%. Urenco USA, for example, received NRC authorization to enrich up to 10% and completed an initial production run
at 8.5% in December 2025, demonstrating that commercial U.S. LEU+ capability is beginning to emerge. A higher-enrichment KRONOS
configuration would still require the applicable fuel qualification and licensing treatment, and we therefore do not assume that switching
enrichment levels is administratively frictionless.
LIS Technologies provides potential long-term enrichment optionality, but remains early-stage and outside NNE's direct control.
NNE has invested $2MM in related-party LIS Technologies Inc. (LIST), whose CRISLA laser-isotope-separation technology was assessed
at Technology Readiness Level 4 in March 2025. This indicates laboratory validation of key components but leaves substantial scale-up,
demonstration, licensing and financing work before commercial deployment. LIST was also one of six companies selected by DOE in
December 2024 under the Low-Enriched Uranium Enrichment Acquisition Program, with LIST serving as prime contractor and NNE as
a key subcontractor. Each selected vendor received a minimum $2MM contract and eligibility to compete for future procurement; the
award should not be interpreted as a large-scale enrichment-capacity commitment or a secured source of KRONOS fuel.
Laser enrichment could ultimately offer lower capital intensity and a smaller physical footprint than conventional centrifuge enrichment,
but those benefits remain prospective at LIST's current development stage. We therefore treat LIST as strategic optionality rather than
the foundation of our near-term fuel-supply assumptions.
NNE's Argentina initiative could create a more tangible upstream conversion pathway if converted into definitive agreements.
NNE signed a non-binding memorandum of understanding with state-owned Dioxitek in August 2025 to evaluate Argentina's uranium-
conversion infrastructure and subsequently submitted a private proposal in April 2026 for the potential joint development of natural
uranium hexafluoride (UF₆) production capacity using Dioxitek's existing infrastructure. NNE's June 2026 quarter filing states that
technical, financial and implementation work remains underway and that the company has not yet entered into a definitive agreement
to commercially launch its fuel-supply business.
Conversion alone would address only one step of the fuel cycle. Natural UF₆ produced in Argentina would still require enrichment and
downstream fabrication into qualified TRISO/FCM fuel. Management has discussed the potential use of established Western enrichment
capacity as part of this broader strategy. Urenco USA represents a credible example of such capacity: its New Mexico facility is the only
operating commercial U.S. enrichment plant, is authorized for enrichment up to 10%, and is currently expanding production. However,
we do not assume a dedicated NNE-Urenco arrangement in our base case absent a definitive agreement.
Commercial-scale Western HALEU supply remains constrained despite meaningful government-backed expansion. Centrus's
Piketon, Ohio facility is already producing HALEU under DOE-supported arrangements and reached approximately 900 kg of cumulative
production by June 2025. DOE nevertheless continued to characterize the U.S. market as lacking a commercial HALEU source at that
time. In January 2026, DOE awarded $900MM task orders to both Centrus and General Matter to develop additional domestic HALEU
enrichment capacity, while awarding $900MM to Orano for expanded LEU capacity. These programs improve the medium-term supply
outlook but do not yet create a deep merchant market capable of serving all prospective advanced-reactor deployments.
Relative to peers, NNE has fuel flexibility but less project-specific supply certainty today. Oklo currently has a more defined near-
term pathway through access to recovered DOE HALEU and a June 2026 letter of intent with Centrus covering potential supply for up
to five Aurora powerhouses beginning in 2029. NNE, by contrast, has not yet disclosed a definitive agreement securing the full KRONOS

BUSINESS MODEL
Monetization is likely to skew toward customer-owned reactor deployments, supplemented by technology, fuel and lifecycle-
services revenue. NNE has positioned KRONOS to support multiple ownership structures, including direct reactor or technology sales,
leasing and build-own-operate arrangements under which NNE could sell electricity through long-term contracts. Based on recent
commercial activity, our base case assumes that customer-owned deployments account for the larger share of initial commercial volumes.
The clearest example is NNE's August 2026 strategic framework with Tillman Digital Gateway, which contemplates an independent-
power-producer-style structure under which Tillman or its affiliates would finance, develop and own project power infrastructure while
NNE would supply reactors and fuel and support development and operations. The framework remains non-binding, however, and final

BaRupOn provides another potential large-scale deployment opportunity, with NNE having completed a feasibility study evaluating
staged deployment of KRONOS units to support up to 1 GW of demand at BaRupOn's Texas industrial and data-center campus. We view
the project as supportive of a customer-funded deployment model, but do not treat the current feasibility arrangement as a binding
reactor-purchase commitment.
Under a customer-owned structure, we would expect NNE's economics to comprise a combination of reactor technology or equipment
revenue and recurring lifecycle services rather than ownership of the underlying power asset. Potential service lines could include fuel
procurement, fuel delivery and transportation, licensing and engineering support, operator training and longer-term maintenance or
technical services. The model resembles elements of X-energy's published commercial framework, which combines reactor technology
fees with recurring services and fuel revenue while project owners fund and own the generating assets. We do not assume that every
KRONOS deployment will include the same scope or margin profile, and final economics will depend on contractual allocation of
equipment supply, engineering, construction and performance obligations.

POTENTIAL UPSIDE DRIVERS
■
Pipeline frameworks converting to binding contracts. Turning the non-binding Tillman Digital Gateway (2 GW mid-2030s, 6 GW+
by 2040), Super Micro, and BaRupOn (1 GW Texas campus) frameworks into signed, creditworthy agreements would validate pricing
on both the reactor-sales and PPA sides of our model and materially de-risk the reactor-sales ramp we currently don't begin modeling
until 2030.
■
A second data-center-scale award signed. Management has described advancing discussions on a further large deployment beyond
the reference 210 MWe/14-unit block underpinning our estimates, including continued progress on the BaRupOn feasibility study
toward up to 1 GW of capacity. A signed award of this kind sits entirely outside our current estimates and would represent meaningful
upside to our reactor-sales build.
■
AI-driven power demand continuing to outstrip supply. Faster-than-expected data-center buildout, continued gas-turbine lead-
time extensions, and combined-cycle cost inflation would strengthen the case for NNE's compact, co-locatable KRONOS footprint and
could accelerate customer conversion timelines across the entire pipeline described above.

NNE

FOAK schedule or cost overruns. Delays at UIUC including slippage on the NRC's Environmental Assessment or Safety Evaluation
would push out the 2H27 construction start and 2029–2030 operation target, while cost overruns beyond the disclosed $300–350MM

reaches into the hundreds of millions of dollars annually by the early 2030s; a financing gap of this scale will likely require additional
ATM usage, a follow-on equity raise, or project-level debt that could dilute per-share value beyond what our current estimates capture.
