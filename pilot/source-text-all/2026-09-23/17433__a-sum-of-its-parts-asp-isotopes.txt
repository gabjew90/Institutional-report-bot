A sum of its parts
Amidst the hum of the World Nuclear Symposium in London last week, ASP Isotopes
stepped out of the crowd to tell its story. Gathered in the Bill Boeing Lecture Theatre
at No. 4 Hamilton Place - the storied home of the Royal Aeronautical Society - the
management team held their inaugural Capital Markets Day. It was a room worthy of
the moment: a building steeped in the legacy of aviation pioneers who once believed
they could conquer the skies, now lending its historic walls to a new generation with
ambitions just as bold. But ASP’s gaze is fixed differently. Their ambition is not airborne,
but elemental: to forge the advanced materials and banish the shortages that constrain
our future. They seek to arm medicine in the war against disease, power the next epoch
of computing, and anchor the renaissance of nuclear energy. The horizon they claim is
small - measured in atoms - yet the world they mean to build is vast. Lofty, for sure.
For all their shrewdness in snapping up undervalued assets - from Molybdos and Klydon
to PET Labs and Renergen - management has, for now, collided with the stubborn grit
of the physical world. Whiteboards can yield to the friction of process. As those following
the story know - bottlenecks have appeared, most of them fairly simple mechanical
culprits. Compressors, for example, have accounted for 97% of plant downtime in the
ASP process.
To their credit, leadership met the moment with candor and offered a clear-eyed mea
culpa at the event. To fix many of the issues, they have diagnosed a relative famine
of specialized vendors in their niche market, and are choosing self-reliance instead.
When the market cannot supply it, they build it. By bringing much part design and
manufacturing in-house, they have transformed many equipment lead times measured
in years into days and weeks.
And we find that resolve is already bearing fruit. Plant uptime climbed from 58% in May
to 90% by July. Designing a proprietary compressor for the ASP process changed the
equation entirely, yielding superior flow rates, sharper pressure ratios, and a dramatic
drop in energy consumption.
Compressors tell only part of the story, and management is actively working through
other bottlenecks as well. Now comes the heart of the matter: theory is turning into
reality, and the whiteboard is slowly becoming the income statement. With management
guiding that the first helium shipment is arriving in weeks - followed by Si-28, Yb-176,
and C-14 in 2H26 - the rubber is finally meeting the road. If management can hold true
to its promises, we believe multiple positive catalysts await this fall and winter.
Beyond the hard grind of operations, another shadow has dimmed the enterprise's
grand design and value: sheer complexity. It is one thing to capture a constellation of
undervalued assets; it is quite another to conquer them all at once. They are running
and acquiring radiopharmacies, scaling 2 enrichment technologies, and coaxing natural
gas and helium from the earth - all at once.
We admire the bench strength leadership has assembled for this ascent, but the market
craves a narrative it can simply hold in its hand. Right now, the story is a bit dizzying, in
our opinion.

Fortunately, management sees the fog. As CEO Paul Mann declared of the nuclear fuels
subsidiary, Quantum Leap Energy, “the goal is to spin this out as soon as possible.”
And in June, the company moved to merge Noble Africa with ENDRA Life Sciences,
forging a dedicated Nasdaq-listed helium platform for the Virginia Gas Project. We
generally dismiss sum-of-the-parts arithmetic - unless the parts are actually set free.
And, that is the path management has chosen. If these transactions cross the finish
line, they may not merely illuminate hidden value; they could dismantle the fog.
Couple an anticipated operational ramp in 2H26 with a deliberate corporate unbundling,
and you have a potential material convergence of catalysts with the weight to move the
stock, in our opinion.
Now on to some numbers.
Management has set “aspirational” 2031 EBITDA targets of $330-700M – excluding
Quantum Leap Energy (QLE). To give those numbers some context, they have also
stated that following Phase 2’s completion, projected helium and LNG production -
largely secured by 5-15 year take-or-pay contracts - will nearly fulfill those targets.
Simultaneously, PET Labs is driving organic growth and a new nuclear medicine cycle.
And while forecasting stable isotopes is “more challenging,” new materials – like the
ones ASPI expects to deliver, can improve compute efficiency.
Delivering on those targets will require capital. The company laid out plans including a
global cyclotron deployment for radiopharmacies, additional isotope plants in locations
including Iceland for Yb-176, Si-28, and Phase 2 for LNG and helium. The good news
is that much of that could come from credit facilities of close to $800M from the U.S.
Development Finance Corporation and Standard Bank of South Africa. The company
also had ~$255M in cash as of 2Q26. We would not doubt the need for more equity
capital, but much non-dilutive funding appears to be potentially available.
Regarding QLE, while management harbors broader ambitions, the TerraPower
partnership alone could generate over $100 million in annual EBITDA for a decade (see
our previous note).
Looking toward the early 2030s, ASP's constituent parts offer material profit potential
driven by genuinely game-changing technology potential. Material risks and capital
requirements remain, but this year could mark the pivot toward execution, ramping
revenue, and value realization through corporate action.
In our view, ASP is a sum of compelling parts.

What is an isotope? According to the IAEA, isotopes are atoms with the same
number of protons but a different number of neutrons. Put simpler, an isotope is just
a different version of the same element, like various models of a car. All carbon atoms
have 6 protons, but some have 6 neutrons (Carbon-12) and some have 8 neutrons
(Carbon-14). Isotopes are important because they are useful in medicine, nuclear
power, and various other industries. The hard part is making them useful.
• Why is separating them so hard? The primary difference between isotopes is
their mass. Nature delivers isotopes pre-mixed, and because isotopes of the same
element are chemically identical, they cannot be separated using conventional
chemistry. ASP Isotopes' business is built on using proprietary enrichment
technologies to exploit tiny mass differences and produce the high-purity isotopes
needed for nuclear fuels, medical applications, and semiconductors.
Ytterbium-176: commercial samples shipped; commercial shipments expected
by 2H26. During ASPI's recent capital markets day, management indicated there were
delays with yttberium-176 production caused by "long lead items that we could not get
quickly enough to build the continuous processing vessel."
In addition, during 4Q25, ASP's Yb-176 plant experienced production delays caused
by "a power surge in eastern Pretoria," which was an infrastructure delay unrelated
to the technology and damaged one of the company's lasers, causing delays. ASP has
since repaired the laser and is working towards establishing commercial production
with initial commercial shipments expected in 2H26. Management highlighted strong
customer demand of ~2kg per year and anticipates current manufacturing capacity of
1kg per year at its existing plant.
• Ytterbium-176 applications: some background. Lutetium-177 is a key isotope
required in developing Pluvicto, a highly effective treatment for early stage prostate
cancer, currently suffering from a supply shortage. Lutetium-177 is produced from
ytterbium-176: ytterbium-176 is placed inside a nuclear reactor, where it captures a
neutron to become ytterbium-177. The newly formed ytterbium-177 then undergoes
radioactive decay, resulting in lutetium-177.
• New continuous production vessel scales output. At its recent capital markets
day, management announced that it had completed construction of its continuous
production vessel and that "it is being commissioned right now... we expect to
have some news and data on that during the next several weeks." The previous
ytterbium production vessel could process a three-hour batch three times per week.
Management indicated that three continuous processing lines running in parallel
should enable the company to reach an annual production rate of approximately one
kilogram of ytterbium.

Carbon-12: the interim operational confidence booster for Carbon-14. ASP has
been commercially producing carbon-12 at its carbon-14 enrichment facility. Carbon-12
enrichment has served as an interim product for the company until it receives its long-
awaited Carbon-14 feedstock. Over the long-term, ASP does not plan to prioritize
this Carbon-12 having enriched the production to establish "process stability and
operational confidence while awaiting the final back of [Carbon-14] feedstock from the
customer."
• Carbon-12 applications: some background. Carbon-12 is used in defense
applications, specifically electromagnetic pulse (EMP) and cloaking devices.
Carbon-12 defense applications leverage a scientific concept known as nuclear spin,
essentially the fabrication of small magnetic fields. EMPs are susceptible to magnetic
fields, which can help identify and weaken enemy chips. Moreover, the sole use
of isotopically pure carbon-12 - given the elimination of nuclear spin in isotopic
isolation - can make allied devices harder to identify (for greater detail, please refer
to our previous note).
Carbon-14: feedstock resolution; initial shipments expected by mid-year 2026.
ASP's C-14 business has been subject to significant delays stemming from issues with
receiving feedstock from its supplier. On the 4Q25 earnings call, the company noted

production shortly thereafter. ASP "anticipates initial C-14 commercial shipments
around mid-year, contingent on the timely receipt of feedstock from the Canadian
supplier."
• ASP signed a $2.5M+ carbon-14 purchase agreement. On the 4Q25 earnings

• Carbon-14: potential $100M+ opportunity. Management estimates commercial

• Carbon-14 applications: some background. Carbon-14 is an isotope used as
a radiolabeling compound (carbon-dating and pharmacokinetic tracing) due to its
relatively innocuous emission of alpha radiation and its long half-life.

markets day, ASPI leadership presented data showing that Europe and Asia
collectively account for more than 50% of global helium demand but contribute
only a minimal share of global supply. Management noted that prices "have risen
from approximately $400/Mcf to over $2,000/Mcf in some cases, with a significant

• Qatar helium processing facility, which produced roughly one-third of
the world's helium, was impacted during the Iran conflict. At ASPI's capital
markets day, management stated, "the world is essentially short about 50% helium

• Russian export controls further impact supply. On April 14th, in response to
the war in the Middle East, Russia "imposed temporary export controls on helium to
maintain a stable supply to the domestic market," which will remain in effect until
the end of 2027. ASPI anticipates that the export controls will further impact global
supply and prices and believes that "these export controls are likely to show their
effect on the global market during the second half of 2026."

Increased focus on semiconductors and quantum computing
ASP Isotopes emphasized the importance of silicon-28, carbon-12,
helium, and fluorinated gases for semiconductor and quantum computing
technologies. At ASP Isotopes recent capital markets day, Natalie Grancharov
Camacho, who recently joined ASPI after 22 years in the semiconductor industry at
Intel, discussed how and why ASPI's isotopically pure materials are essential to cutting-
edge technologies. Natalie argued, "with the semiconductor industry, we are not bound
anymore by the engineering piece... but when it comes to having the materials and
physically producing them, this is where it gets a little tricky."
Why are Silicon-28 and Carbon-12 essential for quantum computing? Quantum
computers use qubits, which IBM defines as the basic unit of information used to
encode data in quantum computing. Importantly, qubits do not rely on traditional
ones and zeroes. Instead, they can exist in a state representing both a one and a zero
simultaneously. According to ASPI management, "in order to achieve these states,
they need to be in a perfect environment that has no noise." This requires the use of
isotopically pure silicon-28 or carbon-12, positioning ASPI at the center of the supply
chain for a growing industry.
Why is Carbon-12 valuable for power electronics? Power electronics, defined
by Schneider Electric, are a part of electrical engineering that controls and changes
electric power using semiconductor devices. According to ASPI management,
isotopically pure Carbon-12 improves the thermal conductivity of diamond-based
semiconductor materials. This enables more efficient heat transfer, higher power
capabilities, and improved performance in advanced electronics.
• At its recent capital markets day, ASPI management said, "we're looking at Carbon
12 for thermal insulation materials. Chips gets hot, and we want to draw that heat
away from it as efficiently as possible. So the industry looking at a lot of different
materials for that and one of the materials is a thermal interface material. So
looking at some collaborations to see if our Carbon 12 could be a viable option for tin
materials in advanced packaging."
Heliums importance spans many industries. At their recent capital markets day,
ASPI highlighted the critical role helium plays across the semiconductor, aerospace,
and automotive manufacturing sectors. Management estimated they "think the semi
industry worldwide uses maybe 20%-25% of all the world's helium... the government
has just signed an order to triple the amount of rocket launches between now and
2030... every time a SpaceX Starship launches, it uses an entire days supply of global
helium."
• Why is helium important for semiconductors? Helium's unique properties of
being non-reactive with other materials, being exceptional at transferring heat, and
being atomically very small make it irreplaceable for semiconductor manufacturing.
Helium gases cool wafers, help maintain atmospheric control in process chambers,
and test chips for leaks. ASPI's management said, "if a fab hasn't got helium, it has
to shut down. It will lose more profit in that day than it spends the entire year on
helium. So it has an incredible ability to pay whatever price it needs to pay to have
helium."

• What are Fluorinated gases? F-gases (Fluorinated gases) are a family of man-
made gases made up of fluorine combined with other elements. Common examples
include nitrogen trifluoride (NF₃), sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆), and carbon Tetrafluoride
(CF₄). These gases are highly reactive and can break apart or remove materials at
the atomic level, which is why they are valuable in advanced manufacturing.
• Why is uranium fluorination important? According to Ryno Pretorius, CEO of
Quantum Leap Energy, ASP's uranium enrichment technology requires uranium to
first undergo fluorination. Fluorination of uranium, according to the World Nuclear
Association, is the chemical process of reacting uranium with fluorine to produce a
gas called uranium hexafluoride (UF₆). This gaseous form is necessary for uranium
enrichment.
• Why are Fluorinated gases important for semiconductors? Fluorinated gases
are the chemicals used to etch microscopic patterns into chips. They are also used
to deposit certain materials onto semiconductor wafers during manufacturing. When

(e.g., USA).

countries, including Iceland, and other end markets, including medical and semiconductor.

have yet to be approved by the US NRC. We think approval is likely longer term but does represent the risk to HALEU demand.
• Commercial timing for nuclear energy applications remains uncertain as technical feasibility needs to be firmly established.
Uranium enrichment is also subject to thorough regulatory approvals and policy considerations.
• Balance sheet & additional debt. The company’s ambitious plans likely imply additional capital needs over the medium to long
term. To execute on plans, management may raise incremental debt.
