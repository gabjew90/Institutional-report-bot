 
Margin-Call-Related Selling Takes Gold Back to Its 
Fair-Value 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, NOV 04, 2026 - 12:00 PM 
Authored by Simon White, Bloomberg macro strategist, 
Gold selling last week had the hallmarks of a liquidity-raising move to stem losses elsewhere. 
The decline has taken the precious metal back under its fair value for the first time since 2023, 
providing a durable, fundamental base for prices to gradually recover. 
Gold fell 3.7% on Friday, with no obvious catalyst. Yields and the dollar were lower, but with 
some ugly price action in the stock market centered around semis stocks (the SOX faced its 
41st worst decline in over 30 years), the likely cause was raising cash to cover losses in equity 
positions and other assets, as well as CTA-related selling as long-term momentum signals 
turned negative. 
It’s somewhat illogical that investors often reach for the gold piggy bank first when financial 
stress hits. Yes, it’s generally very liquid, but it’s also the only mainstream asset that can provide 
orthogonal risk to financial assets, as it is no-one’s liability (when held in its allocated, physical 
form). 
The selling has taken gold under my fair value for the metal, based on real yields, the dollar, the 
S&P and copper, on a year-over-year basis, for the first time since bullion embarked on its 
monumental ~185% rally in 2023. 
 

 
 
The fundamental reasons for owning gold are the same as they were last year when the rally 
had yet to top out.  
Inflationary and deflationary tail-risks are elevated due to massive and persistent fiscal deficits, 
huge financial system leverage, and the weaponisation of the dollar. 
But the metal became more like a meme stock as everybody wanted a piece of it when it was 
rallying.  
The hot money has now largely departed, a lot of it now ironically nursing losses in US and 
Korean tech stocks (and perhaps selling what gold they had left to stem the losses there). 
Thus far the retail crowd, as judged by US gold ETF flows, have not yet capitulated. But despite 
the excessive focus on precious metal ETFs, they are only a sliver of the gold market, 
accounting for a mere 1.8% of above ground stocks. 
 

 
 
Perhaps US investors are taking a leaf from the book of who have been the savvier gold 
investors over the past few years: those based in China and the rest of Asia.  
They were buying before investors in the US and Europe in the early days of bullion’s rally, and 
they did not sell earlier this year, based on flows into Asia-domiciled gold ETFs, even as 
outflows from US ETFs rose. 
The price action in gold is unpalatable (it’s lower again today), but its return to looking cheap 
from a fundamental perspective for the first time in several years should soon provide a durable 
base for prices to recover.
