Competing for capital in a boom economy 
     
   
 
 
Growth remains biased to the upside 
The economy continues to outperform expectations. Strong nominal GDP growth (6.6% 
in Q2 and likely headed higher), robust and potentially significant wealth creation, 
resilient labor markets, and accelerating AI investment are supporting activity despite 
elevated interest rates. That strength is reflected primarily in rising real yields, raising 
the hurdle for lower Treasury yields and reinforcing the higher-for-longer rate backdrop. 
Trump 2.0: 1990s-style growth and disinflation 
We believe the Trump administration is pursuing a 1990s-style strategy of using strong 
nominal growth and technology-driven productivity gains to improve the fiscal outlook 
and ultimately reduce inflation. This is likely a multi-year effort, with AI’s largest 
disinflationary benefits emerging closer to 2028-2032. For investors, the message is 
clear: do not underestimate the focus on growth and its implications for bonds 
(negative) versus equities/risk assets (positive). 
Capital demand continues to grow 
The market is absorbing an extraordinary amount of financing demand. Treasury 
borrowing and AI-related investment are competing for capital at a time when financing 
needs across both the public and private sectors continue to grow. At the same time, the 
U.S. government is borrowing $1.0+ trillion annually to fund interest expense alone, 
fueling concerns that increasing amounts of future issuance may be required simply to 
service outstanding debt. Meanwhile, new Chair Warsh seeks to return the Fed to a more 
minimalist 1990s-style communication regime, creating additional policy and rate 
uncertainty. The adjustment mechanism has been higher yields, not wider spreads. 
Duration is becoming harder to own 
The supply of long-duration debt continues to grow (see our report, BofA - US Rates 
Viewpoint). Treasury borrowing remains elevated, while AI-related investment is 
increasingly being financed through long-dated corporate bond issuance. Recent efforts 
by Scott Bessent to support the long end of the Treasury market through expanded 
buybacks underscore how important financing costs have become to policymakers. Yet 
long-term yields continue to move higher as markets demand greater compensation to 
finance expanding government and corporate borrowing. 
Own carry, not duration 
We continue to view duration as the least attractive risk in fixed income. Total return 
sector rankings in Exhibit 1 support that view. While our rates strategists forecast a 
4.5% 10-year Treasury yield at year-end 2026 & 2027 (currently, 4.91%), market is 
increasingly testing the opposite outcome; technical strategist Paul Ciana’s 5.50%-
5.75% target range provides an important risk reference point. We remain underweight 
duration, overweight credit risk. We prefer AAA CLOs, leveraged loans, high yield bonds.

 Competing for capital in a boom economy 
Growth remains biased to the upside 
Rates up, risk-on (from last month, see our report, BofA - The Fixed Income Digest) 
remains intact. The economy continues to outperform expectations. Strong nominal GDP 
growth (6.6% in Q2 and likely headed higher), robust and potentially massive wealth 
creation, resilient labor markets, and accelerating AI investment are supporting activity 
despite elevated interest rates. That strength is reflected primarily in rising real yields 
(Exhibit 2), raising the hurdle for lower Treasury yields and reinforcing the higher-for-
longer rate backdrop. 
Exhibit 2: Nominal (white) v real 10y UST yield (amber) v 10y inflation breakeven (red) 
Strong nominal GDP growth is pushing real UST yields higher (2.5%), which in turn has driven nominal 10y 
yield higher (4.9%). Meanwhile, market-based inflation expectations such as 10y inflation breakeven remain 
stable, indicating the 2026 rise in bond yields is largely a growth problem, not an inflation problem. If policy 
remains focused on pushing nominal growth higher, as expected, further increases in real and nominal 10y 
yield are expected. Note that real yields ranged from 3.25% to 4.3% in the late 1990s, well above today’s 
level, and growth was not impaired, suggesting more upside risk for real and nominal 10y yields 
Source: Bloomberg

bonds (negative) versus equities/risk assets (positive).

Exhibit 3: Nominal yoy GDP growth (white) v 10y UST yield (amber) 
High nominal GDP growth in the 1990s, which averaged 5.5%, was not deterred by high interest rates. The 
nominal 10y UST yield averaged 6.7% and ranged from a high near 9.1% and a low near 4.2%. Currently, 
growth is relatively strong at 6.6% in 2Q26 and is likely headed higher, as the Trump Administration seeks to 
replicate the 1990s experience of using growth to address fiscal challenges and facilitate an AI-driven 
technology boom that ultimately brings down inflation. Higher nominal growth likely will be accompanied by 
higher nominal 10y yield 
Source: Bloomberg

Exhibit 4: Nominal yoy GDP growth (white) v core PCE inflation (amber) 
Treasury Secretary Bessent’s recent assertion that growth does not produce inflation is supported by the 
1990s experience, when tech-driven high nominal GDP growth was accompanied by a dramatic decline in 
core PCE, from a high of 4.4% in 1990 to just 1.0% by 1998. We think the Administration seeks to replicate 
the 1990s experience, with another (ultimately) disinflationary technology boom

sector rankings in Exhibit 1 support that view. While our rates strategists forecast a

duration and overweight credit risk. We prefer AAA CLOs, leveraged loans, and high yield 
bonds. We caution on duration exposure in UST, IG corporates, MBS, munis, and 
preferreds

Economics – Aditya Bhave, Stephen Juneau, Shruti Mishra 
US Watch: Aug jobs: The good, the strong and the stable  
• 
This was a hawkish jobs print all the way: NFP beat expectations, prior two months 
revised up & 3-mo average above breakeven. 
• 
U-rate held at 4.1% for good reasons. Wage growth also rebounded. Labor market is 
stable but not inflationary 
• 
For the Fed, we still think Aug inflation matters most. But this is positive news for 
us, given the move up in Sep pricing. 
CPI Inflation Watch: August US CPI preview: Make or break  
• 
For August CPI, we forecast a trend-like 0.22% m/m increase and expect core PCE 
to come in slightly firmer at 0.24% m/m. 
• 
If correct, core PCE would be running at a 2.9% annualized pace and the y/y rate 
would edge up to 3.4%. 
• 
In our view, that should be enough to keep the Fed on track for a September rate 
hike. 
US Economic Weekly: In the interest of time  
• 
A renewed focus on deficits is warranted given the rise in rates, with high interest 
costs now the key challenge. 
• 
We expect next week’s inflation data to be firm enough to keep the Fed on track for 
a September rate hike.
