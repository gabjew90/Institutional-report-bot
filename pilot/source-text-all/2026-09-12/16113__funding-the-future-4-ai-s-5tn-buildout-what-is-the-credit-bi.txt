Funding The Future #4: AI’s $5tn buildout – 
what is the credit bill? 
     
   
 
 
Two variables, trillions of outcomes 
With the current AI capex cycle likely to overshoot historical precedents, we turn to 
announced datacenter capacity expansions as a way to generate more concrete 
estimates. However, highly variable inputs of needed GWs and cost/GW create a wide 
range of possible outcomes. We comb through prominent internal and external sources, 
calculating estimates using bottom-up and top-down frameworks, to arrive at our base 
case of ~$5.0tn in capex spending through 2030, increasing to $7.7tn at the upper end. 
Funding need sits in the IT layer 
Of the $5.0tn base case capex bill, we estimate growth capex accounts for $3.0tn, 
including $2.1tn of IT equipment and $1.4tn of chips. This is where incremental funding 
needs are concentrated. Maintenance adds $2.0tn in IT spend, taking total IT capex to 
roughly $4.1tn and chip spend to $2.7tn given IT investment is large, front-loaded and 
subject to recurring refresh requirements. By contrast, longer-lived construction, power 
and cooling assets should require less maintenance funding over the forecast horizon. 
A small headline gap hides a $0.7tn divide 
Adding non-AI related expenses brings aggregate capex through 2030 to $6.9tn against 
$6.6tn of OCF, implying only a modest deficit, based on our analysis. But the headline 
masks sharp dispersion: consensus expects better quality issuers to generate enough 
OCF to cover capex. Fringe issuers face the real funding pressure, with roughly $1.7tn of 
capex against $1.0tn of OCF, leaving an estimated $0.7tn deficit.  
The funding bill is bigger than the deficit 
The cash flow deficit is not the only reason issuers will tap external capital. High-quality 
borrowers may raise debt despite positive FCF to bridge front-loaded capex, preserve 
liquidity and retain flexibility for shareholder returns or M&A. We estimate total external 
capital needs at about $1.2tn through 2030, comprising roughly $0.5tn from higher-
quality issuers and $0.7tn from the rest. Most of that funding should come through debt, 
given an estimated after-tax cost near 5% versus an equity cost above 11%. 
Large supply, manageable technicals 
Raising all $1.2tn of needed funding as debt would translate to ~$300bn/year of 
incremental credit supply. Spread across five years and multiple markets, we think this 
volume should be absorbable – particularly if issuance is paced. The opportunistic nature 
of borrowing also creates a natural feedback loop: if spreads become uneconomic, 
issuance can shift to other markets or capex can be delayed. The likely credit impact is 
therefore episodic technical pressure rather than a sustained market-clearing problem. 
HY and Loan weekly recap 
Our AI Weekly Pulse now tracks a broader set of private deals, providing a more 
complete view of the AI credit market's size: $568bn issued YTD, including $259bn in IG 
& $256bn in PC (Exhibit 13). HY spreads widened 3bps this week (now at 271bps). Loans 
returned 0.09% WTD, as $18bn of supply (52% for LBOs) outpaced $1bn of demand.

 What to expect from the datacenter 
buildout? 
Acceptance of hyperscaler/chip-related expenditures as a seemingly bottomless pit has 
somewhat been normalized by the markets in this capex cycle. However, amid the surge 
in credit supply (see report: Situation Room), and its knock-on impact on other fixed-
income markets (see report: US Rates Viewpoint), it’s important to create guardrails 
around AI-linked issuance. In this report, we reconcile top-down TAM-based, bottom-up 
pipeline-based, and fundamental issuer-level capex expectations, and consolidate 
multifaceted internal and external narratives, to quantify:  
a) 
capex expectations based on announced capacity 
b) 
expense split between GPU funding and other types of IT investments. 
c) 
Needs for future debt funding. 
Investor Takeaways 
• 
US datacenter expansion estimates over the next five years vary widely, from 48-
91GW based on bottom-up DC pipeline lists to 115GW in chip demand-driven 
models. These numbers are diff from ex 2. Build costs per GW also have a range: 
$25-50bn/GW.  
• 
Matrices based on these range of inputs create a wide dispersion of possible 
outcomes. Our base estimate for cumulative AI capex spending from ’26-’30 is 
$5.0tn factoring in inflation and maintenance capex, increasing to $7.7tn in a high-
growth scenario.  
• 
The incremental build portion (growth capex) is estimated at $3.0tn out of the total 
$5tn. Of this, IT spending is estimated to constitute $2.1tn, while the subset of 
expenditure on chips could reach $1.4tn. This is where incremental capital will be 
most required.  
• 
The eight largest operators in the AI space are projected to generate $6.6tn of OCF 
over 2026-30 against total capex of $6.9tn (including non-AI spend), implying a 
modest funding deficit of $0.3tn.  
• 
However, underlying dispersion is high. Based on consensus estimates, the 
strongest four issuers can largely self-fund the buildout, generating OCF of $5.5tn 
vs $5.2tn of capex. The remaining four issuers face a shortfall of $0.7tn in FCF 
terms and are more likely to be reliant on external funding, in some cases supported 
by long-term contracts and take-or-pay structures. 
• 
In addition, deficit is not the only factor that drives external financing. While those with 
cash deficits will likely rely on external funds, so could those that have cash surplus – in 
order to fund other expenditures such as dividends/M&A, and to retain strategic 
flexibility. This is the reason why the highest-quality issuers, counterintuitively, are also 
the largest issuers of debt currently, and may remain that way.  
• 
In total, we estimate a need of $1.2tn in external funds to fund AI capex. We think 
debt issuance will be the chosen channel of fundraising given its attractive cost vs 
equity.  
• 
Spread across five years, and multiple credit market segments, we think $300bn/year 
remains manageable in terms of credit technicals, especially if spaced out. 
• 
Further, suppliers such as NVDA and AVGO are increasingly acting as credit 
intermediaries by backstopping demand or are partnering directly with capital

Forecasting growth capex  
Our previous forecasting runs in the FTF series relied on benchmarks set by prior cycles. 
With increasing likelihood of AI-led capex cycle overshooting all historical precedents, we 
turn to more concrete ways of generating our estimates – namely, relying on announced 
datacenter capacity expansions through which we calculate needed Gigawatts (GWs). 
Within this framework, there are broadly two forecasting approaches: 
Estimating incremental GW capacity needed  
Bottom-up (DC pipeline-based): Starts with announced projects, utility 
interconnection queues, and contracted developments, then adjusts for permitting, 
power availability, and construction lead times. These forecasts tend to be more 
conservative as they are limited to deals under construction/announced and assume 
historical rates of project completion. 
Top-down (chip TAM-based): Starts with a total addressable market for AI chips, 
adjusted for hardware refresh cycles to isolate net new deployment, which is then 
converted into the capacity (GW) needed to host it. The output is therefore anchored to 
chip supply forecasts and assumes power, permitting, and construction constraints 
resolve favorably. Because physical bottlenecks are not explicitly modelled, these 
estimates typically sit at the higher end of the range (eg LBNL base case, BNEF bull 
case).  
While most available research has been focused in a singular direction, there are some 
sources that combine the two methodologies. Exhibit 2 summarizes some of the most 
prominent forecasts. Overall, we estimate 67GW in base case expansion of new 
incremental capacity from 2025-2030, and 115GW in the bull scenario. Note that this 
accounts for just growth capex, and factoring in maintenance capex is also required to 
get a sense of the total capex needs (discussed later).

Forecasting maintenance and total capex 
To estimate maintenance capex needs, we take a base of cumulative installed base until 
2025 (49GW) + expected incremental capacity over the next five years. Assuming IT 
equipment has an average useful life of six years, the annual refresh requirement is one-
sixth of the installed base at prior year-end. Maintenance is largely an IT capex story 
since non-IT infrastructure has a much longer life. We also adjust the prices by 10% y/y 
for IT equipment inflation. Over a five-year horizon, total IT maintenance capex comes to 
$2.0tn in our base case (63GW equivalent refreshed at $32bn/GW), of which accelerators 
account for $1.3tn. Exhibit 7 shows GWs refreshed along with average inflation-adjusted 
unit costs to generate our range of maintenance capex estimates.

Expect total debt issuance of ~$1.2tn through 2030 
This is what ultimately matters most to credit investors: translating capex estimates to 
future debt needs. We do this by comparing propensity to generate internal funds vs 
propensity to spend across issuers that are expected to generate the highest 
expenditures in the context of the AI/cloud buildout – core Hyperscalers (Microsoft, 
Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Oracle, SpaceX), alongside Neoclouds (Coreweave, Nebius).  
Internal vs external funding 
Bloomberg consensus for 2026-28 places combined operating cash flow (OCF) for these 
eight companies at $3.3tn, against total capex of $3.7tn (AI capex $2.9tn), implying a net 
funding need of $0.4tn over the next three years (Exhibit 11). Prorating these estimates 
to 2030 lifts total OCF to $6.6tn, and total capex to $6.9tn3, keeping the “deficit” still at 
$0.3tn. Adding in financing for dividends, buybacks and M&A, which are discretionary, 
but which likely continue across the high-quality issuers, lifts the total needed funds to 
$0.8tn.

Dispersion: Headline deficit lands at $0.3tn because one part of the cohort offsets the 
other. Consensus estimates from BBG put the big four issuers (Amazon, Alphabet, Meta 
and Microsoft) to be net FCF positive over 2026-30, with total OCF of $5.5tn, exceeding 
their capex needs of $5.2tn. Remaining issuers (SpaceX, Oracle, CoreWeave and Nebius) 
have more acute external funding needs, with estimated capex ($1.7tn) significantly 
outstripping OCF generation ($1.0tn). Unsurprisingly, this is also where investors are the 
wariest, pushing funding costs higher and requiring structural backstops like take-or-pay 
arrangements and long-dated capacity contracts to finance issuer builds.   
Cash preservation: Despite being FCF positive, high-quality issuers have also been the 
largest issuers of debt. This is likely because these issuers can afford the coupon to 
preserve balance sheet cash and create future strategic flexibility.  
Other spending needs: As mentioned before, other financing needs such as dividends, 
buybacks and M&A ($0.5tn cumulative) should be factored in.  
OCF estimates can change: Deficit estimates are highly sensitive to OCF estimates of 
the big four issuers. Should OCF be only 5% lower, the size of the total funding needs 
expands by 0.3tn given the sheer size of base numbers.  
In aggregate, these factors point to cumulative external funding needs of $1.2-$1.5tn: 
$0.5-$0.8tn from high-quality issuers and $0.7tn from the more fringe ones. 
Equity vs debt 
The decision to fund via debt or equity is a matter of cost. With cost of Hyperscaler debt 
running at 5% (after tax) vs cost of equity which is more than double at 11%, we expect 
debt funding to be the fundraising channel of choice for the foreseeable future.

Since a large portion of this debt funding remains opportunistic and driven by high-
quality issuers, allows the stock and cost of debt to remain in check, beyond which debt 
funding will not make economic sense, and an issuer might go to alternate markets 
(equity, converts), or abandon capex plans completely. This is an important feedback 
mechanism in well-functioning markets, a hint of which we saw play out in the July AI-
spread turmoil which halted issuer plans to place more debt. 
This also means capital is not the likely bottleneck for the AI-build, and other 
physical/regulatory factors will function as the primary inhibitors of the pace of 
expansion.  
Supply chain backstop & vendor financing 
One factor lowering the cost of debt for some pre-revenue names in the space is vendor 
financing – where a share of IT-layer spend is underwritten by chip suppliers for their 
customers. These suppliers are the biggest beneficiaries of the AI buildout – consensus 
forecasts NVDA and AVGO to generate $1.2tn in combined free cash flow over 2026-28. 
However, these firms are not term-lending or even funding datacenter builds off their 
own balance sheet. Instead, they are backstopping chip financing indirectly, through 
take-or-pay type structures, residual-value guarantees and more recently by 
guaranteeing a portion of the debt.  
These structures work differently but converge to the same result: vendor credit rather 
than the labs sets the clearing level, which is what turns an otherwise unfinanceable 
exposure into bankable paper. Nvidia's neocloud take-or-pay backstop does this through 
cash flow: recent arrangements (e.g. Sharon AI) have incorporated a six-year minimum 
revenue guarantee on deployed GPU capacity that rents back unused compute at a fixed 
rate, giving lenders a contractual revenue floor in place of hyperscaler offtake. Nvidia 
has also provided residual-value support for SB Energy’s PORTS-Pike campus, where 
OpenAI is the 20-year tenant and Nvidia the exclusive chip supplier. The guarantee does 
not cover rent as it falls due, but instead backstops any shortfall to a specified minimum 
value after an OpenAI default or insolvency and an unsuccessful re-leasing or sale 
process.  
Broadcom instead wrapped the collateral directly through its recently launched AI XPV 
Platform, guaranteeing the senior notes and the chips' residual value across $31bn of 
the Platform’s inaugural $35bn debt package raised with Apollo and Blackstone (87% of 
the debt stack). Comparing pricing between the tranches in that deal demonstrates the 
impact: the guaranteed A2 notes cleared at 5.75% against 8.5% on the unwrapped 
second-lien. By absorbing the residual value and merchant demand risk, vendors widen 
the lender base and lower the clearing yield, which should pull forward considerably 
more issuance against the IT layer than the labs could raise on their own credit. The 
offset is contingent liability at the suppliers, with BofA’s analyst modelling Broadcom's 
peak RVG exposure at roughly $370bn by mid-2029 if the platform scales to 20GW (see 
report: Broadcom Inc.).

CLO Spreads and Arbitrage 
As of Sep 11th, AAA CLO primary spreads are at 124bps, at slightly lower levels as 
compared to start of year (125bps) (Exhibit 50). Arbitrage as of Aug 28th is 210bps, 
higher than its 190bps level heading into the year, but down slightly from early March 
peak of 231bps (Exhibit 51).  
Exhibit 51 shows CLO spread levels by tranches. CLO arbitrage is a widely followed 
statistic in the loan market and represents the theoretical spread that managers can 
capture by issuing CLOs. Exhibit 51 compares CLO asset (Loan) spreads to the weighted 
average spreads of CLO liabilities. The difference between these two values is 
theoretical arbitrage and represents the current attractiveness of creating new CLOs. 
Our asset pricing is based on both primary (higher weight) and secondary loans, and 
liability pricing is based on the most current CLO primary pipeline from LevFin Insights.
