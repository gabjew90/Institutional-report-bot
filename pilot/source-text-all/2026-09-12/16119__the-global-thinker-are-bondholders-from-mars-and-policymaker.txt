Do markets and policymakers speak the same language? 
Treasury Secretary Bessent and Fed Chairman Warsh have introduced important changes 
in how policymakers interact with markets. While Bessent's more interventionist 
approach appears at odds with Warsh's more laissez-faire stance, both seem to share a 
common flaw: the belief that market outcomes can be improved by withholding 
information rather than strengthening the policy framework. The two approaches are 
also internally inconsistent. Warsh wants to learn more from market signals, while 
Bessent seeks to influence the prices that generate those signals. By impairing price 
discovery, one policy risks undermining the information the other is trying to extract. 
Higher real rates are the path of least resistance 
Interest rates are not historically high but normalizing to pre-2008 levels. The recent 
increase in long-term interest rates is driven by fundamentals: higher fiscal deficits 
post-pandemic and strong AI-related investment. The move is mostly in real rates and it 
is a global phenomenon rather than US-centric, concentrated in developed economies. 
Japan’s exit from YCC and heavy FX intervention is also putting pressure on global rates. 
Absent a collapse in AI-driven investment, lower fiscal deficits or widespread financial 
repression, the path of least resistance remains toward higher real interest rates.    
This is not a Draghi moment 
Interventions in financial markets are justified in cases of market failure, liquidity 
breakdown, externalities or coordination problems due to multiple equilibria. None of 
these conditions are present in US bond markets. This is not a Draghi moment. 
Intervention can end up being destabilizing and introduce moral hazard considerations by 
suppressing market signals. Intervention is not a substitute for addressing fundamentals. 
Policymakers can have asymmetric information but markets aggregate information more 
efficiently than any policymaker. Capping the long end of the curve effectively easy 
financial conditions and put more pressure on the Fed to hike down the road. 
Fiscal policy is stepping on a rake 
Fiscal policy is in disarray in developed economies and there is no political will in sight 
to address fiscal deficits. In the US, the deficit is north of 6% of GDP, half of that being 
the interest bill, 70% of spending is entitlements, and 15% is defense. Buying back long-
term debt with short-term liabilities could potentially introduce rollover risk and further 
conditioning of monetary policy. Fiscal responsibility is a necessary condition for price 
stability. Fiscal dominance is not around the corner, but it is not far away either. 
Forward guidance is in the eye of the beholder 
Chairman Warsh wants to learn from markets and suppress forward guidance. But 
markets don’t price what the Fed should do, they price what they expect the Fed to do. 
And if the long end is influenced by US Treasury intervention, signals are even more 
distorted. The Fed should communicate its reaction function, which is the nominal 
anchor for the system. In our view, at Jackson Hole, Warsh ended up delivering too much 
guidance, which might end up costing the Fed degrees of freedom or credibility.

 Everybody is talking 
Treasury Secretary Bessent and Fed Chairman Warsh introduced structural changes in 
the way they interact with markets. Bessent's more interventionist strategy might 
appear to contrast with Warsh's more laissez-faire approach. But both strategies share a 
common weakness: they implicitly assume policymakers can improve market outcomes 
by withholding information from markets rather than improving the underlying policy 
framework. 
Bessent's approach appears to rely on the government having superior information 
about the "correct" level of asset prices and being able to push markets toward that level 
through intervention. But markets aggregate information more efficiently than any 
policymaker. Intervention works to address a market failure, a coordination problem or a 
financial stability externality. It is never a substitute for poor fundamentals. In this case, 
intervention makes market prices less informative for an efficient allocation of capital. 
Warsh's approach runs into the mirror-image problem. If the Fed deliberately reveals less 
about its reaction function in the hope of learning from market prices, those prices 
become less informative precisely because investors must spend more resources 
guessing what the Fed will do. The Fed then risks observing market movements that are 
partly responses to uncertainty about the Fed itself, mistakenly interpreting them for 
independent information about the economy. 
In both cases there is a circularity. Bessent wants policy to correct the market because 
he believes policymakers know more than the market, while Warsh wants the market to 
inform policy while making policy less transparent for the market to understand. 
A more robust framework is the opposite: policymakers should be transparent about 
their objectives and rection function, allow markets to aggregate information about 
fundamentals, embrace market discipline, and intervene only when there is a clearly 
identified market failure rather than when they simply dislike a market signal. 
Connecting the dots 
The U.S. Treasury has recently taken a series measures intended to influence asset 
prices. These include intervention in foreign-exchange markets to support the yen and 
an Operation Twist-style strategy that expands buybacks of long-term Treasury 
securities. Initially, these operations will be financed from existing cash balances, with 
short-term bill issuance likely to play a larger role over time. Markets have viewed these 
actions as a tentative first step toward influencing conditions at the long end of the 
Treasury curve. They were implemented against the backdrop of a significant shift in the 
Federal Reserve's communication framework under Chairman Warsh. 
Much of the discussion surrounding these policy actions focused on their most 
immediate, first-order effects. In this report, we adopt a different perspective, 
examining instead their broader interactions and implications for the conduct of fiscal 
and monetary policy. 
To frame the discussion, we begin by dissecting the recent increase in interest rates. We 
first document a set of stylized facts and then assess the underlying drivers of higher 
yields. 
We subsequently analyze the conditions required for successful market intervention and 
conclude that many of these preconditions were absent during the recent episodes of 
intervention. This observation leads us to what we view as the Achilles' heel of the U.S. 
economy: the fiscal outlook. 
Next, we evaluate the Federal Reserve's evolving communication strategy and its 
implications for volatility across fixed-income markets. 
Finally, we bring these strands together by examining the interaction between fiscal and 
monetary policy, highlighting the challenges and potential tensions that may arise as

Why are interest rates moving higher? 
Two stylized facts stand out. First, while attention has naturally centered on the United 
States, the increase in interest rates is a global phenomenon spanning both advanced 
and emerging economies (see Exhibit 1). Second, the adjustment has been driven 
predominantly by rising real rates rather than by higher inflation expectations (Exhibit 2). 
Together, these observations point to a conclusion that is becoming increasingly difficult 
to ignore: global interest rates are unlikely to revisit the extraordinarily low levels that 
characterized the post-Global Financial Crisis era. Rather, rates appear to be normalizing 
toward levels more consistent with those prevailing before the Lehman collapse. Yet 
periods of rising rates often expose underlying vulnerabilities. As in the late 1990s and 
early 2000s, important imbalances are once again emerging beneath the surface. 
Assessing their significance requires understanding the forces shaping both short- and 
long-term interest rates globally. 
Fiscal deficits meet AI investment boom 
The aftermath of the Global Financial Crisis was characterized by an unprecedented 
combination of financial repression, large-scale asset purchases by central banks, fiscal 
stimulus, excess global savings, and rapid globalization. Although fiscal policy 
subsequently became somewhat more restrained across advanced economies, these 
forces collectively contributed to an environment of exceptionally low interest rates that 
persisted for more than a decade. 
By the middle of the 2010s, however, this equilibrium had begun to shift. Global 
investment gradually recovered, globalization started to plateau, and fiscal positions in 
several advanced economies deteriorated. These developments marked the early stages 
of the secular rise in global interest rates. 
The pandemic accelerated this transition. In response to the unprecedented shock 
caused by COVID-19, policymakers deployed record monetary and fiscal stimulus. While 
these measures were effective in supporting the recovery, they were only partially 
reversed once economic activity normalized.

At the same time, a series of adverse supply shocks pushed real interest rates below 
their equilibrium levels and generated substantial excess liquidity. Combined with 
persistently expansionary fiscal policies, these conditions fueled a massive rally in asset 
prices, with associated wealth effects feeding back into the real economy. 
A broken record…with a new track 
Over the past several years, we have extensively discussed the limits of fiscal policy and 
their implications for interest rates in the United States, the United Kingdom, the Euro 
Area, and Japan (see the report The Global Thinker: The case for no Fed cuts and The 
Global Thinker: Around the world in 5 questions). The underlying issue is therefore not 
new. What differed in the post-crisis period was the macroeconomic environment. 
Limited investment opportunities, abundant global savings, and ongoing central-bank 
purchases allowed markets to absorb growing fiscal deficits while keeping borrowing 
costs at historically low levels. 
What has fundamentally altered the global savings-investment balance over the last 18 
months is the AI-driven investment boom, centered largely in the United States. 
Persistent fiscal deficits remain important in explaining the level of interest rates across 
advanced economies. However, they are less useful in explaining the recent acceleration 
in rates. Instead, the surge in AI-related investment has materially increased demand for 
capital. At the same time, rising asset prices have supported consumption through 
wealth effects, reducing effective saving. The combination of stronger investment and 
lower saving has placed additional upward pressure on equilibrium real interest rates 
(see Exhibit 3). 
AI is the canary in the coalmine 
Importantly, AI-related investment should not be viewed as the root cause of higher 
rates. Rather, it has exposed pre-existing vulnerabilities associated with persistent fiscal 
imbalances across advanced economies. In this sense, the AI boom has acted as a 
catalyst that revealed constraints that had previously been masked by an environment of 
excess savings and abundant liquidity. 
This adjustment has been reinforced by the gradual reversal of quantitative easing 
across many advanced economies. As central banks reduce the size of their balance 
sheets, private investors are required to absorb a larger share of sovereign debt 
issuance. The return of market-based price discovery, particularly at the long end of yield 
curves, has therefore amplified the upward pressure on interest rates at a time when 
investment financing needs are rising.

Viewed through the lens of the global savings-investment framework, the recent 
increase in interest rates reflects a shift in equilibrium conditions. The global savings 
schedule has moved inward, while the demand for investment has increased. Higher 
interest rates are the natural outcome of this adjustment. More importantly, this 
framework helps explain why rates remained relatively contained for so long despite 
worsening fiscal positions. A prolonged period of exceptionally abundant savings created 
the illusion that fiscal trajectories were more sustainable than they actually were. 
A fundamentals-driven adjustment 
It is important to emphasize that the increase in real interest rates has, so far, been 
largely orderly. The evidence suggests a repricing driven primarily by economic 
fundamentals rather than by market dysfunction. In particular, the adjustment reflects a 
combination of a higher equilibrium real interest rate (r*) and rising risk premia. 
Notably, higher risk premia have emerged despite the still-low probability of nominal 
default on domestic-currency sovereign debt issued by advanced economies. Instead, 
investors appear increasingly concerned about the risk of losses in real terms. Such 
losses can arise through higher inflation, financial repression, or a combination of both. 
Consequently, sovereign risk is not reflected solely in bond markets. It may also manifest 
itself through currency depreciation. Indeed, the greater the degree of financial 
repression in domestic fixed-income markets, the more likely these adjustment 
pressures are to emerge through foreign-exchange markets. 
Finally, although the focus on U.S. interest rates is understandable given the central role 
of U.S. capital markets in determining global financial conditions, developments 
elsewhere also matter. In particular, the rapid normalization of interest rates in Japan is 
increasingly relevant. As domestic yields rise, Japanese investors, who remain among the 
largest holders of foreign assets, face stronger incentives to repatriate capital. This 
dynamic could exert additional upward pressure on global yields, including those in the 
United States. 
Intervention in markets: a bad idea? 
Financial markets aggregate information in the most efficient manner available. Asset 
prices provide powerful signals for the allocation of capital and play a central role in 
directing financial resources toward their most productive uses. However, prices reflect 
more than just economic fundamentals and risk premia. They also embed the effects of 
market positioning, liquidity conditions, balance-sheet constraints, and limits to 
arbitrage. These technical factors can introduce noise into prices and complicate the 
process of extracting information about underlying fundamentals. 
This distinction is critical when assessing whether policy intervention is warranted. 
Intervention is justified when market outcomes are distorted by failures in market 
functioning, coordination problems, or financial-stability externalities. It is far less 
compelling when the objective is simply to push prices toward levels preferred by 
policymakers. 
Market failures can emerge when dealers and arbitrageurs are unable or unwilling to 
intermediate. Under such conditions, forced sales may drive asset prices significantly 
away from their fundamental values, while spreads reflect liquidity shortages and market 
plumbing constraints rather than new information about economic prospects. In these 
circumstances, temporary intervention can help restore market functioning and improve 
price discovery. 
Coordination failures provide another rationale for intervention. When multiple equilibria 
are possible, self-fulfilling expectations can push markets toward an inefficient outcome. 
A classic example is a sovereign debt market in which fears of default lead investors to 
demand higher yields. The resulting increase in borrowing costs weakens fiscal 
sustainability, ultimately validating the initial concerns. In such cases, intervention can 
help coordinate expectations around the more efficient equilibrium.

Financial-stability externalities constitute a third category. Fire sales, balance-sheet 
constraints, and liquidity shortages can generate feedback loops that amplify market 
stress and widen the gap between prices and fundamentals. By alleviating these 
constraints, policymakers can prevent temporary dislocations from evolving into broader 
systemic risks. 
The key principle is that intervention is most effective when it addresses a genuine 
distortion that prevents prices from reflecting fundamentals. When prices are primarily 
conveying information about changes in underlying economic conditions, attempts to 
resist those signals are unlikely to succeed and may ultimately undermine policy 
credibility. 
When does intervention work? A few success cases 
In many cases intervention is justified and properly executed it works. We will illustrate 
it with 3 relatively recent examples. The first one is the liquidity crisis of March 2020, 
which is an example of limits to arbitrage combined with fire sale. The second one is the 
intervention of the US Treasury in the FX market in Argentina ahead of midterm 
elections. The third one is the famous Draghi’s “Whatever it Takes” to save the Euro. In 
each case, although not very visible, there are hidden costs that are important to 
consider. 
Case 1: March 2020 liquidity crisis in the US 
In March 2020, the US Treasury market went under a lot of pressure as the COVID shock 
intensified and the demand for cash exploded. Institutional investors, in particular 
leveraged hedge funds, foreign official institutions and mutual funds sold Treasuries 
aggressively to raise cash and meet redemptions and margin calls. The flow of selling 
was so big that dealers quickly hit their balance sheet constraints and could not absorb 
the unprecedented volume of selling.  
Liquidity in the Treasury market collapsed and bid-offers increased sharply, and with that 
volatility, feeding back into higher margin calls and the need for fire selling Treasuries. 
Cash-futures pricing got severely distorted, forcing leveraged hedge funds exposed to 
arbitrage the cash-futures basis to sell. The key point is that the selling pressure on US 
Treasuries was not driven by a deterioration in fundamentals but a need to raise cash. 
To restore market liquidity, the Fed intervened by massively expanding repo operations 
and purchased all the US Treasuries and agency securities that the market wanted to 
sell, effectively using its balance sheet as a replacement of the dealers’ balance sheets, 
becoming the buyer of last resort until liquidity was restored. 
The key here is that the Fed was correcting a market failure due to a combination of 
liquidity stress, limits to arbitrage and self-reinforcing fire-sale loop that limited normal 
price discovery. The Fed was not trying to engineer financial repression to override the 
repricing due to investors’ perception of more deteriorated fundamentals, such as higher 
fiscal deficits or inflation expectations. 
Case 2: US Treasury intervention in Argentina ahead of midterm elections 
Last year, ahead of the Argentine’s October midterm elections, investors became 
worried that a poor result for the incumbent Javier Milei would weaken his presence in 
Congress and derail the much-needed fiscal adjustment and structural reforms, 
generating a self-reinforcing negative dynamic. Expectations of political defeat leading 
to policy reversal and devaluation of the currency, which triggered a hedging demand for 
dollars by households and corporates, higher sovereign spreads, tighter domestic 
financial conditions, weaker economic sentiment and recession. In turn, this was 
increasing the probability of President Milei’s party doing poorly in the midterm 
elections. 
This case represents a good example of multiple equilibria. In the good equilibrium, 
investors believe Milei will do well in the midterm elections, reforms and market friendly

policies will continue, the peso and spreads remain stable, the economy muddles trough 
and Milei end up doing well in the midterm elections. In the bad equilibrium, investors 
believe that other investors will hedge against the prospects of Milei doing poorly in the 
midterm elections, the peso selloff, the central bank cannot control the FX market due to 
insufficient reserves, financial conditions tighten, the economy tanks and Milei end up 
doing poorly in the elections. 
Notice that markets are not necessarily changing their views about the fundamentals of 
the economy, but it is more a case of coordination failure. By offering a $20 billion swap 
line to Argentina, the US Treasury became the ultimate backstop in case of a run against 
the currency, effectively changing the expectations of investors. At the end, only $2.5 
billon of the swap was executed, and Milei’s party did well in the midterm elections. 
Case 3: Draghi’s “Whatever it takes” 
This is the cleanest example of a central bank intervention designed to eliminate a bad 
self-fulfilling equilibrium, rather than subsidizing a fundamentally insolvent government. 
By mid-2012, sovereign spreads of countries such as Span and Italy had risen 
dramatically, in part reflecting legitimate fiscal and banking risks, but an increasingly 
important component was denomination risk. That is, investors feared these countries 
would leave the euro.  
As in the previous example, the rise in yields made the sovereign debt less sustainable, 
weakening the banking system that had exposure to those bonds, tightening financial 
conditions, worsening the recession and fiscal position, in the end making the default 
event more likely. In this sense, it was an example of multiple equilibria, where a country 
can be solvent at normal interest rates but insolvent at crisis interest rates. Markets 
could coordinate on a bad equilibrium even if the country might have remained solvent 
at normal borrowing costs. 
On July 2012, the ECB Governor Mario Draghi announced the ECB was ready to whatever 
it takes within its mandate to preserve the euro. The commitment was made concrete in 
September through the announcement of the OMT (Outright Monetary Transactions), 
where the ECB stood ready to purchase unlimited quantities of eligible sovereign bonds 
in the secondary market, particularly maturities of one to three years.  Crucially, OMT 
was conditional on the government entering and complying with an EFSF (European 
Financial Stability Facility) and ESM (European Stability Mechanism) adjustment 
program. The adjustment program was designed to mitigate moral hazard considerations 
and put fundamentals in order. 
Interestingly, the OMT was never executed. The credibility of the unlimited backstop 
changed investor incentives. Markets quickly coordinated into a good equilibrium where 
spreads tightened. Fiscal conditionality through EFSF/ESM compliance ensured the good 
equilibrium was fundamentally sustainable, while ECB commitment prevented markets 
from coordinating on the bad equilibrium. 
Will intervention works this time? Japan meets US 
The Treasury's recent interventions, first in support of the yen and later through the 
expansion of long-dated Treasury buybacks, have a common underlying objective: 
influencing prices in markets that are sending increasingly uncomfortable signals about 
underlying macroeconomic conditions. While some aspects of these measures may be 
justified by liquidity or market-functioning considerations, others appear aimed at 
dampening information contained in market prices. 
History suggests that distinction matters. Interventions can mitigate temporary 
dislocations, but they rarely succeed in reversing trends driven by fundamentals. When 
prices reflect deteriorating fiscal dynamics or a shift in the savings-investment balance, 
intervention is not a cure. It merely delays the adjustment. Ultimately, fundamentals 
tend to prevail.

Japan: The good, the bad and the ugly 
Japan has a long history of foreign-exchange interventions with mixed results. While 
interventions can generate an immediate market reaction by forcing the unwinding of 
speculative positions, their effects have typically proven short-lived. In the absence of a 
meaningful adjustment in the monetary and fiscal policy mix, interventions have often 
amounted to little more than temporary patches.  
Ultimately, real exchange rates are determined by fundamentals. Japan's well-known 
structural challenges, including an aging population, weak productivity growth, and 
subdued potential output, continue to weigh on long-term growth prospects and are 
therefore consistent with persistent depreciation pressure on the yen over time. 
Recent history illustrates these limitations. In 2022, Japanese authorities spent more 
than ¥9 trillion supporting the currency. In 2024, interventions were even more 
aggressive, totaling roughly ¥15 trillion over a two-month period. In both instances, the 
impact on the exchange rate proved temporary. Once the effects of intervention faded, 
carry-trade dynamics re-emerged, reflecting the continued attractiveness of higher-
yielding U.S. assets relative to domestic Japanese alternatives. 
The more recent joint intervention by Japan and the United States differs from previous 
episodes and can be analyzed through three lenses: the good, the bad, and the ugly. 
The Good 
The participation of the United States enhances the credibility of the intervention and 
strengthens its signaling and coordination effects. Market participants are naturally 
more reluctant to challenge an intervention that carries the backing of both countries.  
Moreover, access to the Federal Reserve's FIMA (Foreign and International Monetary 
Authorities) repo facility allows Japan to obtain dollar liquidity without liquidating its 
Treasury holdings. This reduces the risk that foreign-exchange intervention translates 
into large-scale Treasury sales, thereby limiting potential spillovers into the U.S. bond 
market and improving financial-market stability. 
The Bad 
For intervention to have durable effects, it must ultimately be supported by changes in 
underlying fundamentals. While the Bank of Japan is expected to continue normalizing 
monetary policy, there is little evidence that Japan is prepared to implement the 
magnitude of monetary and fiscal adjustments required to alter the longer-term outlook 
for the currency.  
As a result, intervention is unlikely to address the root causes of yen weakness. Instead, 
it risks creating moral hazard by encouraging market participants to expect periodic 
official support while delaying the necessary adjustment process. 
The Ugly 
The greatest risk is that repeated intervention generates increasingly severe policy 
inconsistencies. Japan could find itself simultaneously defending the yen while 
maintaining a monetary and fiscal policy stance that remains fundamentally inconsistent 
with the desired exchange-rate objective. Such contradictions are rarely sustainable. 
Over time, they can distort capital allocation, weaken market discipline, and undermine 
price discovery. 
Moreover, persistent intervention may ultimately shift pressures elsewhere in the 
financial system. If exchange-rate adjustments are suppressed while underlying 
imbalances remain unresolved, the burden of adjustment could fall on domestic bond 
markets. In that scenario, attempts to support the currency might come at the cost of 
greater volatility at the long end of the Japanese government bond curve, particularly as 
monetary normalization proceeds and private investors are required to absorb a larger 
share of sovereign issuance.

Ultimately, the success of any intervention depends less on the size of official 
operations than on their consistency with the underlying policy framework. When 
intervention reinforces a credible adjustment in fundamentals, it can accelerate the 
transition toward a more stable equilibrium. When it seeks to offset fundamentals, it 
merely delays the adjustment and increases the risk of larger distortions later on. 
US Treasury buyback: Lost in translation 
The recent expansion of the U.S. Treasury's buyback program, often described as an 
Operation Twist-style strategy because it involves the purchase of long-dated securities 
financed through the issuance of short-term liabilities, highlights the distinction 
between desirable and undesirable forms of market intervention. 
Initially, the program could be interpreted as a relatively benign measure aimed at 
improving market functioning. The scale was modest, with buybacks increasing from 
roughly $2 billion to $4 billion in a market segment exceeding $1 trillion. At the end, this 
week Treasury Secretary Bessent announced Treasury buybacks up to $6bn, which fell 
short of market’s expectations and the long end of the curve sold off on the 
announcement. Viewed in isolation, such operations could plausibly be justified as a tool 
to address liquidity frictions or market segmentation in off-the-run Treasury securities. 
That interpretation became more difficult to sustain once subsequent communications 
were considered. A program originally presented as a market-liquidity tool increasingly 
appeared to be associated with an effort to influence the pricing of long-term 
government bonds. 
The shift in perception intensified when Secretary Bessent suggested that the Treasury 
could ultimately repurchase as much as $1 trillion of long-dated securities while arguing 
that market participants were operating with incomplete information. The implication 
was that long-term yields were deviating from values consistent with underlying 
fundamentals. When combined with concerns that Japanese intervention could result in 
Treasury sales and spill over into the U.S. rates market, the broader narrative 
surrounding the intervention changed considerably. 
The challenge is that intervention cannot permanently offset weak fundamentals. 
Indeed, if markets perceive an intervention as inconsistent with underlying economic 
realities, the opposite effect can occur. Investors may choose to test policymakers' 
resolve, resulting in greater rather than lower volatility. The market reaction following 
the announcement broadly fits this pattern, with an initial rally in Treasuries 
subsequently giving way to a reversal. 
The notion of asymmetric information is particularly delicate. If policymakers possess 
information that is materially different from that embedded in market prices, then 
transparency is generally more valuable than intervention. Market prices are informative 
precisely because they aggregate dispersed information from millions of participants. To 
the extent that policymakers attempt to influence prices without revealing the 
underlying information that justifies their view, price signals may become less 
informative and less efficient as guides for capital allocation. 
Crucially, possessing privileged information does not necessarily imply a superior ability 
to process and aggregate information relative to financial markets. The risk is therefore 
that intervention weakens the informational content of prices without improving the 
market's understanding of fundamentals. If policymakers believe markets are mispricing 
risk because they are operating with incomplete information, the most effective remedy 
is often greater transparency rather than stronger intervention. 
There is also a risk that such interventions create a coordination problem. In the absence 
of clear evidence of market dysfunction, repeated efforts to influence yields can be 
interpreted as a signal that authorities are increasingly concerned about the 
sustainability of current financing conditions. Rather than reassuring investors, 
intervention may prompt markets to reassess risk premia and question why policymakers

believe action is necessary. Unless supported by a sufficiently powerful and credible 
policy framework, interventions intended to reduce volatility can ultimately end up 
amplifying it. 
More broadly, intervention itself becomes a powerful signal. It can help coordinate 
expectations around a desirable equilibrium when markets are disrupted. However, it can 
also coordinate expectations around an erroneous assessment if policymakers' diagnosis 
of the problem proves incorrect. The larger the intervention and the weaker the evidence 
of market dysfunction, the greater this risk becomes. 
As with the discussion of Japan, the Treasury's intervention can be viewed through the 
lens of the good, the bad, and the ugly. 
The Good 
The buyback program may improve market functioning in specific segments of the 
Treasury market. By supporting liquidity in off-the-run securities and alleviating 
temporary segmentation, it can enhance price discovery and reduce frictions that are 
unrelated to fundamentals. 
The Bad 
To the extent that the program suppresses term premia that are correctly reflecting 
deteriorating fiscal dynamics, rising financing needs, or a higher equilibrium real interest 
rate, it risks obscuring valuable market signals. In that case, intervention becomes less 
about improving market functioning and more about resisting the information embedded 
in market prices. 
The Ugly 
The most adverse interpretation is that the program could potentially be interpreted as 
an early step toward a broader regime of financial intervention. Under this scenario, 
policymakers increasingly rely on balance-sheet policies and market intervention to 
contain borrowing costs rather than addressing the underlying drivers of fiscal 
deterioration. Such an approach would weaken price discovery, create a de facto fiscal 
policy put, and amplify moral hazard by reducing incentives to confront the fundamental 
issue: the trajectory of the fiscal deficit. 
Ultimately, the success of the buyback program depends on whether it addresses 
genuine market imperfection or attempts to counteract a market signal. If the problem is 
liquidity, intervention can help. If the problem is the fundamentals, intervention can only 
postpone the adjustment. 
What are the limits of fiscal policy? 
Fiscal positions deteriorated significantly across advanced economies in the aftermath 
of the pandemic. For several years, these vulnerabilities remained largely hidden by 
exceptionally low interest rates, abundant liquidity, and central-bank balance-sheet 
expansion.  
More recently, however, the sharp increase in real interest rates has brought debt 
sustainability back to the center of the policy debate. Absent meaningful fiscal 
consolidation, higher financing costs threaten to erode fiscal space and reduce 
governments' ability to respond to future economic downturns (see Exhibit 4, Exhibit 5). 
The recent AI-driven investment boom has accelerated this process, but not because it 
created the fiscal problem. Rather, it exposed it. By contributing to a rise in equilibrium 
real interest rates, the investment surge acted as a stress test for public finances. In this 
sense, AI is best viewed as the canary in the coal mine, not the root cause of the 
problem. The underlying challenge is that many advanced economies entered this period 
with fiscal positions that had become increasingly reliant on an environment of unusually 
low borrowing costs.

The spillover of higher U.S. real interest rates into global financial markets has further

considerations are likely to exert increasing influence on both financial markets and the 
policy environment. Fiscal dominance is not around the corner, but neither is it a distant 
theoretical possibility (The Global Thinker: Let’s talk about inflation risks). 
Time inconsistent fiscal policy 
To understand the source of these vulnerabilities, it is useful to revisit the 
macroeconomic environment that prevailed after the Global Financial Crisis. In a world 
characterized by exceptionally low interest rates, fiscal trade-offs appeared largely 
absent. Governments could increase debt-financed spending during downturns with little 
pressure to rebuild fiscal buffers during subsequent expansions. This experience led 
many economists and policymakers to conclude that substantially higher debt levels 
were sustainable and, in some cases, desirable. The argument was often framed within 
the secular stagnation paradigm, in which insufficient aggregate spending justified a 
more active role for fiscal policy. 
The intellectual foundation for this view rested on the debt-sustainability condition 
commonly summarized as (r - g). As long as the real interest rate (r) remained below the 
economy's growth rate (g), debt dynamics appeared manageable and governments could 
maintain higher debt burdens without triggering explosive debt trajectories. 
The problem with this argument is that if debt is being issue to finance permanent 
increases in spending, such as entitlements, then it is politically very costly to reverse 
policy to put the fiscal house in order. A classic time inconsistency problem. 
Central banks sometimes help too much 
Central banks became an essential part of this equilibrium. Through successive rounds of 
quantitative easing and other unconventional policies, they absorbed large quantities of 
public and private debt that financial markets may not have been willing to finance at 
prevailing yields. By warehousing duration risk on their balance sheets, central banks 
compressed term premia, lowered borrowing costs, and eased financing conditions 
across the economy. 
In effect, quantitative easing relaxed market-imposed fiscal constraints. Governments 
benefited from an environment in which increasing debt outstanding did not translate 
into a proportional increase in financing costs. As a result, fiscal policy gained 
considerable room to operate while the discipline traditionally imposed by higher 
interest rates remained largely absent.

That environment is now changing. As central banks shrink their balance sheets and 
market-determined risk premia normalize, fiscal authorities face a very different set of 
constraints. Higher debt levels that appeared sustainable in a world of near-zero interest 
rates may prove considerably more difficult to finance in a regime characterized by 
structurally higher real rates. 
The key implication is that the limits of fiscal policy are becoming increasingly visible. 
The issue is not simply the size of existing debt stocks, but the interaction between 
elevated debt levels, higher borrowing costs, and reduced policy space. Governments 
that fail to restore more sustainable fiscal trajectories may find themselves entering the 
next downturn with less room to support growth, while central banks face increasing 
pressure to account for fiscal sustainability concerns when conducting monetary policy. 
Ultimately, the post-crisis lesson may not be that debt constraints disappeared, but 
rather that they were temporarily obscured by an extraordinary combination of financial 
repression, quantitative easing, and exceptionally low equilibrium interest rates. As those 
conditions fade, the underlying trade-offs of fiscal policy are re-emerging. 
US fiscal policy 
Fiscal backing is a necessary condition for price stability. And fiscal policy is the Achilles 
heel of the US economy. The headline fiscal deficit will reach 6.5% of GDP next year, 
with 3.3% of GDP being the interest bill (and growing, see Exhibit 6). These numbers are 
simply too high for this stage of the cycle. Public debt is already above 120% of GDP 
and the Treasury is increasing the fraction of debt issuance on the front end of the curve 
to avoid validating much higher rates in the long end (Exhibit 7).  
The Operation Twist recently announced by Secretary Bessent moves in the direction of 
shortening the duration of government liabilities even further to contain the long end of 
the curve. A significant shortening of duration would condition the conduct of monetary 
policy absent fiscal consolidation. Gradually, the US is starting to check all the boxes that 
lead to fiscal dominance.  
Recently, President Trump urged the Fed to cut interest rates arguing that the US 
government is paying a higher interest rate than its credit rating would justify. Although 
not advisable, it is not unusual that Presidents express their views on monetary policy. 
However, pushing the central bank to cut rates to reduce the government’s interest bill 
is not a compelling argument, flirting with a case of fiscal dominance.

Without fiscal consolidation, higher rates is the path of least resistance 
Geopolitical developments and the weaponization of US Treasuries are reducing the 
appetite of foreign buyers, which coupled with a persistently high fiscal deficit leaves

Fed vs Treasury: Look who is not talking 
Even under strict central bank independence conditions, monetary and fiscal policy 
influence each other in very complex ways. In this section, we analyze how Bessent's 
intervention strategy affects the conduct of monetary policy and how monetary policy 
feeds back into fiscal dynamics. 
Is this a Kohn day or a Bernanke day? 
Chairman Warsh started his Jackson Hole speech comparing his hiking experiences with 
former Vice Chairman Don Kohn and former Chairman Ben Bernanke, contrasting the 


[Page 14]
 
 
14 
The Global Thinker | 10 September 2026 
 
 
brisk style of Kohn vs the more leisurely style of Bernanke. And then he asked "Is this a 
Kohn Day or a Bernanke Day?". 
After listening his relatively hawkish speech and moreover, after listening Waller's 
relatively dovish speech, it is becoming evident that Chairman Warsh was trying to tell 
us more than an innocent joke with that analogy. 
The Bernanke trail is the slow, pre-emptive route: start hiking earlier, move gradually, 
and reduce the risk that inflation or financial conditions get far enough out of line to 
require a more painful tightening later. The Kohn trail is the alternative: wait longer but 
if the inflation outlook deteriorates, be prepared to hike faster and more aggressively. 
The question is then not really whether to hike, but whether to hike slowly and pre-
emptively today or risk having to hike much faster tomorrow. 
Forward Disguidance 
This framing helps explain why Warsh's Jackson Hole speech was so forceful. In our view, 
Warsh's main objective at Jackson Hole was to remedy the poor impact his 
communication strategy at July press conference had on markets. Back then, he 
communicated that markets themselves had done much of the tightening and therefore 
the Fed could afford to wait.  
A mild correction at Jackson Hole would not have been enough to restore credibility. He 
needed to be bolder. In July he hinted he was on the Bernanke trail. At Jackson Hole, 
Warsh had to convince markets that a switch to Kohn trail was still a real possibility. 
Despite insisting that he wants less forward guidance and more learning from markets, 
the need to repair July’s credibility damage pushed Warsh into a communication trap. 
Because he had more reputational capital tied to reversing the July message, he could 
not simply describe a neutral reaction function. He had to lean in the only direction that 
could restore credibility.  
By emphasizing that inflation remains too high, financial conditions are not restrictive 
enough, the labor market was fine, and the Fed still had work to do, Warsh effectively 
told markets that the default path had shifted toward the Kohn trail. He did not formally 
commit to a hike, but he gave much more directional guidance than his philosophy of 
communication would suggest. 
Waller and the freedom of not being the Chair 
Waller’s speech this week, certainly more dovish in nature, reflected the freedom of 
having to remedy the July’s communication breakdown. He didn’t have the need to prove 
he is willing to hike. That is why he properly describe his reaction function they way it is, 
a state-contingent rule. Given that the labor market is okay, If inflation continues to 
improve, don’t hike, if inflation deteriorates, hike.  
The asymmetry between Warsh and Waller is not only explained by potentially different 
readings of the economy. There is a relative skin in the game component. The seat of 
the Chairman has more skin in the game. And the Chairman speech was to some extent 
path-dependent. Waller had more flexibility to be transparent about conditionality. 
Paradoxically, the policymaker arguing against forward guidance ended up giving more 
of it, while the policymaker who openly describe the conditional nature of his reaction 
function gave markets less information about the actual path of rates.   
The lesson: more transparency means less commitment 
The hiking analogy captures the distinction neatly. Warsh effectively hinted that the Fed 
might have waited too long, so be prepared for the Kohn trail. Waller effectively hinted 
that here is the trail map, if the terrain stays benign, take Bernanke trail, if it 
deteriorates, take Kohn trail. The first tells market which trail is becoming more likely. 
The second tells markets how the Fed will choose between them.

The communication lesson is evident: the more clearly the Fed explains the rule 
governing the choice between the slow pre-emptive hike and the delayed aggressive 
hike, the less it needs to guide markets about which trail is will actually take. 
Fed's search for the optimal degree of transparency 
Chairman Warsh's speech at Jackson Hole was a welcome development at the margin, 
but substantial work remains to be done. As the July press conference demonstrated, 
moving from extensive forward guidance to virtually no guidance at all risks generating 
unnecessary volatility. The market immediately priced the shift through a bear 
steepening of the Treasury curve, wider inflation breakevens, and higher rates volatility.  
Rather than reducing uncertainty, the new communication framework increased 
uncertainty about the Federal Reserve's reaction function. Excessive forward guidance 
can distort price discovery, but the absence of guidance can create its own inefficiencies 
(see Exhibit 9), and higher risk premia (see the report Global Economic Weekly: Anna 
Karenina Principle of Monetary Policy).  
The combination of a steeper yield curve, lower equity prices, and a weaker dollar is 
uncomfortably close to the market reaction typically associated with credibility shocks in 
emerging-market central banks. The Federal Reserve was beginning to face a growing 
credibility challenge, and the Jackson Hole speech appeared aimed at arresting that 
deterioration. Nevertheless, markets are likely to continue testing the Fed's commitment 
to restoring and maintaining price stability. 
Chairman Warsh's initial press conference was not particularly informative. While 
reaffirming the Fed's commitment to price stability, it offered little insight into the 
strategy required to achieve it. The Jackson Hole speech represented an improvement by 
explicitly re-establishing 2% PCE inflation as the policy objective. However, it still fell 
short of clearly articulating the Fed's reaction function. Although Warsh reiterated that 
the policy rate remains the primary instrument for delivering price stability, he provided 
little discussion of how balance-sheet policy fits within the broader framework. 
More importantly, while he shared his assessment of the economy and suggested that 
inflation currently represents a greater concern than employment, he stopped short of 
explaining how those views would translate into future policy decisions. In practice, a 
reaction function is not defined by policymakers' objectives, but by how policy responds 
to incoming data and evolving risks. Ironically, despite his reluctance to engage in formal 
forward guidance, his characterization of economic conditions effectively acted as a 
form of forward guidance.

The reaction function is the nominal anchor 
Why is it so important to explain the reaction function? Because inflation is ultimately a 
monetary phenomenon, and every monetary regime requires a nominal anchor. Under 
monetary targeting, the money supply served that role. If money growth was fixed at 
3%, inflation would converge in the long run to money growth adjusted for the 
economy's trend growth rate. 
Under an interest-rate-based regime, however, the price level is not pinned down by the 
money supply itself. Instead, determinacy requires a systematic feedback rule describing 
how policy responds to the evolution of inflation and economic activity. In practice, the 
reaction function becomes the nominal anchor of the economy. A poorly understood 
reaction function weakens that anchor, increasing uncertainty not only about the future 
path of interest rates but, more importantly, about the future path of inflation. The 
result is greater macroeconomic volatility and a higher risk that inflation expectations 
become unanchored. 
Forward guidance is in the eye of the beholder 
There is considerable confusion about what forward guidance actually means and how it 
differs from a reaction function. Forward guidance provides information about the future 
path of policy. A reaction function, by contrast, explains how policymakers will adjust 
policy as economic conditions evolve. The distinction is important because one concerns 
future outcomes, while the other concerns the decision-making process that generates 
those outcomes. 
Forward guidance is a communication tool designed to influence expectations about 
future monetary policy, particularly policy rates. Importantly, there is a significant 
difference between calendar-based and state-contingent guidance. An example of the 
former is: "rates will remain at their current level for the rest of the year." An example of 
the latter is: "rates will remain low until unemployment falls below 5%." The second 
statement preserves substantially more flexibility because it conditions future policy on 
the state of the economy rather than on a predetermined timeline. 
Explaining the reaction function is a different exercise altogether. It requires describing 
how the Federal Reserve evaluates the state of the economy, weighs competing risks, 
and responds to evolving macroeconomic conditions. A particularly important example 
concerns the treatment of supply shocks and whether they should continue to be largely 
ignored when they become increasingly frequent and persistent. This discussion was 
notably absent from Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks. 
In practice, explaining the reaction function is akin to explaining the effective Taylor Rule 
that guides policy decisions. No central bank follows a mechanical rule, and policymakers 
must always contend with uncertainty surrounding data quality, model specifications, 
and parameter estimates. Yet these uncertainties do not prevent the FOMC from making 
decisions. The more clearly policymakers explain their analytical framework and 
decision-making process, the more efficiently markets can incorporate new information 
and the less unnecessary noise contaminates asset prices. 
The discussion of supply shocks also raises a broader question regarding the inflation 
target itself. An inflation target is essential for accountability. However, it is well 
understood that optimal monetary policy should generally look through temporary supply 
shocks as long as inflation expectations remain well anchored. The challenge arises 
when the economy is repeatedly hit by supply disruptions. In such an environment, 
policymakers cannot continuously invoke supply shocks as an ex post explanation for 
failing to deliver inflation at target. At some point, a tension emerges between the 


[Page 17]
 
 
The Global Thinker | 10 September 2026

stated objective and the available instruments. Ignoring that tension risks undermining 
the credibility of the target itself. 
Central banks can have predictable reaction functions without having predictable policy 
paths. Good central banking does not require telling markets where interest rates are 
going. It requires explaining why rates will ultimately go wherever they need to go. When 
the reaction function is well understood, markets can form their own expectations 
without relying on explicit policy commitments. 
Viewed through this lens, the Jackson Hole speech largely compensated for the 
uninformative and confusing July press conference. The positive market reaction may 
therefore have reflected the low bar established after July rather than a fundamental 
improvement in communication. Warsh is now effectively back to square one. Markets 
will continue to test the Fed's credibility.  
The issue is not whether the Committee hikes in September. The issue is whether the 
decision is consistent with a clearly articulated reaction function. After Jackson Hole, 
failing to hike for reasons that appear inconsistent with the Fed's stated assessment of 
the economy could trigger an adverse market reaction, particularly at the long end of the 
yield curve, as investors reassess the credibility of the policy framework. 
Hall of mirrors or people reacting to incentives? 
Warsh's justification for abandoning forward guidance is that he wants markets to 
process information independently and form their own expectations. In his view, the 
Federal Reserve can learn from market prices only if those prices reflect investors' 
independent assessments of the economy rather than attempts to reverse-engineer Fed 
communication. 
The challenge is that market participants respond to incentives. Their objective is not to 
price what the Fed should do, but rather what they expect the Fed will do. If the Fed fails 
to provide a sufficiently clear description of its reaction function, that uncertainty will 
inevitably be reflected in asset prices. As a result, market prices will contain not only 
information about economic fundamentals, but also uncertainty about the Fed's own 
decision-making process. This makes the Fed's inference problem more difficult rather 
than easier. 
Markets are perfectly capable of pricing policy mistakes. Even when investors disagree 
with the likely policy path, they have every incentive to price the path they expect 
policymakers to follow. Consequently, observed market prices may reflect anticipated 
policy errors as much as underlying economic conditions. The less clearly the Fed 
communicates its reaction function, the harder it becomes to disentangle those two 
effects and extract meaningful information from market signals. 
In this sense, a well-articulated reaction function complements rather than undermines 
market discipline. By reducing uncertainty surrounding the policy framework, it allows 
asset prices to reflect economic news more efficiently and improves the informational 
content of market signals. If the objective is to maximize what the Fed can learn from 
markets while minimizing unnecessary noise, then providing a clear description of the 
reaction function is not a substitute for market pricing but a prerequisite for interpreting 
it correctly. Forward guidance is not necessarily needed at this stage of the cycle. A 
clearly communicated reaction function is. 
Is Bessent making Warsh’s job more or less difficult? 
Bessent's attempt to influence conditions at the long end of the Treasury curve can only 
operate directly through nominal yields. Whether real interest rates ultimately decline 
depends on the response of inflation expectations. If inflation expectations remain 
relatively contained, lower nominal yields would translate into lower real yields, resulting 
in an effective easing of financial conditions.

Under that scenario, the intervention would place additional pressure on the Federal 
Reserve to tighten monetary policy. The front end of the curve would need to reflect not 
only a stronger policy response to easier financial conditions, but also the fact that the 
buyback program is financed through the issuance of short-term liabilities. In effect, part 
of the duration removed from the long end would reappear at the front end of the curve. 
However, the opposite outcome is also possible. If the intervention leads investors to 
demand a higher risk premium, either because of concerns about fiscal sustainability or 
because policymakers are perceived as interfering with price discovery, long-term yields 
could ultimately rise rather than fall. In that case, financial conditions would tighten, 
providing the Fed with greater scope to wait and assess the impact on economic activity 
before adjusting policy. 
This asymmetry underscores the importance of understanding the drivers of market 
prices. A decline in yields driven by improved market functioning has very different 
implications from a decline driven by reduced term premia. Similarly, an increase in 
yields driven by stronger growth prospects sends a very different signal than an increase 
driven by rising fiscal or inflation risk premia. The appropriate policy response depends 
not on the direction of market moves, but on the forces driving them. 
Beware of how to read markets 
As discussed earlier, real interest rates have been trending higher not only in the United 
States but across much of the global economy. But does a rise in real interest rates 
necessarily imply tighter financial conditions? Interestingly, Chairman Warsh appeared to 
offer two different interpretations. During the July press conference, he suggested that 
the FOMC's decision to remain on hold was partly justified by the market doing some of 
the Fed's work through higher yields and an effective tightening of financial conditions. 
By Jackson Hole, however, he argued that financial conditions were hardly restrictive. 
Beyond this apparent inconsistency lies a more fundamental question. If higher real 
interest rates are primarily the result of an investment boom, particularly in the United 
States, then they should not be interpreted as an exogenous tightening of financial 
conditions. Rather, they represent the endogenous market response to stronger demand 
for capital. In that case, higher real rates are not necessarily evidence of restrictive 
monetary conditions. If anything, they may be the consequence of financial conditions 
being sufficiently accommodative to support a surge in investment demand. 
Viewed through this lens, the increase in real rates reflects a rise in the equilibrium real 
interest rate, or r*. As r* increases, a given policy rate becomes less restrictive over time. 
Put differently, the policy stance may be easing relative to the economy's underlying 
equilibrium even as market interest rates move higher. This interpretation is particularly 
relevant for the United States, where investment demand appears to be the dominant 
driver of higher real yields. 
The picture is different for economies that are not experiencing a comparable 
investment boom. In those cases, the increase in real interest rates resembles more 
closely an exogenous tightening of financial conditions imported from abroad. Higher 
global yields raise borrowing costs, tighten financial conditions, and weigh on economic 
activity without the offsetting benefit of stronger domestic investment demand. 
A different interpretation is required when rising yields are driven by higher risk premia 
rather than by stronger demand for capital. In that case, the market is requiring greater 
compensation to finance a given level of investment, whether due to fiscal concerns, 
inflation uncertainty, or other sources of risk. The economic implications are materially 
different. An increase in real rates driven by higher r* reflects stronger underlying 
demand and may imply less restrictive policy than commonly assumed. An increase 
driven by higher risk premia, by contrast, represents a genuine tightening of financial 
conditions. Understanding which force is dominating is therefore critical for both 
monetary policy and the interpretation of market signals.

Is the steeper curve signaling less policy credibility or a policy mistake? 
Conventional wisdom holds that a bear steepening driven by higher inflation breakevens 
reflects concerns about central-bank credibility, whereas a steepening driven by higher 
real rates reflects either an increase in the equilibrium real interest rate, r*, or a higher 
fiscal risk premium. While this distinction is useful, it can also be misleading. If markets 
believe the Federal Reserve is falling behind the curve but remains ultimately committed 
to restoring price stability, then fewer hikes today imply more aggressive tightening 
tomorrow. In that scenario, a steeper real yield curve, particularly at the front end and in 
the belly of the curve, may simply reflect expectations of a more forceful policy response 
in the future rather than a rise in equilibrium real rates or fiscal concerns. 
A similar logic applies to inflation breakevens. Their behavior cannot be interpreted 
independently from expectations regarding the Fed's reaction function. When Warsh 
assumed the chairmanship, his initial press conference was perceived as more hawkish 
than expected. Inflation breakevens declined as real yields rose by more than nominal 
yields. Some observers subsequently interpreted the decline in breakevens as evidence 
that the Fed no longer needed to tighten policy. But this reasoning confused cause and 
effect. The decline in inflation compensation was itself the endogenous response of 
markets to a perceived shift in the expected reaction function of the Federal Reserve. 
More broadly, market prices should not be treated as exogenous inputs into the policy 
process. They are equilibrium outcomes that reflect expectations about future policy. 
Changes in inflation breakevens, real rates, and risk premia often represent the market's 
assessment of how the central bank is likely to react to evolving economic conditions. 
Interpreting those variables correctly therefore requires understanding the underlying 
causality. A decline in inflation breakevens is not necessarily evidence that inflation risks 
have diminished. It may instead reflect increased confidence that the central bank will 
act forcefully enough to contain those risks. Reading causality in reverse can lead both 
policymakers and investors to draw precisely the wrong conclusions from market prices.
