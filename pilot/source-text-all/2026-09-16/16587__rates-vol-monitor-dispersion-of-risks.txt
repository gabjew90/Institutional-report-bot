Rates Vol Monitor: Dispersion of Risks
15 September 2026 | 3:11PM EDT
Rates volatility has been generally subdued through much of the rise in yields, 
n
though last week’s selloff was accompanied by higher vol. While increased policy 
uncertainty has been a focal point in the US, and realized volatility at the very 
front-end of the US curve has risen, forecast dispersion for front-end rates has 
been fairly stable. Controlling for a broader set of macro drivers, the recent move 
up has taken US rates implied vol from the lower end of our fair value range to 
around the midpoint, suggesting vulnerability to an outcome that more clearly 
widens the distribution of policy outcomes. 
We find evidence that mildly hawkish Fed surprises relative to market pricing 
n
tend to deliver better vol-selling returns following the meetings than holds when 
the market put some risk on a hike. Assuming the Fed delivers the more or less 
fully priced 25bp hike on Wednesday, we think the broader implications for vol 
will likely hinge on the forward-looking indications from the SEP and/or press 
conference. A one-to-two hike median in the dot plot paired with emphasis on 
assessing incoming data would not resolve forward-looking uncertainty, but it 
should allow for some moderation in vol by removing the tail of a policy-driven 
widening of the rate distribution.   
While our economists’ baseline is consistent with a more measured, 
n
data-dependent signal on the path ahead, last week’s ECB decision illustrated 
the potential impact of a strongly hawkish message as a source of sharp, 
front-end led vol upside. Conversely, a surprise hold at the September FOMC 
would likely support higher volatility further out the curve. 
Further out the curve, Treasury’s increase in long-end buybacks has not had an 
n
observable impact on long-end swaption vol relative to shorter tenors. There has 
been a decline in the correlation between the 2-to-5y part of the curve and 
longer-tenors since mid-August, however, supporting curve volatility. We find 
that periods where growth and policy impulses go hand in hand (weak 
growth/dovish policy, strong growth/hawkish policy) on average correspond to 
lower correlation between points along the curve, and thus higher curve vol (all 
else equal). Given starting levels of curve vol and our macroeconomic and policy 
baseline, we think curve options are best used as hedges against the more 
extreme tails.

Dispersion of Risks 
 
 
Energy volatility has been the predominant focus for global rates markets for much of 
the year, but a shift in Fed communication and to an extent Treasury’s approach to its 
supply decisions were sources of policy uncertainty this summer. We have shown 
previously that greater policy uncertainty can contribute to higher long-end yields and a 
steeper curve via increased term premium, and realized vol has risen at the very 
front-end of the US curve versus other markets (Exhibit 1). But that increase in very 
front-end vol has not translated to a consistent increase in broader rate volatility even as 
yields have moved higher. Instead, implied vol has adhered more closely to the relatively 
stable dispersion in forecasts of 1y ahead policy rate outcomes, which suggest that, 
despite the near-term reaction function uncertainty, policy rate expectations have 
moved higher in relative unison rather than via widening the distribution (Exhibit 2). Even 
after the uptick in US rates vol that accompanied last week’s selloff, once we control for 
a broader set of macro fundamentals, we find that implied vols are near the midpoint of 
our estimated fair value range. In some sense, the under-explored risk to the recent rates 
regime remains a more pronounced widening of the distribution around medium-term 
rate outcomes.  
 
Chairman Warsh’s speech in Jackson Hole reduced the uncertainty surrounding his 
assessment of the economy. Further, comments from other FOMC members also 
contributed to greater clarity around a perceived reaction function and helped to 
reestablish the front-end of the curve as the predominant driver in rallies and selloffs. 
While our economists see the case as less clear-cut than market pricing implies, they 
nonetheless expect the Fed to hike rates at this week’s meeting. Assuming delivery of 
the near fully priced move, vol implications are likely to hinge on signals about the path 
ahead. 
Our economists suspect the Fed will want to nudge the market away from pricing an 
October hike (currently pricing about 12bp) too confidently, and they expect a 10-8 
majority to show one hike in 2026 (with a risk of a majority for two if a sufficient number 
of participants see hikes as a normal response to higher oil prices). Following past hikes 
where markets priced in about 75-to-90% of the move, we find that vol-selling

surprise (Exhibit 3). Paired with delivery of a hike at the September FOMC, we thus 
expect a measured signal on the forward-looking path to partially moderate the recent 
spike in front-end volatility, steepen tail curves, and leave volatility across the curve 
sensitive to the data and energy prices. Under our baseline where inflation news is 
sufficiently benign to keep the Fed on hold beyond Wednesday’s meeting, we would 
expect volatility to moderate alongside a gradual erosion in hike risks. 
A dovish surprise, such as a hold, would likely boost realized and implied volatility across 
the surface, following the playbook from the June FOMC, where longer-dated vol 
increased despite the brief reprieve in short-tail and short-expiry swaptions (Exhibit 4). 
With expectations for a rate hike notably higher now than in July, we think such an 
outcome would be a more much notable vol-shock to the market. We think a hold would 
not immediately reduce future hike expectations but would instead provide further 
uncertainty around the front-end while keeping inflation concerns and higher term 
premium intact. 
 
Last week’s ECB meeting offered a template for the potential impact of an overtly 
hawkish signal accompanying a hike, with upper left vol leading a sharp repricing across 
the surface as the signal opened up the distribution around the policy path. An 
approach that leans against inflation risks via more forceful policy tightening can work to 
insulate longer-term forwards against bearish pressure further out the curve, but it 
brings greater risk of knocking the market out of the relatively low vol regime. 
Looking across past cycles, whether the first hike corresponds to a clear move higher in 
volatility depends in part on the priced follow-through, with vol displaying some 
directionality with forward rates. The two instances where the onset of hikes led to 
materially higher vol across the surface in the aftermath were in 1999 and 2022, which 
were the only two instances in which front-end forwards were meaningfully higher three 
months following the first hike (Exhibit 5& Exhibit 6). The one-and-done hike in 1997 
saw volatility move steadily lower (even though yields rose temporarily in the immediate 
aftermath of the hike).

Outside the front-end, Treasury’s surprise increase in long-end buybacks has coincided 
with the recent long-end flattening but has not lowered the relative level of long-end vol 
versus shorter tenors. While tactically we think a reduction in local supply can support 
the long-end on ASW and curtail the risk of supply-driven long-end volatility, evidence 
from long-end supply reductions in the UK and Japan have shown that underlying macro 
drivers still dominate. With any supply effect likely to show up first and foremost in 
spreads, and uncertainty around Treasury’s reaction function still present, we are 
skeptical that the buybacks should meaningfully lower long-end swaption volatility. 
All these factors combined have taken realized curve correlations lower over the 
summer, with 2s10s hitting local lows, helping to support curve vol1 (Exhibit 7). Further 
out the curve the selloff has been more in parallel, with 10s30s correlations recovering 
from the low points seen earlier in the Iran conflict. This highlights the mixed signals for 
curves recently. Usually, lower correlation (either via strong flattening or steepening) 
comes along clear macro signals in one way or another – such as when strong growth 
and hawkish policy pricing or weak growth and dovish policy go hand in hand (Exhibit 8: 
the red columns). 
Instead, when markets reflect more tension between drivers, such as softer growth 
pricing but hawkish policy expectations, curve shifts are typically more parallel and 
correlation is high. From current, elevated levels of curve vol, and under our 
macroeconomic and policy baselines, we thus think long curve vol expressions are best 
viewed as hedges for the extreme tails.

Buying Options - Investors who buy call (put) options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes below (above) the strike

Selling Options - Investors who sell calls on securities they do not own risk unlimited loss of the security price less the strike price. Investors who sell 
covered calls (sell calls while owning the underlying security) risk having to deliver the underlying security or pay the difference between the security 
price and the strike price, depending on whether the option is settled by physical delivery or cash-settled. Investors who sell puts risk loss of the strike 
price less the premium received for selling the put. Investors who sell put or call spreads risk a maximum loss of the difference between the strikes less 
the premium received, while their maximum gain is the premium received. 
For options settled by physical delivery, the above risks assume the options buyer or seller, buys or sells the resulting securities at the settlement 
price on expiry.
