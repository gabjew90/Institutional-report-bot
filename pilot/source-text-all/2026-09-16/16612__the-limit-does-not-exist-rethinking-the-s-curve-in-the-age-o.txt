Today’s compute acceleration is best measured in terms of token throughput, 
which captures the processing power of the compute system in the real world. A 
token is the most basic unit of data an AI system parses, usually a word, part of a 
word, or punctuation. TFLOPS, or trillion floating point operations per second, is a 
term for the number of AI calculations the computing system can perform in one 
second. TFLOPS tracks peak theoretical compute in the absence of other 
bottlenecks. More TFLOPS raises the theoretical maximum for token throughput. 
However, the best computer systems minimize the cost and maximize the speed 
and power efficiency of token manipulation in the entire system. Real-world 
performance increasingly depends on how effectively the system can convert 
theoretical compute into token throughput.

Beyond the GPUs, you need CPUs to help direct traffic, memory chips to store and feed tokens, optical

latency (friction), which often requires placing them as close together as physics allows.
The path forward is visible. We have a line of sight to future improvements in each of these areas 
along multiple S-curves of growth.

Increasingly, AI is limited not by how fast GPUs can compute but instead by how quickly they can access 
data. Data, stored in memory, has become the fuel that keeps AI processors running. Every AI model 
depends on moving enormous amounts of data between storage, memory and the GPU. Processing 
tokens relies most on one specific type of memory, DRAM, which stores the data most frequently 
accessed by the GPU during computation. Adding memory bandwidth and shortening the distance 
between the GPU and DRAM chips increases the utilization rate of the GPU. These are the main levers 
today’s engineers are focused on to improve memory.

From roughly the 1960s through the early 2010s, DRAM bandwidth improved at a relatively steady pace of 
about 7%–10% per year. During that era, memory was largely viewed as a commodity, and DRAM generally 
moved fast enough to satisfy ordinary computing workloads. As a result, innovation was incremental, 
competition was intense, and the industry became notorious for its boom-and-bust cycles. After decades 
of consolidation, the market shrank from roughly 10 major producers to just three global leaders. 
Consolidation helped transform memory from a commodity business to one capable of funding long-term 
innovation. Higher returns allowed manufacturers to invest billions into innovations such as advanced 
packaging and next-generation HBM.
Invented and commercialized between 2013 to 2016, HBM fundamentally changed DRAM memory 
architecture. It dramatically shortened the distance data must travel between memory and the GPU. 
Instead of placing memory chips far from the processor, HBM stacks multiple DRAM dies vertically using 
through-silicon vias (TSVs) and places them millimeters from the GPU using advanced packaging. This 
increases memory bandwidth and reduces latency, power consumption, and physical footprint. It is like 
replacing several spread-out ranch homes with an eight-story apartment building connected by 
lightning-fast elevators. Over the past decade, HBM has increased memory bandwidth by roughly 10x, 
transforming AI system performance.
An Illustration of an HBM Stack
Even with these advances, memory has struggled to keep up with improvements in compute. As we have 
discussed, transistor density historically doubled about every two years under Moore’s Law, and, as a result, 
compute performance advanced far faster than memory bandwidth. Today, a GPU is only as useful as its 
ability to stay fed with data. If memory cannot deliver data quickly enough, expensive processors sit idle 
instead of performing calculations. This growing imbalance became known as the “memory wall”15—when 
processors spend a significant proportion of time waiting for data rather than performing computations.
Today, the outlook is improving. A healthier industry structure and stronger pricing have enabled 
sustained investment in research and development. Each new generation of HBM—now progressing to 
HBM416—delivers higher bandwidth, greater capacity, and better energy efficiency.

Beyond HBM, companies are also working to optimize other types of memory, including DDR memory, 
CXL-attached memory pools, High Bandwidth Flash (HBF), NAND flash storage, and other large-scale 
storage systems. Memory technologies exist in a hierarchy organized by cost, speed, and proximity to 
active computation. The most expensive forms of memory are also the fastest and closest to the GPU. 
Because they prioritize speed, they store relatively small amounts of data. Cheaper forms of memory and 
storage, such as NAND flash and disk drives, are slower but can store vastly larger datasets that are 
accessed less frequently. 
Improving technologies across the memory hierarchy helps move data more efficiently across AI 
infrastructure. Yet, because HBM sits directly beside the GPU and feeds data into computation, it remains 
the most important memory technology and the one advancing the fastest.
While the memory wall has not disappeared, the industry is making meaningful progress toward narrowing 
the gap between compute and memory performance. The next decade of AI compute may depend as 
much on moving data efficiently as performing calculations. GPUs remain the engines of AI, but memory 
is increasingly one of the primary limiting factors.

HBM could not have become a practical reality without advanced packaging. While HBM provided a new 
memory architecture, Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate (CoWoS) made that architecture possible by bringing 
GPUs and HBM physically together. Instead of improving performance by shrinking transistors, CoWoS 
does so by shrinking the distance that data must travel. If traditional DRAM was a warehouse across town, 
CoWoS and HBM moved the warehouse onto the factory floor.
In 2012, TSMC invented CoWoS, but the broader idea of advanced packaging has become an industry-
wide priority. Nearly every major AI-chip company is now using some version of the CoWoS process, 
including EMIB from Intel and I-Cube from Samsung.   
This seemingly simple idea fundamentally changed AI system design. CoWoS mounts the GPU and 
multiple HBM stacks side by side on a thin silicon interposer containing thousands of high-speed 
connections. By dramatically shortening communication paths, CoWoS enables the enormous memory 
bandwidth modern AI processors require while reducing latency and the energy required to move data.

As we discussed in the memory section, moving data efficiently has become more important than 
performing computations.
Packaging has, therefore, evolved from a manufacturing afterthought into one of the most strategically 
important technologies in semiconductors. In the past, packaging primarily protected chips and 
connected them to a circuit board. Today, it determines how much memory can be attached to a 
processor, how quickly those components communicate, how much power the system consumes, and 
ultimately how much AI work can be performed.
Innovation is far from over. Today’s CoWoS systems connect GPUs and HBM stacks to the silicon 
interposer using microscopic spheres of metal called solder bumps. Although incredibly small, these 
bumps still introduce resistance, consume space, and limit how densely connections can be packed. 
The next major advance is hybrid bonding, which replaces these solder bumps with direct copper-to-
copper connections, enabling much finer interconnect density, lower power consumption, and 
substantially higher bandwidth. Combined with larger interposers, more HBM stacks, and next-generation 
packaging technologies, hybrid bonding could once again reshape AI system performance.
As AI shifts from a compute-constrained problem to a data movement problem, packaging is becoming a 
competitive advantage rather than simply a manufacturing afterthought. Just as HBM redefined memory, 
advanced packaging technologies such as CoWoS are redefining how entire AI systems are built. In the 
coming decade, breakthroughs in packaging may prove to be just as important as breakthroughs in chip 
design itself.
Networking: Turning Thousands of GPUs into One Computer 
If the data in an AI compute rack is a sports car, networking is the highway system, fiber optics/copper are 
the asphalt on the road, and communication protocols are the traffic lights. When a road is thin, full of 
potholes, and without traffic lights, the sports car needs to move much more slowly. Networking is about 
improving the road. 
Large AI models are trained on thousands of GPUs that continually exchange activations, gradients, and 
parameters. As AI systems grew from one GPU to thousands of GPUs, computing moved from an 
individual sport to a team sport. Networking is what allows thousands of processors to work together as 
one computer. 
Advances in optical networking enable thousands of GPUs to communicate at high speed and 
behave as a single system.19

Over the past decade or so, the industry has doubled leading-edge networking bandwidth around every three 
to four years. That’s a compounded improvement of approximately 25-30% per year, or a 16x increase over the 
past 12-14 years. AI demand has now accelerated the rate of improvement to one doubling every two years. 
As is the case with the memory companies, industry consolidation over the past 20 years has left a small 
number of larger, better-capitalized networking companies that can invest in research and development at a 
scale smaller competitors could not. In the pre-AI era, doubling networking bandwidth every three to four 
years was generally sufficient for traditional enterprise and cloud workloads. As AI training clusters grew from 
thousands to hundreds of thousands of GPUs, however, networking became a strategic bottleneck rather 
than a commodity and created a massive incentive to invest in improving this technology. 
The industry is increasingly investing in new fiber optics technology to replace copper. Relative to copper, 
optical networking uses less power, has higher bandwidth in a smaller physical footprint, and retains better 
signal integrity. This technology was first implemented for long-distance communication because, depending 
on the application, fiber-optic links can transmit data 100x-400x farther than typical copper connections. 
Now, optical networking is moving progressively inward. Fiber first displaced copper over long-haul links 
and between data centers, then between racks, and is now beginning to move inside rack-scale 
computers. Over time, this progression may allow increasingly large collections of GPUs to behave as a 
single computing system.
The industry uses three terms to describe what parts of the AI Factory are being connected: scale-out, 
scale-up, and scale-across. Each is at its own stage in the optical transition. 
1.	 Scale-out, which connects servers and racks throughout the AI Factory, is already mostly optical 
because the distances are longer.20
2.	 Scale-up, which connects GPUs within a server, pod, or rack, is in the middle of the transition from 
copper to optics, driven by rising GPU counts and exploding bandwidth needs. This is driving interest 
in near-packaged optics (NPO) and, eventually, co-packaged optics (CPO), technologies that 
progressively move the optical transceiver closer to the GPU. Each step reduces power consumption 
and shortens the electrical path, allowing for more efficient racks.21

3.	 Finally, scale-across is the next frontier. AI Factories are already connected by fiber. The challenge is 
no longer limited to transmitting data over long distances but also involves coordinating increasingly 
large collections of compute. In our view, future advances in networking and software may allow 
geographically distributed GPU clusters to behave more like a single computing system.22 23
As AI clusters grow, fast communication is becoming as important as computation. Just as with memory 
and advanced packaging, the key bottleneck is increasingly data movement rather than computation 
itself. A GPU cannot perform useful work while waiting for data from memory, storage, or another GPU.
Twenty years ago, networking connected individual computers performing separate tasks. Today, 
networking enables thousands of computers to compute as one. Scaling an AI Factory isn’t just about 
building a faster GPU; it is about making 100,000 GPUs behave like one enormous processor. 
If advances in memory, packaging, and networking continue, the payoff will be enormous. As a reminder, 
GPU utilization sits at around 30%-50% today, and GPUs are among the most expensive components in 
the AI Factory. One source suggests that GPU utilization could rise to as high as 85% if all of these 
bottlenecks improve.24 In that case, those expensive GPUs will deliver much higher returns on 
investment, since they will spend much more of their time engaged in useful AI computation.25

US Power Demand
The next constraint for AI might not be semiconductors at all. AI is driving the need for more power after a 
long period of low demand growth and stability. AI clusters increasingly resemble industrial factories that 
require utility-scale power and sophisticated cooling. The challenge is to get ever larger quantities of 
electricity flowing into the same physical footprint, which requires advances in power delivery, cooling, 
and data center design. 
An 800VDC architecture is a new power system for data centers that uses higher voltages and fewer 
power-conversion steps to preserve energy on the journey from power plant to data center. As it stands, 
electricity passes through multiple power-conversion stages before reaching the GPU, with energy lost as 
heat at each step. An 800VDC architecture removes the intermediate power-conversion steps, enabling 
huge power savings. 
In 2026, work has begun to implement 800VDC architecture, with full, facility-level commercialization 
expected by 2029. The solid-state transformer, a new technology currently in development, will be a 
critical component.
This new 800VDC architecture is forecast to reduce total energy losses by 5%.29 This number might seem 
small, but it translates into real savings at the immense scale of the current AI buildout. Applied across the 
roughly 12,000 data centers worldwide, that efficiency gain would be enough to power about two million 
US homes.30 
Once the power arrives at the data center, it requires more advanced cooling techniques to disperse 
the heat generated during computation. Fan cooling is unlikely to meet the needs of the large AI 
Factories of the future. New technologies include direct-to-chip liquid cooling, two-phase cooling, and 
immersion cooling.31
We are in the early stages of this transformation. Engineers are now redesigning this form of power 
delivery from the ground up, and, in many cases, bringing their own power that does not hook into the 
grid. Shifts in power architecture combined with advanced cooling point the way to viable >1 MW GPU 
racks, which could translate to a 10x increase in power usage.

Tensor Compute: The Engine  
While memory, packaging, and networking determine how efficiently data moves through an AI system, 
GPU processors (cores) determine how quickly that data can be transformed into useful work. The 
improvement of these cores drives the improvement of the GPU itself. The breakthrough came in 2017 
when NVIDIA introduced tensor cores in its Volta (V100) architecture, but similar matrix-math-specific 
architectures now exist across the AI industry.32 For simplicity, we will discuss tensor cores specifically.  
Tensor cores are specialized processors that work alongside general-purpose cores (called CUDA cores)33 
and are designed specifically for matrix multiplication, the calculations that dominate modern AI workloads.34 
By specializing in just one type of calculation, tensor cores can be far more efficient than CUDA cores. They 
dramatically increase theoretical compute power without a proportionate increase in chip size or power 
consumption. The introduction of tensor cores marked the beginning of the modern AI GPU. 
Tensor cores are improving on multiple vectors with every GPU generation. They feature improved 
architecture, denser transistor designs, faster clock speeds, and more efficient number formats, 
transitioning from FP16 to BF16 to FP8, and, most recently, FP4.35 Collectively, these innovations have 
produced extraordinary gains in theoretical AI compute.

Today, GPUs don’t reach their theoretical computing maximums. This distinction is important because 
addressing the other bottlenecks will unlock far more computing power without adding more chips. A 
GPU capable of 50 petaflops delivers much less value if it spends half its time waiting for data and/or 
can’t get the power it needs. That is why innovations in memory, packaging, and networking have become 
essential. They do not increase a GPU’s theoretical performance—they increase the productivity of the 
system outside the lab and in the real world.
Think of the system of GPUs as a car. TFLOPS measure the engine’s maximum horsepower. If the engine 
doesn’t have enough fuel (memory), a reliable fuel supply (power), a short distance to travel (packaging), 
and a good road and highway system (networking), it can’t reach maximum speed.   
The future of AI performance will come from improvements in both theoretical compute and utilization. 
Tensor cores will continue to become more specialized for AI mathematics, increasing theoretical 
compute capability, while advances in memory, packaging, networking, software, power delivery and 
cooling will ensure that more of that compute can be used in practice. The best AI systems will not simply 
have the highest potential TFLOPS—they will maximize the time the GPUs spend calculating for training 
and inference and minimize friction and delays from data movement. 
One important measure to track the improvement of the entire system will be tokens per watt.38 Since the 
first generation of AI-focused GPUs was released in 2012, tokens per watt has improved by ~1,000,000x.39 40

Conclusion: The AI Scaling Era for Investors 
In our experience, great innovations that are significantly better than what came before find a market. We 
see that the roadmap for AI scaling has a clear and executable vision. These advancements are 
happening now, and the improvements are beyond anything we have ever seen.  
In this paper, we have focused on AI compute and the major factors driving its advancement. We do not 
pretend that we have compiled an exhaustive list of AI-related innovations. We do, however, highlight that 
new technologies and scientific breakthroughs—including but not limited to the semiconductor—are 
driving compute power to increase at an exponential rate, even as Moore’s Law asymptotes.  
In fact, there is a recursive element at work here. As AI improves, these technologies may loop back to 
feed their own acceleration. As the models increase in power, they may contribute to solving the memory 
wall, implementing co-packaged optics, or optimizing clean energy generation. As more time passes, AI 
will also likely solve even harder problems such as protein function within cells, disease management, 
quantum computing and other high technological priorities.
On Sunday, July 19, 2026—while the rest of us watched the World Cup Final—mathematician Levent 
Alpöge asked Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5 to identify a counterexample to the long-standing 
Jacobian Conjecture, a problem first posed in 1939.41 This conjecture belongs in the group of 
questions you might get on the first day of a very advanced math class that would earn you 
immortality in the class if you solved it.42 Alpöge posted the solution to the problem on X the 
following day in a 216 character tweet.
That is the sort of problem-solving that we expect will increase in frequency as AI compute improves at 
the system level. 
These gains are multiplicative rather than additive. In our minds, memory, networking, CoWoS, etc. are 
like multiple S-curves stacking on top of each other. Faster processors are more valuable when they 
spend less time waiting for memory. Faster memory is more valuable when packaging brings it closer to 
the processor. A larger cluster is more valuable when networking allows it to behave as one computer. All 
that hardware becomes more productive when a better algorithm requires fewer calculations to reach the 
same result—or uses additional calculations more intelligently.
We believe it is important not only to recognize that compute is accelerating but also to see that 
the path forward, at least within the AI Factory, is already visible. The current system still contains 
large, identifiable bottlenecks with plausible paths to improvement. 
This also matters for investors. Mean-reversion investing benefits from retaining the status quo. But investing 
in innovation works best in periods of significant change and disruption. We believe that is the case today. 
Every great technology eventually slows. Candles became light bulbs. Light bulbs barely changed. 
Airplanes stopped getting faster. Smartphones stopped being revolutionary. But computing never 
stopped. In fact, we may be at the knee of its curve today.
For 60 years, we improved the transistor. Today, we improve the system. Tomorrow, we may improve the 
factory. As one exponential growth vector is slowing, new vectors are taking off with room for 
improvement.  The object of innovation keeps changing, but the exponential curve continues. That is why 
we believe a new age of innovation is upon us. And, to indulge in a small morsel of the hyperbole we 
rejected in the opening letter, it will change everything.
