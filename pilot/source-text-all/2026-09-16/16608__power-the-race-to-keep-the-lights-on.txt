01
The new 
power paradigm
The global economy is undergoing a structural shift. For a long time, 
efficiency gains and the transition from manufacturing to services 
has allowed major economies to grow faster than electricity use.
But the relationship between electricity demand and economic 
growth is now changing. 
For the first time in three decades, global electricity demand is rising 
faster than economic output – disrupting a long-standing link 
between power consumption and economic growth.
Is the shift sustainable and what does it mean for investors?

Demand: a new era of electricity consumption
Four structural forces are fuelling electricity demand:
1.
Data centre boom – Needed to run AI, data centres are the 
fastest-growing contributor, expected to account for about 10% 
of global demand growth through to 20301 
2.
Transport electrification – Electric vehicle (EV) adoption 
is a major driver in Europe and China
3.
Air conditioning and heat pumps – Cooling and heating 
technologies are rapidly increasing demand, particularly in 
response to climate change and heat waves. China, India and 
the US are among the biggest markets
4.
Industry – Factories are energy intensive, especially in China 
and other emerging markets, while demands from light industry 
and green manufacturing are growing

02
The global 
race for power
The power-demand story differs across the world.
In the US, AI data centres are driving rapid load growth, pushing 
hyperscalers, the cloud providers building the AI infrastructure, to 
seek off-grid solutions.
Europe faces grid congestion and price volatility, while China is 
becoming the biggest driver of electricity growth, supported by rapid 
build-out of renewables.
What these economies share are supply-demand frictions that could 
yet limit the build out of AI, slow the energy transition and, 
ultimately, hold back growth.

US: AI’s hunger for power set to be met – for now
As the centre of the AI boom, the US faces a steep 
rise in electricity demand, with hyperscalers alone 
set to be responsible for close to two-thirds of the 
increase.1
Utilities expect to add enough capacity, with 
133 GW of usable supply due by 2030.2
But supply will remain constrained until early 2028.
To avoid grid delays and secure dedicated power, 
hyperscalers are turning to off-grid solutions such 
as solid oxide fuel cells and distributed generation.

Europe: demand rising amid grid bottlenecks
EVs, heat pumps and cooling systems should drive 
most of the expected 2.3% rise in power demand by 
2030.1 
Europe imports more than half of its energy,2 mainly 
oil and gas, but renewables and nuclear provide more 
than 70% of power generation.2 Gas still drives 
wholesale prices, affecting demand and regional 
differences.
Geopolitical events have strengthened Europe’s 
decarbonisation and energy-independence goals, but 
ageing grids and connection delays remain near-term 
bottlenecks to power growth.

China: the new engine of global electricity growth
China’s electricity demand is outpacing its economy, rising 
about 6–8% annually versus GDP growth of 
around 5%.1
Key drivers include AI infrastructure, mass EV adoption, 
industrial electrification and expanding manufacturing 
capacity.
A mismatch between power supply and demand locations 
can create local shortages and reliability risks.
China is responding with a major power-generation build-
out, targeting roughly 200–300 GW of 
renewable additions a year.

18
AI, electrification and climate adaptation are making the global economy more 
energy intensive. In the coming years, electricity demand is expected to surge, 
led by China and other emerging markets.
As the world becomes more dependent on electricity, the constraint is shifting 
from energy supply to power delivery. Electricity is becoming a strategic 
economic resource, shaping AI adoption, competitiveness, inflation and 
geopolitical resilience. Economies and businesses with reliable power are 
likely to thrive; those facing bottlenecks may fall behind.
Capturing the opportunities – and managing the risks – will require active 
positioning across public and private markets.
