From core to problem
The European credit hierarchy has flipped. French 10-year yields now sit above Greece, Italy is trading
as the safer credit, and the country that once lectured Europe’s periphery on fiscal discipline
increasingly finds itself on the wrong side of the divide. We have written extensively about Les
Misérables over the summer — for example here and here — but the latest move in French bonds
deserves another look.
French bond yields
French bond yields are now trading above many of the countries France once considered credit
inferiors, including Greece. The periphery-core hierarchy that has organized European rates since the
last crisis is being rewritten in real time, and France is on the wrong side of it.
Haver
Meloni over Macron
Greece isn't an isolated comparison. Italy is now seen as the safer credit than France. Read that again.
Meloni's Italy, the perennial punchline of European sovereign risk, is the calmer name on the desk
versus Macron's France. Just another day of the map being redrawn.
Bloomberg
Falling behind
France is way behind everyone else in closing its deficits since the COVID shock. While the periphery
did the unglamorous work of "repairing finances," France simply did not, and the bond market has
finally decided to charge for it.

Almost everything at a discount
Investors are now applying the "France discount" to every sector except luxury. Everything domestic,
everything exposed to French fiscal and political risk, gets marked down. The only escape hatch is the
global handbag.
Haver
Even luxury cracks
Except the escape hatch is jammed too: LVMH just printed its lowest level since November 2020, the
Covid lockdown lows. The stock is down 50% since the summer of 2023. When the last thing anyone
still wanted to own trades back to pandemic prices, the "discount" is no longer selective.

Bloomberg
What does Stanley say?
Morgan Stanley’s Deputy Chief European Economist Jean-Francois Ouvrard sees the 2026 public deficit
reaching 5.2% of GDP, above both 2025’s 5.1% and the government’s 5.0% target, as higher interest
costs and pressure from social security offset what initially looked like improving central-government
finances. Under the hood, a €6.9B one-off transfer is flattering the cash numbers, while interest
payments were already running €4.9B above last year through May. For rates, that keeps Morgan
Stanley cautious on France: the team has re-entered shorts versus EU bonds as fiscal slippage, a
difficult 2027 budget process and growing political uncertainty leave room for further French
underperformance.
What does BofA say?
France is increasingly the Euro area's weak spot: Q2 GDP growth was revised to zero, the rare print that
failed to beat BofA's initial forecast. More importantly, financial conditions have been tighter than
elsewhere in the Euro area since the energy crisis; until recently that mainly produced faster
disinflation without crushing growth, but in 2026 the weakness has begun spreading into real activity.
That makes fixing the fiscal deficit harder just as potentially messy 2027 budget negotiations and
presidential — and likely snap parliamentary — elections approach.
As BofA says: “Market scrutiny of France is unlikely to fade.”
Goldman complicates the story
So is France simply a short? Not quite. The sell-side agrees on many of the problems, but not
necessarily on the trade.
GS: "We prefer buying weakness in French bonds as we remain cautious on French equities."
﻿France has unquestionably moved from European core to European problem child. But even after
Greek yields crossed below French yields, Goldman thinks weakness in OATs should be bought rather
than chased. The French problem is real; whether it becomes Crisis 2.0 is another question.
