•
The US Economy Overcomes The ‘Waves Of The Ocean’ | Despite economic crosscurrents, the US economy remains on solid footing. The 
consumer, who accounts for roughly two-thirds of GDP, continues to demonstrate resilience, with real-time activity metrics still pointing to 
healthy spending and rising household wealth providing ongoing support. Meanwhile, investment tied to the AI buildout is becoming an 
increasingly important driver of economic growth. The benefits extend well beyond AI itself, supporting demand for data centers, power 
generation, electrical equipment, construction and infrastructure required to connect these systems. Together, these forces continue to 
support the economic expansion. While the cycle is maturing and is now in its sixth year, we see few signs that the underlying currents are 
turning meaningfully less supportive and expect 2.3% GDP in 2026 and 2.2% in 2027. 
•
Monetary Policy Navigating ‘A Difficult Stretch’ | Much like Odysseus navigating a perilous stretch of sea, the Federal Reserve (Fed) must 
steer between competing risks while keeping the economy on course, balancing between restoring price stability and preserving economic 
momentum. With the labor market remaining healthy, the Fed’s focus has shifted to inflation. While a combination of shocks—tariffs, 
energy, and continued investment demand tied to the AI buildout—has kept inflation elevated, these pressures should begin to ease. We 
anticipate the Fed will deliver one additional rate increase before year-end (likely December) as part of a mid-cycle adjustment to bring 
inflation back to target in a timely manner. Further progress on inflation could open the door to lower rates toward the end of next year. 
Until then, the Fed is likely to maintain a cautious stance and keep policy restrictive unless labor market conditions materially weaken.
•
Higher Yields Help Investors ‘Navigate The Journey’ | The sharp rise in yields from the post-pandemic lows has improved the resilience 
of fixed income within diversified portfolios. While higher yields cannot eliminate every storm, they can help keep portfolios balanced 
when turbulence appears, as they have throughout history. Today’s fixed income market offers advantages that didn’t exist five years ago: 
attractive income; an income buffer that can help absorb losses; and compelling total return potential if growth softens. More importantly, 
investors can earn generous yields without taking excessive credit risk. While yields remain elevated, cooling inflation and easing 
geopolitical tensions should help bring the 10-year Treasury yield back toward our 4.5%-4.75% target range over the next 12 months. While 
the US national debt remains a long-term concern, we do not view this as a near-term threat as demand for US Treasuries remains healthy. 
•
US Equities Continue To ‘Lead The Voyage’ | The US equity bull market will shortly enter its fifth year with remarkable resilience. In fact, 
corporate America has delivered far stronger earnings than we typically see at this stage of the bull market—and that strength is expected 
to continue in the quarters ahead. History provides another tailwind, as both the fifth year of a bull market and 12 months following the 
midterm elections have historically been supportive for returns. While concerns surrounding Fed rate hikes, higher long-term interest 
rates, and geopolitical risks bear watching, the earnings outlook should help offset any near-term obstacles. As a result, our 12-month S&P 
500 target is 8,450 (~11% upside from current levels). Technology remains the market’s Achilles: powerful, dominant, and central to market 
leadership. Investors continue to search for an Achilles heel, citing valuation concerns, market concentration, and reliance on a handful of 
tech leaders. Yet robust earnings growth and more reasonable valuations suggest the sector’s strengths continue to outweigh these risks.
•
‘Different Shores, Different Opportunities’ Globally | While the US remains our preferred destination, supported by stronger economic 
growth, superior earnings momentum, and leadership in artificial intelligence, some opportunities abroad remain compelling. As Odysseus 
learned, some destinations prove more rewarding than others. We continue to favor EM Asia, one of the clearest beneficiaries of the global 
AI investment cycle. Japan also stands out among developed markets, supported by solid earnings growth, a return to modest inflation, 
and meaningful technology exposure. Meanwhile, Latin American equities could face headwinds from lower oil prices next year.
The Long Voyage. This year has marked another remarkable chapter for the global 
economy and financial markets. Investors have navigated no shortage of challenges, from 
geopolitical conflicts and trade tensions to elevated energy prices and shifting interest rate 
expectations. Yet despite these headwinds, the economy continues to expand, corporate 
earnings remain resilient, and markets have steadily climbed the proverbial wall of worry.

OCTOBER 2, 2026
Economy
•
Nonfarm payrolls were much weaker than expected in September, increasing by just 29,000, while July and August were revised down 
by a combined 60,000. Overall, the report points to softer labor market conditions and supports the Fed remaining on hold in October.
•
Personal consumption (PCE) was stronger than income growth in August, with real PCE up 0.6% MoM vs. 0.0% for real disposable 
income. Meanwhile, headline and core PCE inflation were below expectations, remaining unchanged at 3.4% & 3.0% YoY, respectively.
•
Consumer confidence fell much more than expected in September, declining to 81.9 and reaching its lowest level since April 2014. The 
share of respondents describing their financial situation as ‘good’ fell below those describing it as ‘bad’ for the 1st time since July 2022.
•
Focus of the Week: Monday’s September ISM Services PMI will take center stage and is expected to expand for the 27th straight month, 
with particular attention on the Prices Index after it reached its highest level since August 2022 last month. Friday’s Michigan 
Sentiment will also be in focus and is expected to remain subdued, echoing the weakness seen in this week’s Consumer Confidence.
Equity
•
Amidst the rise in interest rates, equity performance has been far from uniform. While the headline S&P 500 is down less than 2% from 
highs, the S&P 500 Equal Weight Index is 7% off recent highs and is on pace to decline for its seventh consecutive week (the longest 
streak since 2022). To put this into perspective, in the other two instances that the equal-weight index saw seven consecutive weekly 
declines (2002, 2022), the headline S&P 500 was down 11% and 14%, respectively. This highlights how mega-cap tech areas have kept 
the index afloat. At the constituent level, the weakness  is even more pronounced. Roughly 40% of S&P 500 stocks are currently in bear 
market territory (down 20% or more from highs), and the average stock sits 20.1% below its 52-week high. While this highlights a 
market that appears far weaker than the headline index would suggest, it does point to opportunities beneath the surface.
•
Despite recent weakness, 2026 has been a strong year for the S&P 500, with YTD price gains of 12%. Since 1933, there have been 36 
instances when the S&P 500 was up 10% or more through 3Q. In those years, 4Q momentum tended to continue, with returns averaging 
+4.6% with positive results 83% of the time, including each of the last seven occurrences. While history provides useful perspective, 
corporate fundamentals ultimately drive markets, and they remain supportive of further equity gains once interest rates stabilize. With 
the 3Q26 earnings season set to ‘unofficially’ kick off October 10, this could provide a key catalyst for equities moving forward.
•
Focus of the Week: Next week, earnings results from PepsiCo and Delta Air Lines will offer investors a timely read on the health of the 
consumer, providing insight into spending trends across both necessities and discretionary categories.
Fixed Income
•
October rate hike odds fell from 73% to ~20% after FOMC Vice Chair Williams said there was no urgency to tighten again while signaling 
just one more hike this year, pulling the 2-year Treasury yield below 4.80%. Long-term Treasuries remained under pressure, however, 
with the 10-year yield briefly reaching 5.34%—its highest since 2002—before French fiscal concerns and softer September job gains 
pulled yields lower. Even so, elevated yields continue to improve return opportunities across high-quality fixed income. 
•
The spread on the Bloomberg high yield index has widened back near its 3-year average alongside the spike in rate volatility and 
pressure on CCC-rated issuers (9% of the index). Rising borrowing costs and an upcoming maturity wall for lower-quality bonds issued 
during the pandemic’s low-rate environment are weighing on the weakest issuers—CCC spreads climbed above 1,000 bps for the first 
time since 2023. However, spreads on the Bloomberg IG index remain near 80 bps, highlighting the dispersion within corporate  credit.
•
Focus of the Week: ISM services, September’s FOMC meeting minutes and Treasury auctions (3-, 10-, 30-year notes) await investors.
Washington Policy
•
President Trump and executives from the largest US AI firms reached a voluntary agreement on frontier model safety. Companies 
including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, and Nvidia all agreed to strengthen internal controls, independent audits, and board-level 
oversight of advanced systems, but the framework remains voluntary. 
•
Iran remains the largest near-term geopolitical risk to the otherwise more stable policy backdrop. President Trump rejected Iran’s 
latest seven-day ceasefire proposal, which would have reopened the Strait of Hormuz and restarted negotiations in exchange for lifting 
the US naval blockade and unfreezing Iranian assets. 
International
•
This week the Reserve Bank of Australia delivered its fourth 25 bp rate hike of 2026, becoming the second G20 central bank—after 
Indonesia—to implement 100 bps of tightening year-to-date. This is the latest case study of how monetary policies around the world 
have diverged over the past nine months. The inflationary backdrop means that many banks are in tightening mode, though in varying 
degrees. The Fed has hiked 25 bps, while its counterparts in the euro zone, Japan, and South Korea have hiked 50 bps. In the case of 
Japan, the policy rate has reached 1.25%—the highest since 1995—though it remains very low by international standards.
•
At the same time, several major banks—in the UK, Canada, China, and India—have kept rates steady on a YTD basis. China’s lack of 
tightening reflects the fact that headline inflation has been below the central bank’s 2% target since the start of the year, as higher 
import costs are offset by domestic manufacturing overcapacity. In the UK, inflation has been above-target since October 2024, but 
the Bank of England has been erring on the side of supporting the labor market… for now: futures point to an ~85% likelihood of a 
hike in November. Finally, amid cooling inflation in Latin America’s largest economies—Brazil and Mexico—rates have been cut YTD by 
125 and 50 bps, respectively, though they remain towards the upper end of the international spectrum.
