Completing Our IG and HY Supply Outlook  
 
 
Spreads (finally) reach our targets—risks skewed toward more widening 
After remaining resilient for much of September, the tone in the USD and EUR credit 
markets weakened over the past several sessions in response to a combination of factors 
including elevated rates volatility, a renewed focus on the supply backdrop (AI-related 
and otherwise), and the potential for commodity market restrictions in the form of a 
possible US diesel export ban. 
With the widening in recent days, IG and HY spread levels in both regions have quickly 
approached our quarter-end forecasts for 3Q2026. For example, USD IG spreads 
reached 82bp on September 29, just shy of our forecast of 85bp. While they finished 
3Q2026 at 80bp, the tone as we write on October 1 once again feels skewed towards a 
weaker backdrop. 
Meanwhile, USD HY spreads of 311bp slightly surpassed our 3Q2026 target of 305bp. 
EUR IG spreads of 90bp were just shy of our forecast of 91bp, while EUR HY spreads of 
321bp were just above our target of 320bp. 
Our year-end (4Q2026) spread targets reflect levels that are modestly tighter vs. today’s 
valuations. But this largely reflects the typical year-end spread tightening which is often 
visible in late December as the pace of supply slows. Between now and early December, 
however, we see scope for a more volatile backdrop and continue to view the risks to our 
forecasts as tilted towards more widening, with the March 2026 levels as our base case 
in a moderate downside scenario. While a sharp downturn in growth is likely a necessary 
catalyst for sustained and material spread widening, we nonetheless see scope for 
additional weakening in credit if rates volatility increases further (Exhibit 1).

For France, the key concern for markets remains the path of fiscal consolidation. France 
already has a high debt burden, at roughly 118% to 119% of GDP. And our economists 
expect the debt ratio to rise further—toward 125% by the start of 2030—absent a more 
meaningful fiscal adjustment. They note that stabilizing debt-to-GDP would require 
France to run a primary surplus of roughly 1% of GDP, compared with a primary deficit 
of around 2.5%, currently. 
For EUR credit, the relevant question is how French fiscal risk will spill over into 
corporate credit spreads. At the headline level, French corporate spreads have remained 
relatively resilient. The share of French IG non-financial bonds trading inside 
similar-maturity OATs has risen to 27%, the highest level since 2011, where they reached 
40% for one day in November (Exhibit 2, left panel). 
This time, however, the majority of firms trading inside OATs are at the long end of the 
curve, particularly in the 10-year-plus segment. In 2011-2012, the outperformance of 
corporations vs. the sovereign was more evenly distributed across maturities (Exhibit 2, 
right panel). We think this reflects the duration-led nature of the current sell-off in 
global yields. 
 
The fact that a growing number of French corporations now trade inside OATs suggests 
the market is differentiating between sovereign and corporate risk, rather than treating 
the two as identical. Indeed, French non-financials have continued to trade broadly in 
line with the rest of developed-market credit recently (Exhibit 3, left panel). 
That said, risk-adjusted spread differentials tell a more nuanced story. After controlling 
for rating, duration, and sector differences, French credits still trade tighter than peers—
although this premium has compressed significantly in the post-Covid period from 
roughly 40bp in 2022 to just 3bp today (Exhibit 3, right panel).

IG and HY supply forecasts for 2027 
In early September, we raised our forecast for full-year gross supply in the USD IG 
market to $2.3 trillion (from $2.1 trillion), as the typical summer slowdown in issuance 
failed to materialize—driven in part by the swift pace of AI-related issuance. We also 
introduced a $2.4 trillion gross supply forecast for 2027. These higher gross issuance 
numbers equate to a net supply forecast of $1.0 trillion for 2026, and $1.15 trillion for 
2027. 
This week, we outline our supply forecasts for the remaining IG and HY corporate bond 
markets that we track: USD HY, EUR IG and EUR HY. 
USD HY 
Year-to-date USD HY gross issuance of $276 billion leaves 2026 on track to rank as the 
heaviest volume year since the pandemic-era record of $462 billion in 2021. 
Nonetheless, at the current pace, it appears unlikely to meet our current full-year 2026 
forecast of $400 billion. As a result, we are adjusting that forecast downward to $340 
billion. We are expecting $375 billion of gross USD HY issuance in 2027 (Exhibit 5). 
 
The key driver behind our expectation for higher issuance in 2027 is the AI-related 
financing wave. While higher rates may have postponed some issuance in 2H2026, we 
believe this supply will eventually make its way through the market given the large 
backlog of data center construction in progress. We also expect borrowers will 
proactively begin to address the 2029 maturity wall, which is steep relative to 2028 
(Exhibit 6).

At current valuations and given our expectation for heightened dispersion, we prefer moving 
up in quality within EUR HY. 
Similar to the US, we prefer HY bonds given the loan market's weighting toward software 
(and the potential negative sentiment overhang). The reaction function of the ECB is 
increasing borrowing costs for floating rate issuers, given the central bank's focus on 
inflationary pressures.
EUR IG tranches offer carry pickup relative to USD IG CLO tranches. While the policy and 
macroeconomic picture is less supportive in Europe, subordination should protect tranches 
up in the capital structure from a continued idiosyncratic default picture. 
While we previously had a preference for IG vs. HY, we now view the case for an outright 
overweight in IG as less obvious based on valuations, rate sensitivity and supply related 
technicals. 
For floating rate investors, we prefer CLO mezzanine tranches vs. leveraged loans. 
Historical data points to low impairment rates for BB and BBB CLO tranches, and current 
valuations offer attractive carry and a cushion against moderate to elevated default 
outcomes.  Elsewhere, we are neutral between senior CLO tranches and USD IG; we would 
use spread widening to add exposure in CLOs. We are also neutral between CLO AAA and 
CMO floaters. 
While we previously had a slight preference for IG vs. HY, we now view the case for an 
outright overweight in IG as less obvious based on valuations, rate sensitivity and supply 
related technicals. 
The substantial software sector exposure in the loan market leaves us favoring the HY 
market, at least until we receive more clarity on the refinancing of the 2028 loan maturity 
walls. The lower-rated distribution of loans and our economists' base case for no rate hikes 
in 2026 are also contributing factors. 
Agency MBS offers an attractive diversification benefit vs. a portfolio of IG corporate bonds.
We view the additional spread compensation of hybrids vs. BBBs as moderately attractive in 
the context of the tight spread environment. We also expect the hybrid spread premium to 
compress vs. BBBs, as the hybrid market grows further.
Regional
We prefer owning BBBs over the higher-rated segments of USD IG (As and AAs), as a way 
to capture additional spread. We also see scope for balance sheet deterioration at the 
highest end of the rating spectrum in IG. 
We recently shifted our view to favor BBBs over both AAs and As in the EUR market, owing 
to an improved macroeconomic backdrop which now favors a broad-based, down-in-quality 
stance within IG. 
We expect BBs will continue to encounter a challenging supply technical due to AI-related 
supply. As a result, we favor the B rated cohort. The CCC universe is highly idiosyncratic and 
requires careful credit selection, in our view.
