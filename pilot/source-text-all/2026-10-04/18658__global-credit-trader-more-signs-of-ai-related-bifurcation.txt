More Signs of AI-Related Bifurcation 
 
 
The rise of the AI theme has increased the bifurcation across and within credit markets. 
For investors, the key question is which of the emerging differentials have staying power, 
and what exposure to the AI theme means for index selection and the macro drivers of 
spreads. We lay out three axes of differentiation—across the AI vs. non-AI divide, within 
the AI ecosystem itself, and across cash vs. synthetic indices—and take each in turn. 
Our broad conclusion is that the gaps across the AI vs. non-AI divide and across cash vs. 
synthetic markets are structural, but the differentiation within the AI ecosystem is still 
being repriced as investors reassess how insulated hardware and infrastructure names 
really are. That repricing extends to the way AI risk is expressed: single-name AI CDS now 
trades structurally cheap to its reference cash, a gap we see as durable given the 
hedging demand tied to the buildout. Taken together, these dynamics argue for a more 
deliberate approach to expressing macro and cross-currency views in credit.

Since June, USD IG credit performance has been bifurcated across the AI theme, with AI 
spreads having moved wider, as concerns over market concentration and new supply 
made investors hesitant to establish long positions. Bearish positioning peaked ahead of 
hyperscaler earnings but has eased somewhat since, as capex volatility was less than 
anticipated.

to only 6bp in non-AI credit through Wednesday’s close (Exhibit 1). Given a smaller AI 
footprint, the gap is much narrower in the EUR IG market where hyperscaler bonds trade 
13bp wider than non-hyperscalers and the broad AI ecosystem trades only 2bp wider 
than non-AI bonds.

Lease obligations are sizable and warrant monitoring  
Credit investors have also been increasingly focused on the lease commitments of the 
hyperscaler sector. Using the most recent data (and SEC filings) from the ongoing 
earnings season, we have tracked $1.5 trillion in aggregate lease commitments for the 
sector (Exhibit 6). For context, as of our last update (on July 27th), this amount stood at 
$1.2 trillion. Of this amount, $1.0 trillion is associated with leases which have not yet 
commenced (vs. $725 billion previously).  
As a result of the nuances of lease accounting, these ‘uncommenced’ lease 
commitments are not reflected in the financial statements, even though they will result in 
future lease payments (or other legally binding obligations) in upcoming years. Until the 
‘lease commencement date’ is reached, these obligations are simply disclosed in the 
10-K and 10-Q footnotes. (See further below for a refresher on lease accounting.)  
From a credit perspective, this treatment can understate leverage and future liquidity 
needs as these obligations are eventually recognized and contractual payments come 
due. 
 
Differing rating agency approaches  
The rating agencies—Moody’s, S&P and Fitch—also vary in how they approach 
lease-related leverage adjustments, which could have implications for ratings, in our 
view. For example, Moody’s incorporates finance and operating leases into its adjusted 
leverage metrics only after the lease has commenced. By contrast, uncommenced leases 
are not captured in historical leverage calculations. Rather, they are considered as part 
of a qualitative overlay of hyperscalers’ future financial obligations. Fitch takes a similar 
approach to Moody’s.

Meanwhile, S&P takes a more conservative stance and does incorporate the discounted

A refresher on lease accounting  
Under US GAAP, ASC 842 guides the treatment of leases. While there are many 
important nuances to lease accounting, in simple terms, a lease liability—which is the 
financial obligation to make future lease payments—is recognized only at the lease 
commencement date. This is defined by the accounting guidelines as the date on which a 
lessor makes an underlying asset available for use by a lessee. The liability is the present 
value of all future lease payments, discounted by the rate implicit in the lease or the 
lessee’s incremental borrowing rate. On the same lease commencement date, a 
right-of-use (ROU) asset associated with the lease is also recognized on the balance 
sheet. 
As lease payments are made, the liability shown on the balance sheet decreases while 
the offsetting asset is amortized over the lease term. On the income statement, for 
finance leases, amortization is recorded on the ROU asset, while interest expense is 
recorded on the lease liability. For operating leases, an expense is recorded (on a straight 
line basis) over the term of the lease. 
An uncommenced lease, however, does not result in recognition of an asset or liability, as 
the lessor has not yet made the underlying asset available for the lessee’s use. PwC’s 
lease accounting guide states specifically: “A lessee cannot lease an 
incomplete/in-construction asset, so understanding the asset being leased and what 
work is being performed on the asset may be important when assessing commencement 
date. The commencement date specified in the lease contract or the date when lease 
payments begin are not typically strong indicators of when a lessee has obtained control 
of the leased asset.”  
That said, as mentioned earlier, the legally binding obligations of the lease (including 
cash outflows) are reflected in the footnotes of the financial statements, allowing 
investors to track these figures.

At current valuations and given our expectation for heightened dispersion, we prefer moving 
up in quality within EUR HY. 
Similar to the US, we prefer HY bonds given the loan market's weighting toward software 
(and the potential negative sentiment overhang). The reaction function of the ECB may also 
increase borrowing costs for floating rate issuers, given the central bank's focus on 
inflationary pressures.
EUR IG tranches offer carry pickup relative to USD IG CLO tranches. While the policy and 
macroeconomic picture is less supportive in Europe, subordination should protect tranches 
up in the capital structure from a continued idiosyncratic default picture. 
We prefer IG over HY given the more challenging growth, inflation, and monetary policy mix 
in the Euro Area.
For floating rate investors, we prefer CLO mezzanine tranches vs. leveraged loans.
