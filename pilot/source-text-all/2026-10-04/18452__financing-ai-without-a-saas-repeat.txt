Financing AI without 
a SaaS repeat 

Executive summary 
Direct lenders built underwriting frameworks that fueled a boom in software mergers and 
acquisitions, particularly Software as a Service (SaaS) deals, on the view that the sector paired 
scalable equity upside with sticky, seemingly uncorrelated credit cash flows. Generative AI 
complicated that view, exposing correlation across software credits that had not been priced 
in. As the industry turns to financing a far larger buildout of AI compute infrastructure, this 
whitepaper considers whether the same underlying pattern, underwriting guidelines shaping 
deal flow rather than the other way around, deserves a closer look. Instead of avoiding AI 
compute exposure altogether, the more useful question is not whether to participate, but how 
deliberately to set concentration limits as the buildout continues, rather than after it 
has already run its course.
Key 
takeaways:
•	 Software was not simply popular with credit investors; its economics were 
tailor-made for credit, combining scalable equity upside with sticky, seemingly 
uncorrelated cash flows.
•	 That uncorrelated story unwound once AI made technological obsolescence a risk 
shared across the entire software sector, revealing lenders’ concentrated exposure.
•	 Underwriting guidelines actively shape which deals sponsors pursue and 
at what price, so credit investors bear real responsibility for concentration risk 
rather than simply reacting to deal flow.
•	 Financing the AI compute buildout will require roughly $1 trillion in private 
credit, more than half of the industry’s current assets under management, delivered 
through a range of largely untested, bespoke structures.
•	 Unlike SaaS, AI-related credit risk looks more speculative and more likely 
to be correlated with the broader economy, raising the stakes of repeating 
a concentration mistake.
•	 The lesson from software is not to avoid AI compute exposure altogether, but to 
set explicit concentration limits before the buildout accelerates further, not after.

Pilloried for excessive exposure to software deals, many direct lenders seem 
fated to relive this experience with data centers. One would have thought that 
the “SaaSpocalypse” would have made credit investors more cautious about 
accumulating concentrated exposure to specific sectors, no matter how promising. 
But that thought doesn’t seem to have occurred to those eager to finance as 
much of the AI compute buildout as possible.
Credit instruments offer a concave payoff such that the capped upside of “winners” 
cannot offset the downside of “losers.” When it comes to portfolio construction, 
loss minimization is the name of the game. Yet, lenders’ investment opportunity set 
depends on “sponsored deal flow” generated by private equity firms and corporate 
borrowers’ M&A, project, and asset-backed finance needs. A natural tension results, 
as equity sponsors’ search for convex upside (i.e., “long volatility”) conflicts with 
creditors’ preference for stable and predictable cash flows (“short volatility”).

Unfortunately, it now appears that the cash flows of software companies 
are correlated after all. That’s not due to unforeseen dependence on the broader 
economy but due to the shared risk of technological obsolescence. 
By constructing a “credit box” designed to minimize cyclical risk and state-
contingent cash flows, direct lenders facilitated a boom in software deals that 
accentuated their exposure to potential losses from AI disintermediation.
In an April 2026 edition of The Carlyle Compass, we examined the software 
repricing directly, finding that most management teams do not expect 
AI to replace their existing software subscriptions outright. Instead, the shift 
toward AI-centric architectures is expected to reduce legacy maintenance 
spending, lower consulting costs, and raise the productivity of existing staff, 
suggesting AI may prove more complementary to SaaS platforms than the 
bearish narrative implies.
The piece also traces how the pandemic’s end delivered two compounding 
shocks to software valuations: a halving of revenue growth as the broader 
economy reopened, and a sharp rise in interest rates that hit long-duration, 
terminal-value-driven assets especially hard. The emergence of coding-capable 
large language models added a third pressure, challenging the assumption 
that software spending was effectively non-discretionary.

In a June 2026 edition of The Carlyle Compass, we looked at the tools 
available to software borrowers facing maturity pressure. Upfront fees and 
spread increases for rolling lenders are standard responses, and additional 
payment-in-kind coupons offer another lever, but only to the extent lenders 
actually value them. The most effective tool remains meaningful risk 
reduction for existing lenders through a repayment, at par or otherwise, 
funded by new issuance or fresh capital.
The piece draws a distinction that matters for concentration risk: for cyclical 
businesses, time tends to work in every stakeholder’s favor, since existing 
lenders can wait out a downturn while new capital arrives to bet on recovery. 
Software’s distress, by contrast, looks secular and, in some cases, existential 
rather than cyclical, meaning that lenders are more likely to require real 
paydown and de-risking rather than a wait-and-see approach.

One might presume that this experience would lead credit investors to tighten 
absolute risk concentration limits and prioritize sectoral diversification. 
Credit investors are not prisoners to deal flow; their underwriting guidelines partly 
determine the sectors sponsors target and the prices they can bid for those 
assets. The boom in software M&A volumes and valuation multiples could not have 
run as far as it did if creditors widened spreads in proportion to concentration risk 
or refused incremental software exposure entirely.
This is worth keeping in mind as the industry turns to financing the next boom. 
With hyperscalers’ internally generated cash flow nearly exhausted, future growth 
in AI capex will require far more external finance. Bank analysts estimate that 
private credit will finance roughly $1 trillion of this compute buildout, equal to more 
than half of the industry’s current AUM (Figure 3).

Data center financing takes many forms: construction and power finance, 
GPU-collateralized asset-backed financing, loans to off-balance-sheet special 
purpose vehicles (SPVs), direct senior secured facilities for neoclouds, and 
traditional M&A and add-on lending. Nearly every structure looks bespoke, with 
credit enhancement provided by residual value guarantees, hell-or-high-water 
leases, take-or-pay capacity charges, step-in rights, completion support, insurance 
wraps, and other innovations. For an industry designed to earn incremental 
returns from a complexity and illiquidity premium, private credit seems tailor-made 
for these tailored structures.

But whereas SaaS plausibly offered credit-friendly recurring cash flows, AI-related 
lending has a far more speculative character. The economics of the industry 
look far from settled; we don’t know the size of the ultimate pie (aggregate enterprise 
and consumer AI spend) and lack clarity on how it will be sliced between 
hardware, infrastructure, model, and application layers.
The existing take-out market for data center-related construction debt seems 
small3 relative to the trillions of dollars of stabilized assets soon-to-be-delivered. 
The rapid evolution in high-density chips and data center architectures could 
upend depreciation and demand assumptions, leading to a sudden shift in credit 
ratings and deterioration in exit market liquidity. Workouts for these structures 
remain untested, with residual value guarantees and other support provided by 
a handful of investment grade companies. 
And while software offered uncorrelated performance – during the pandemic 
downturn, the non-renewal rates on many types of SaaS subscriptions fell below 
the default rate on speculative grade loans – data center-linked default rates 
seem likely to covary with those of the broader portfolio. AI capex accounts for 
such a large share of final demand growth that any downturn is likely to have 
negative spillovers on other sectors (construction, energy, capital equipment, 
etc.) and broader consumption given falling asset prices’ implications for 
household net worth.
WHY AI-RELATED CREDIT 
RISK IS A HARDER 
PROBLEM THAN SAAS
In a July 2026 edition of The Carlyle Compass, we examined a related 
paradox: markets punished memory chip manufacturers for reporting 
unusually strong earnings, rather than rewarding them, because record 
profits at one link in the AI value chain raise questions about whether 
other links can earn adequate returns on their own capital commitments.
The piece notes that public filings suggest AI compute backlogs exceed 
$2.2 trillion (now over $2.5 trillion).2 For AI infrastructure providers, those 
contracts are simultaneously an asset, the present value of compute rental 
payments from AI labs, and a liability, the present value of the hardware, 
data center rent, and utility costs required to deliver that promised compute. 
If downstream layers cannot convert usage into sufficient revenue, today’s 
outsized memory profits function as a tax on the rest of the value chain.

AI compute appears to be ushering in epochal change, and its allure for equity 
investors is easy to understand given the potential for outsized returns across 
the entire value chain. But its suitability for such a large share of credit portfolios 
seems less obvious given loans’ capped upside. In our view, avoiding exposure 
entirely would be senseless; many deals offer the chance to collect the complexity 
premium private lenders get paid to deliver, and there are plenty of ways to 
participate in the buildout without direct exposure to compute infrastructure. 
But the failure to institute clear and conspicuous concentration limits on AI 
compute exposure may prove to be the biggest mistake of all.
Carlyle’s midyear outlook addressed this crowding-out question directly. 
Research reports projecting more than $5 trillion in AI-related capital 
spending through 2030 imply that data centers would account for virtually 
all of the US economy’s net capital formation, given that net US fixed 
investment has been running at roughly 3% of GDP, or about $950 billion 
annually in 2026 terms.4
That framing underscores why AI capex is not a self-contained story confined 
to a handful of hyperscalers and their lenders; it is large enough, relative 
to the rest of the economy, that its financing choices carry macroeconomic 
consequences well beyond the credit book of any single lender.
