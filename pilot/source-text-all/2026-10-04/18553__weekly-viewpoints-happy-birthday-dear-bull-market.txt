Happy birthday, dear bull market
Weekly viewpoints
Burkhard Varnholt, Senior Financial Market Adviser, UBS Switzerland AG
This month, we raise a glass to the fourth birthday of a global bull market. 
Like most bull markets, it emerged from a dark hour. In addition to a 
global bear market, 2022 marked something of a geopolitical turning 
point: Russia’s invasion of Ukraine cast the old security and energy orders, 
as well as the resilience of global supply chains, in a new light. Much has 
happened since then. And despite all the pessimistic predictions, stock 
markets have continued to rise. We explain why we still see upside ahead
—and recommend several cultural masterpieces on the subject.
 
1. How structural changes are lifting stock 
markets
Nearly four years ago, on 12 October 2022, the S&P 500 fell to a low of 3,491 
points after a painful bear market in stocks and bonds. Germany’s DAX index 
stood just above 12,000 points. Today, the two indices trade above 7,600 
and 25,500, respectively—more than twice those levels. In hindsight, it was 
the perfect time to invest in stocks. It was the “darkest hour.”
Winter was approaching. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine had destabilized 
Europe. Its industry, economy, and society faced security, energy, and power 
crises for which they had never really prepared. Concerns about power 
outages, stagflation, and rising heating and production costs unsettled many. 
Memories of the pandemic’s supply chain disruptions and inflationary spiral 
were still fresh. China’s solidarity with Russia also shook the deflationary 
“Made in China” model of globalization.
And yet stock markets began to rally and have since more than doubled. 
Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 and the resulting energy 
crisis gave rise to something of a new world order. The dynamic recalls 
Friedrich Schiller’s William Tell. In Act 4, Scene 2, the 85-year-old Baron of 
Attinghausen, on his deathbed, similarly describes the emergence of a new 
world order:
“The old order falls, the times are changing, and new life blossoms from the 
ruins.”
Here, Friedrich Schiller describes how the seeds of a new era are already 
sprouting amid the ruins of Europe’s feudal age. The Swiss Confederation is 
taking shape. Across large parts of Europe, the bourgeoisie and merchants 
sweep away the old feudal order in favor of a new, liberal economic order.
In retrospect, 2022 also marked such a turning point. An old world order 
built on three pillars—1) an old peace order; 2) an old energy order; and 3) an

old order of globalization—had come apart. But this is where the meaning 
of Schiller’s words becomes clear. History never ends. When an old order 
collapses, a new one arises from the rubble.
The emergence of a new world order is driving a global increase in public 
investment—and deficits. Much of this spending is directed toward security, 
energy, and infrastructure, as well as supply chain and climate resilience. At 
the same time, the industrial artificial intelligence (AI) revolution is adding to 
this momentum through a boom in data centers and demand for electricity 
and semiconductors.
The upheavals of 2022 did not lead to stagflation or the end of history. 
Instead, we are witnessing renewed economic momentum. So far, this 
appears to be the most investment-intensive decade of the postwar era. This 
is another reason we refer to the “Roaring 20s” investment scenario—and 
developments so far have been consistent with it.
“Roaring 20s”: The story of an economic revival
Over the past four years, global stock markets have reached one record 
high after another because corporate earnings are growing faster than most 
analysts expected. What many initially interpreted as a short-term recovery 
has proved to be a structural upswing with global reach. In Europe and 
emerging markets, the earnings of listed companies also grew by an average 
of 22% and 36%, respectively, in the latest quarter.
We therefore see the real story of this bull market as less about prices and 
more about an economic revival.
Once again, the world is experiencing a period of technological change, 
accompanied by high investment and rapid innovation. And once again, 
many observers seem to underestimate the economic consequences. 
According to Joel Mokyr, last year’s Nobel laureate in economic sciences, 
what is extraordinary about industrial revolutions is “the invention of new 
methods of inventing."1 This enables incremental follow-on innovations, 
higher productivity, and stronger economic growth whose scale cannot be 
estimated at the outset.
Our “Roaring 20s” scenario therefore rests on two pillars. The first is 
productivity. Every sustainable stock market rally needs a fundamental 
foundation. In the current cycle, that foundation is productivity growth. The 
combination of artificial intelligence, automation, digitalization, and new 
business models is increasing corporate efficiency to a degree that would 
have been difficult to imagine just a few years ago. Productivity may be the 
most important economic variable of all. It enables profits to rise without 
companies having to increase prices accordingly. It finances higher real wages 
without intensifying inflationary pressure. And it expands society’s economic 
possibilities.
Innovation is typically disinflationary. Producing more with the same number 
of working hours creates prosperity without creating scarcity. This is precisely 
why many inflation concerns in recent years proved exaggerated. While the 
global economy expanded strongly, price pressures gradually eased again.

Artificial intelligence is not the only driver, of course. But it is probably the 
most visible expression of a broader development. Every major technological 
revolution typically begins with heavy investment and skepticism among 
contemporaries. Only later does the extent to which it transforms the 
economy and society become clear. We believe the AI revolution is still in an 
early phase.
The second pillar of the new “Roaring 20s” is the emergence of a new world 
order. Globalization is not disappearing. It is changing form. Governments 
and companies are increasingly investing in security, energy independence, 
critical infrastructure, and resilient supply chains. This is true not only in 
Europe but also in the Middle East, Asia, Latin America, and Africa. Systems 
once organized exclusively for efficiency are increasingly also being designed 
for resilience. This development comes at a cost. But it also generates growth.
In recent decades, governments generally held back while the private 
sector accounted for most investment. Today, this dynamic has changed. 
Governments are financing defense spending, power grids, semiconductor 
factories, energy systems, and strategic infrastructure projects on a scale not 
seen for generations. Added to this are enormous private investments across 
the AI value chain. The result is a historic global investment wave.
New capacity is being created simultaneously, from US data centers and 
European power grids to Asian production sites. What may initially look like 
a consequence of geopolitical tensions is proving to be a powerful engine of 
economic growth.
It is therefore unsurprising that the global economy has grown so much in 
absolute terms. According to the International Monetary Fund (IMF), nominal 
global gross domestic product (GDP) is around USD 118tr. It was only USD 
11tr in 1980 and about USD 34tr in 2000. In addition, record-high bank 
capital buffers and low bankruptcy rates worldwide currently underscore the 
resilience of the overall economy.
Some argue that a strong global economy will lead to higher interest rates. 
Long-term yields have indeed risen in many countries. But that does not 
automatically mean the end of the bull market. Higher interest rates have 
put pressure on price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios in many markets. Despite the 
index reaching a record high, the forward P/E ratio of the fast-growing US 
semiconductor industry is only 16.4x. South Korea’s Kospi index has returned 
more than 60% year to date. Even so, its forward P/E ratio is only around 5.3x. 
The S&P 500 has gained around 13% year to date. But its forward P/E ratio 
has fallen to 19.2x from 21.8x. Globally, earnings and productivity growth 
have more than offset the rise in interest rates—or the decline in P/E ratios.
In addition, the forces dampening inflation and interest rates remain largely 
intact. Innovation can improve supply and lower prices. China’s excess 
capacity continues to export deflation to the global economy. Rising life 
expectancy is compelling pension funds to buy increasingly longer-dated 
bonds. All of this is disinflationary and therefore supports stock markets.
This bull market is also likely to experience setbacks. Stock market corrections 
are a normal feature of markets. For instance, AI adoption, investment or 
innovation could slow. Inflation could keep rising, forcing central banks to 
raise interest rates further. Or the various geopolitical crises could escalate. 
While we do not expect these to occur, investors must be aware of such risks 
that could cut the bull market short. Still, investors who see the rally’s end 
in every geopolitical conflict, interest rate increase, and headline are missing

The fourth birthday of this bull market is more than an anniversary. It also 
suggests that the market’s foundations and momentum are stronger than 
the noise of the daily news cycle. Proliferating innovation, global productivity 
and earnings growth, the emergence of a new world order, and the new 
“Roaring 20s” will likely continue to support global stock markets. In our 
view, investors should therefore focus less on when to exit and more on how 
to diversify more effectively and remain invested for longer.
2. Will debt and deficits drive interest rates 
higher?
Rising government debt and chronic deficits are among the less welcome 
side effects of an unleashed global economy in which few countries are still 
willing to submit to the constraints of a strictly binding debt brake. Japan’s 
government debt ratio has increased five-fold over the past 50 years, from 
50% to around 250% of economic output. According to the IMF, the US 
government debt ratio is around 120% of GDP. Switzerland remains a notable 
exception. But in our view, high government debt and deficits does not 
automatically mean a reversal in interest rates or a stock market downturn.
The academic theory of “crowding out,” under which rising government 
borrowing must force capital market yields higher, is often unconvincing in 
practice. Japan’s low interest rates have demonstrated for years that highly 
developed, heavily indebted economies can follow a different path: Beyond 
a certain debt level, the political incentive for financial repression increases, 
potentially extending to direct yield curve control by the government and 
central bank. The United States also kept financing costs artificially low 
through government yield controls from 1942 to 19512. Nevertheless, the 
combination of high debt, deficits, and suppressed bond yields did not 
prevent the US from becoming the world’s most dynamic economy and 
dominant equity market.
Economic history provides another reason to be cautious about simple causal 
relationships: After the Napoleonic Wars, Britain carried government debt of 
more than 200% of gross domestic product and nevertheless rose to become 
the leading economic and financial power of the 19th century. The level of 
government debt did not determine its path to prosperity; innovation, capital 
formation, and productivity gains did.
In our view, today’s widespread alarmism about government debt therefore 
misses a central point: The level of public debt provides only limited 
information about a stock market’s future performance. Other factors 
ultimately matter more for investors: earnings growth, interest rates, 
technological progress, productivity gains, and innovation. In our view, 
investors seeking to build wealth over the long term should focus less on 
alarming fiscal scenarios and more on the fundamentals of economic value 
creation.

3. Time out: Culture for a changing world
For those who prefer to explore global transformation through music or 
literature, many classics offer inspiration. Here is a subjective list of my 
personal favorites:
In literature, alongside Schiller’s William Tell, there is Tomasi di Lampedusa’s 
masterpiece The Leopard (1954), with its key line: “If we want things to stay 
as they are, things will have to change.” The novel’s account of the decline 
of the Sicilian aristocracy during the Risorgimento was masterfully adapted 
for film by Luchino Visconti in 1963, starring Burt Lancaster and Claudia 
Cardinale.
Joseph Roth’s The Radetzky March (1932) creates an unforgettable and vivid 
panorama of Europe’s turning point and the decline of its long-dominant 
Habsburg monarchy. It is Roth’s magnum opus and a seminal work of 20th-
century European literature.
Two more novels belong on this list. Honoré de Balzac’s Père Goriot (1835) 
dissects French society after the revolution. Although the aristocracy has 
formally regained power, a new god has long since assumed true control: 
money. Balzac was a master of ironic social criticism whose view of humanity 
often bordered on cynicism. Witty, biting, and entertaining, he was one of 
the founding figures of 19th-century literary realism.
Finally, Anton Chekhov’s The Cherry Orchard (1904) portrays the Russian 
aristocracy’s inability to avert its economic decline. The old, beautiful, but 
unproductive cherry orchard is eventually bought at auction by a former serf, 
who has it cut down to build vacation homes—a radical symbol of the arrival 
of commercial modernity and a new era.
For those who prefer music, Ludwig van Beethoven’s Symphony No. 3,
Eroica (1804), is another recommendation. It was originally dedicated to 
Napoleon Bonaparte, who for Beethoven symbolized the dawn of a free, 
bourgeois world order. When Napoleon crowned himself emperor, Beethoven 
angrily tore up the title page. Musically, the Eroica marks the revolutionary 
transition from courtly classicism to the emotional freedom of Romanticism.

in the saddle, cycling toward Venice.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
