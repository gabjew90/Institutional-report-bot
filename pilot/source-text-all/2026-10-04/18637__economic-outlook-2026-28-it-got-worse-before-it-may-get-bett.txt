Executive
•	Summer brought little of the calm markets had priced in before it began. Three 
tensions we had been tracking escalated through the season, surfacing as fresh 
flashpoints by early September: a fresh energy shock out of the Middle East, a bond 
sell-off that has spread from the US to Japan to Europe (and drawn erratic policy 
responses from central banks and governments alike) and renewed doubts about the AI 
boom – talk of chip and power shortages, bubble concerns and underwhelming returns 
on frontier-model spend.   
•	But none of this has yet broken the real economy. Q2 growth surprised to the 
upside, carried by net exports and Germany‘s stimulus in Europe, and by robust AI-
related investment in the US and Asia. Forward-looking indicators such as European 
manufacturing PMIs at four-year highs and strong US Q3 nowcasts show little sign of 
deterioration, albeit with marked divergence across sectors.  
•	The Great Unwind: the case for an orderly path forward. Our baseline continues to 
be that these three tension points will ease rather than escalate, but that call rests 
on specific mechanisms. In the report we also model three downside scenarios and 
their economic and capital market impacts, each based on a disorderly release of the 
tensions.
	o The energy shock risk. We expect the conflict to stay contained and that 
rerouting, demand destruction and alternative supply do the job energy markets 
need them to do, easing prices and letting the inflation shock fade. This rests on 
the assumption that the Saudi pipeline outage does not extend past a month. If 
the outage runs longer, the shortfall could exceed 8Mb/d and Brent could stay 
near USD100/bbl, turning what is now a contained shock into a stagflationary 
shock. 
	o The bond sell-off risk. We assume inflation moderates without meaningful 
second-round wage effects, and that sovereign markets can absorb elevated 
issuance without a disorderly rise in risk premia. The assumption doing the real 
work here is political: the US midterms don‘t produce further fiscal slippage or 
erratic policy interventions and European elections (France, Italy) do not add fiscal 
uncertainty. Note that the real rate component of the US 10 treasury rates has 
increased by a full percentage point this past year confirming that bond markets 
also price more AI growth. In the downside, self-reinforcing yield–fiscal loop can 
take hold and force central banks to stabilize bond markets.
	o The AI capex cycle risk. We think that AI capex decelerates slightly to peak at 
USD1trn in 2028, with value creation shifting from infrastructure build-out toward 
adoption and productivity. This rests on the assumption that infrastructure and 
power bottlenecks stay manageable, financing conditions don‘t tighten sharply 
and AI earnings keep validating the spend. A sharper investment retrenchment, 
given how much of US and Asian growth and equity performance now runs 
through AI, could spill from technology into corporate investment and trade, and 
from wealth effects into consumption.

•	Our scenario in numbers: Global GDP growth slows from +3.0% in 2025 to +2.6% 
in 2026, recovers to +2.8% in 2027, and eases again to +2.6% in 2028. US growth 
moderates from +2.1% (2026) to +1.8% (2028); the Eurozone recovers from +1.0% to 
+1.3%; Asia stays the main engine at around +4.4%. We forecast Brent to average 87 
USD/bbl in Q4 2026 before easing to 76 USD/bbl on average in 2027 as Gulf export 
flows gradually normalize both in and around Hormuz but refined-product markets 
are running out of buffers, and prices should remain elevated for the next few quarters. 
European gas prices should stay elevated through year-end, with TTF averaging around 
EUR65/MWh in Q4 2026, before easing to about EUR36/MWh in 2027. Inflation stays 
1–2pps above target in Europe and the US until H2 2027, which is why we still expect 
one more Fed hike to 4.00–4.25% and two more ECB hikes to 3.00% by year-end, with 
cutting cycles starting only once inflation is back at target – earliest end-2027 – and 
terminal rates of 3.50% (Fed) and 2.25% (ECB) reached in 2028.   
•	What it means for markets. We see bond yields dropping when markets start pricing 
in our central bank scenario of a lower terminal rate in both Europe and the US. 
The 10y Bund is expected to gradually drop from current levels to 3.3% at the end 
of 2026 and further to 3.0% in 2028 (US 10y from 4.7% end of 2026 to 4.3% in 2028). 
Credit spreads will slightly widen from current low levels given strong supply from 
the technology sector. Equity markets will finish the year with double digits again but 
decelerate to high single digits in 2027 and 2028, with valuations normalizing to historic 
levels (S&P500 2026-2028: 14%, 8%, 8%, Eurostoxx: 15%, 9%, 8% total return). The euro 
will gain gradually against the dollar, reaching 1.18 in 2028 as policy uncertainty and 
a weaker economy in the US will weigh on the greenback. Private markets are entering 
a more selective phase: Private equity faces a record ~13,300-company exit backlog, 
while infrastructure benefits from the AI buildout, with data-center investment expected 
at USD 5–7trn this decade.

The summer turned three risks we had been 
monitoring into part of our base case: a fresh energy 
shock out of the Middle East, a bond sell-off that has 
spread from the US to Japan to Europe and renewed 
doubts about the AI boom. Yet the real economy has 
not broken. Q2 growth surprised to the upside on net 
exports, Germany‘s stimulus and robust AI-related 
investment, and forward-looking indicators show limited 
sign of deterioration so far, even as bond markets tell 
a more troubled story, with yields at multi-year highs 
and equities still reading the growth story rather than 
the bond one. In our baseline scenario, global growth 
slows from +3.0% in 2025 to +2.6% in 2026, recovers to 
+2.8% in 2027 as the chokepoints fade and eases back to 
+2.6% in 2028 as that support wanes. Within this, the US 
moderates from +2.1% to +1.8%, the Eurozone recovers 
from +1.0% to +1.3%, and Asia remains the main growth 
engine throughout at around +4.4%. This path holds 
only as long as the three chokepoints unwind rather 
than escalate, and each rests on a specific, breakable 
assumption. On energy, we assume the conflict stays 
contained and that rerouting, demand destruction and 
alternative supply let prices ease, contingent on the 
Saudi pipeline outage not extending past a month; a 
longer outage could push the shortfall above 8Mb/d and 
Brent back near USD100/bbl, turning a contained shock 
into a stagflationary one. On bonds, we assume inflation 
moderates without a wage spiral and that sovereign 
markets absorb elevated issuance without disorderly 
repricing, a call that rests on European elections (France, 
Italy) and the US midterms not adding fresh fiscal 
uncertainty; if that gives way, a self-reinforcing yield-
fiscal loop could force central banks to choose between 
fighting inflation and stabilizing bond markets. On 
AI, we assume capex keeps supporting growth while 
decelerating, peaking above USD1trn in 2028, contingent 
on power and infrastructure bottlenecks staying 
manageable and earnings continuing to validate the 
spend; an investment retrenchment here would spill from 
technology into corporate investment, consumption and 
trade more broadly. Growth, inflation and rates in our 
baseline hold only as long as these three assumptions 
hold too.

If our assumptions do not hold, we will stumble into 
one or several downside scenarios ranging from 
stagflation to demand-side deflationary recessions. 
Table 2 sets out what happens if our assumptions 
on chokepoints break. Should Middle East de-
escalation fail, the shock persists rather than fades: 
oil stays above USD100/bbl into 2027 and the picture 
turns stagflationary, with US and Eurozone growth 
undershooting the baseline. The Eurozone would see 
a recession, while inflation runs hotter and keeps rising 
through 2027, forcing the Fed and ECB to hike further 
to 5.00% and 4.00%, respectively. Long yields spike 
first before reversing sharply lower once the growth 
damage bites, and risk assets sell off hard through 2026 
– equities barely positive, credit and sovereign spreads 
wider, private equity, private debt and real estate all 
negative – ahead of a partial rebound in 2027. An AI 
bubble burst plays out in the opposite direction: the

notably, infrastructure, which turns negative for the only

Global trade of goods in volume is expected to grow 
at +4% y/y in 2026, driven mainly by AI, which masks 
a more divergent global economy. AI-driven demand 
will contribute 4.8pps to global trade, which together 
with European defense spending (+0.2pp) and carryover 
effects (+1.4pp) add further support. Without these 
offsetting forces, the underlying picture would be 
considerably weaker: The Strait of Hormuz closure is 
estimated to subtract 2.1pps from trade growth, while 
the ongoing trade war erases a further 0.4pp. The rest 
of the global economy is contracting in trade terms, and 
the +4% headline number conceals what is effectively a 
two-speed world.
Global goods trade growth is volume is expected to 
ease to +2.9% in both 2027 and 2028 as the forces 
supporting the 2026 headline begin to fade. AI-
driven demand will remain the largest contributor to 
growth (+1pp), but at slower phase than prior years as 
deceleration starts. Hormuz normalization is unlikely to 
be linear, causing stock rebuilding to take longer and 
extend to 2028. Defense spending remains a positive but 
diminishing contributor. The trade war persists through 
2027, with US tariffs continuing to disrupt established 
supply chains, subtracting 0.3pp from growth. In parallel, 
free-trade agreements (FTAs) signed over the past 
12 months will begin generating measurable export 
gains from 2027, adding around 0.1pp annually, with 
beneficiaries concentrated in the EU, India, Mercosur, the 
UAE and Australia.
At a country level, the clearest winners are economies 
embedded in AI supply chains, concentrated in 
Asia-Pacific, while structurally the US trade war 
unleashes the so-called “China shock 2.0” amplifying 
China’s presence through global supply chains. 
South Korea and Taiwan are posting export growth 
rates of +10.5% and +30% y/y, respectively, driven by 
semiconductor exports and other AI inputs. Mexico, 
Vietnam and Malaysia are benefiting from supply-chain 
diversification, while Israel’s technology exports are 
also on the rise. In the developed world, the US and the 
Netherlands stand out. The structural consequence of US 
tariffs, combined with China‘s weak domestic demand, 
are amplifying China’s role in the global supply chain 
under the „China shock 2.0.“ Unable to sell as much into

the US, China is redirecting exports through the global 
south and into Europe at record volumes, causing Europe 
to be squeezed from both sides: more Chinese imports 
competing with domestic industry, and fewer US export 
opportunities due to tariffs. 
The effective US tariff rate currently stands at 
9.2% and is expected to reach 12% following 
the implementation of the second Section 301 
investigation on overcapacity in October. Export losses 
are estimated at USD42bn this year, after USD74bn in 
2025. We expect tariffs on China at 32%, with potential 
for lower rates on non-sensitive goods and fewer 
restrictions as relations are likely to remain stable in the 
near term. However, the trade war is likely to intensify 
after the US midterm elections as a shift in Congressional 
control would place constraints on the administration‘s 
domestic policy agenda and push it to rely more heavily 
on trade tools. On the downside, potential risks could 
push the average US tariff rate up to 21.3%. The most 
likely scenarios include higher-than-expected tariffs 
under the overcapacity Section 301, the opening of 
a new Section 301 investigation targeting countries 
accused of transshipment on behalf of China, and 
continued tensions with Canada following the escalation 
over the summer. While we do not expect further 
developments in the near term, the threat of 50% tariffs 
on Canadian autos remains while USMCA renegotiations 
progress very slowly. Finally, the EU should also remain 
watchful, with two main tariff risks: one linked to the 
global services tax and the other tied to the new US 
sanctions on Russia, allowing for tariffs on Russian 
energy importers.
The global fiscal outlook continues to deteriorate at 
the margin as structural spending pressures – defense 
foremost – combine with the renewed cost of energy-
related support measures. Many governments were 
initially reluctant to intervene, treating the current 
shock as transitory; the repeated extension of these 
„temporary“ measures has instead become a recurring 
drag on consolidation efforts. Looking at the regions, 
US fiscal policy is unlikely to turn significantly restrictive 
amid a likely divided Congress (or a Democratic 
sweep) after the November midterm elections. In 
this environment, we would expect only limited fiscal 
consolidation in 2027 and 2028, to the tune of 0.5% 
of GDP in 2027 and 0.6% in 2028 of structural deficit 
improvement, relying on a mix of discretionary and social 
spending restraint and „stealth“ taxation increases. In 
the Eurozone, political risk is an increasingly relevant 
input for spread dynamics: With France, Italy and 
Spain all facing elections in 2027, pre-electoral tax cuts 
and spending pledges raise the risk of fiscal slippage 
precisely as consolidation becomes more pressing. 
Moreover, the phase-out of NGEU disbursements adds 
a further headwind: As grants are booked as fiscal 
revenue, their decline mechanically weighs on headline 
deficits for the bloc‘s largest beneficiaries (Southern 
European countries in particular), compounding the loss 
of investment support at a time when fiscal buffers are 
already thinning.

Inflation rates will remain elevated until the first half 
of 2027 as indirect effects of elevated input costs filter

Capital markets outlook: 
Cruel (bond) summer coming to an end
Capital markets had a difficult summer as the energy 
shock and renewed inflation concerns pushed 
government bond yields sharply upwards to multi-year 
highs. Equities proved more resilient: Strong earnings and 
AI-related investment kept returns positive despite rising 
rates, while credit spreads remained close to historical 
tights, supported by solid corporate results. In currencies, 
EURUSD stayed broadly range-bound, while emerging-
market assets benefited from improving fundamentals 
but now offer less spread compensation. Private markets 
are becoming more selective: Private equity remains 
constrained by a record exit backlog, private debt 
continues to deliver attractive income despite pockets of 
stress, infrastructure is benefiting from the AI buildout and 
real estate faces renewed refinancing pressure. Looking 
ahead, we expect the summer repricing to prove orderly 
rather than the start of a broader market break. As the 
energy impulse fades in our baseline outlook, we see 
scope for gradually declining long-term rates, while equity 
returns should moderate as valuations normalize. 
The summer selloff in long-term rates had three 
synchronized drivers: the feedback loop between 
energy prices and policy rate expectations, firmer 
economic activity and a higher term premium. The 
repricing of the rate path was the strongest driver. The 
term premium was the swing factor capturing supply-
demand imbalances emerging over summer in a 
government bonds market that is moved at the margin 
by price-sensitive, leveraged investors that react to 
tighter funding conditions. Firmer economic activity on 
either side of the Atlantic acted as underlying upside 
trend. But we think US late-cycle dynamics will resume 
in 2027 as the energy impulse fades and growth returns 
to potential. Markets will price rate cuts. Long-term 
rates will anticipate lower growth, bringing 10y yields 
to 4.5% in 2027 and 4.3% in 2028. The US term-premium 
rebuild looks largely complete and is actively contained 
by Treasury operations (buybacks and active Treasury 
issuance). The hawkish Fed turn should itself re-anchor 
the long end by restoring inflation-fighting credibility.

to twelve month horizon. It captures some of the policy-

carries less term-premium risk than the long end. At current 
levels, intermediate maturities show the best combination 
of positive real returns and convexity.
Within the Eurozone the risk premium now sits in 
France rather than Italy or Spain. The OAT-Bund spread 
reached highest value since the Euro crisis (around 
100bps) as markets began pricing a scenario in which the

tightening impulse is missing as NGEU support fades, and 
upcoming elections are an upside risk we would treat as 
temporary. There is no systemic fragmentation risk within

the currency pair. We expect this pattern to continue. 
With the US midterm elections likely to increase political

EM equities are the cheapest way into the AI value 
chain. Consensus EPS growth of +72%, +23% and +10% 
over FY1-FY3 is driven by semiconductors, and AI supports 
around a quarter of Asian growth. Yet the market still 
prices EM semis at a discount to US peers: Their combined 
USD4trn market capitalization is below Nvidia alone, 
and technology weighs 21% in MSCI ACWI, of which only

monetization. SK Hynix and Samsung trade near 4x PE, 
with new long-term contracts extending the outlook 
and securing payback, whereas US semis command 
three times the multiple for twice the growth. The cycle 
has limits: We estimate AI capex to be roughly halfway 
through and expect it to peak above USD1trn in 2028, 
when normalizing returns on equity would imply a 20-
45% PE discount that our target already anticipates. We 
forecast MSCI EM total returns of +31% in 2026, +12% in 
2027 and +7% in 2028 – ahead of developed markets next 
year, fading only as the semiconductor cycle matures.
Across the USD16.7trn private capital industry, two 
forces are doing most of the sorting. Rates, having risen 
sharply again on both sides of the Atlantic rather than 
easing as underwritten, are pushing financing costs back 
toward levels many portfolios were not built for, and AI is 
rewriting which businesses deserve a premium and which 
do not. Each corner of private capital wears this pressure 
differently. Private equity is stuck at the exit, sitting on 
a record backlog of unsold companies as chastened 
investors keep fundraising in a multi-year drought. Private 
debt still pays its keep, with direct lending returning 
almost 11% last year, but cracks are showing among 
smaller funds and the most AI-exposed borrowers, where 
write-downs are creeping higher. Infrastructure is the 
outlier success story, its 12-13% annual return leaning on 
the USD5-7trn the world is expected to spend this decade 
wiring up data centers for the AI boom. Real estate‘s 
fragile recovery is now at risk of stalling as financing costs 
move higher again instead of easing, and AI‘s dampening 
effect on office demand leaves the sector doubly exposed. 
The thread running through all four is dispersion. Capital 
is flowing to contracted, well-covered, AI-aligned assets 
and fleeing everything else, widening the gap between 
winners and laggards to levels rarely seen. 
Private equity‘s story in 2026 is increasingly one of 
exits, or the lack of them. US buyout funds are still sitting 
on some 13,300 unsold companies, most acquired in the 
frothy days of 2020-21 at prices that assumed multiples 
would keep rising. Instead, across US buyout deals, 
multiple expansion contributed 37.3% of value creation in 
the 2011-13 vintages but just 0.5% in 2020-22, with EBITDA 
margin expansion and revenue growth now doing almost 
all the work (Figure 9). That is a much harder way to earn 
a return, and it shows up directly in the exit numbers. US 
exits fell -46.3% in the second quarter, kept alive mainly 
by a handful of large sales rather than a broad clearing 
of the backlog, while dealmaking itself dropped -37.5% 
as the Fed reopened the door to rate hikes and software, 
previously the industry‘s favored sector, fell -65.7% on 
AI-driven concerns about its growth outlook. With so 
little cash flowing back to investors, distributions remain 
thin and the incentive to hold rather than sell persists. 
Europe tells a related story, deal value rose +6.9% in 
the second quarter, but its own deals-to-exit ratio has 
climbed from 2.4x to 2.9x, an early sign that the same 
operational reckoning may be coming. The lesson on 
both sides of the Atlantic is the same: Returns now have 
to be built company by company rather than bought 
through re-rating, and only sponsors who can genuinely 
grow revenue and margins will convert their backlog into 
distributions. We expect private equity to return 7-9% this 
year, rising to 11-12% in 2027 and 2028 as the exit market 
gradually reopens.

Private debt has spent 2026 absorbing shocks it was 
largely built to withstand. Oil spikes and rate scares are 
not new for this asset class. The summer‘s Hormuz-driven 
energy shock pushed spreads a little wider, but the move 
has been modest. Spreads remain close to historic lows, 
and leveraged loan defaults, while ticking up, are still only 
a small drag on returns. In this environment we expect 
defaults to normalize gradually as energy prices and 
rate expectations settle back down. Some of the recent 
concern bundles together several separate issues – retail-
vehicle redemptions, loan quality, software exposure and 
fund-wrapper design – and treating them as one story 
overstates the risk. The more interesting divergence is in 
valuations rather than credit quality. Private NAVs have 
held steady, while listed vehicles trade near 17% discounts, 
and payment-in-kind income, now around 8% of BDC

borrowers, whose interest coverage is squeezed by still-
high floating coupons. The sensible approach, as in most 
late-cycle credit markets, is to be selective rather than 
pull back, favoring seniority, strong collateral coverage 
and full disclosure over older, more richly priced positions 
elsewhere in the portfolio. We expect spreads to hold 
around 550bps through year-end, cruising at similar levels 
through 2027 and 2028 as the asset class settles into a 
steadier, income-led phase.
Infrastructure is expected to sustain strong 
performance, underpinned by AI buildout as a growth 
lever. Data centers continue to dominate the deal market 
this year. Despite strong new-build across major regions, 
demand keeps outstripping supply (Figure 14), holding 
vacancy at record lows while rents keep growing strongly. 
With power, permitting, and land constraints sustaining 
the scarcity, the economics stay attractive for ready-to-use 
data centers contracted long-term to investment-grade 
counterparties. Two tailwinds are strengthening the case. 
First, the token economics are getting more compelling: 
Usage keeps surging despite significant efficiency gains 
as workloads shift from chatbots to agentic, reasoning-
heavy models and the paradigm grows more compute-
intensive. Token prices are stabilizing at the frontier 
even as serving costs keep falling, supporting provider 
margins. Hyperscaler AI revenue is now beginning to cover 
the capex depreciation, pointing to a more sustainable 
cycle. Second, rising data sovereignty demands across 
regions add a further durable tailwind. We continue to 
see infrastructure as a strong play in the AI supercycle – 
the foundation on which the whole AI stack is built – with 
tangible, contracted earnings rather than speculative 
upside.

The income-led recovery in real estate continues, 
but higher long-end yields weigh on pricing and 
refinancing. Liquidity is returning ahead of pricing: 
Investment volumes are up 21% y/y in the US and 10% 
in Europe, drifting back to pre-Covid norms, while prices 
are stabilizing and cap rates are broadly flat. Values 
remain 14-16% below the 2022 peak in aggregate on 
both sides of the Atlantic, with US offices down more 
than 40% and Europe offices down over 20%, against 
high-single-digit drawdowns in US retail and industrial 
and European residential. Fundamentals are supportive: 
shrinking new supply and resilient occupier demand 
should underpin a progressive, income-led recovery, but 
the path is gradual, with no sector expected to regain its 
peak before 2028 and offices to barely move (Figure 15). 
Rates are the constraint. With 10y yields at multi-decade 
highs on renewed inflation pressure and fiscal concerns, 
private appraisal cap rates still sit near 2021 levels. 
Marks have yet to absorb the move, so further outward 
yield shift and headwinds on refinancing and liquidity lie 
ahead.
