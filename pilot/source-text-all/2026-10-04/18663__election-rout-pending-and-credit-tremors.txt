Election rout pending and credit tremors 
Verbier 
Trading financial markets around consumer confidence data has never made too much sense to GREED & 
fear, most particularly in a world of ever more K-shaped economies. But the same does not apply from a 
political standpoint. And the latest US consumer confidence is such as to make GREED & fear assume, until 
proven otherwise, that a rout is facing the Republicans in the forthcoming mid-term elections. The Conference 
Board’s US consumer confidence index declined by 6.7 points to 81.9 in September, the lowest level since 
April 2014 (see Exhibit 1), compared with consensus expectations of 89.0.  
Exhibit 1: The Conference Board's US Consumer Confidence Index 
  
Source: Bloomberg, The Conference Board 
 
That is not necessarily terrible for markets, and GREED & fear has read a lot of late about how the US stock 
market usually rallies after the mid-terms. But, there again, the stock market has historically done relatively 
badly going into the mid-terms whereas that has not been the case so far this year, primarily because of the 
stellar earnings growth driven by the highly earnings-accretive AI capex cycle. The LSEG I/B/E/S consensus 
data as of 25 September shows that S&P500 3Q26 earnings are expected to rise by 30.2% YoY, up from 16.1% 
growth expected last October. The IT sector has the second-highest forecast 3Q26 earnings growth of 65.1%, 
up from 22.3% last October (see Exhibit 2). All 12 sub-industries in the IT sector have higher forecast earnings 
than a year ago, with the semiconductor and semiconductor materials & equipment sub-industries having the 
highest earning growth (131.7% and 68.9%, respectively).

Clearly, a counter-trend bond rally can happen at any time after such a big move with the 10-year Treasury 
yield up 82bp last quarter, and doubtless contrarians would highlight that sentiment indicators on Treasuries 
are almost universally bearish, or at least GREED & fear assumes that they are. But GREED & fear has no interest 
in playing such a rally; though GREED & fear has to admit that if Kevin Warsh really raises the federal funds 
rate by 75bp this year that could be positive for the long end. Meanwhile, yesterday’s core PCE inflation data, 
the Fed’s favourite inflation indicator, has served to reduce expectations of a rate hike this month. The core 
PCE price index rose by 3.0% YoY in August, compared with consensus expectations of 3.3%. While the annual 
revisions to GDP and PCE data show that core PCE inflation has been revised down by 0.3-0.4ppt in the 
previous three months to 3.0% YoY in July (see Exhibit 7). The money markets are now discounting “only” 
30bp in further rate hikes by the end of this year, down from 52bp in mid-September.  
As for the sell-off in the long end, the political fallout is a mortgage rate at 7.3%. This is another major negative 
for the While House incumbent, with the mortgage applications index having collapsed. The mortgage 
applications index declined by 6% WoW in the week ended 25 September and is now down 37% YoY (see 
Exhibit 8).

All this is why the 47th American president’s polling on the economy remains dire. The same negative polling 
continues on the Iran War, as has been the case ever since the conflict commenced. A Reuters/Ipsos poll 
conducted 17-20 September shows that 62% of Americans disapprove of US military strikes on Iran while 67% 
disapprove of Trump’s handling of the economy. Writing the Asia Maxima quarterly over the past week, there 
are clearly three dominant issues facing investors in GREED & fear’s view. The first, and perhaps most 
important for equities, is the duration of the AI capex cycle and whether there will be adequate returns from 
the massive spending. The second is the renewed realisation that G7 government bonds are in a structural 
bear market and what that means. The third is the state of play in shifting geopolitics. This week, GREED & 
fear has heard two completely contrasting narratives as regards the state of play in the US-Iran negotiations. 
What is clear to GREED & fear is that Iran, by sending its president and foreign minister to the United Nations 
General Assembly last week, wanted to be seen to be trying to negotiate. Still, GREED & fear also takes the 
view that Iran is serious when it says it will only negotiate on the basis of the MOU signed by Donald Trump in 
Versailles in June. 
The contrary view is that the economic pressures on Tehran are becoming such that they will be forced to be 
more accommodative. The reason GREED & fear does not buy this is that Iran has a demonstrated ability to 
take a lot of pain. Still, a key variable is the attitude of China as that is the one country with leverage over 
Tehran. In GREED & fear’s view, President Xi Jinping will have been pleased that the Iranians were in New York 
being seen to try and negotiate, though he skipped the UN General Assembly during his US visit last week. 
Meanwhile, the optics of the Chinese president’s state visit to America were much better for Xi, in GREED & 
fear’s view, than for his American counterpart. 
Staying on the subject of China, this week has seen some moves to support domestic demand, most 
importantly with policies focused on the all-important residential property market. The State Council executive 
meeting on Monday, chaired by Premier Li Qiang, stated, among other things, that the government will roll out 
policies and measures aimed at stabilising the real estate market and promote employment and income 
growth (see Xinhua article: “Chinese premier chairs State Council executive meeting on macro policies, 
investment”, 28 September 2026). This was followed the next day by the Ministry of Finance and People’s 
Bank of China announcing a set of policies of which the most important is a new mortgage interest rate 
subsidy. The subsidy is offered to first-time buyers of units of up to 120sqm and worth Rmb1.5m or less. 
Eligible new borrowers will receive an interest subsidy of an annualised one percentage point up to Rmb1m 
of mortgages for a maximum of five years. 
Given the maximum amounts involved, this is primarily aimed at supporting lower-income households in tier-
three and tier-four cities. This is why the new policy has not excited the stock market, with the MSCI China 
Property Index up only 2.6% since the announcement (see Exhibit 9). Still, it is a sign that the authorities are 
not completely oblivious to bad data on the “let them eat rice” angle. For this new policy came out after data 
showing an outright contraction in household borrowing, as discussed here last week (see GREED & fear – 
The virtues of small over large, 24 September 2026). Thus, household loans declined by 1.6% YoY in August, 
with a record decline of Rmb1.03tn recorded in the first eight months of this year (see Exhibit 10). This is also 
the first time there has been a central government nationwide measure to support housing demand.

with a 2.7% decline in the G7 bond index. It has also outperformed year-to-date, rising by 4.8%, compared with

compared with a 19.4% decline in the G7 government bond index (see Exhibit 13).

Meanwhile, the start of a new quarter is a good time to review the performances of the rest of GREED & fear’s 
various long-only portfolios. It should be noted that none of the portfolios can own cash. All performances 
recorded are as of the end of last quarter.   
The long-term performance of the Asia ex-Japan thematic portfolio remains, for now, relatively satisfactory. 
Since its inception 24 years ago at the end of 3Q02, the portfolio has risen on a total-return basis by 4,792% 
in US dollar terms, compared with a 1,128% increase in the MSCI AC Asia ex-Japan Index and a 1,379% 
increase in the S&P500 (see Exhibit 14). This means the portfolio has risen by an annualised 17.6% since 
inception, compared with an annualised 11% increase in the MSCI AC Asia ex-Japan Index and an annualised 
11.9% gain in the S&P500.   
Exhibit 14: Asia ex-Japan long-only portfolio total-return performance vs MSCI AC Asia ex-Japan (US$ terms) 
  
Note: Data up to 30 September 2026. Source: Datastream, Jefferies 
 
The portfolio outperformed last quarter, rising by 3.2% on a total-return basis, compared with a 0.2% decline 
in the MSCI AC Asia ex-Japan Index. But it remains an underperformer year to date, rising by 13.6%, compared 
with a 26.0% gain in the regional benchmark, reflecting the underweight in the major semiconductor stocks. 
Three stocks, TSMC, Samsung Electronics, and Hynix still account for 33.5% of the MSCI AC Asia ex-Japan 
benchmark, though down from 35.4% at the end of 2Q26.  
The portfolio ended last quarter 20% invested in India, way down from a peak of 50% in mid-February 2024, 
and 40% invested in China/Hong Kong. The portfolio also has 26% invested in Taiwan and Korea. It should be 
noted again that the stated performance of the absolute-return portfolio is on a total-return basis. 
The absolute-return thematic portfolio for Japan, introduced on 17 March 2005, outperformed last quarter. It 
rose on a total-return basis last quarter by 10.2% in US dollar terms, compared with a 7.4% gain in the Topix. 
Still the portfolio remains a slight underperformer year to date, rising by 21.7% in US dollar terms compared 
with a 22.8% gain in the Topix. 
From a longer-term perspective, the Japan portfolio is now up 661% in US dollar terms on a total-return basis 
since inception, while the Topix has risen by 261% in US dollar terms over the same period (see Exhibit 15). 
This translates into an annualised gain of 9.9% in US dollar terms since inception, compared with a 6.1% 
annualised gain for the Topix.   
Again, the portfolio may not own cash. As with the Asian portfolio, the above-stated performance is on a total-
return basis. The Japan portfolio remains a combination of domestic demand names and exporters.
