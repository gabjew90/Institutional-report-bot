Spotting dislocations beneath the surface
As we discussed yesterday in our letter on the US consensus (again here), Wall Street remains broadly constructive on US equities, with strategists’ year-end S&P 500 targets currently clustered around 8,000 supported by a prevailing narrative around
resilient earnings growth and continued AI investment that continues to help offset headwinds from higher bond yields, inflation concerns and higher oil prices.
However, and while the US equity rally still appears resilient at index level (despite recent bouts of volatility), a look beneath the surface reveals underlying corrective behaviour that is far more severe than headline indices suggest. More than 30% of
S&P 500 constituents are already in bear‑market territory, while over half of Consumer Discretionary stocks and nearly half of Technology stocks have fallen more than 20% from their respective peaks.

Importantly, this hidden correction has occurred despite a broadly resilient earnings backdrop, and in many cases, valuations have compressed far more sharply than earnings expectations, creating a widening disconnect between price action and
fundamentals. As a result, interesting dislocations appear to be emerging in stocks that have already absorbed much of the recent macro uncertainty while continuing to exhibit positive earnings momentum. Recent developments may also bring
some support. One example is the August US Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) inflation report which recently helped soften the policy narrative by showing that both headline and core inflation came in noticeably cooler than anticipated.
Also Micron’s results, which earlier this week brought more evidence at the corporate level that AI‑related spending remains robust. Such developments support the view that earnings remain a key driver of equity performance while also serving to
reduce the perceived risk that rising yields might become a more persistent headwind. Following the recent run-up in yields, investors will be eagerly looking for signs the recent bond‑market sell‑off is beginning to stabilise, on the basis that falling
yields, particularly if accompanied by some stability in the oil price, might permit a refocus on fundamentals rather than discount rates. As we discussed recently in our letter “Higher yields – when might they bite?” (again here), historically, some of
the strongest rebounds have occurred when rate‑driven selling pressure has started to fade.
So today, against this nervous short-term market backdrop, we focus on some of the sectors and themes that have recently suffered drawdowns despite maintaining resilient or improving earnings revisions, on the basis that any relief on rates might
allow fundamentals to reassert themselves in market pricing.
The recent selling has not primarily been about earnings
The gap that has emerged between stronger earnings revisions and weaker price performance suggests that higher discount rates have recently been the more significant headwind for the S&P 500 than weakening fundamentals.
Historically, earnings revision momentum (ERR) and 12‑month S&P 500 performance have tended to move in the same direction, underlining the close link between changes in profit expectations and equity returns. The current phase is unusual
because that relationship has weakened: as illustrated below, the ERR for the S&P 500 (in red) has risen to one of its strongest levels in years, while index performance (in beige) has softened amid a sharp rise in bond yields. This dislocation suggests
that the recent decline has been driven more by valuation pressure and higher discount rates than by a broad deterioration in corporate earnings expectations.
Importantly, this divergence has emerged while revision ratio remains in positive territory. In previous episodes, gaps between earnings revisions and market performance have not always persisted, with either revisions weakening over time or equity
markets recovering as attention returned to fundamentals. At this stage, however, consensus earnings expectations still appear relatively resilient, indicating that recent market weakness may reflect macro and rate‑related concerns more than a
material deterioration in the profit outlook:

So where do earnings remain resilient despite recent market weakness?
While higher discount rates have driven a broader valuation reset, earnings momentum remains resilient in several parts of the US market. In Technology, Health Care and Industrials in particular, earnings trends remain relatively firm despite weaker price action.
The rise in US bond yields has weighed on equities, but the impact has varied meaningfully across sectors. This creates a clearer distinction between areas where weaker share prices appear to reflect softer fundamentals and those where market
weakness looks larger than the change in earnings expectations would suggest. Viewing sectors through the lens of distance from 52‑week highs and earnings revision ratios (ERR), several divergences stand out.
Information Technology is the clearest example, with the strongest earnings momentum in the S&P 500 (ERR 65.4%) while trading just 1.0% below its 52‑week high. Health Care also combines solid earnings revisions (43.9%) with a relatively limited
drawdown (-2.8%). Energy and Financials continue to show supportive earnings momentum (both at 30.4%), although these indices have suffered somewhat larger pullbacks. Industrials are notable: despite a strong ERR of 49.3%, the sector currently
trades 10.2% below its 52‑week high, suggesting that higher rates and valuation pressure have outweighed improving earnings expectations.
By contrast, weakness in Materials, Consumer Discretionary and Utilities appears more consistent with softer or muted earnings momentum, with ERR at -4.4%, 5.7% and 0.0%, respectively.
Overall, the US sector picture suggests that recent market weakness has not been driven uniformly by deteriorating fundamentals. In parts of the market, especially Technology, Health Care and Industrials, earnings trends remain relatively firm
despite weaker price action. If bond yields stabilise, sectors where earnings revisions have stayed constructive despite recent de‑rating could see performance move more in line with underlying fundamentals.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 01/10/2026.
Analysts remain firmly tilted toward cyclical growth
Analyst recommendations continue to highlight a preference for growth and economically sensitive sectors. Technology, telecoms, energy, materials and industrials currently have the highest proportion of buy ratings.
The chart below shows the proportion of buy ratings for each US sector today (red) versus three months ago (beige), and it illustrates how, on this basis, Technology remains the clear favourite, with 71.6% of stocks carrying a buy recommendation.
Telecoms and energy follow at 69.1% and 66.8%, while materials and health care also enjoy strong support, with buy ratings of around 65%. At the other end of the spectrum, consumer staples remain the least favoured sector at 48.3%, followed by
utilities (53.5%) and real estate (54.7%).
Recommendation trends over the past three months have been broadly stable. Energy has recorded the largest improvement, with buy ratings rising from 64.3% to 66.8%, while materials, industrials and real estate have also seen modest gains. In
contrast, technology, telecoms, health care, consumer discretionary and financials have experienced slight declines, although analyst sentiment overall remains firmly skewed towards cyclical and growth‑oriented sectors:

With this in mind, going forward we take a closer look at Technology, Energy and Industrials, examining how recent price action, earnings revisions and technical signals line up, and screening each sector for companies where fundamental
momentum, valuation and trend characteristics may be most aligned in each case.
1. Technology
Prices have softened, but earnings momentum remains strong, with the earnings revision ratio still the highest in the market. At the same time, the AI theme has been evolving from a broad momentum trade into a more selective phase, favouring companies that can
convert AI investment into monetisation, pricing power, free cash flow generation and attractive returns on capital.
US technology has weakened despite still‑elevated earnings revisions. While the earnings revision ratio has eased from its mid‑2026 peak near 80%, it remains strong at around 65%, indicating that the sector continues to enjoy one of the most
supportive earnings backdrops in the market. However, relative performance has softened materially, with rolling 12‑month returns falling from above 50% earlier this year to roughly 30%.
A notable gap has therefore emerged between fundamentals and price action. Earnings revisions (dark brown line) remain at historically strong levels, while sector performance (light beige line) has trended lower in recent months. This divergence
suggests that investor enthusiasm has cooled despite resilient earnings momentum, leaving US technology performance weaker than the revisions backdrop would typically imply.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 01/10/2026.
Micron’s results (released Wednesday) helped reinforce the view that the AI cycle remains strong, but leadership is becoming more selective. The company delivered a major earnings and guidance beat, highlighted tightening memory supply–demand
conditions through 2027–28 and signalled continued strength in AI‑driven data‑centre demand.

The results support the broader thesis that the next phase of the AI trade is likely to favour companies that convert AI spending into revenues, earnings and free cash flow (FCF). Memory, semiconductors, servers, networking and power‑equipment
suppliers continue to benefit directly from the AI infrastructure build‑out, while investors are becoming more demanding towards hyperscalers and other capital‑intensive beneficiaries. In short, the AI theme appears to be evolving from a broad
momentum trade towards a more selective focus on monetisation, pricing power, cash‑flow generation and returns on capital.

2. Energy
Earnings momentum has rebounded from deeply negative levels, and the earnings revision ratio is now back above 30%. At the same time, XLE and XOP have broken out of key resistance zones, highlighting improving technicals even as investors remain cautious about
the sustainability of the earnings recovery.
US energy fundamentals have improved sharply throughout 2026, with the earnings revision ratio rebounding from deeply negative levels at the start of the year to above 30%. This marks one of the strongest improvements in earnings momentum
across the sectors, reflecting a significantly more constructive profit outlook than investors faced just a few quarters ago.
Despite this recovery, relative performance has been less consistent and has recently softened after a strong rally earlier in the year. A disconnect has therefore emerged between the improving fundamentals and recent price action. This is illustrated
below: sector earnings revisions (dark brown line) continue to trend higher and are now at their strongest levels since 2022, while rolling 12‑month returns (light beige line) have pulled back from recent highs. Historically, stronger revisions have often
been associated with better relative performance for the sector. The recent divergence suggests that investors remain cautious about the sustainability of the earnings recovery, leaving energy performance weaker than the underlying fundamentals
would typically imply.

3. Industrials
Earnings momentum remains solid, but sub‑sector performance is highly dispersed. Higher rate expectations, persistent inflation and elevated fuel costs have weighed on risk appetite, yet capex‑linked industrials exposed to electrification, power infrastructure,
automation, aerospace and defence have outpaced more services‑heavy and fuel‑intensive businesses. This “great divide” within US industrials reflects stronger fundamentals for structural capex beneficiaries than for traditional cyclicals more dependent on the economic
cycle.
US industrials’ earnings momentum has strengthened sharply this year. The earnings revision ratio climbed from negative levels early in the year to above 50% during the summer and remains robust at around 48%. However, rolling 12‑month
performance has weakened from nearly 40% in April to approximately 12%, showing that price momentum has not kept pace with the improving earnings backdrop.
A clear disconnect has therefore emerged between fundamentals and price action. Earnings revisions (in red) remain close to their strongest levels since 2021, while rolling 12‑month returns (in beige) have continued to decline. 
This divergence suggests that investor sentiment has become more cautious despite resilient earnings momentum, leaving US industrials’ performance weaker than the revisions backdrop would typically imply.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 01/10/2026.
The sector has recently suffered its second drawdown of more than 10% this year as higher rate expectations, persistent inflation and elevated fuel prices have weighed on risk appetite. The headline index is down both over the last week and month
and currently trades about 11% below its 52‑week high; yet it still shows a positive year‑to‑date return of 7.6%.
Notably, and as illustrated by the data in the table below, performance under the surface is highly uneven. Capital‑goods segments such as Construction & Engineering, Heavy Electrical Equipment, Agricultural & Farm Machinery and Construction
Machinery retain strong year‑to‑date gains of roughly 30–50%, despite weaker returns over the last one to three months and sizeable gaps versus their 52‑week highs, suggesting markets still price in robust capex, reshoring and infrastructure trends.
By contrast, more services‑heavy or fuel‑intensive areas such as Research & Consulting Services, Data Processing & Outsourced Services, Passenger Ground Transportation and Environmental & Facilities Services show double‑digit negative
year‑to‑date returns and are 30% or more below their 52‑week peaks, while Cargo Ground Transportation and Air Freight & Logistics have also lagged amid concerns over trade volumes and input‑cost inflation. 
Overall, the combination of higher rate expectations, sticky inflation and elevated fuel prices has amplified dispersion across S&P 500 industrial sub‑sectors, supporting capex‑linked manufacturing pockets while penalising more services‑heavy and
fuel‑intensive businesses.

Despite a strong earnings backdrop, valuations for S&P 500 industrials have already started to de-rate. The 12-month forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio peaked above 26-27x earlier in 2026 but has now compressed to about 22.6x, only slightly above
its five-year average of 21.1x.
In other words, the market has moved from paying a historically rich multiple for the sector to assigning only a modest premium, even as earnings revisions remain close to their strongest levels since 2021. While valuations have already adjusted
meaningfully, investors appear to be demanding a higher risk premium in response to higher rate expectations, persistent inflation concerns and elevated fuel costs.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 01/10/2026.
US manufacturing data corroborate the recent improvement in the industrial backdrop.  In 2026, the ISM Manufacturing Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI) has stayed in expansion territory every month, with mid‑year readings around 55–56 (55.6 in
July and 54.6 in August), well above the sub‑50 levels seen through most of 2023–24. 
This suggests that, at the margin, demand for manufactured goods is firming rather than weakening, even as valuations for industrials have de‑rated and price performance has softened, reinforcing the message that investor risk appetite is lagging
the underlying macro momentum.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 01/10/2026.
The traditional view of US industrials as a single cyclical trade is becoming increasingly outdated. Beneath the surface, a clear divide is emerging between structural capex beneficiaries and more traditional industrial cyclicals. 
Companies exposed to electrification, grid infrastructure, AI‑related power demand, industrial automation, aerospace and defence are benefiting from powerful long‑term drivers and continue to post exceptionally strong earnings revisions. By
contrast, businesses tied to freight, railroads, logistics, building services and broad manufacturing activity remain more dependent on Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI) trends, trade flows and interest‑rate sensitivity, with a lower earnings revision
ratio (ERR) of around 54%.
While this fundamental divergence has not yet translated into a major performance gap since the April lows, recent price action suggests investors are beginning to differentiate between the two groups. Since late July, the Electrification/AI capex
basket has clearly outperformed both the broader industrials sector (XLI) and the Traditional Manufacturing, Freight & Logistics basket, reflecting growing investor preference for companies benefiting from multi‑year spending on power
infrastructure, data centres, defence and automation.
The most notable observation is that many structural winners continue to report rising backlogs, accelerating orders and strong earnings revisions despite recent share‑price weakness. This suggests that the market has focused largely on higher rates
and valuation compression while underappreciating the strength of underlying demand.
Within industrials therefore, the more interesting fundamental story lies less in the broad sector and more in the subgroup exposed to electrification, power infrastructure, automation, aerospace and defence. Fundamentals between these areas and
traditional cyclicals are already diverging, and recent performance suggests the market is only beginning to recognise that distinction.

The stock screen focuses on the subgroup of industrials most exposed to electrification, AI infrastructure, automation, power equipment and aerospace & defence, areas that currently show one of the strongest combinations of earnings revisions and
structural demand trends within the sector. To isolate names with the most attractive blend of fundamental and price‑momentum characteristics, the screening comprises those that currently achieve a Global Grade above 50 in our in‑house
quantitative screening process.
The results are dominated by companies benefiting from the physical deployment of AI and the broader infrastructure cycle. Bloom Energy, Vertiv, Quanta Services, Eaton, GE Vernova and Emerson provide direct exposure to power generation,
electrical infrastructure, grid upgrades and data‑centre investment. Howmet, RTX, Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman and TransDigm offer exposure to aerospace and defence, where order books and backlogs remain near record levels.
Several names stand out because strong fundamentals have not yet been fully reflected in share prices. GE Vernova, Vertiv, Caterpillar, Lockheed Martin and Northrop Grumman continue to exhibit robust earnings revision momentum, with
expanding backlog visibility and supportive long‑term demand drivers and despite recent valuation compression. In many cases, higher bond yields have weighed more heavily on multiples than on underlying earnings expectations.
Taken together, the screen reinforces the conclusion that the opportunity within industrials is increasingly concentrated in companies linked to power, electrification, automation, aerospace and defence, rather than in traditional freight, logistics
and manufacturing businesses whose earnings remain more dependent on the economic cycle, and plays into the thesis that the next phase of market leadership could come from companies enabling AI deployment, grid modernisation and strategic
infrastructure investment; names which combine some of the strongest earnings momentum in the sector with long‑duration growth drivers and rising order visibility.

The Equity quantitative grades
Our equity quant process is one by which we rank and score the stocks within our equity universe according to various short-term (i.e.
EPS growth) long-term (i.e. quality) and positioning (i.e. technical) characteristics, and on relative and/or historical basis as
appropriate. The various scores make up a stock’s total or ‘global' grade’:Growth Grade: The Growth Grade is a proprietary formula
made up of earnings revision momentum, past earnings growth, earnings stability, and current and long-term earnings growth.  A
grade above 55 is considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45 and neutral between 55 and 45.
EPS Grade: The EPS Grade is a proprietary formula consisting of current and forward EPS growth, change and surprise data.  An EPS grade
above 60 or below 40 is considered predictive for future out/under performance.Sales Grade: The Sales Grade is a proprietary formula made
up of current and next year’s sales momentum, past sales growth, sales stability, and current and long-term sales growth. A grade above 55 is
considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45, and neutral between 55 and 45.
Value Grade: The Value Grade is a proprietary formula made up of estimated P/E, P/B, P/S and P/CF ratios. 40% of the grade is based on
historical values and 60% on current market data.  A grade above 55 suggests a stock is cheap, below 45 expensive, and neutral between 55
and 45.
Quality Grade: The Quality Grade is a proprietary formula that focuses on the balance sheet (i.e. change in accruals, change in free cash flows
and profitability). A grade above 55 suggests a healthy balance sheet.Credit Grade: The Credit Grade focusses on the passive side of the
balance sheet.  It is divided into three sub-components to assess both short and long-term solvency.  A grade above 55 suggests a strong
capital structure, while a grade below 45 suggests a weak one.
Money Flow Grade: The Money Flow Grade is a proprietary formula that gives the accumulation/distribution based on the volume flows of a
stock. A grade above 55 indicates good money flow and a grade below 45 suggests weak money flow.Smart Sentiment Grade: The Smart
Sentiment grade is a contrarian indicator based on investor positioning measures such as the days-to-cover ratio, the put-call ratio, and the
short-interest ratio.  A weak grade suggests ‘too much’ optimism.
Relative Strength (RS) Grade: The RS grade measures the price momentum of a stock over its 1-year price performance.
Global Grade: The Global Grade is a weighted average of the Growth, EPS Sales, Value, Quality, Credit, Money Flow and Smart Sentiment
Grades.

The Exposure Optimisation Model
The PTS Exposure Optimisation Model (EOM) is a proprietary model designed to give tactical signals on the prevailing short-term
regional equity market regime. The model is applied to each market on a mutually-exclusive basis, with output expressed by way of
exposure scores (US; Europe; Japan and EM); in each case an average of underlying factors scores with dynamic weighting applied
according to the prevailing structural market volatility regime. Exposure score ratings are: very bullish (80-100%); bullish (60-80%);
neutral (40-60%); bearish (20-40%); and very bearish (20-0%). Factors are as follows:
Regime: A combination of technical indicators, moving averages and a Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance (CPPI) strategy, the regime
score is designed to reflect short-term market momentum in the region;
Valuation: Built around the forward price-to-sales ratio, the dynamic valuation score is based on a calculation that is re-set on bull/bear
market regime changes and calibrated to facilitate regional comparison;
Economics: The economics score draws on real economy activity data (i.e. heavy truck sales and shipping traffic), economic signals from
financial markets (i.e. yield-curves) and medium-term economic indicators (i.e. PMIs and unemployment data); 
Flows: The flows score draws upon both active and passive weekly EPFR fund flows data giving us an indication of risk appetite going into the
week ahead; 
Market Sentiment: The market sentiment score is built around classic regional market risk indices (i.e. VIX; ARMS) as well as soft survey data
(i.e. U.of Michigan Consumer Sentiment). The scoring system is applied in a similar fashion at both regional and market level, while at sector
level we refer only to the Regime, Flows and Valuation factors.

Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator that measures the speed and change of price movements. It ranges from 0 to 100 and
is typically used to identify overbought or oversold conditions in a market. An RSI above 70 suggests that a stock may be overbought, while an
RSI below 30 indicates that it may be oversold.
The Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD): A trend-following momentum indicator that shows the relationship between two
moving averages. It is calculated by subtracting the 26-period Exponential Moving Average (EMA) from the 12- period EMA. The result is the
MACD line. A nine-day EMA of the MACD, called the signal line, is then plotted on top of the MACD line, which can function as a trigger for
buy or sell signals. When the MACD crosses above the signal line, it is typically considered a bullish signal, and when it crosses below, it is
considered bearish.
Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis: Ichimoku Cloud is a popular technical analysis tool used to identify potential trends and
support/resistance levels in financial markets. It consists of several components, including the Kumo (cloud), the Tenkan-sen (conversion
line), the Kijun-sen (base line), and Senkou Span (leading span).
