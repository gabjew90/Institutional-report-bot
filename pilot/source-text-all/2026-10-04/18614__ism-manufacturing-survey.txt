ISM Manufacturing Index Fell in 
September; Remains in Expansion
Headlines 
 
The Institute for Supply Management (ISM) Manufacturing Purchasing 
Managers Index (PMI) fell modestly in September 2026, a second 
consecutive monthly decline. 
 
 
The index remained above the 50 mark that indicates expansion for the 
ninth straight month. 
 
 
Manufacturing hiring was a bright spot in the September 2026 report, rising 
to near the four-year high reached in July. 
 
 
Commodity prices spiked in September 2026 after trending downward 
from oil price-induced highs since May. 
 
 
New orders rose while production and backlog of orders fell in September 
2026, all supporting the same narrative of solid demand but rising cost 
pressures. 
 
 
Construction spending rose 0.9% in August.
Details
The ISM Manufacturing PMI fell for a second 
consecutive month in September 2026, posting a 
result of 54.5 (see Figure 1). U.S. manufacturing has 
rebounded strongly in 2026 after enduring outright 
contraction for most of the three years prior. 
September’s decline was small, with only a 0.1 index 
point loss for the month. 
 
The employment and new orders components both 
increased, consistent with ongoing demand growth. 
But prices paid and current production fell, marking 
the reality that meeting ongoing demand continues 
to require difficult planning decisions. 
The ISM Manufacturing PMI is a diffusion index that 
measures the net share of manufacturers reporting 
improving versus deteriorating conditions across a 
range of business activities. Readings above 50 
indicate expansion in manufacturing activity. 
September’s reading of 54.5 marked the ninth 
consecutive 
month 
above 
the 
expansionary 
threshold of 50. Four of the five components used to 
calculate the overall index were above 50 in 
September, with only inventories in contraction. 
Three 
rose 
over 
the 
month--new 
orders, 
employment, 
and 
supplier 
deliveries--while 
production and inventories fell.

Over the past several years policy developments 
have heavily influenced manufacturing activity. 
More recently, however, firms appear to be 
responding increasingly to underlying supply and 
demand fundamentals. Higher interest rates will 
weigh on demand growth over the coming year. 
After raising the federal funds rate by 25 basis points 
in September, PNC expects the Federal Open 
Market Committee to raise the rate by an additional 
50 basis points through the first quarter of 2027. This 
would take the rate to a range of 4.25% to 4.50% by 
early spring. 
 
New orders remain strong, according to the ISM 
Manufacturing survey, rising to 55.3 in September 
from 53.7 in August. The new orders component has 
been above 50 every month in 2026. At the same 
time elevated input costs and expected moderation 
in demand growth could limit firms' willingness to 
expand capacity. There is an element of catch-up 
at play, though, with hesitancy across the U.S. 
manufacturing base given the past two years’ tariff 
policy uncertainty; this is illustrated by persistent 
strength in the survey’s backlog of orders sub-index 
over the past year. 
 
Between the conflicting influences of past under-
expansion, current growth and the potential for a 
future slowdown, U.S. manufacturers have to walk a 
very fine line in balancing supply and demand over 
the next year. 
 
The ISM Manufacturing survey reported that hiring 
in the sector continued its upward trend in 
September. The Employment component of the 
survey rose to 52.7 for the month, retaking most of 
August’s decline to 51.2. The employment sub-index 
has posted results above the breakeven level of 50 
for three consecutive months. This trend aligns with 
Bureau of Labor Statistics employment data 
showing 
seasonally 
adjusted 
manufacturing 
employment rising for three consecutive months 
through August, and it now sits only 0.2 point below 
its March 2025, pre-“Liberation Day” level (see 
Figure 2). 
 
Construction spending rose 0.9% in August from 
July, according to the Census Bureau. Private 
residential 
spending 
rose 
1.1%, 
despite 
high 
mortgage 
rates, 
while 
private 
nonresidential 
spending rose 1.0%. Public spending rose 0.2%. Data 
centers remain a key source of strength in 
construction spending.
