Steady path to tighter Fed policy 
The AI capex boom, which is providing significant momentum to the US and global econ-
omy, and high energy prices are driving the current global bond sell-off. Markets are grap-
pling with two tasks: recalibrating their estimate of the near-term neutral rate and pricing 
a restrictive stance sufficient to return inflation to target. This process is volatile, with 
yields moving sharply higher. In the US, financial markets currently price about 90bp of 
Fed tightening over the coming year, which appears reasonable to us. While inflation re-
mains elevated, the profile does not suggest that there is a need for an aggressive front-
loading of hikes, but rather as steady, measured sequence of policy adjustments.  
Over in China, the government rolled out new measures to support home sales and infra-
structure investment. We expect faster fiscal execution in Q4 and these targeted 
measures to provide support, but not to lift domestic demand sustainably. We therefore 
remain neutral on China equity, with IT and financials as our preferred sectors. 
Finally, in the equities space, banks will be closely watched during the upcoming Q3 earn-
ings season. Focus will be on trading revenues after recent warnings over slowing fixed in-
come trading activity. Issuance activity may also have slowed somewhat and, given the re-
cent widening in credit spreads, loan-loss provisions will be in focus. Apart from banks, 
energy should deliver on sky-high expectations, given widening refining margins. Lastly, AI 
capex guidance will remain a key topic, while hyperscalers will start to report only by the 
end of the month.

US macro 
Inflation argues for steady tightening 
Financial markets expect about 90bps of Fed tightening over the coming year, reflect-
ing higher energy prices, an improving labour market and likely a higher near-term 
neutral rate. We agree rates must rise but see December as the likeliest time for the 
next increase. Headline inflation may reach around 4% by year end, while core 
measures are running at around 2.5%. Other gauges suggest underlying pressures 
have eased: the rise in core inflation since the end of last year has come mainly from 
acyclical components, driven in turn by tariffs, AI-related supply chain price pressures 
and strong pricing power. Looking ahead, tariff-induced inflation should cool, but 
stronger demand and energy pass-through will keep pressure on prices.  
 
Investors have marked up their expectations for interest rates after the Fed’s September hike, 
Chair Warsh’s hawkish remarks, the rise in energy prices and strong economic data. We agree 
that rates will need to go higher, but inflation data do not, in our view, warrant a rapid hiking 
cycle. December remains the likeliest date for the next move. Markets now expect about 90 
basis points of tightening over the coming year –a reasonable adjustment given a probably 
higher near-term neutral rate, an improving labour market and inflation that remains too high. 
The rest of this note examines the inflation picture in more detail. 
 
Headline inflation is plainly too high. Elevated energy prices are likely to push annual inflation 
to around 4% by year end (Exhibit 1). Over the past 6 to 12 months, core CPI and PCE inflation 
have run at annualised rates of 2.4% to 3%. Our new measure of “instantaneous” inflation, 
which gives greater weight to recent readings (see appendix for more details), puts both 
measures of core inflation a little above 2.5% (Exhibit 2).  
 
 
Central banks focus on core measures because they strip out volatile food and energy 
prices and offer a better guide to future headline inflation. They also consult other 
measures, which use statistical techniques to get at underlying price pressures. One is 
the trimmed mean: each month, it removes the largest price rises and falls from the bas-
ket before calculating the aggregate. Exhibit 3 compares an average of these measures 
with core PCE inflation. The chart shows that core PCE has risen since the end of last year, 
while other measures of underlying inflation have continued to fall, albeit from higher lev-
els. They now stand at levels that have previously been consistent with 2% core PCE

The distinction between cyclical and acyclical inflation helps explain the gap. The San 
Francisco Fed classifies items in the PCE consumer basket according to how closely their 
prices move with the business cycle. Its measure suggests that acyclical components ac-
count for all, and slightly more, of the rise in core inflation since the end of last year (Exhibit 
4). Our measure of core cyclical services inflation, which strips out energy, housing and 
acyclical items, shows little sign of pressure from the cycle. If anything, it has been falling, 
in line with weaker wage inflation and the delayed effects of last year’s cooler economy 
(Exhibits 5 and 6). The rise in acyclical inflation, by contrast, probably reflects tariffs, price 
pressures in the AI supply chain and the pricing power that strong demand has given firms.  
 
 
 
Looking ahead, some of these acyclical pressures should ease as the effect of tariffs on 
goods prices fades. Our instantaneous measure of core PCE goods inflation has fallen to 
1%, from 4% earlier this year (Exhibit 7). Cyclical inflation, however, is likely to rise as 
growth remains strong and the output gap appears positive and widening (Exhibit 6). 
Higher energy prices may also feed through to other parts of the basket, including food.  
 
Taken together, these forces suggest that the Fed will struggle to bring inflation back to 
2% without pushing rates into restrictive territory. This does not require, however, a rapid 
tightening cycle, with consecutive or half-point increases. We expect the Fed to move grad-
ually but steadily, allowing time to assess the data and where the neutral rate lies.

Appendix: Instantaneous inflation – What is it and why is it useful?  
The concept of instantaneous inflation was introduced by Professor Jan Eeckhout (2023), 
who argued against the backward-looking bias inherent to the year-over-year (YoY) infla-
tion rate. The latter is calculated as the growth rate of a given price index over the period 
of one year; this calculation is mathematically equivalent to the unweighted geometric 
mean of the annualised month-over-month inflation rates. This characterisation explains 
why, in periods of strong price swings (such as those following the recent energy shock), 
true price level changes emerge in the YoY rate with a six-month delay on average. On the 
other hand, annualised rates solely based on observations from recent months overweight 
noise and exaggerate short-term momentum. 
 
Instantaneous inflation is a geometric mean of monthly inflation rates, too, which, how-
ever, places more weight on recent observations. When noise in sample data is moderate, 
this metric provides a good balance between smoothing and reactivity, thus a better indi-
cation of upcoming price pressures. 
 
This dynamic is visible in the developments of the headline Consumer Price Index inflation 
(Exhibit A1). Since monthly readings can be quite volatile, instantaneous inflation avoids 
the extreme swings otherwise visible in the three-month annualised rate, yet it picks up 
changes in the inflation trend sooner than both the annual and six-month annualised 
rates.

China macro and equity 
New measures support growth at the margin 
Earlier this week, the Chinese government rolled out new measures to support home 
sales through a mortgage subsidy programme and infrastructure investment through 
the People’s Bank of China’s relending tools. We expect faster fiscal execution in Q4 
and these targeted measures to provide just enough support to reach the govern-
ment’s growth target, but not to lift domestic demand sustainably. We therefore re-
main neutral on China equity as the earnings outlook will likely remain relatively weak. 
Within China equity, the IT sector is well-supported by the AI boom while financials 
enjoy a stabilisation of the net interest margin, higher fee income and active policy 
support. 
 
On Tuesday, the Chinese government announced new measures to support domestic de-
mand, shortly after the State Council pledged stronger policy to meet the growth target of 
4.5%-5%. The measures include a new mortgage subsidy programme, and a lower interest 
rate and an expanded list of eligible sectors for targeted sectoral lending from the central 
bank. These targeted measures, together with stronger fiscal execution in Q4, should pro-
vide just enough support for the economy to reach the growth target. Growth momentum 
already improved in September as the PMIs picked up (Exhibit 1).  
 
The announcement is timely as we enter the golden week holiday period in China, usually 
an important season for primary market home sales. The new mortgage subsidy of 100bp 
targets new mortgages for first home purchases with a cap of 120sqm in size and RMB 
1.5 million in value. The measure aims to boost demand in lower tier cities where the 
housing market remains in oversupply. In 2024, the government took the opportunity right 
before the golden week to ease housing policy. Home sales picked up strongly, but the 
impact was short-lived (Exhibit 2). We expect the new measure to lend support to home 
sales at the margin, but it is not a game changer as it does not change households’ income 
and employment expectations. Moreover, the headwind from the phasing out of the pre-
sales model remains (more details in EM Weekly from September 15).    
 
 
The second measure targets domestic investment. The People’s Bank of China (PBoC) has 
lowered the interest rate on its Pledged Supplementary Lending (PSL) from 1.75% to 1.5% 
and expanded its target sectors to include the “six networks,” including water, power, 
computing, next-generation communication, underground utility and logistics networks.

The PSL allows the policy banks to borrow from the PBoC to relend to specific sectors. It 
was originally created to support the housing sector from 2015-19 and was used again in 
2023-24 (Exhibit 3). The PBoC’s relending programme for technological innovation and 
upgrades will also be expanded. Altogether this could boost investment over the next 12 
months, but near-term impact is likely small. 
 
In our view, the recently announced policy measures are not strong enough to fully offset 
the structural headwinds that the Chinese economy is facing. The K-shaped economy will 
likely persist. Exports, especially of the tech sector, will likely continue to rise but domestic 
demand, which accounts for a far greater part of the economy, will remain challenging. 
The K-shaped economy can also be seen from the supply side, with a stark contrast be-
tween the tech sector and the rest of manufacturing (Exhibit 4).  
 
What does it mean for China equity? Through the end of September, MSCI China was down 
by 12.7%, underperforming MSCI ACWI by 24.5%. The underperformance mainly reflects 
the lacklustre earnings trajectory where earnings estimates have been constantly revised 
downward since March (Exhibit 5). Earnings will likely remain muted given subdued do-
mestic demand. The market is not particularly cheap compared to its own history. With a 
forward P/E ratio of 10.2x, MSCI China trades only slightly below its 15-year average of 
10.8x. Hence, we maintain our neutral stance on the Chinese market at the index level.  
 
 
Given the macro backdrop, it is also important to be selective within the Chinese market. 
Two sectors stand out in our view: information technology (IT) and financials. China has 
emerged as a strong competitor to the US in artificial intelligence (AI), and an important 
player in the AI infrastructure supply chains. Strong demand for compute hardware, both 
domestically and abroad, has led to a sharp rise in the sector’s earnings. As global AI 
capex will likely rise further in 2027, the strong earnings momentum will likely be sus-
tained. Valuations have corrected notably after the July tech selloff and are now back to 
their historical average of about 21x, a reasonable level given the sector’s strong growth. 
 
China’s financial sector has delivered decent performance year to date, up by 7.2%, out-
performing the broad market by nearly 20%. This was mainly due to an improvement in 
major banks’ earnings outlook, which is less a reflection of broad economic health and 
more a product of net interest margin stabilisation after years of compression, a capital 
market-driven fee income recovery, reduced property drag, and active policy support. The 
sector currently trades at 6.0x forward P/E, in line with its historical average. Major banks’ 
dividend yield of around 4% is also attractive, compared to the 10-year Chinese govern-
ment bond yields at less than 1.7%.

US equities 
Q3 preview: Banks entering choppier waters 
The Q3 earnings season is starting in earnest on October 12, with consensus expect-
ing another bumper quarter. Banks take the lead with seven large names reporting in 
the first week. Focus will be on trading revenues after recent warnings over slowing 
fixed income trading activity. Issuance activity may also have slowed somewhat, and, 
given the recent rise in credit spreads, loan-loss provisions will be in focus. Apart from 
banks, energy should deliver on sky-high expectations, given widening refining mar-
gins. Lastly, AI capex guidance will remain a key topic, while hyperscalers will start to 
report only by the end of the month. 
 
The US earnings season is starting in earnest on October 12 and will ramp up over the 
following weeks. More than half of S&P 500 constituents will have reported after the week 
of October 26 (Exhibit 1). After an impressive second quarter, which delivered underlying 
EPS growth (ex one-offs) of 34%, a post-pandemic record (Exhibit 2). Consensus expecta-
tions for Q3 growth are only slightly below those levels. At 29%, expectations are even 
above the level at the beginning of the Q2 season, when the consensus was “only” expect-
ing 25% EPS growth.

One key driver of S&P 500 EPS growth in the second quarter was the energy sector, which 
saw its earnings rise by more than 150% yoy. Q3 consensus expectations are once again 
for more than 100% yoy growth (Exhibit 3). This is far above the levels suggested by oil 
price increases alone. Yet it appears achievable as sector earnings have been super-
charged by refining margins. In Q3, these may even have improved relative to Q2, as im-
plied by the widening spread between gas prices and oil prices (Exhibit 4).  
 
The sector which traditionally dominates the first weeks of reporting is financials, with 
seven of the largest US banks reporting in the first week of the season from October 12. 
2026 has so far been an outstanding year for banks, driven by earnings growth beyond 
interest-sensitive net interest income (Exhibit 5). Trading revenues, a strong pick-up in 
issuance volumes and solid investment banking activity have helped EPS growth to top 
20% in Q1 and Q2. Consensus expectations for Q3 are at a meagre 3.3%. This is partly a 
function of base effects, as EPS picked up strongly in Q3 2025, but also a result of fading 
support from non-interest sources of income. Banking CEOs have recently warned about 
disappointing fixed income trading in Q3, while equity trading is supposedly still strong. In 
addition, an increasing number of companies have shelved their IPO plans, citing unfa-
vourable market conditions. This may run deeper. The pickup in high-yield spreads, most 
notable in the CCC segment but spreading to the broader index, is a reflection of these 
increasingly difficult conditions (Exhibit 6). Beyond the earnings data, a focus in banking 
sector reports will thus also be on loan-loss provisions and the management assessment 
of borrower strength. In general, it appears as if banks are in a difficult spot. Higher yields 
usually start to weigh on credit quality, while lower yields will likely weigh on net interest 
income.  
 
 
In conclusion, Q3 may prove to be another bumper earnings season, with energy EPS likely 
to be supported by strong refining margins. On the other hand, banks may start showing 
signs of weakness following a series of strong quarterly results. Hyperscaler capex guid-
ance will be another focal point, yet there appear to be fewer concerns about potential 
downgrades than in Q2.

Energy prices have risen more than oil prices suggest 
• 
The crack spread is the difference between the price of refined fuels, such as 
gasoline or heating oil (diesel), compared to the crude oil used to produce them 
• 
This spread has surged to the highest level in decades, indicating that the supply 
of refined products is constrained relative to crude oil.  
• 
The shortage is primarily due to (1) refinery disruptions as a result of the wars in 
the Gulf and in Ukraine, (2) export restrictions for fear of domestic shortages and 
(3) depletion of inventories 
• 
Consequently, households and corporations face much higher energy prices than 
the crude oil price would suggest, making the task for central banks more com-
plicated
