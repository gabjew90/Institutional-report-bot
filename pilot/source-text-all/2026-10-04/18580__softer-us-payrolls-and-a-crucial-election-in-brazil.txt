US Week In Review 
The unemployment rate ticked up from 4.141% to 4.175% in September, and nonfarm payrolls 
rose by 29k, softer than expectations of 90k. In addition, the prior month was revised down to 
133k (from 162k), bringing down the 3-month moving average to 51k. The three-month run rate 
is still far above most measures of the breakeven pace and the unemployment rate still suggests 
full employment as the participation rate increased from 61.6% to 61.8%. The highest gains were 
in private education and health services (+20k), construction (+11k), and leisure and hospitality 
(+10k), while headline payrolls were dragged down by losses in government (-17k where -13k is 
local), information (-10k), professional services (-9k) and financial activities (-7k). Overall 9 of 14 
sectors recorded gains, although most were only single-digit increases.  
This data represents a bit of a normalization from last month, but the same story generally holds 
– the labor market is stable and not adding to any inflationary impulse. Wage growth was even 
lower than the typically tepid gains in the last year, rising only 13bps m/m. We continue to think 
the labor market is a bit shakier than the headline numbers suggest, but this data is unlikely to 
shift the broader decision-making calculus for the Fed as inflation remains the higher concern, 
but with wages lower and the jobs picture a bit less rosy, it certainly decreases the urgency to 
hike in October (and perhaps December, if inflation data cooperates).

Headline PCE rose 0.3% m/m in August, in line with expectations, while core PCE came in at 
0.2% m/m, below the 0.3% expected. The previous month’s readings for both were revised down 
significantly, lowering the y/y rates for headline PCE from 3.7% to 3.4% and for core PCE from 
3.3% to 3.0%. The last three core PCE prints now all round to 3.0%, and the revised monthly 
increases brought the 3m annualized rate for core to 2.05% y/y.  
Beyond inflation, personal income fell short of expectations at 0.2% versus 0.5% expected, while 
nominal personal spending bounced back, rising 0.9% on the month. In real terms, personal 
spending increased 0.6% m/m. However, the savings rate fell from 4.6% to 4.1%, its lowest level 
since mid-2022. Meanwhile, GDP growth for Q2 was revised up from 1.5% to 2.2%. Overall, the 
downward revisions and the softer than expected core PCE strengthen the case for the Fed to 
remain on hold at its October meeting, while also allowing policymakers to avoid raising rates 
days ahead of the midterm elections.

The Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index deteriorated in September, falling 6.7 points 
to 81.9, its lowest level since 2014. The main drag on the headline index was the Present Situation 
Index, which dropped 7.9 points to 109.3, its lowest level since 2020, while the Expectations Index 
declined 5.9 points to 63.6. The survey period ran from September 1 to 23, including the Fed’s 
rate hike and ongoing geopolitical tensions. Over the next six months, consumers expect both 
business conditions and the labor market to weaken and have become more pessimistic about 
the labor market. The labor market differential, which is the share saying jobs are plentiful minus 
the share saying jobs are hard to get, plunged from 4.2 to 1.7 in September, its lowest level since 
the pandemic.

The ISM Manufacturing Index edged down from 54.6 to 54.5 in September, below expectations 
of 55.0 but with the manufacturing sector expanding for the ninth consecutive month. New orders 
and the backlog of orders increased with their indexes rising 1.6 and 4.6 percentage points 
respectively, and customers’ inventories fell 1.2 percentage points to 41.6, all indicating resilient

costs, reversing the prior easing trend in cost pressures.

US Rates Strategy 
The US and indeed global fixed income selloff over the course of September was significant.  
While we have been looking for higher long-end yields all year, the extent to which the market 
has sold off is greater than even we expected.  That said, while we think broadly the levels in the 
long-end are fair, we do not see any near-term catalyst to stem the tide. Indeed, if anything we 
find the front-end of the curve more attractive, and frankly easier to quantify in regard to Fed 
pricing versus the myriad of challenges facing the long end.  
More specifically, we think the market pricing for 90bps of additional hikes (reached earlier this 
week) over the next 12 months is excessive, both in the light of the view from Natixis US 
Economics for one additional hike in December, and when one looks at past cycles.  2s-funds 
(another way of looking at what the market is pricing for future Fed action) has quickly risen to 
price in hikes and is approaching the peaks last seen in the 2015-2019 cycle, both leading into 
that cycle and after the small pause in 2017.  We would argue as we are not entering a full rate 
hike cycle (akin to 2004 and 2022, and less than 2015), and more likely a modest adjustment of 
policy rates (certainly no more than four hikes and in our view one), the front end has more than 
amply priced in hikes here and if anything is looking attractive.

LatAm Highlights 
  Brazil – Elections this Sunday   
Brazil’s general election will be held on October 4, with a second round on October 25 if no 
candidate secures more than 50% of the vote in the first round. Recent polls suggest that the 
presidential race remains concentrated between President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (Lula) and 
Flávio Bolsonaro. We conducted a short survey starting last week to assess policy and BRL 
expectations under different electoral outcomes. The responses suggest that participants see a 
near-term appreciation scenario for the BRL under a Bolsonaro victory, while associating a Lula 
win primarily with fiscal concerns and a weaker BRL. This is broadly consistent with our 
comprehensive note in Brazil – A crucial election, HERE, where we argue that Flávio Bolsonaro 
has a slightly higher probability of winning than President Lula and that Congress is likely to 
remain tilted toward the center/center-right. Read more on our Brazil – Election Survey Results, 
HERE.

  Colombia – Hikes resumed 
BanRep increased its policy rate by 25 bps to 12.25%, surprising the market. Four members voted 
for the 25 bps hike, one voted for a 50 bps increase, and two board members voted to pause. At 
the press conference, Finance Minister Gomez indicated, surprisingly, that he had voted for a 
hike. Normally, finance ministers, who are voting members of the board in Colombia, tend to be 
on the dovish side. This time around, Gomez might be part of the hawks! 
The press release cites the overall rise in inflation and inflation expectations as the primary 
reasons for the tightening: “Total inflation in August increased again, reaching 6.2% annually. The 
main pressures came from the food and regulated groups, with inflation rates at 6.1% and 6.8%, 
respectively. Service inflation picked up to 7.2% annually. Core inflation—excluding food and 
regulated items—increased to 6.1%, reaching its highest level over the past year. The 
materialization of the El Niño phenomenon could continue to exert upward pressure on food and 
regulated items. Economic analysts’ inflation expectations for December 2026, according to the 
September survey, recorded a new increase and stood at 6.8%, while those for a two-year horizon 
decreased to 4.0%. Expectations derived from the public debt market remain considerably above 
the target.” 
The press release also indicates that monetary tightening in some developed economies 
contributed to an environment that supports further hikes in Colombia. Finally, the economy

expanded strongly in Q2, by 3.4% y/y, but seems to have decelerated in Q3. This also supported

In the press conference with Governor Villar and Finance Minister Gomez, the two main 
messages, in our view, are that the board is committed to moderating inflation and that more hikes 
might be needed if—or when—El Niño pushes inflation higher. Second, there seems to be greater 
coordination between the government and the central bank. Specifically, the government seems 
committed to cutting spending and reining in the fiscal deficit, which should help BanRep’s efforts 
to fight inflation. 
However, for the government to cut spending, it first needs to pass the 2027 budget and then 
pass the so-called “Rescue Law.” Having said that, even after the spending cuts, the fiscal deficit 
would be very large at around 7% of GDP in 2026 and 2027. In other words, even with the
