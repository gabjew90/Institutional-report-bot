China GDP Tracking: A Real-Time Nowcasting Framework 
 
 
China’s official real GDP growth slowed to 4.3% yoy in Q2 from 5.0% yoy in Q1, raising 
investor concerns over whether full-year growth can still reach the official 4.5–5% target 
(Exhibit 1). Growth in the first half of 2026 averaged 4.7% and remained broadly on 
track relative to the growth target, and the July Politburo meeting sent more dovish 
signals compared to the April Politburo meeting. However, July and August activity data 
showed persistent softness, prompting us to lower our Q3 real GDP growth forecast to 
4.4% yoy from 4.6% yoy previously. Against this backdrop, the key questions for 
investors are whether growth can stay within the target range and whether renewed 
policy support will emerge if momentum weakens further. 
This uncertainty is compounded by the fact that GDP forecasting has become harder 
since the Covid pandemic. The mean absolute error of Bloomberg consensus forecasts 
for quarterly GDP growth over the past few years has been roughly three times larger 
than in the pre-pandemic period (Exhibit 2). 
In our view, two factors help explain the deterioration in forecast accuracy. First, the 
Covid shock and subsequent reopening introduced unusually large swings and volatility 
into year-over-year growth rates, especially in contrast to the stable official real GDP 
readings historically. Second, the policy reaction function and policy transmission have 
shifted over time. Before Covid, cyclical easing during economic downturns tended to be 
sizable and its impact on growth was more immediate. Post-Covid, policy easing has 
become more measured and the impact of such easing on growth seems more muted 
and fleeting. Therefore, forecasts relying on historical patterns may have introduced 
larger forecasting errors in recent years.  
 
These forecasting challenges make a more systematic real-time tracking framework 
useful. In the US, models such as the Atlanta Fed’s GDPNow and the New York Fed Staff 
Nowcast update current-quarter GDP estimates as new macro data arrive, translating 
high-frequency indicators into a timely reading on quarterly GDP growth. GDPNow 
follows a bottom-up expenditure-based national-accounts approach, mapping source 
data such as retail sales, housing starts, construction spending and trade data into GDP 
expenditure components. Our US Economics team’s GDP tracking adopts a similar 
approach. By contrast, the New York Fed Staff Nowcast uses a top-down dynamic factor

The same approach is hard to replicate in China. Unlike major economies such as the US, 
the Euro area and Japan, which publish quarterly GDP levels and growth rates by major 
expenditure component, China does not publish quarterly GDP by expenditure 
component and only reports the contributions of final consumption, gross capital 
formation and net exports to headline GDP growth. We therefore construct a GDP 
nowcasting model that combines soft survey data, trade data, and production-side 
output indicators to assess underlying growth momentum and identify the potential 
window for incremental policy easing1. 
Our GDP Nowcasting Model 
We estimate the GDP nowcasting model using more recent data in which the relationship 
between activity indicators and GDP was less distorted by pandemic-related shocks: 
2017Q1-2019Q4 and 2023Q2-2026Q2, excluding the Covid and reopening period from 
2020Q1 to 2023Q1.2 
We construct the GDP nowcasting model around three separate models, each 
reflecting the information set available at different points in the quarter. 
The PMI signal model provides the earliest read on monthly activity.3 We regress 
current-quarter GDP growth on official manufacturing PMI and non-manufacturing PMI, 
using PMI readings to provide the initial estimate of within-quarter momentum before 
hard activity indicators become available. Exhibit 3 suggests the model performed 
better before Covid, when GDP growth was smoother and its relationship with survey 
indicators was more stable. 
As the data flow broadens, we then incorporate a bridge indicator model that 
combines timely soft and hard indicators.4 Specifically, we relate GDP growth to 
non-manufacturing PMI, real export growth, auto output growth and 
one-quarter-lagged GDP growth.5 This model is used once the relevant monthly 
indicators for the first month of the quarter are released. This model helps capture 
growth momentum across key sectors and gauge the implications for headline GDP 
growth. 
Finally, once monthly activity indicators are available mid-month, we use a 
production-side model that builds headline GDP growth from sectoral activity. We

Model Evaluation: Comparing Real-Time Accuracy 
We evaluate the real-time in-sample performance of the three models using root mean 
squared error (RMSE), where lower values indicate smaller forecast errors and higher 
accuracy (Exhibit 4). Four takeaways stand out. First, early survey indicators provide a 
useful initial signal before hard activity data are available. Second, forecast accuracy 
improves as the quarter progresses, as subsequent releases—particularly third-month 
indicators given stronger quarter-end residual seasonality—materially improve the 
estimate. Third, the framework outperforms a simple AR (1) benchmark, suggesting that 
incorporating real-time data adds forecasting value. Finally, an equal-weighted average 
of the three models performs better than any single specification by reducing 
idiosyncratic noise and incorporating signals from both survey and hard data.

forward the previous quarter’s growth rates for these two sectors, which together account for around 12% of 
nominal GDP currently.

Based on the RMSE results, we use an equal-weighted model average to track GDP 
growth (Exhibit 5). Early in the second month of each quarter, the nowcast relies on the 
PMI model, as survey data provide the main within-quarter signal. As monthly hard data 
such as trade and auto production become available, we move to a two-model average 
of the PMI and bridge indicator models. After first-month activity indicators are released 
around the 15th of the second month, the nowcast shifts to an equal-weighted average 
of all three models for the rest of the nowcasting window, with each model updated as 
its underlying data are released. 
 
Our View on 2026 Q3 GDP 
Exhibit 6 shows how our China GDP nowcasting model would have performed in recent 
quarters. Because this exercise is based on backward-looking in-sample analysis rather 
than real-time nowcasts produced at the time, the results should be viewed as indicative 
of the model’s signal extraction rather than as a definitive historical track record. That 
said, the framework has generally delivered a more directionally accurate signal than 
consensus forecasts around GDP releases: when GDP outperformed consensus 
expectations, the tracker was also above consensus; when GDP missed expectations, the 
tracker was below consensus.
