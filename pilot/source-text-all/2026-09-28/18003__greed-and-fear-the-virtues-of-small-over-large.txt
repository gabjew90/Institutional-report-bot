The virtues of small over large 
Mumbai 
The AI story has received a new boost of late with the perceived most successful consumer application yet.  
GREED & fear refers to Meta’s Muse which will allow for a further curated offering for advertisers so long as 
people are prepared to give the data to a central data base which in Meta’s case they are. Still the perceived 
success of Muse, which has led to a 21% increase in Meta’s share price since Muse was launched on 8 
September (see Exhibit 1), is not an endorsement of the massive capex pouring into data center construction. 
Indeed, it is worth recalling that Meta reportedly initiated negotiations with Anthropic in July on leasing up to 
US$10bn of its data center computing capacity over two years (see New York Times article: “Meta in Talks to 
Lease Computing Power to Anthropic in Potential $10 Billion Deal”, 17 July 2026). This came after Anthropic 
had already signed a US$45bn three-year contract with Elon Musk’s SpaceX for computing power as well as 
multibillion dollar massive compute guarantees with Amazon and Google’s cloud businesses.  
Exhibit 1: Meta Platforms' share price 
  
Source: Bloomberg 
 
This raises the issue of whether it makes sense for Anthropic to have committed to such costly compute. In 
this respect, the recent comments by Messieurs Amodei, Altman and Musk that they want to slow down AI, 
makes GREED & fear suspicious that they are now seeing a slowdown in revenue in the core corporate market 
as the reaction against “tokenmaxxing” has set in. In this respect, it is also interesting that Meta Chairman 
Mark Zuckerberg last week rejected head on the calls for such an industry-wide slowdown. This call for a 
slowdown is most surprising in Anthropic’s case as it was preparing for an IPO. For such reasons the suspicion 
of a slowdown in revenue growth is further suggested by the news last Friday that Anthropic’s IPO has now 
been delayed to November (see Wall Street Journal article: “Anthropic Shifts Planned IPO to November”, 18 
September 2026).

On that point, while in India’s financial capital this week, GREED & fear came across a just published research 
report which, by monitoring activity across Anthropic servers, estimates that the server utilisation has declined 
by 26% since the peak levels recorded in January this year. This is based on a so-called congestion index 
which tracks how busy the servers were, estimated from how long requests waited in the queue, expressed 
relative to how busy they were at the peak in January - February 2026. Clearly, if the servers are under utilised 
it may mean less revenue and, therefore, can be a sign of overbuilt capacity. For those interested GREED & 
fear shows below the table from the report with the relevant data (see Exhibit 2 and Swerve Research 
Technologies report: “Hyperscaler Overbuild, On-Premise SLM Substitution, and the Anthropic Case Study”, 
September 2026).

GREED & fear is no tech expert. But this finding, if correct, is extremely interesting to GREED & fear as well as 
being highly topical. The same research report and another article GREED & fear also recommends published 
last month (see Klement on Investing article: “If this is true, the hyperscalers are toast” by Joachim Klement, 
19 August 2026) both reference an academic study conducted by a team from Stanford University which was 
first published last November and most recently revised on 6 September (“Intelligence per Watt: Measuring 
Intelligence Efficiency of Local AI” by Jon Saad-Falcon, Avanika Narayan and other researchers, Stanford 
University & Together AI, 6 September 2026). This study compared the performance of small language models 
(SLMs), which is the model which in GREED & fear’s view will be increasingly adopted by corporates, with the 
performance of large language models (LLMs) run in data centers. The findings are that “SLMs” are catching 
up fast in terms of performance with energy and compute costs that are between 50% and 85% lower. Now it 
is true that LLMs are, for now at least, still way ahead in agentic AI applications. But that may not last forever 
while applications that can be run on personal computers are vastly cheaper as regards the cost of energy.  
The above also means there may be less need for expensive chips than previously assumed (i.e. CPUs rather 
than GPUs).  
So, the message from the Stanford study and the above related reports is that enterprise system is 
decentralizing. That suggests companies, in the reaction against tokenmaxxing, are restructuring AI budgets 
away from centralized “cloud endpoints” towards so-called “on premises” SLMs.

While banks already have to be “on premises” for obvious data security reasons, it would seem to GREED & 
fear that every company will also want to be “on-prem” for the same data security reasons, if they can afford 
it.  For such reasons hyperscaler-funded data centers risk becoming the most “capital-destructive stranded 
assets” in the history of technology, a judgment which Zuckerburg and Musk also may seemingly share given 
their demonstrated desire to rent out compute capacity.  
None of this means that AI is not here to stay, nor that the demand for compute will keep rising. But it does 
mean that the AI story will be due for a big reset at some point in the not so distant future. Are the four 
hyperscalers really going to spend US$990bn on capex in 2027 which is the current estimate of the 
consensus? (see Exhibit 3) GREED & fear severely doubts it.

A report published by Jefferies’ India office earlier this month, titled India's New Industrial Revolution, features 
six growth areas combining long-term government support and growing private sector participation. They are 
space, semiconductors, solar manufacturing, data centers, electronics and aerospace (see Jefferies research 
India’s New Industrial Revolution, 9 September 2026).  
The report went viral in terms of the attention garnered on social media, not least because the government 
highlighted it (see News on AIR article: “India’s New Industrial Revolution driven by domestic market, private 
participation & policy support, says Jefferies”, 10 September 2026). Government policy support is visible in 
these emerging sectors through the likes of incentive schemes, tax holidays and localisation requirements.  
The decision to push advanced manufacturing should be seen in the context of the undoubted threat to the 
Indian IT services sector posed by AI, even though this is not yet showing up in job losses. In the most recent 
earnings season, the ten biggest quoted IT services companies actually reported adding 6,200 jobs. If this is 
not a big number, it is better than an outright decline. Meanwhile, employment at multinationals’ global 
capability centres (GCCs) still appears to be growing as reflected in rising demand for office space. Net 
absorption of office space has risen from 21m sf in 2021 to a record 61m sf in 2025 and a forecast 60m sf 
this calendar year, according to Jefferies’ India office (see Exhibit 4). India has already added 111 greenfield 
GCCs so far in 2026, crossing the 100-centre mark in just eight months, according to a report by UnearthIQ 
last Friday (see BusinessLine article: “India attracts more than 100 global capability centres in just 8 months of 
2026”, 18 September 2026). These 111 GCCs are expected to create 70,000-75,000 jobs.

Meanwhile, more days in India convinces GREED & fear that the best structural growth story top down remains 
energy in terms of the private sector build out of electrification via the expansion of renewable energy, 
primarily in the form of solar. China has led the way here with the economies of scale created by its grid build

Solar is already cheaper than coal in China thanks to battery storage technology. If that happens in India, the 
resulting likely collapse in the cost of power can only be a major positive for the country’s growing 
manufacturing ambitions. Meanwhile, an interesting insight from the abovementioned Jefferies report is that, 
in spite of the common perception that India has failed in manufacturing relative to services, India is second 
only to China globally in many traditional areas of manufacturing, be it production of crude steel, cement, 
ammonia, mobile phones or solar modules. But equally clearly, India remains far behind China in all these 
areas. For example, China produced 960.8m tonnes (Mt) of crude steel and 1,700 Mt of cement last year, 
compared with 164.9 Mt of crude steel and 470 Mt of cement in India.   
Meanwhile, returning to the subject of energy, a presentation which generated a lot of attention at last week’s 
Jefferies India Forum was that made by Hitachi Energy India. The company’s CEO, Venu Nuguri, projected 
total final energy demand to increase by 50% and electricity demand by 100% to 3,365 TWh by 2035. As a 
result, the share of electricity in total final energy demand is expected to rise from 19% in 2023 to 25% in 2035 
on the back of a surge in renewables and improved “storage and smart demand management”.  
Hitachi Energy, given its expertise in transmission, is a picks and shovels play on this theme. That is 
understood by the market given that it is trading at 71x 12-month forward earnings having rallied by 68% year 
to date (see Exhibits 6 and 7). Still at a time when India has been out of favour on the reverse AI perception, it 
is worth reminding non-specialists that India continues to enjoy its own structural growth story which will 
prove resilient at a time when the current near all-consuming focus on AI diminishes.

The essence of his argument is that, after noting that Kevin Warsh’s “inconsistent and imprecise comments” 
about his sensitivity to “data dependence” had “done him no favours”, the Fed chairman in his renewed 
articulated concerns about inflation has decided to take away the 75bp of easing which occurred in 2025 at a 
time when the Fed has now not met its 2% inflation target for more than five years. Suddenly there is no more 
talk that a central bank should look through oil price spikes triggered by supply disruptions on the view that 
they are temporary. Instead the Fed chairman is now focusing on the squeeze on regular Americans caused 
by rising prices as reflected in the fact that real income growth has turned negative as indicated in aggregate 
wage data. This is clearly not reflected in aggregate consumption data which is supported by the discretionary 
spending of the top 10% of Americans households benefitting most from the stock market. Thus, US real 
compensation of employees, deflated by PCE inflation, declined by 0.2% YoY in July while real personal 
consumption was up 2.1% YoY (see Exhibit 14). Compensation of employees is defined by the Bureau of

Hold - Describes securities that we expect to provide a total return (price appreciation plus yield) of plus 15% or minus 10% within a 12-month period.

below $10, the expected total return (price appreciation plus yield) is plus or minus 20% within a 12-month period. For Underperform rated securities with an
average security price consistently below $10, the expected total return (price appreciation plus yield) is minus 20% or less within a 12-month period.
