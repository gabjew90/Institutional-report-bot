The anatomy of oil product markets: It all points to greater tightness 
 
◼ 
From tight to critical: In this week’s CCA, we examine the ever-growing evidence of 
global oil-product tightness, connecting infrastructure disruptions, refinery 
outages, freight inflation and strengthening physical prices with the deterioration in 
inventories, exports and refinery supply. 
◼ 
Infrastructure losses deepen the squeeze: The uncertain restart of Saudi Arabia’s 
7 mb/d East-West Pipeline, with repairs potentially taking up to eight weeks, has 
compounded Russian refinery outages of around 4.6 mb/d. Russian product exports 
are already down 1.1 mb/d year to date, leaving both crude and product markets 
increasingly stressed.  
◼ 
The screen price understates refiners’ crude costs: Dated Brent has risen to 
almost $127/bbl versus roughly $105/bbl for front-month Brent, lifting the physical 
premium above $20/bbl from only $3.5/bbl in August. Immediate barrels are 
therefore substantially more expensive than headline futures suggest, exposing 
acute prompt-market scarcity. 
◼ 
The inventory buffer is gone: Total US crude stocks, including the SPR, have fallen 
to their lowest level since 1984. The market consequently has far less capacity to 
absorb another supply shock.  
◼ 
Red Sea disruption is becoming more structural: Monthly traffic through Bab el-
Mandeb and Suez has fallen from around 2,500 vessels to approximately 1,000, 
forcing more cargoes around the Cape of Good Hope. The additional 10 to 14 days 
at sea raise fuel, insurance and crew costs while tying up scarce tanker capacity. 
◼ 
VLCC freights costs have increased more than tenfold: The cost of transporting 
crude from Ras Tanura to Ningbo has surged from around $4.5 million to nearly $63 
million. Freight has consequently risen from approximately 5% to 25% of the cargo’s 
value, materially increasing the cost of moving marginal barrels.  
◼ 
Higher freight ultimately reaches product prices: Refiners pay the delivered cost 
of crude, including freight, rather than the headline futures price. As transport costs 
lift the landed feedstock cost from roughly $102 to as much as $125/bbl, refiners 
must recover part of that increase through higher product, but freight remains only 
one component of exceptional product cracks. 
◼ 
Refinery supply is falling despite strong margins: Global refinery supply is 
running near 78 mb/d, approximately 6 mb/d below the seasonal median and almost 
8 mb/d lower year on year. The shortfall is concentrated in Russia, Asia-Pacific and 
the Middle East, limiting the supply response that elevated cracks would normally 
generate. 
◼ 
Product exports are declining across the barrel: Global gasoline exports are down 
24% year on year, diesel exports 17%, jet fuel exports 24% and naphtha exports 36%. 
With fewer replacement barrels available, regional shortages are becoming harder 
and more expensive to resolve. 
◼ 
Low inventories and rising outages complete the tightness story: OECD gasoline 
and diesel stocks remain below seasonal norms, while global refinery outages have 
reached around 12 mb/d and unplanned outages are more than double last year’s 
level. High utilization may support near-term output but sustained hard running also 
raises the likelihood of further failures and maintenance, reinforcing the product 
squeeze.

Oil products: from tight to critical 
This week’s CCA examines the growing evidence of tightening global oil product markets. We assess 
the implications of the recent East-West pipeline disruption, worsening Russian refinery outages, and 
the increasing disconnect between crude oil and diesel markets as exceptional product tightness 
drives product prices higher. We also explore the sharp rise in freight rates, now up by an order of 
magnitude of tenfold in some key routes, and how these costs are increasingly feeding through to end-
user prices. In addition, we highlight how physical market conditions continue to strengthen, with spot 
prices once again accelerating ahead of nearby futures. Finally, through a series of charts, we present 
the anatomy of product market tightness, showing declining inventories, weaker exports, lower 
refinery supply, and rising refinery outages, all pointing to an increasingly constrained global market. 
Refining margins are extremely high reflecting the need for products. 
Product tightness intensifies as pipeline disruptions and Russian outages bite 
Brent crude recently climbed to almost $110/bbl as geopolitical risks intensified following attacks on 
Saudi energy infrastructure on September 10. As 10-year Treasury yields breached 5%, Trump's 
priorities increasingly shifted from geopolitical pressure to economic stability and attempted to talk 
down the oil prices implying there was progress being made to end the war. With oil prices contributing 
to inflation fears and higher borrowing costs, the administration became more focused on talking 
down energy markets and reinforcing expectations that supply disruptions would prove temporary. 
Crude prices dropped to $104/bbl yet dated Brent and product cracks remain extremely elevated and 
continue to tell the true story. 
When the East-West pipeline comes back online is still uncertain, and we have seen the postponement 
of regional talks on Hormuz shipping security. The closure of Saudi Arabia's 7 mb/d East-West pipeline 
has heightened concerns that supply disruptions could broaden beyond current shipping constraints. 
While significant uncertainty remains around both the scale and duration of lost supply, recent history 
suggests caution in drawing conclusions. The April attack on the same pipeline reduced flows by 
around 0.7 mb/d and was repaired within days. Nevertheless, early assessments indicate the latest 
damage may be more extensive, with some estimates suggesting repairs could take up to eight weeks 
and potentially affect residual export capacity at Yanbu. 
Saudi export flows had been recovering prior to the attacks. Although August exports fell sharply as 
Yanbu loadings collapsed, recent vessel movements suggested that exports had largely returned to 
June levels through a combination of rerouting and higher utilisation of eastern export routes. 
Even so, Red Sea exports remain substantially impaired. Volumes remain approximately 3.3 mb/d 
below June levels, with increased use of Suez and the SUMED pipeline offsetting only a fraction of the 
losses. Most of the adjustment has instead come through higher exports from Gulf coast terminals, 
where shipments have increased by roughly 3.3 mb/d since June. 
Taken together, the attacks underscore a notable escalation in the conflict and highlight the 
vulnerability of regional energy infrastructure. As a result, the likelihood of a severe supply shock has 
increased. Under a scenario where military tensions continue to intensify and disruptions become 
prolonged; Brent prices could rise well beyond current levels and potentially exceed $150/bbl (as we 
discussed here). 
Russian refinery outages currently remain at 4.6mb/d, keeping refined product exports down 1.1mb/d 
year-to-date, while Russia crude exports declined by just over 1mb/d over the last two months 
following attacks on ports. Taken all together, both the crude and product markets become more 
stressed with each passing day.

The price you see is not the price refiners pay 
The most striking feature of today's oil market is not the outright level of Brent, but the divergence 
between physical and financial crude prices. Since 2017, the Dated Brent premium has generally 
fluctuated around zero, with only temporary spikes during major supply disruptions. In September 
2026, however, the premium surged above $20/bbl, reaching one of its highest levels of the last decade. 
 
This suggests that the current stress is concentrated in the prompt physical market rather than in 
futures markets. Immediate barrels have become significantly more valuable than future barrels. 
 
The divergence accelerated sharply during the summer. Between early August and mid-September, 
Dated Brent rose from roughly $92/bbl to $127/bbl, while front-month Brent increased from 
approximately $89/bbl to $105/bbl. As a result, the premium expanded from around $3.5/bbl in August 
to more than $21/bbl today. 
In practical terms, this means the crude price paid by refiners is materially higher than the headline 
Brent price displayed on financial screens. The market is assigning a growing premium to immediate 
physical availability, a classic sign of tightening prompt balances.

September OMR reinforces this interpretation. The report highlights severe disruptions to Gulf exports, 
continued constraints on flows through the Strait of Hormuz, shrinking inventories and rapidly 
tightening physical markets, particularly for Atlantic Basin barrels.  
Similarly, Energy Aspects notes that reduced Saudi flows into Europe are forcing refiners to compete 
more aggressively for North Sea, West African and US barrels, increasing the value of prompt physical 
crude relative to futures contracts. (Wherefore art thou, Joliet?) 
The Buffer is gone 
The inventory picture helps explain why the market has become increasingly sensitive to disruptions. 
IEA’s September Oil Market Report estimates that global observed inventories have declined by more 
than 500 million barrels since the start of the crisis as governments and the private sector released 
stocks to offset the loss of supply. Inventories have effectively acted as a hidden source of production, 
cushioning the impact of the Gulf disruption on consumers.  
However, that buffer is no longer abundant. Total US crude inventories, including the Strategic 
Petroleum Reserve, have fallen back to levels last seen in the mid-1980s. While inventories do not 
directly determine the Dated Brent premium, they define the market's ability to absorb shocks. A 
system operating with historically low buffers is naturally more vulnerable to additional disruptions 
than a system holding abundant spare inventories. 
Red Sea disruptions have reconfigured global shipping routes 
Red Sea shipping patterns remain deeply affected by the security crisis that began in late 2023. Attacks 
on commercial vessels transiting the Bab el-Mandeb Strait prompted major shipping companies to 
avoid the Red Sea corridor, causing traffic through both Bab el-Mandeb and the Suez Canal to fall from 
roughly 2,500 monthly crossings to around 1,000. Instead, vessels have increasingly rerouted around 
the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10-14 days to voyages between Asia and Europe while increasing fuel, 
crew and insurance costs. More than two years after the initial disruptions, traffic patterns show little 
sign of normalisation, suggesting that rerouting has become structural rather than temporary. 
The impact is also evident in physical oil flows. Deseasonalized oil transit through major maritime 
chokepoints recently fell to around 30 kb/d below seasonal norms, the weakest reading since late 
2022. The deterioration accelerated following disruptions to the Strait of Hormuz, highlighting the 
vulnerability of global energy supply chains to disruptions at key maritime corridors. Meanwhile, the 
sharp reduction in Red Sea traffic has weighed heavily on Egypt's economy through a significant 
decline in Suez Canal revenues.

Deseasonalized Oil Flows Through Key Maritime Chokepoints 
Red Sea Disruptions Trigger a Lasting Shift in Shipping Routes 
 
 
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/Commodities, data from Bloomberg 
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/Commodities, data from Bloomberg 
Oil transit through strategic maritime chokepoints has fallen to its lowest level of the past six years in 
May. Average transit volumes declined from 61.2mb/week during 2020-2025 to 48.2mb/week in 2026, 
equivalent to a fall from 6.29 to 4.68 days of global oil consumption. 
While the series briefly dropped to 4.45 days of consumption during the 2020 pandemic shock, the 
underlying drivers are fundamentally different. The 2020 decline reflected a broad-based contraction 
across several shipping corridors, consistent with weaker global oil demand. By contrast, the 2026 
downturn has been overwhelmingly driven by disruptions to the Strait of Hormuz. 
Weekly oil volumes transiting Hormuz fell by 18.5mb/week, accounting for most of the decline in global 
chokepoint flows. The weakness subsequently propagated downstream to the Strait of Malacca, where 
volumes declined by a further 12.8mb/week, highlighting the importance of Gulf exports for Asian 
supply chains. 
Importantly, traffic through the Cape of Good Hope, the Suez Canal and the Strait of Dover increased 
over the same period. This suggests that the current weakness is not the result of a generalized decline 
in global trade but rather reflects a significant reconfiguration of oil shipping routes following 
disruptions to one of the world's most important energy transit corridors.

Freight inflation raises the cost of delivering Middle Eastern crude 
Freight costs for VLCC cargoes travelling from Ras Tanura to Ningbo have risen dramatically in 2026. 
Assuming a Worldscale (WS) flat rate of USD 19.6 per metric ton, the cost of shipping a fully laden VLCC 
carrying approximately 2 million barrels of crude oil increased from around USD 3.8 million in January 
2026 to nearly USD 57 million on September 17th. What was traditionally a marginal component of 
crude pricing has become a meaningful determinant of delivered costs for Asian refiners. 
The impact is equally evident in relative terms. Using the average of Oman FOB and Dubai FOB crude 
prices as a benchmark for cargo value, freight costs increased from roughly 3% of barrel value before 
the disruption to 23%. As a result, freight has evolved from a secondary consideration for buyers into 
a key driver of arbitrage economics and crude procurement decisions. 
The increase has been driven primarily by elevated war-risk insurance premiums and disruptions 
affecting key maritime corridors. Longer voyage distances and reduced effective fleet availability have 
further amplified the rise in freight rates. Although strong refining margins have partially offset the 
impact, higher transportation costs have nevertheless increased the delivered cost of Middle Eastern 
crude into Asia. 
Relative to historical norms, the current freight burden remains exceptionally elevated, highlighting 
the sensitivity of global oil markets to disruptions affecting critical shipping routes. 
How freight prices feed into the product cracks 
The recent surge in freight costs is becoming an increasingly important contributor to the global 
product market tightness. To illustrate: a refinery buys crude on a cost-and-freight basis. If the crude 
costs $100/bbl at the loading terminal and freight is $2-3/bbl (a normal level before the war), the 
delivered crude cost is $102-103/bbl. If VLCC freight now jumps to $25/bbl, roughly the level we see 
today, the same refinery is now paying $125/bbl delivered. The refinery's economics are based on this 
delivered crude cost, not the FOB crude price. 
In order to maintain margins, refiners must recover that higher input cost through the prices of 
gasoline, diesel, jet fuel and other refined products. The marginal barrel sets the market, meaning that 
if the highest-cost refiner requires a higher diesel or gasoline price to cover its costs, product prices 
tend to adjust upwards. In that sense, freight becomes embedded in product prices through the 
refinery's feedstock cost.

However, freight alone does not explain today's elevated product cracks because the lack of vessels is 
a result of the closure of the strait and the closure has other more direct impacts. If freight rises by 
$25/bbl, that increases the delivered crude cost by $25/bbl, but product cracks can expand by 
substantially more than that when the market is short physical product and there is an ever-growing 
competitive bidding process to secure crude as well as products. The reason is that product prices are 
ultimately determined by the balance of refined-product supply and demand, not solely by crude input 
costs. 
In the current environment, freight is acting as an amplifier of a much broader tightening cycle: 
• Higher freight raises delivered crude costs. 
• Elevated refining margins encourage refiners to run harder. 
• Higher utilization rates increase maintenance and probability of unplanned outages. 
• Outages reduce refinery output. 
• Product exports are already declining in several regions. 
• Refinery supply growth remains limited. 
• Product inventories are low and continuing to draw. 
• We continue to see damage to infrastructure in the Mid-East and Russia. 
As a result, freight costs are not just increasing costs directly; they are contributing to a market where 
replacement barrels become more expensive and harder to source. The physical market must 
therefore bid product prices higher to ration demand and attract supply. That is why freight rates that 
may represent only around 25% of the landed crude cost can have an outsized impact on product 
cracks when inventories are falling and supply is constrained.  
Diesel says Brent should be above $150/bbl 
A concrete illustration of these elevated freight costs is diesel, which is up 138% this year. Due to the 
refinery business model, refining margins (cracks) have historically maintained a relatively stable 
relationship between diesel and Brent. This summer, however, this relationship weakened, and we 
observed lower crude prices relative to refined products. As we show in the next section, this 
divergence can be largely explained by product dynamics relative to crude dynamics. Based on the 
historical relationship though, we estimate that Brent should be trading above $150/bbl to be 
consistent with the traditional economics of the refining business. We argue that the restoration of this 
relationship has been a major driver of the recent catch-up in crude prices relative to products, and 
that the longer the conflict lasts, the more crude scarcity will converge with the diesel scarcity already 
priced into the market.

Together, these disruptions have materially reduced the global supply of refined products despite 
exceptionally strong refining margins.
