The three musketeers and a resilient India 
Delhi 
The Federal Reserve has raised rates for the first time since July 2023 with the federal funds rate now 3.75-
4% as expected. Importantly, the bond market did not sell off, though it did not rally as Fed Chairman Kevin 
Warsh might have hoped for (see Exhibit 1). Still he has avoided a further sell-off in the long end, which would 
have been GREED & fear’s base case if there had been no rate hike. The 10-year Treasury bond yield is now 
4.94%.  
Exhibit 1: US 10-year Treasury bond yield 
  
Source: Bloomberg 
 
Aside from the focus on the Fed and bond market, there have been two important developments over the past 
week. The first was the bizarre spectacle of Messieurs Amodei, Altman and Musk all publicly agreeing to hit 
the brakes as regards the development of AI at the so-called “frontier”. What is the rationale for such a move, 
most particularly in the case of Anthropic in the lead up to an anticipated IPO? GREED & fear’s cynical advice 
is to look at the incentives. A coordinated pause sold as “safety” driven could be viewed as a way to build a 
regulatory moat, most particularly if there is a subsequent plan to ban open-source models, most particularly 
from China. The other possibility, and it is not mutually exclusive, is that they are realising that the cost of 
compute is becoming unsustainable and/or that the models are starting to plateau. If that is the case, why 
not attempt to lock in a cartel now? 
GREED & fear has no inside track. But offers the above thoughts as plausible explanations for what is 
undoubtedly a bizarre development. Meanwhile, China will not be slowing its development of AI at all.  But the 
focus in the mainland has always been about developing cost effective applications rather than the Silicon 
Valley obsession with reaching so-called AGI.

The second notable development was the Houthis capture of the port of Mokha and two islands in the Red 
Sea and the damage inflicted on the 750-mile-long East-West oil pipeline which runs from the Persian Gulf to 
Yanbu, a port on the Red Sea where two Saudi refineries are located. It remains bizarre in the extreme to 
GREED & fear that Saudi Arabia should have reactivated its conflict with Yemen when the Red Sea route was 
its way of reducing its vulnerability to events in the Strait of Hormuz, as previously discussed here (see GREED 
& fear - World War III and capex aversion, 30 July 2026). But this is what has now happened. The result is that 
Iran now controls the flow of oil out of the Strait of Hormuz and the Houthis the flow out of the Strait of Bab 
al-Mandeb.    
This is a nightmare scenario for markets, which also has potentially hugely significant geopolitical 
implications. The need to have energy exposure in a portfolio as the only viable hedge is more obvious than 
ever with the only surprise that Brent crude oil at US$106/bbl is not much higher (see Exhibit 2). But the diesel 
crack spread continues to highlight the growing stresses in the system (see Exhibit 3).

It is reasonably clear that for India to become a prime point of focus again for emerging market equity 
investors the AI story has to blow up. This is because TSMC, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix still account 
for 29% of the MSCI Emerging markets Index, compared with 11% for India (see Exhibit 4). Whereas before 
the AI story kicked in at the start of 2023 India was the best structural growth story globally which is why it 
was also beginning to attract the attention of global investors, and not just emerging market specialists. 
Three and a half years on and in the Indian capital again this week for the Jefferies 5th India Forum, it remains 
evident to GREED & fear that the structural growth story remains intact with the economic data remarkably 
resilient given the obvious challenges posed by geopolitics to a country which remains a major consumer of 
energy.

Source: Factset, MSCI  
 
Indeed the economic performance has been much better than anyone was expecting the last time GREED & 
fear was in India six months ago (see GREED & fear - A question of timing, 5 March 2026). This is best captured 
by bank credit which was growing by 19.1% YoY at the end of August (see Exhibit 5). Credit growth is strong 
across all segments.  But corporate lending and loans to SMEs have been particularly robust. Corporate loans 
rose by 21.6% YoY in July, while loans to micro, small and medium industrial enterprises were up 24.9% YoY 
(see Exhibit 6). This trend is also supported by healthy deposit growth which rose by 17.8% YoY in August.

While there is a base effect partly at work the pickup in lending to SMEs suggest that the GST reforms and 
labour reforms at the end of the last year, combined with the focus of the third Modi government on improving 
the ease of doing business and removing the last vestiges of the “Licence Raj”, may be starting to see some 
dividends.  These reforms were discussed in more detail in GREED & fear’s last visit (see GREED & fear - 
Physical graffiti, 26 February 2026). The pickup in corporate lending also suggests that the long anticipated 
private sector capex cycle may finally be happening. This was certainly the view of one chairman of a major 
listed bank GREED & fear met this week. 
The result is that India looks on track to achieve 6.5-7% real GDP growth and nominal GDP growth of around 
11-12% this fiscal year. The view of Jefferies’ head of India research Mahesh Nandurkar in his presentation at 
this week’s event is that the nominal GDP acceleration should lead to a pickup in earnings growth from 14% 
this fiscal year to 17% next year starting 1 April (see Exhibit 7).

GREED & fear was in Tokyo briefly over the past weekend. There is no doubt that the recent stabilisation of the 
yen has coincided with growing expectations of renewed monetary tightening. It is a near certainty that the 
Bank of Japan will raise rates tomorrow by 25bp to 1.25%; while another 25bp hike is expected before the end 
of the year with the only argument being whether it will be in October or December. 
If this is the backdrop GREED & fear still believes that the BoJ’s innately cautious governor Kazuo Ueda would 
be well advised to hike rates by more than the expected 25bp this week. This would further increase the 
chances of putting a floor under the yen while also creating an argument for foreigners to buy long-dated 
JGBs on a non-hedged currency basis. 
But such an outcome remains extremely unlikely given Ueda’s caution. Still a move greater than 25bp can be 
justified with estimates of the so-called “neutral rate” moving from 1.5% to 2% in recent months. For such 
reasons it is quite likely that the uncollateralized overnight call rate will reach 2% if not higher in this tightening 
cycle. The last time the rate was 2% was in September 1993 when the BoJ cut rates by 75bp from 2.5% to 
1.75% as the central bank began to wake up to the deflationary implications of the bursting “Bubble” (see 
Exhibit 21).

Still GREED & fear was interested to hear that a reaction is now coming against what Japanese officialdom 
perceives to be excessive activism as reflected, for example, in foreign activists buying stakes in companies 
and then seeking to sell them to the growing number of foreign private equity companies with operations in 
Tokyo.   
This new approach will increasingly take concrete form. One example is METI’s proposed revision to its 
original “Guidelines for Corporate Takeovers” which was originally issued in August 2023. Press reports 
suggest METI wants to shift the focus from purely market criteria to the need to ensure long-term gains in 
value through capex and R&D spending. On this point, the METI published on 30 July three new supplementary 
documents on the guidelines for corporate takeovers. One central theme of the revision is that an acquisition 
is not considered as “desirable” solely because of a high purchase price. It noted that “whether or not an 
acquisition is desirable should be determined on the basis of whether it will secure or enhance corporate value 
and the shareholders’ common interests” (see METI release: “METI Formulates Key Points and Q&A on the 
Guidelines for Corporate Takeovers”, 30 July 2026). 
Another initiative among several pointing in a similar direction is the Financial Services Agency’s (FSA) expert 
panel on the Revisions of the Corporate Governance Code. The revisions have already been finalised and were 
published on 21 July. They again emphasise long-term sustainability over a focus on near-term returns. The 
FSA and the Tokyo Stock Exchange said in a joint statement that the revised Code aims to “encourage 
companies to prioritise substantive initiatives toward value creation over the medium-to-long-term”.  
Another more technical change, albeit an important one, relates to the so-called “Large shareholding reporting 
system”, commonly known as the “5% Rule”. Under the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act (FIEA), a 5% 
holding must be declared and every 1% increase/decrease in ownership thereafter. But since 1 May the rules 
have been expanded to include use of derivatives which were being used to build positions such as total return 
swaps and stock lending arrangements.  
All this suggests to GREED & fear that the era of foreign activism may have peaked in Japan, a development 
which Korea’s chaebols will doubtless be monitoring carefully. While the Tokyo Stock Exchange-led corporate 
governance reform has been a major theme in the equity market in recent years, it has been subordinated of 
late to the AI theme.  
Meanwhile, from an overall Japanese equity market standpoint, GREED & fear would continue to favour AI 
plays, so long as the AI capex cycle is running, and banks and insurers so long as the normalisation of 
monetary policy is proceeding. Such sectors account for 31% of GREED & fear’s Japan long-only equity 
portfolio (see Exhibit 36).
