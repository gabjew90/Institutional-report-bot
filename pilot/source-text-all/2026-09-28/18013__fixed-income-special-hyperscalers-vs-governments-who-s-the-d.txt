Fixed Income Special 
Hyperscalers vs governments: Who’s the dog, who’s the tail? 
Key points 
 
Evolving savings-investments equilibrium. Global savings remain abundant, but are 
recycled through private investors rather than official reserve managers. AI boom is making 
investment more capital-intensive, with spending concentrated in data centres and AI 
infrastructure, driving growing external financing needs despite broadly stable aggregate 
investment rates. 
 
DM governments face tougher competition for funding, while their fiscal deficits have 
become structurally larger. Major central banks are reducing bond holdings, and foreign 
central banks are not buying much. Meanwhile, private investors are more price and return 
sensitive, preferring equities and corporate bonds. 
 
Hyperscalers’ net debt issuance rivals that of major sovereigns but has been relatively 
well absorbed by the credit markets. The Technology sector has widened, while the impact 
on the credit spreads of other sectors has been muted so far. Hyperscalers' short maturity 
bonds issued this year have performed well, but most of the longer maturity bonds have 
struggled, adding to the overall pressure on the long end rates.

Changing competition for funding 
The global savings glut is persistent, but the flow of capital is changing. Current account 
surpluses continue to expand, driven primarily by North Asia's important role in the AI supply 
chain, while surpluses in Europe and among oil exporters have stabilised (Graph 1).  
Graph 1. Current account surplus still expanding, dominated by North Asia 
 
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/Economics, IMF 
The AI boom is not raising overall investment rates, but it is changing the composition of 
investment. Aggregate investment as a share of GDP has remained broadly stable (Graph 2), yet 
an increasing share of spending is concentrated in machinery and equipment (Graph 3). The 
capex boom is narrowly concentrated in AI-related demand, which is much more capital-
intensive with highly uncertain revenue prospects and increasingly relying on external 
borrowing.

The challenge for sovereigns is not only larger deficits, but also a less supportive investor 
base. Developed-market governments continue to run structurally elevated fiscal deficits (Graph 
4) at a time when central banks are reducing bond holdings (Graph 5) and foreign reserve 
managers are no longer a significant source of demand (Graph 6). Meanwhile, private investors 
increasingly favour assets offering higher expected returns, including equities and investment-
grade corporate credit (Graph 7). The result of such aggregate demand evolution is a growing 
competition for capital between sovereign borrowers and large corporate issuers, particularly as 
AI-related financing needs continue to rise

Record 2026 issuance by hyperscalers 
Hyperscalers are emerging as sovereign-scale borrowers. According to SG estimates, AI-
related capex is on track to reach $800bn this year and could exceed $1tn in 2027 (Graph 8). As 
free cash flow is increasingly absorbed by investment spending, external financing needs 
continue to rise, with debt remaining one of the key funding sources. Net bond issuance by 
hyperscalers has already exceeded $200bn this year, making them one of the largest borrowers 
globally, larger than most developed-market sovereign issuers (Graph 9). On gross issuance level, 
hyperscalers YTD supply accounts for more than 20% of the overall US IG issuance across 
currencies – and an even higher share at the long end. This is one of the fastest growing industries 
in terms of bond supply, responsible for more than half of the increase in US IG Corp issuance 
across all currencies in Jan-Sep 2026 vs Jan-Sep 2025 (Graph 10).

Competition with the sovereign debt. USD-denominated credit supply by corporates has been 
expanding faster than sovereign issuance and now IG corporates account for up to 22% of IG 
issuance (Graph 16). Looking at the August AUD Alphabet issuance, the buyers of hyperscaler 
bonds are mostly traditional real-money investors such as asset managers, insurers, pension 
funds and foreign institutions (Graph 17). At the same time, government bond markets are 
becoming more reliant on those same investors as central banks reduce balance sheets and 
foreign official demand weakens. The overlap between the two investor bases continues to 
increase as borrowing needs are rising across both sectors.

Hyperscalers as a potential structural driver of long-end yields. On its own, hyperscaler 
supply is unlikely to drive a major repricing in rates or credit markets. However, combined with 
elevated fiscal deficits, reduced central bank demand and ongoing term premium rebuilding, it 
adds further pressure at the long end of the yield curve. The evidence so far supports this view: 
shorter-dated hyperscaler bonds have generally performed well after issuance, while longer-
dated bonds have underperformed (see below, Graph 23 and 24), suggesting that the market is 
absorbing the credit risk more easily than the duration risk.

No indigestion in credit markets yet 
Despite the scale of supply, credit markets have absorbed it remarkably well. USD and EUR 
investment-grade spreads have widened only modestly year-to-date, mostly on the back of the 
ongoing macro and geopolitical uncertainties. However, while the general credit markets have 
been very resilient amid the global turmoil, the heavy hyperscalers’ issuance has had an impact 
on the Technology sector. This is not too surprising given how sharply the sector has grown in 
both the euro (+48% since late 2024) and USD (+42%) markets (Graph 18, 19).  
Graph 18. Growth of the Technology sector in EUR 
 
Graph 19. Growth of the Technology sector is USD

We note that investors continue to view hyperscalers among the highest-quality credits in the 
market, although there are questions regarding off balance sheet debt. Still, we see that the 
strong demand from asset managers, insurers and pension funds has largely kept pace with the 
increase in issuance as the new issues are absorbed quickly. 
However, and not too surprisingly, the rapid growth shown above has put pressure on the 
sector’s spreads (Graph 20, 21). In the EUR market, the Technology sector is some 15bp wider 
year-to-date (vs 7bp for the iBoxx EUR Corporates index) while in the USD market, the sector is 
25bp wider (vs  2bp for the iBoxx USD Corporates index).

The good news is that while the growth in the Technology sector is strong in absolute terms, it is 
very moderate compared to the overall market size. Both the EUR and USD corporate indices 
have been growing for almost 20 years in almost linear fashion (Graph 24).  
Graph 24. iBoxx EUR and USD Corporates notional size 
 
Source: Markit, SG Cross Asset Research/Credit 
In fact, USD IG index has grown at a 9% rate annually since 2008 and the EUR IG index at 7%. Year-
to-date the numbers stand at 5.8% and 3.8% respectively, slightly below the long-term trends, 
despite the very strong new issue volumes on both sides of the Atlantic.  
And so far, we haven’t seen a major impact on the different corporate indices. As we noted above, 
the USD index is some 2bp wider year-to-date, while the euro index is some 7bp wider and for us, 
this is more the result of the ongoing macro uncertainties (oil prices, sovereign bond sell-off, etc) 
rather than the heavy supply and performance of hyperscalers, highlighting that the impact on 
the general credit markets remains very limited.
