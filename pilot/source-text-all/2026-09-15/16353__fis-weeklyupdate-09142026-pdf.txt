What happened last week?
U.S. Treasuries sold off sharply in the holiday-shortened week, with yields across the curve 
reaching fresh 2026 highs as investors increased expectations for a September Fed hike. Rising 
oil prices and escalating Middle East tensions reinforced inflation concerns, while firmer global 
policy signals, including an ECB hike, added to upward pressure on yields. Treasury’s expanded 
long-end buyback program also disappointed investors, with the announced $6 billion 10- to 20-
year operation falling short of some expectations, although solid 3-, 10-, and 30-year auctions 
provided only brief relief. Friday’s CPI matched the 0.4% headline forecast but core inflation 
came in slightly hotter, pushing September hike odds to roughly 85–90%. The curve bear-
flattened as front-end yields underperformed: the 2yr rose 26 bps to 4.63%, the 10yr increased 
19 bps to 4.97%, and the 30yr climbed 11 bps to 5.35%, leaving the 2s10s spread at 34 bps.

The Week Ahead: Data/Events/Observations
•
Key Economic Releases/Events: Attention will center on 
the FOMC decision, updated projections and Chair Warsh’s 
press conference. Markets are nearly fully pricing a 25 bp 
hike following the CPI report, though some economists 
remain skeptical that policymakers will tighten. The U.S. 
data calendar is also busy, with retail sales, industrial 
production and import prices the key releases. Housing 
starts,  building  permits,  pending  home  sales  and 
homebuilder sentiment will provide further signals on rate-
sensitive activity, while the New York and Philadelphia Fed 
surveys will help refine the broader growth outlook.
•
September Fed Hike Is Now Largely Priced In: Rate-hike 
odds have surged to 88% going into the September FOMC 
meeting, leaving limited scope for the decision itself to 
deliver a hawkish surprise (Chart 1). If the Fed hikes as 
priced, attention will shift to whether September is framed 
as a one-off recalibration or the start of a broader tightening 
cycle. Conviction remains far from unanimous, however, 
with a meaningful contingent of economists still expecting 
the Fed to hold, leaving scope for a sharp repricing if 
policymakers stand pat.
