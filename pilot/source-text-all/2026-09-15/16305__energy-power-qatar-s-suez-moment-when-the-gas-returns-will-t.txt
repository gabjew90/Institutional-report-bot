In November 1956, British and French warships still believed the Suez
Canal belonged to whoever controlled its banks. They were wrong by
about a decade. The canal reopened within months; the assumption
that European powers set the terms of Middle Eastern logistics never
did. History's cruelest lesson about chokepoints is not that they close,
it is that reopening them and repairing what they broke are two entirely
different projects, and only one of them shows up on an infrastructure
timeline.
Qatar is now living the modern, commercial version of that lesson.
Over the past six months, its LNG exports collapsed from 40m tonnes
to 4.45m. The number of Qatari LNG carriers making it through
Hormuz fell even harder: from 509 to 18. QatarEnergy has extended
force majeure to buyers in Pakistan, Bangladesh and Italy through
October, the fourth such extension since February. The headline
number, a 96% volume collapse, is almost the least interesting one.
The number that should keep every LNG portfolio manager in Tokyo
and Milan awake is $24bn: roughly five months of Qatari government
revenue, quietly transferred to whichever supplier had spare capacity
sitting idle at the right latitude.

EVERY CHOKEPOINT CRISIS ENDS THE SAME WAY
History does not repeat, but chokepoints apparently do, and they
follow a script depressingly consistent for people who study wars for
a living but somehow keep being surprised by geography. A strait or
canal closes. Prices spike. Statesmen give solemn speeches about
"temporary disruptions." Then, eighteen months later, the waterway
reopens exactly as promised, and nobody who mattered is still using
it the way they used to.
Suez in 1956 gave the world the template. The canal reopened
by April 1957, barely five months after Nasser's nationalization
triggered war. But Britain, whose refineries had run on the
assumption that Middle Eastern crude was essentially a domestic
utility, accelerated the shift toward larger tankers and supply chains
capable of economically bypassing the canal via the Cape of Good
Hope.; rendering the canal's reopening almost beside the point for
its most important customer. The lesson was not "chokepoints get
fixed." It was "customers build their way out of dependence exactly

The 1973 Arab oil embargo taught the same lesson to a
different generation. OPEC's embargo lasted five months. America's
response, the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, CAFE fuel-economy
standards, Alaska's Trans-Alaska Pipeline fast-tracked through
Congress, lasted decades and structurally shrank the leverage of
the very producers who imposed it. China learned the lesson pre-
emptively rather than the hard way: Hu Jintao's 2003 warning about
the "Malacca Dilemma", the fear that a single strait carrying 80%
of China's oil imports gave hostile navies a stranglehold on its
economy, quietly justified twenty years of pipeline diplomacy through
Kazakhstan, Myanmar and Pakistan, built almost entirely to avoid
ever needing Malacca's goodwill.
Qatar's predicament fits the pattern almost too neatly to be
comfortable. Ras Laffan will reopen. The question this note keeps
returning to is whether "reopening" and "recovering" are actually the
same word, because in Suez's case, in 1973's case, and in Beijing's
two-decade hedge against Malacca, they demonstrably were not.

THE NUMBERS BEHIND THE REROUTE
Ras Laffan ran at just 23% utilization in July, with the missile strikes
that knocked out roughly 13mtpa across Trains 4 and 6 not expected
to be fully repaired until 2027, at an estimated cost of $3bn.
ExxonMobil's share of Qatari LNG has fallen from 13mt to about 4m;
ConocoPhillips's from 2.5m to roughly 1m. Middle East LNG output
overall sat 65% below last year's levels on the latest trade data, while
the United States, without firing a shot, absorbed 32% of global LNG
exports almost by default .
The price data confirms the market has now caught up to that reality:
• JKM spot hit $24.09/MMBtu on 3 September, up roughly 90%
from BMI's pre-disruption 2026 forecast of $12.50/MMBtu; a
forecast BMI has since revised to $15.5 for the full year.
• JKM peaked at $25.40/MMBtu in early March when the force
majeure first hit, and has since built what analysts now describe as
a structural floor of $16-20/MMBtu for the next 18-24 months.
• European storage sits at 63-65% of capacity heading into winter,
the lowest level for this time of year in roughly two decades, versus
a 90% five-year average for early November.
• Closing that gap requires over 100 TWh of additional gas, worth
roughly €7bn at current prices, at an injection rate Europe has not
attempted since the 2022 crisis.

THE IRONY SITTING INSIDE ADNOC'S OWN BALANCE SHEET
Here is the detail that makes this note genuinely uncomfortable
for anyone still selling a "Gulf solidarity" narrative: the same six
months that gutted Qatar's LNG franchise are the exact six months in
which ADNOC's international investment arm, XRG, quietly finished
building out a stake across all five trains of a liquefaction project in
Texas.
The timeline reads almost too cleanly. XRG closed an 11.7% stake
in Rio Grande LNG's first three trains in September 2025, before the
war, when this looked like ordinary portfolio diversification. It then
raised that stake by another 7.6% in January 2026, as the conflict
entered its second month. By July, with Ras Laffan running at 23%
utilization and Doha telling buyers to expect years, not weeks, of
disruption, XRG completed its build-out across all five Rio Grande
trains and locked in a 20-year, 1.9mtpa offtake agreement from
Train 4. In April, ADNOC's leadership told the Financial Times it was
evaluating roughly $30bn more in US gas investment specifically
because the war was disrupting global energy markets.
None of this is disloyalty. It is precisely the rational, self-interested
hedge every well-run national oil company should be running, and
Abu Dhabi, to its credit, started running it before the war even began.
But that is exactly what makes it such an inconvenient data point
for the "this is temporary" camp. If the smartest capital sitting inside
the Gulf's own energy establishment is systematically routing new
growth capital toward a chokepoint-free American alternative rather
than back into Hormuz-dependent capacity, the market should stop
asking whether Qatar's problem is cyclical and start asking whether
ADNOC has already answered that question with its checkbook.
There is a sharper way to put it. Every LNG buyer renewing
a Qatari contract this decade is, in effect, taking a risk that
Abu Dhabi's own national oil company has spent a year quietly
declining to take with its own balance sheet.

STRUCTURAL OR CYCLICAL? THE MARKET IS STILL
ARGUING
Two of the most powerful voices in this market are, right now, making
almost opposite bets. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on 4
September that oil could fall to $40-50/bbl once the Iran conflict
ends, arguing the post-war market will be "very much oversupplied"
given how much new production is coming online.
Bullish street consensus flag $120/bbl price as the risk case if
shipping attacks intensify further, with warnings that Brent could
still average $100 through 2027 if Hormuz disruptions persist. With
Brent trading near $100 as we publish, the two camps are effectively
arguing about a $40-80 range around where prices currently sit.
Note what both camps agree on, even while disagreeing on direction:
the disruption itself is treated as reversible, priced entirely off
whether Hormuz reopens. Neither view engages with the question
this note is actually asking, not "where does oil go," but "does LNG
market share come back once the price does." Those are different
bets, and conflating them is, we think, the market's biggest blind spot
right now.

VERDICT: THE FLAG RETURNS TO DOHA'S MASTS, BUT DO
THE CUSTOMERS?
Ibn Khaldun, whom this series has already invoked once this year,
would recognize this pattern too; though the mechanism at work
here is commercial rather than military. His central insight was that
a dynasty's cohesion frays not when its capital falls, but when the
people who once depended on it quietly stop assuming it will always
be there. Substitute "asabiyyah" with "long-term offtake contract,"
and the theory translates almost too neatly: what breaks first is not
the asset, but the assumption.
Ras Laffan will rebuild. The trains will run again, probably by 2H27,
and the flag will fly over a facility once again producing near
its 77mtpa nameplate capacity. That was never the interesting
question. The interesting question, the one Bessent and Goldman
are arguing about from the wrong angle, focused on oil rather than
contracts, is whether the buyers in Tokyo, Milan and Karachi who
spent 2026 building around Qatar's unreliability will fully unwind
those arrangements once the unreliability ends, or whether, like
Britain after Suez and China after Malacca, they will simply keep the
workaround permanently, because a workaround, once built, rarely
gets torn down out of nostalgia.
We think the latter is closer to the truth, and that Abu Dhabi's own
national oil company already agrees with us, one Texan LNG train at
a time. Six centuries after Ibn Khaldun watched dynasties mistake
military recovery for lasting strength, the Gulf's newest lesson in the
same mistake may not be written in blood or borders at all; it may
be written in twenty-year contracts, quietly signed by buyers who no
longer need Doha to say yes.

THREE EXAMPLES FROM HISTORY
Three episodes, four decades apart, teach the same lesson: physical
disruptions end faster than the buyers who fled them return.
Iran’s global oil-production share fell from approximately 8-9%
before the revolution to around 3-4% by 1986. Within OPEC, its
share declined from roughly 17-18% to around 11-12%. Many
buyers replaced disrupted Iranian barrels with alternative Middle
Eastern and non-OPEC supply, and Iran did not recover its earlier
position in several core import markets. Libya's 2011 civil war did
worse: output collapsed from 1.65mbpd to under 100,000, Gaddafi
