Forecast changes: oil price surge to trigger more rate hikes 
Chart 1: Energy: a bigger shock 
Chart 2: Central banks react to higher energy prices

Berenberg. 
In %, policy rates. Market pricing as of 11 September. Sources: Bloomberg, 
Berenberg.  
• 
More pain in the pipeline: Just over two weeks ago, we adjusted our energy price assumptions to reflect the still-
constricted Middle East supplies. The further escalation of the conflict in the Persian Gulf and the advance of the Iran-
aligned Houthis to the Bab al-Mandab Strait mean that our late-August call for a drop in the price for a barrel of Brent 
crude from an average of $90 in August to $75 per barrel in mid-2027 already looks too optimistic (see Chart 1). We now 
assume that the oil price will remain at or modestly above $100 until late 2026 before gradually declining to $75 in late 
2027. A period of high oil prices is usually followed by a major correction in subsequent years as supply recovers while 
demand softens. We therefore project that the oil price will drop to $65 by end-2028 instead of stabilising at $70.  
• 
Consumers pay the price: In addition to sky-high oil prices, more expensive natural gas (now c85% above its late-April 
level) will hurt consumers this winter. Likely food supply constraints due to the European summer drought and the 
interruption of Russian and Ukrainian grain exports amid persistent mutual strikes will exacerbate the pain. We thus 
raise our 2027 Eurozone CPI inflation call from 2.2% in late August to 2.6% now. Similarly, we lift our forecast for UK 
average CPI inflation next year from 2.1% to 2.5% now. Inflation will likely hover above 3% yoy until March 2027 in both 
the Eurozone and the UK before dropping back towards the central banks’ 2% targets over the remainder of the year. 
• 
Double squeeze now – relief later: Higher energy and food prices curtail gains in real disposable incomes and raise 
costs for many businesses. In addition to the two 25bp rate hikes which the European Central Bank (ECB) has already 
delivered, markets now price in a substantial tightening cycle (see Chart 2). This has already raised fixed interest rates 
in the real economy, such as those on mortgages. This dual squeeze on domestic demand will weigh on growth over 
the winter. As a result, we lower our call for Eurozone growth (ex-volatile Ireland) in 2027 from 1.3% to 1.1% after 1.1% 
in 2026 (unchanged). In 2028, a sharper oil price correction can propel Eurozone growth to 1.7%, up from our previous 
call of 1.6%. Helped also by the unfolding German stimulus, the Eurozone is heading for a mini-boom in 2028 after 
subdued growth in late 2026 and early 2027, in our view. For the UK, sustained momentum in economic activity over 
the summer has led us to revise up our 2026 GDP growth forecast from 1.2% to 1.3%. For next year, however, we now 
project UK growth of 1.3% instead of 1.4%, followed by an acceleration to 1.8% in 2028 (unchanged).  
• 
Fed and ECB rate hikes ahead: In his Jackson Hole address, US Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh argued that the 
Fed “has work to do” unless inflation declines towards the 2% target at sufficient speed. After unchanged US CPI 
inflation of 3.4% yoy in August, we now expect the Fed to raise the target range of its federal funds rate this Wednesday, 
16 September, by 25bp. In the Eurozone and the UK, we see no major second-round effects such as an acceleration in 
wage pressures or in non-energy-related components of core inflation. Nonetheless, the ECB has already responded to 
higher energy prices with two rate hikes. This pattern suggests that the ECB will likely do it again in reaction to the new 
spike in energy prices. Having so far projected a long ECB pause until final rate hikes to a 3% deposit rate in late 2028, 
we now expect a 25bp hike on 17 December. The risks are skewed towards an additional move on 29 October already.  
• 
BoE falling in line: Due to the weak UK labour market, the BoE is reluctant to hike for fear of triggering a downturn in 
employment. However, strong growth over the summer suggests that the economy can largely weather a solitary hike, 
while failing to follow other central banks and significantly undershooting market pricing would invite downward 
pressure on the pound that could push inflation up further. Therefore, we now expect the BoE to tighten policy by 25bp 
on 5 November. We push back our call for two 25bp cuts in Bank Rate from H1 2027 to late 2027/early 2028.
