Headlines 
 
 
 
 
We expect the Fed to hike rates by 25bps at this week's FOMC meeting, beginning a 
renewed hiking cycle that should bring two additional rate hikes in December and 
March of next year. Markets are currently pricing in roughly an 90% chance of a 
rate hike this week. 
 
 
The case for rate hikes is simple: the Fed has made almost no progress in returning 
inflation to target since early 2024, and trend inflation has worsened this year. While 
it is tempting to explain away this lack of progress with a rotating set of supply 
shocks or idiosyncratic factors keeping inflation elevated, the truth may be much 
simpler: there is still too much demand in the economy chasing too little supply. 
 
 
There are few signs that the economy is slowing enough to generate the additional 
disinflation the Fed needs, at least not in a timely enough manner for a Fed that is 
clearly growing impatient. Private-sector demand is still growing above 3%;  
corporate profits have rebounded sharply; suggesting firms continue to possess 
considerable pricing power, and job growth has strengthened this year. 
 
 
While the labor market is not yet a source of inflationary pressure, the Fed has to 
be asking itself at what point that changes. Continued improvement in hiring and 
wage growth would make it even harder for inflation to return to target. If the Fed 
doesn't hike a few times now, it risks having to hike much more aggressively later. 
 
 
Outside of the FOMC meeting this week, we expect Wednesday’s retail sales print 
to show consumer spending rebounded in August, keeping Q3 growth on a firm 
footing. Additional data on housing starts and industrial production should support 
our view of robust third quarter growth.

FOMC should hike this week 
We expect the Fed to hike the fed funds rate by 25 bps 
at this week's FOMC meeting, beginning a renewed 
hiking cycle that should bring additional rate 
increases in December and March. Market-implied 
odds of a September hike jumped to around 90% 
following last Friday's CPI report. This reinforces our 
that inflation remains too elevated for comfort, even 
if a large, one-time increase in cell phone plan prices 
boosted core CPI inflation to 0.3% m/m compared to 
expectations of a 0.2% increase. 
 
Figure 1: Market-implied odds of a September hike (%) 
 
 
The case for additional rate hikes is straightforward: 
the Fed has made little progress returning inflation to 
target since early 2024, and trend inflation has 
worsened this year. While it has been tempting to 
attribute the lack of progress to a rotating cast of 
supply shocks and idiosyncratic factors, the simpler 
explanation may be the correct one: demand remains 
too strong relative to supply. 
 
Looking beneath the surface, shelter inflation rose just 
0.26% in August, lowering its year-over-year rate to 
3.0%, a pace broadly consistent with 2% overall 
inflation. But core goods prices still increased 0.11%, 
while core services excluding housing, or “supercore” 
inflation, accelerated to 0.42% from 0.35% in July. 
More concerningly, this acceleration has occurred 
despite continued easing in wage growth, suggesting 
that underlying aggregate demand remains too 
strong. Left unchecked, that strength will continue to 
keep upward pressure on inflation.

pandemic trend (% chg., y/y)
 
 
There are also few signs that the economy is slowing 
enough to generate the additional disinflation the Fed 
needs.  Private-sector demand is still expanding at a 
better than a 3% annualized pace in Q3, corporate 
profits have rebounded sharply; suggesting firms 
retain considerable pricing power; and job growth has 
strengthened this year. That is not the sort of 
backdrop that typically produces a sustained return 
to 2% inflation and troubles an increasingly impatient 
FOMC.

August retail sales to show a rebound  
Headline retail sales slipped 0.6% month-on-month in 
July, the biggest decline since May 2025 (-1.1%). The 
decline was exaggerated by a shift in Amazon Prime 
Day to June this year, which moved online sales that 
would have normally occurred in July. Consequently, 
sales at nonstore retailers fell 2.2%, the largest drop 
since January 2025. Moreover, sales at gasoline 
stations tumbled 0.9% amid a 3.1% seasonally adjusted 
drop in gas prices in July, also contributing to the 
sharp decline in headline retail sales. The drop in retail 
sales excluding online retailers and gasoline stations 
was a much more modest 0.1%. 
 
Headline retail sales growth is expected to rebound 
0.7% in August amid a sharp jump in job gains, a still-
low unemployment rate and rising gas prices. The 
162,000 increase in nonfarm payrolls in August was the 
largest since March of this year. Our internal card 
data also point to a rebound, with our nowcast 
implying a 0.5% month-on-month increase in retail 
sales despite the later timing of Labor Day shifting 
some holiday spending into September. Employment 
trends in our data, as measured by direct deposit 
payments, have moderated from the much-improved 
pace at the start of the year but remain consistent 
with stronger job growth than in 2025. The number of 
households 
receiving 
unemployment 
insurance 
payments has also trended lower than a year ago.  
Together, stronger job growth and fewer households 
receiving unemployment payments have supported 
higher aggregate payroll income and, in turn, 
spending. The sharp rebound would push the year-
ago growth rate in headline retail sales to 5.1%, the 
first acceleration since May. Retail sales ex-autos is 
forecast to increase 0.5% in August, with the year-on-
year growth rate slipping to 5.6% from 5.8% in July.
