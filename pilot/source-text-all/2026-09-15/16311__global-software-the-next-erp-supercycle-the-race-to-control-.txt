INVESTMENT SUMMARY
The ERP market is entering a once-in-a-generation transition that could prove as significant as the shift from on-premise software to
the cloud. These platform resets occur only every 20-30 years and often reshape industry leadership, business models and valuation
frameworks. We believe AI is pushing ERP from a system of record toward a system of execution, creating a new investment debate:
not who owns enterprise data, but who controls enterprise work. We propose in this note a framework to assess vendors on their
ability to become the trusted execution layer for the AI-enabled enterprise. While Oracle appears best positioned today, we see SAP
and Microsoft as the most strategically compelling names over the next three years.
ERP is reaching a structural inflection point as value shifts from transaction digitization to coordination digitization.
Previous ERP generations standardized processes, centralized data and improved control. The next opportunity is much
larger: reducing the coordination burden across people, functions and systems. Agentic AI, real-time data and modern cloud
architectures are enabling ERP platforms to move beyond recording activity toward coordinating, orchestrating and eventually
executing it. This is a fundamental shift in software economics, not just another product cycle.
The central investment question is who becomes the enterprise execution layer. AI does not reduce the importance of ERP;
if anything, it increases it. Autonomous agents require trusted data, process rules, permissions, governance and auditability before
they can act safely. ERP systems already own many of these assets. The likely winners will therefore be vendors that can translate
AI into controlled execution within mission-critical workflows, rather than those with the most impressive AI demonstrations.
Investors should focus less on AI visibility and more on AI value.
Oracle currently has the strongest integrated-stack story. Our scorecard ranks Oracle first because of the tight integration
between its applications, database, cloud infrastructure and ERP platforms. This architectural coherence simplifies governed
automation and creates one of the clearest closed-loop execution stories in enterprise software today. The trade-off is that
Oracle’s strength is greatest within Oracle-centric environments and may prove less compelling in heterogeneous enterprise
estates.
SAP has the strongest claim to becoming the governed enterprise execution platform. SAP combines deep process
ownership across finance, procurement, manufacturing and supply chain with one of the richest sets of enterprise process
semantics in the industry. If customers continue progressing toward S/4, clean-core architectures, Business Technology Platform,
Business Data Cloud and Joule, SAP could emerge as the safest platform for enterprise-scale autonomous workflows. In our
framework, it carries the highest long-term score because enterprise autonomy ultimately requires trust, governance and process
depth - not just AI capability. The main risk is execution, as customers must first modernize their ERP estates.
Microsoft may be the most disruptive challenger because it increasingly controls the interface where enterprise
work happens. Through Copilot, Microsoft 365, Teams, Power Platform, Azure and Dynamics, Microsoft is embedded in the
daily workflow of hundreds of millions of users. Its opportunity is not necessarily to replace ERP systems, but to become the
orchestration and interaction layer sitting above them. If enterprise work is increasingly initiated, approved and supervised through
AI agents, Microsoft’s strategic position becomes exceptionally powerful.
The competition between incumbents and AI-native companies is likely to be more evolutionary than disruptive. The
bigger risk is not that enterprises abandon SAP, Oracle or Microsoft, but that AI-native vendors and neutral orchestration platforms
capture the workflow and execution layers above traditional ERP systems. AI-native companies benefit from cleaner architectures
and faster innovation, particularly in workflows such as accounts payable, procurement and HR. Yet incumbents retain advantages
in trusted data, governance, compliance and process depth. Our base case is coexistence: AI-native firms drive innovation at the
edges while incumbents retain the transactional core. The real risk for incumbents is not losing the ledger, but losing control of
the workflow layer where future value is created.
The broader implication is that ERP may be undergoing a business-model transition similar to the shift from perpetual
licenses to cloud subscriptions. Vendors are increasingly trying to monetize workflow execution rather than software access,
creating new opportunities around automation, orchestration and governance. However, investors should remain disciplined.
Many customers are likely to remain in the phase of copilots, recommendations and supervised workflows for several years.
The ultimate winners will be those that can demonstrate production adoption, measurable workflow automation and clear
monetization. In our view, Oracle has the strongest position today, but SAP and Microsoft offer the greatest strategic upside as the
industry moves toward enterprise execution. The next ERP cycle will not reward every vendor equally; it will reward those
that can turn intelligence into trusted execution.

WHY ERP IS AT AN INFLECTION POINT
We think that ERP is reaching an inflection point because its role is expanding from recording and standardizing
enterprise activity to helping organizations coordinate, recommend, and increasingly execute business actions.
For the past three decades, Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) has been the operational backbone of large organizations. Its
historical role was clear: bring order to fragmented business operations, standardize critical processes, enforce control, and
create a single trusted source of business information. ERP became the system through which companies recorded transactions,
managed workflows, monitored performance, and governed mission-critical activities across finance, procurement, supply chain,
manufacturing, human resources, and other enterprise functions.
That role remains essential. ERP is not being displaced as the system of record. On the contrary, its importance is increasing
because the next wave of enterprise software builds on the same foundations that made ERP critical in the first place: reliable
data, embedded business rules, process governance, compliance, and auditability.
What is expanding is the source of value. Historically, ERP helped enterprises understand what had happened. The next generation
of ERP is increasingly expected to help determine what should happen next and, in selected cases, trigger or coordinate the
actions required. This marks a shift from ERP as a passive system of record toward ERP as a more active system of intelligence,
orchestration, and execution.
This evolution is being enabled by three major technology shifts: agentic AI, real-time data connectivity, and modern cloud-native
architectures. Together, these capabilities allow ERP platforms to move beyond reporting and workflow automation. They enable
enterprise systems to observe business events, interpret operational signals, recommend responses, coordinate actions across
functions, and progressively automate execution within defined governance controls.
The importance of this shift is not only technological. It also reflects a deeper business need. Large organizations are under
pressure to become more productive, more agile, and faster in execution. Traditional ERP improved efficiency inside departments
and standardized end-to-end processes, but many enterprise activities still depend heavily on human coordination through
meetings, spreadsheets, approvals, manual reconciliations, and institutional knowledge. The next phase of ERP targets this
coordination layer directly.
In that sense, the ERP inflection is not simply about adding AI features to existing applications. It is about redefining the role of
enterprise software. The market is moving from transaction digitization toward coordination digitization; from systems that record
enterprise activity toward systems that can help orchestrate enterprise activity.
ERP’S HISTORICAL ROLE: FROM SYSTEM OF RECORD TO ENTERPRISE BACKBONE
ERP 1.0 - The system of record
ERP was originally designed to solve the problem of fragmented business information by creating one trusted system
of record.
Before ERP adoption became widespread, large organizations typically relied on disconnected systems, departmental databases,
spreadsheets, and manual processes. Finance, procurement, inventory management, manufacturing, and human resources often
operated with separate datasets and separate workflows. This fragmentation made it difficult to obtain a consistent view of the
business. It also created duplication of work, reconciliation issues, weak process control, and limited management visibility.
ERP emerged as a response to this complexity. Its original purpose was to centralize critical business transactions into a single
platform. Orders, invoices, payroll, inventory movements, purchase requests, and financial postings could be captured and
processed within one integrated environment. This created a common data foundation and a single source of truth for the
enterprise.
The value proposition of this first generation of ERP was not intelligence. It was control. ERP helped companies standardize
processes, improve data integrity, enforce compliance, and scale operations with greater discipline. It became the place where
critical business events were documented, governed, and reconciled.

At this stage, ERP was essentially a transactional engine. Its role was to ensure that the enterprise could trust its data and that
core processes could be executed consistently.
ERP 2.0 - The system of process
As organizations became more complex, ERP evolved from recording transactions to managing end-to-end business
processes.
Once ERP had established itself as the system of record, the next stage of value came from process integration. Organizations no
longer wanted only a centralized database. They wanted ERP to coordinate workflows across business functions.
Processes such as procure-to-pay, order-to-cash, hire-to-retire, and record-to-report became embedded within ERP systems.
This allowed organizations to reduce manual handoffs, enforce standardized procedures, and connect activities across finance,
procurement, supply chain, sales, manufacturing, and HR.
This was an important expansion of ERP’s role. ERP was no longer merely a financial ledger or a transaction repository. It became
the operational backbone of the enterprise. It connected functions, standardized execution, and enabled companies to manage
complex operations at scale.
However, execution remained largely human-led. ERP could structure the workflow, enforce the rules, and capture the output, but
people still interpreted information, made decisions, handled exceptions, and initiated the next actions. In practice, ERP improved
process efficiency but did not eliminate the coordination burden across departments.
This distinction matters. Traditional ERP digitized transactions and standardized workflows, but much of the real work of
coordination still happened outside the system.
ERP 3.0 - The system of insight
Cloud computing, analytics, and improved data integration transformed ERP into a stronger source of business insight,
but not yet into an execution platform.
The next phase of ERP was shaped by cloud architectures, modern analytics, machine learning, APIs, extensibility frameworks,
and improved integration with surrounding enterprise applications. These capabilities allowed ERP systems to become more
connected, more scalable, and more useful for decision-making.
ERP platforms increasingly provided dashboards, forecasts, predictive indicators, anomaly detection, and embedded
recommendations. Users could monitor trends, identify risks, and make better-informed decisions directly within business
workflows. Cloud delivery also helped reduce some of the technical friction associated with older, more monolithic ERP
environments.
This phase marked a meaningful improvement. ERP could now do more than capture transactions and manage workflows. It could
surface insights and help users understand what was happening across the business.
Yet a key limitation remained. Even when ERP generated valuable insights, the system still relied on users to interpret those
insights, decide what to do, and trigger the required actions. ERP could inform decision-making, but it did not fundamentally
change the execution model. It reduced technical complexity, but not necessarily decision complexity or coordination complexity.
This is why the transition now underway is more significant than a normal product upgrade. The next phase is not only about better
analytics. It is about moving from insight to action.

ERP 4.0: THE EMERGENCE OF ENTERPRISE RESOURCE EXECUTION
From ERP to ERX
The current ERP transition is different because it extends the platform’s role from insight into execution.
The concept of Enterprise Resource Execution (ERX) is emerging as the next stage in ERP’s evolution. In this framework, ERP
combines its traditional strengths in transactional reliability, process governance, and enterprise data with new capabilities in
intelligence, orchestration, extensibility, experience, and execution.
The distinction between ERP and ERX is important. Traditional ERP has primarily been retrospective and process-oriented. It
records transactions, structures workflows, and generates reports that help users understand past and current operations. ERX
extends this role. It aims to help the enterprise sense what is happening, reason about what should happen next, coordinate
activities across systems, and increasingly execute actions within defined rules and controls.

Agentic AI introduces a different paradigm. AI agents can observe business events, reason across datasets, coordinate tasks,
and perform selected actions within predefined policies and controls. Instead of simply surfacing information to users, ERP

combine enterprise data, process knowledge and governance into an AI-enabled execution platform.

Real-time data makes continuous execution possible
Real-time data connectivity allows ERP to move from periodic monitoring toward continuous interpretation and
response.
Earlier generations of ERP largely relied on historical data, scheduled reporting cycles, and periodic updates. This limited the
system’s ability to support fast operational responses. Companies could analyze what had happened, but the process of identifying
issues, deciding on actions, and coordinating responses often remained slow and manual.
Modern ERP architectures are increasingly built around APIs, event-driven workflows, and real-time data connectivity. This allows
systems to ingest operational signals continuously and react more quickly to changing business conditions.
The significance of this change is substantial. A platform that can continuously interpret business events can support faster
responses to supply chain disruptions, demand changes, operational bottlenecks, or financial exceptions. ERP therefore becomes
more involved in coordinating responses rather than simply documenting outcomes.

This is a necessary condition for the move toward ERX. Execution cannot become more intelligent if the system only sees the

Modern architectures enable intelligence at scale
Cloud-native, modular, and composable architectures make it easier to embed intelligence directly into business
processes.
Traditional ERP platforms were often monolithic and difficult to adapt. This limited the speed at which organizations could integrate
new applications, connect external data sources, or embed advanced analytics and automation into core processes.
Modern ERP environments are increasingly modular, API-driven, and designed to integrate with a broader ecosystem of
applications, services, and AI capabilities. This enables ERP to become less of a standalone application and more of a coordination
layer across the enterprise technology stack.
This architectural shift matters because agentic AI and real-time orchestration require access to data, rules, workflows, and
actions across multiple systems. A closed or rigid ERP architecture limits the ability to coordinate across functions. A more
composable architecture creates the conditions for ERP to connect systems, interpret context, and orchestrate agents across
end-to-end business processes.
In this model, ERP’s strategic value does not come only from owning data. It also comes from owning embedded business logic
and process rules. This supports the idea that the next phase of ERP is not just about “data as an asset,” but also about “rules as
an asset.” ERP vendors sit on top of decades of encoded enterprise process logic. If that logic can be combined with AI and real-
time orchestration, it becomes a powerful foundation for enterprise execution.
THE REAL SHIFT: FROM TRANSACTION DIGITIZATION TO COORDINATION DIGITIZATION
Why traditional ERP did not fully solve enterprise coordination
ERP digitized transactions and standardized processes, but many enterprise coordination costs remained outside the
system.
Previous ERP generations brought major improvements in control, consistency, and process efficiency. They helped organizations
replace fragmented systems with shared data models and standardized workflows. However, they did not fully eliminate the
coordination burden inside large enterprises.
Many end-to-end processes still require extensive human intervention. Business context, exception handling, approvals, trade-
offs, and cross-functional decisions often sit outside the system. Finance, procurement, supply chain, sales, HR, and operations
may all use ERP, but they still rely on people to interpret information, reconcile competing priorities, and coordinate decisions
across organizational silos.
This is why the next ERP transition is strategically important. The opportunity is not simply to automate more tasks inside an
application. The opportunity is to reduce the coordination costs that arise when people, processes, and applications operate
across fragmented organizational boundaries.

In other words, the next generation of ERP is not merely a better system of record. It is an attempt to become a system that

The three core ideas behind next-generation ERP
The most compelling way to explain the ERP inflection is through three shifts: transaction digitization to coordination
digitization, system of record to system of intelligence, and data as an asset to rules as an asset.
The first shift is from transaction digitization to coordination digitization. Traditional ERP digitized orders, invoices, inventory
movements, payroll entries, and financial postings. The next phase aims to digitize the coordination work that happens between
functions, systems, and people. This is probably the clearest explanation of why ERP must evolve.
The second shift is from system of record to system of intelligence. ERP will still need to preserve the trusted transactional core,
but value increasingly comes from the platform’s ability to interpret business state, generate recommendations, and support
execution. This provides the architectural bridge between traditional ERP and agentic ERP or ERX.
The third shift is from data as an asset to rules as an asset. Data remains essential, but ERP’s strategic value also lies in embedded
business logic, process rules, compliance structures, and control frameworks. These rules define how enterprises operate. If AI
agents are to execute safely, they need to operate within these rules. This strengthens the strategic importance of ERP because
ERP platforms already sit at the center of enterprise process governance.
Together, these three shifts provide a more compelling narrative than simply saying that ERP is becoming more intelligent. The
deeper point is that ERP is moving closer to the execution layer of the enterprise.

Technology and business needs are converging
The current ERP inflection is happening because the technology required for intelligent execution is emerging at the
same time as enterprises need faster decisions and greater productivity.
The shift now underway is not just another incremental ERP upgrade. It reflects a convergence of technology and business
pressure.
On the technology side, agentic AI enables systems to reason, recommend, coordinate, and execute selected actions. Real-time
data connectivity allows ERP platforms to continuously interpret operational signals. Cloud-native and composable architectures

On the business side, companies face increasing operational complexity, volatile demand, supply chain disruptions, competitive

Governance remains the constraint and the differentiator
Greater automation makes governance more important, not less important.
The vision of intelligent and increasingly autonomous ERP is compelling, but enterprise adoption will be constrained by the need
for trust, control, transparency, and accountability. Financial processes, compliance obligations, procurement controls, supply
chain execution, and mission-critical operations require auditability and human oversight.
This is why the transition is likely to be gradual. Autonomous execution will probably start with bounded, supervised, and well-

compliance, preserve audit trails, and ensure accountability.
It is also why autonomous ERP needs to be built within the ERP system and not via discrete agents external to the ERP system.
This point is central to the investment debate. The companies best positioned for the next ERP cycle will not necessarily be those
with the most ambitious AI messaging. They will be those that can combine intelligence with trust. In enterprise software, execution
without governance is not enough.
BULL AND BEAR ARGUMENTS
Bull case: ERP’s role expands, increasing strategic value
The bull case is that ERP becomes more valuable because AI expands its role from recording enterprise activity to
orchestrating enterprise execution.
The strongest bull argument is that ERP sits at the center of the enterprise’s most critical data, processes, and rules. If the next
phase of software value comes from coordinating decisions and actions across the organization, ERP vendors are well positioned
because they already manage the transactional backbone of the enterprise.
Agentic AI could therefore increase the strategic relevance of ERP rather than reduce it. AI needs reliable data, process context,
governance rules, and execution rights to act safely. ERP provides many of these foundations. This suggests that AI may reinforce

FROM AI-AUGMENTED ERP TO AUTONOMOUS ERP
THE EVOLUTION FROM SYSTEM OF RECORD TO SYSTEM OF ACTION
The next generation of ERP is not simply adding AI to existing workflows; it represents a fundamental shift from
recording business activity to orchestrating and executing business outcomes.
Historically, ERP systems emerged to create a single source of truth for enterprise data and processes. Over time they evolved
from transaction systems into process management platforms and later into systems capable of generating insights. The next
stage extends this evolution further: ERP becomes an active participant in enterprise operations, capable of understanding
context, recommending decisions, coordinating actions across applications, and increasingly executing tasks autonomously
within predefined guardrails.
Importantly, this transformation should not be viewed as a sudden leap from traditional ERP to fully autonomous operations.
Rather, it is an evolutionary journey characterized by multiple stages of maturity, each delivering incremental value while
progressively increasing the degree of automation and intelligence embedded in the system.
The ERP maturity model: From records to autonomous execution
The path toward autonomous ERP is best understood as a maturity journey in which ERP progressively moves from
recording transactions to governing autonomous business processes.
• Traditional ERP: The system of record
The first generation of ERP was designed to standardize business processes, centralize data, and create control. Its primary role
was to record transactions, enforce workflows, and provide auditability and compliance. ERP became the operational backbone
of organizations because it created a trusted and consistent representation of business reality across finance, procurement,
manufacturing, supply chain, and human resources. However, these systems remained largely passive. They recorded what had
already happened rather than helping determine what should happen next. Human users remained responsible for analysis,
decision-making, and action.
• AI-augmented ERP: The system of insight
The emergence of cloud platforms, analytics, APIs, and machine learning expanded ERP's role beyond transaction processing.
At this stage, ERP begins to generate recommendations, forecasts, anomaly detections, and natural-language insights through
copilots and embedded AI capabilities. The system becomes capable of helping users understand what is happening and what
may happen next. Nevertheless, AI remains primarily advisory. Humans still evaluate recommendations, approve decisions, and
execute actions. ERP becomes a system of insight rather than a system of execution.
• Agentic ERP: The system of supervised execution
The next step introduces AI agents capable of performing bounded tasks. These agents can monitor workflows, identify
exceptions, generate recommendations, trigger actions, and complete specific operational tasks under human supervision. Rather
than merely providing insights, ERP begins participating in execution. The emphasis remains on supervision, transparency, and
clear audit trails. Human approval remains central, especially for decisions carrying financial, operational, or regulatory risk.
• Semi-autonomous ERP: The orchestration layer
As multiple agents emerge across different domains, ERP increasingly evolves into an orchestration platform. It coordinates
actions across finance, procurement, supply chain, manufacturing, and external systems. Instead of automating isolated tasks,
ERP manages entire workflows, dynamically assigning responsibilities between humans and agents. At this point, the strategic
value shifts away from individual AI capabilities toward the ability to coordinate and govern interactions across the enterprise
application landscape.
• Autonomous ERP (ERX): The governed system of action
The final stage is the emergence of Autonomous ERX. Here, ERP becomes capable of executing predefined business processes
independently within enterprise-defined guardrails. Human users no longer spend most of their time processing transactions

The transformation toward autonomous ERP occurs because ERP is uniquely positioned to become the operational brain

ERP already controls many of the assets required for intelligent enterprise execution: business processes, master data,

The future architecture can be understood as a stack composed of six increasingly strategic layers.
• Layer 1: System of record
The bottom layer continues to hold transactional data, master data, financial records, and process histories. It remains the

The second layer connects ERP to external applications, cloud services, partner systems, and data platforms through APIs
and event-driven architectures. More importantly, this layer creates semantic consistency across the enterprise. Rather than

reasoning.
• Layer 3: Agent layer
On top of the semantic layer sits a growing population of specialized AI agents. Some agents analyze invoices, others forecast
demand, monitor inventory risks, optimize procurement decisions, or identify compliance exceptions. Each agent performs
a narrowly defined activity using enterprise context and business rules. Individually, these agents create productivity gains.
Collectively, they become the building blocks of intelligent enterprise execution.
• Layer 4: Orchestration layer
The orchestration layer is arguably the most strategically important element of the future ERP architecture. Its role is to coordinate
agents, workflows, applications, and human participants. It determines which agent should perform which task, when human
intervention is required, and how information flows across processes. Ownership of orchestration increasingly represents
ownership of the enterprise control plane.

Several design principles emerge consistently across the transition toward autonomous enterprise systems.

• Clean core and stable foundations. Autonomy requires a reliable operational foundation. Highly customized ERP
environments, fragmented integrations, and excessive technical debt make automation difficult to scale. A clean, upgradeable
core is therefore a prerequisite rather than an optional modernization initiative.
• Connected and semantic data. AI agents are only as effective as the information they consume. Autonomous ERP requires
unified, high-quality, real-time, business-contextualized data. The objective is not just integration but shared understanding
across the enterprise.
• Composable architecture. Future ERP environments must be modular, API-driven, and event-based. Composability allows
enterprises to add new agents, workflows, and capabilities without disrupting the core transactional platform. Innovation
occurs continuously at the edge while stability remains protected at the core.
• Rules, policies and governance as first-class assets. Business logic, governance policies, compliance rules, and approval
thresholds must be explicitly managed rather than buried within custom code. Autonomous execution depends on clear and
transparent rules that agents can interpret and follow.
• Observability and auditability. Every recommendation, decision, and action must remain traceable. Autonomous systems
require immutable audit trails, explanations, confidence levels, and operational transparency. Trust ultimately determines
adoption.
• Human-in-the-loop governance. Despite advances in AI, enterprise processes contain exceptions, judgment calls, and
regulatory responsibilities. Autonomous ERP therefore evolves toward "governed autonomy" rather than uncontrolled
automation. Humans increasingly move from operators to supervisors, policy owners, and exception managers.
For investors, these design principles create a practical test of product differentiation: agentic depth. A basic AI assistant can
summarize information, answer questions, or draft recommendations. That improves usability, but it does not necessarily change
the operating model of ERP. True agentic depth requires the system to reason over business context, plan a sequence of steps,
act through controlled system interfaces, handle exceptions, and remain observable, auditable, and controllable throughout the
process.
The bull case is that vendors with deeper agentic capabilities can address higher-value workflows. In finance, this could include
exception handling, reconciliation support, payment prioritization, or close-process automation. In supply chain and procurement,
it could include planning recommendations, supplier rerouting, inventory adjustments, guided sourcing, contract checks, or
purchase-order workflows. The more complex and multistep the workflow, the more valuable the agentic layer becomes, provided
that governance is strong enough.
The bear case is that agentic depth is difficult to scale safely. ERP workflows affect cash, compliance, suppliers, customers,
employees, and operational continuity. Generic AI is not enough. Vendors need domain-specific rules, permissions, observability,
audit trails, exception handling, and human-in-the-loop controls. Investors should therefore distinguish between vendors that add
AI at the user-interface layer and vendors that can prove controlled execution inside business-critical workflows. Note that this
bear case likely would lead to ERP as the center of autonomous ERP rather than an external system, but monetization takes time
and is not as large as potentially.

human capital management, complex billing, project accounting, resource allocation, time sheets, and client-specific contract

Why product-centric ERP has a structural advantage
Product-centric ERP starts with a structural advantage because its core processes are already highly standardized,
data-rich, and governed by explicit rules.
Manufacturing, procurement, production, inventory management, logistics, and order fulfillment typically follow well-defined
workflows, creating a clear chain of causality from demand planning through procurement and production to delivery and financial
settlement. These processes generate large volumes of structured transactional data, including bills of materials, routings,
purchase orders, inventory movements, production orders, supplier lead times, and capacity utilization metrics.
This matters because autonomous systems perform best when inputs, outputs, constraints, and objectives are clearly defined.
Product-centric environments provide exactly this foundation. They operate around measurable goals such as improving service
levels, reducing inventory, optimizing working capital, increasing throughput, and lowering operating costs. Because both the
decision variables and desired outcomes are observable, AI models can more easily forecast, optimize, and automate decision-

As a result, many of the prerequisites for autonomy are already present: standardized workflows, abundant structured data,
established governance, and a long history of operational automation.

The bull case is straightforward: product-centric ERP is naturally suited to autonomous execution. Its processes are repeatable,
its data is highly structured, and its decision logic can often be formalized into explicit business rules and optimization frameworks.
This creates an ideal environment for deploying AI agents in bounded operational workflows such as demand forecasting,
autonomous replenishment, inventory optimization, production planning, supplier risk management, and exception-based order
processing. These use cases combine high transaction volumes with clear success metrics, allowing organizations to measure
value and build trust in autonomous decision-making.
The economic benefits are also tangible. Better inventory planning can reduce working capital, improved production scheduling

adoption incentives are strong.
Consequently, product-centric ERP is likely to be one of the first areas where autonomous execution moves beyond decision

Bear case: Product-centric ERP may be slower than it appears.
The bear case is that technology is only one part of the equation. Many manufacturing and distribution companies operate highly

platforms, supplier networks, and plant-level infrastructure.
True autonomy therefore requires more than AI. It requires clean data, integrated architectures, real-time visibility, explainability,

significant barriers to end-to-end orchestration.
There is also a lower tolerance threshold for errors because ERP decisions can directly affect physical operations. Changes
to production schedules, inventory allocation, maintenance plans, or supplier orders can impact safety, compliance, customer
deliveries, and working capital. As a result, organizations may be reluctant to remove humans from the decision loop, even when
AI recommendations are reliable.
The key constraint is therefore not necessarily AI capability but organizational trust. Product-centric ERP may lead in AI-driven
optimization, yet the transition to fully autonomous execution is likely to be gradual, constrained by integration complexity,
operational risk, and governance requirements.
Why service-centric ERP faces a more complex path
Service-centric ERP faces a more difficult path to autonomy because its core workflows are less standardized, more
dependent on human judgment, and supported by less structured data than product-centric environments.
In service businesses, value creation revolves around people, skills, client relationships, project execution, and expertise. Decisions
such as staffing, resource allocation, pricing, or project management rarely follow a simple set of rules. They often require
balancing technical skills, availability, seniority, client familiarity, delivery risk, cost, and team dynamics. Unlike manufacturing
workflows, these decisions are highly contextual and difficult to codify into repeatable autonomous processes.
While service workflows can be digitized, they are often harder to standardize. Project scopes evolve, client requirements change,
and contracts vary across time-and-materials, fixed-price, outcome-based, and hybrid models. At the same time, critical data such
as skills inventories, time sheets, project status, and client satisfaction can be incomplete, subjective, or distributed across multiple
applications.
As a result, service-centric ERP often serves primarily as a financial and HR backbone while many operational activities remain
managed through project management, collaboration, ticketing, or delivery platforms. This fragmentation limits the amount of
context available to AI systems and makes end-to-end autonomous execution more challenging.
This does not mean service-centric ERP cannot become more autonomous. Functions such as payroll, expense management,
billing validation, recruiting, compliance, and workforce planning are well positioned for automation. However, achieving autonomy
across the full service delivery lifecycle remains more difficult because many workflows are coordination-intensive and exception-
driven.
Bull case: Service-centric ERP could leapfrog in selected domains.
The bull case is that service-centric ERP may adopt agentic capabilities rapidly in specific domains. Unlike manufacturing
organizations, many service businesses operate on modern cloud platforms and are not constrained by complex shop-floor
systems or physical production infrastructure. This creates a more flexible environment for deploying AI capabilities.
The opportunity lies in areas where processes are already relatively standardized. Human capital management, payroll, recruiting,
expense management, time tracking, billing validation, compliance monitoring, and workforce planning are natural candidates for

Bear case: These solutions may remain human-supervised for longer.
The bear case is that the most valuable service workflows are also the hardest to automate. Resource allocation, project recovery,
contract interpretation, pricing decisions, client engagement, and workforce management often depend on judgment, experience,
and contextual knowledge that cannot easily be reduced to predefined rules.
The fundamental challenge is that service businesses are people-centric. Employees are both the primary asset and the primary
source of value creation. Decisions about staffing, workload, utilization, and project assignments affect employee engagement,
client satisfaction, delivery quality, and retention. As a result, organizations may be reluctant to delegate these decisions entirely
to algorithms, regardless of technological progress.
Data quality is another constraint. Critical inputs such as skill profiles, time sheets, project health indicators, and client feedback
are often incomplete, subjective, or lagging. This creates a weaker foundation for autonomous decision-making than the highly
structured transactional data found in manufacturing environments.
Finally, many service organizations operate fragmented technology landscapes, relying on separate tools for project delivery,
collaboration, knowledge management, workflow tracking, and client interactions. Without deep integration across these
systems, ERP lacks the full operational context required for reliable autonomy.
As a result, service-centric ERP may experience rapid AI augmentation while progressing more slowly toward true autonomous
execution. AI is likely to become a powerful co-pilot for managers, consultants, recruiters, and finance teams, but human oversight
may remain essential for the most consequential decisions.
Two different autonomy curves
While both product-centric and service-centric ERP are moving toward greater autonomy, the pace and scope of
that transformation will differ because the underlying processes, data structures, and decision requirements are
fundamentally different.
Product-centric ERP is likely to move faster in bounded operational workflows because the processes are more structured, the
data is more transactional, and the optimization objectives are clearer. This creates a better foundation for autonomous planning,
procurement, inventory management, production scheduling, and supply-chain orchestration.
Service-centric ERP is likely to move faster in selected administrative and people-related workflows where automation can reduce
manual effort without taking over complex judgment. However, broader autonomy will probably be slower because service delivery
depends more heavily on context, relationships, skills, exceptions, and human oversight.
The most balanced conclusion is that product-centric ERP has a stronger technical foundation for autonomy, while service-centric
ERP may show faster visible AI adoption in discrete workflow areas. Product-centric ERP may lead in autonomous execution.
Service-centric ERP may lead in AI-assisted coordination. But neither path is automatic. In both cases, the pace will depend on
data quality, process standardization, system integration, governance, and organizational trust.
In summary, product-centric ERP should generally progress faster toward autonomous execution because it is built around
structured workflows, codified rules, and measurable operational outcomes. Service-centric ERP will probably evolve more
gradually because a larger share of value creation depends on human judgment, client context, and coordination across people.
However, service-centric ERP can still move quickly in selected domains such as HCM, billing validation, payroll, time tracking, and
workforce planning, where processes are sufficiently standardized and risk can be controlled.

The key investor debate: Who will control enterprise execution?
The central investment question is no longer who owns the system of record, but who becomes the control layer for
enterprise execution.
The bull case for ERP vendors is compelling. ERP platforms already sit at the intersection of business processes, transactional
records, permission structures, compliance controls, and business rules. As organizations adopt AI agents, those agents will need
trusted data, process context, governance frameworks, and execution rights to operate safely. ERP systems already own many of
these foundational assets. In this scenario, AI increases rather than diminishes ERP’s strategic importance. ERP evolves from a
system that records enterprise activity into a platform that coordinates and increasingly executes enterprise activity.
Importantly, value creation does not require immediate full autonomy. Significant benefits can emerge well before organizations
reach autonomous execution. Productivity improvements, workflow automation, faster decision-making, lower coordination
costs, and improved process consistency can all create measurable value during the transition from AI-assisted ERP to agentic
ERP. The addressable market therefore expands beyond transaction processing toward decision support, orchestration, and
execution.
The bear case is that the orchestration layer may not ultimately belong to ERP vendors. Most enterprises operate heterogeneous
environments spanning ERP, CRM, supply chain software, workflow platforms, collaboration tools, data platforms, and industry-
specific applications. In such environments, customers may prefer a neutral orchestration layer capable of coordinating actions
across the entire technology stack rather than relying on the control plane of a single ERP vendor. ERP would remain mission-
critical, but a significant share of the new value pool could migrate above the ERP layer.
There is also a risk that the transition proves slower than current market narratives imply. Many organizations continue to struggle
with fragmented data, customized workflows, legacy integrations, and inconsistent process governance. AI capabilities may
improve rapidly, but enterprise adoption depends on trust, auditability, explainability, and organizational readiness. Financial
processes, procurement controls, compliance-sensitive workflows, and mission-critical operations are unlikely to become
fully autonomous overnight. As a result, many customers may remain at the stage of copilots, recommendations, supervised
automation, and bounded agentic workflows for an extended period.
A further risk is that vendors overstate the maturity of their products. The market is increasingly exposed to the risk of “agent
washing,” where assistive AI features are marketed as autonomous capabilities. Investors should therefore distinguish between
vendors that add AI at the user-interface layer and vendors that can demonstrate controlled execution inside business-critical
workflows. The key question is not whether the software can generate recommendations, but whether it can safely execute
meaningful work within enterprise-defined guardrails.
Ultimately, the winners are likely to be those platforms that combine four capabilities: a trusted transactional core, strong
governance, modern composable architecture, and credible orchestration of AI-driven workflows. The strategic battleground
is moving from data management toward the coordination of agents, applications, and business processes. The highest-value
position in the software stack may therefore belong not to the platform that stores the most enterprise data, but to the platform
that most effectively translates intelligence into trusted execution.
A practical way to evaluate this transition is to assess vendors along four dimensions: AI integration depth, relevance to target
customers, monetization readiness, and execution credibility.

FROM ERP TO ENTERPRISE EXECUTION PLATFORMS: THE NEW ECONOMICS
FROM SOFTWARE ACCESS TO WORKFLOW EXECUTION
The central implication for software providers is that ERP value may shift from selling access to software toward
managing execution across enterprise workflows. Traditional ERP monetized users, modules, maintenance, upgrades and
cloud subscriptions. Next-generation ERP adds a new layer: software that can interpret business context, coordinate workflows,
recommend actions, trigger execution and enforce governance across finance, HR, procurement, supply chain and adjacent
systems. In simple terms, ERP moves from being a system that records work to a system that increasingly helps run work.
This is not a story about adding AI features to an existing product. It is a deeper business model transition. If ERP vendors
can embed agents and orchestration into mission-critical workflows, they may capture value from the execution of business
processes, not just from user access to applications. The strategic question for investors is therefore not “which vendor has a
copilot?” but “which vendor controls the workflow layer where AI creates economic value?”
The investment debate is becoming sharper because the control point in enterprise software may change. ERP vendors
have a strong claim to this control point because they sit close to trusted data, process logic, permissions, audit trails and system-
of-record execution. But they are not the only contenders. Hyperscalers, AI model providers, data platforms, neutral orchestration
layers, services firms and AI-native entrants may also try to capture the new intelligence and orchestration layer. The upside case
is that ERP vendors become the AI operating layer of the enterprise. The downside case is that ERP remains essential, but part
of the incremental AI value migrates elsewhere.

THE BUSINESS MODEL MOVES UP THE VALUE STACK: FROM MODULES TO WORKFLOW OWNERSHIP
Next-generation ERP expands the vendor business model from selling modules to owning workflow outcomes. The
old model sold systems of record. The new model may sell systems that detect issues, recommend decisions, coordinate tasks
and execute bounded workflows under governance. This creates new monetization layers, including premium AI editions, copilot
access, agent entitlements, orchestration services, governance modules, API usage, data services and potentially outcome-linked
fees.
The strongest business model shift is from “software used by people” to “software that performs work with people
supervising it.” This distinction matters because user access and workflow execution are priced differently. A user seat prices
the right to use software. A workflow agent can price the automation, acceleration or improvement of work. If customers believe
the software is reducing manual effort, improving cycle times or lowering operational risk, the vendor has a stronger basis for
premium pricing.
The bull case is that ERP vendors expand their monetization surface. They can charge for high-value automation in areas
such as financial close, procurement exception handling, forecasting, compliance workflows, workforce planning, inventory
optimization and governance. The rationale is straightforward: customers may accept higher spending when the value is tied to
measurable productivity, risk reduction, better working capital or faster decision-making.
The bear case is that AI becomes a cost of staying relevant rather than a new profit pool. If customers view AI as standard
functionality, vendors may have to bundle it into existing subscriptions while absorbing higher R&D, compute and support costs. In
that scenario, AI improves the product but not necessarily the financial model. The risk is that “smart ERP” becomes commoditized
before vendors can charge enough to offset the cost of building and running it.
THE ECOSYSTEM BECOMES THE NEW COMPETITIVE MOAT
Next-generation ERP is not a standalone product story; it is an ecosystem story. ERP vendors increasingly need deep
partnerships with hyperscalers for infrastructure, AI model providers for frontier capabilities, services firms for implementation
and change management, ISVs (Independent Software Vendors) for industry extensions, data platforms for integration, and
implementation partners for repeatable deployment. The vendor that wins is unlikely to be the one that builds everything alone.
The winner is more likely to be the one that orchestrates the stack while keeping control of the workflow and governance layer.
Hyperscaler partnerships are strategically necessary, but they are also a source of strategic tension. ERP vendors need
scalable cloud infrastructure, access to advanced models, security tooling and distribution reach. However, if the customer’s AI
experience, agent routing and orchestration logic are controlled mainly by the hyperscaler, the ERP vendor may lose pricing power
over the layer where AI value is created. The partnership is therefore both an enabler and a possible disintermediation risk.
ISVs and implementation partners become more important as ERP becomes more vertical and workflow-specific. AI
value is unlikely to come only from generic assistants. It is more likely to come from repeatable industry workflows, pre-configured
templates, domain-specific automation and marketplace extensions. This favors vendors with strong partner ecosystems,
because they can serve long-tail industry demand without building every workflow internally.
Services providers remain critical because agentic ERP is an operating-model change, not just a software upgrade.
Enterprises need help with clean core, data quality, governance, integration, process redesign, testing, change management and
user adoption. Even if AI automates part of the implementation effort, it also creates demand for higher-value transformation
work. The services layer therefore does not disappear; it shifts toward modernization, orchestration, governance and AI-enabled
managed services.

PRICING SHIFTS FROM ACCESS TO VALUE DELIVERED
ERP pricing is likely to evolve gradually rather than change overnight. The base subscription remains important, but vendors
are likely to add new AI pricing layers on top. These may include premium product editions, AI copilots, agent bundles, usage
credits, module-specific AI add-ons, workflow automation fees and selective outcome-linked pricing.
The likely end state is hybrid pricing. Most ERP vendors are unlikely to move entirely away from subscriptions or seats. A more
realistic model combines a base subscription, premium AI tiers, a consumption layer for AI usage and outcome-linked elements in
selected workflows where value can be identified and measured. This matters because the transition needs to preserve recurring
revenue visibility while creating room for AI monetization.
Outcome-based pricing is attractive, but investors should not overstate its near-term impact. Vendors can frame AI
around faster closes, lower inventory, better forecasts, improved cash flow or lower manual effort. However, true outcome
contracts require measurement, attribution and risk-sharing. In practice, the near-term pricing model is more likely to be premium

REVENUE GROWTH COMES FROM DEEPER MONETIZATION OF THE INSTALLED BASE
The biggest near-term revenue opportunity is likely to come from the installed base, not only from new logos. The Cloud
migration has already created a recurring-revenue foundation. AI adds another monetization layer through premium bundles,
agents, workflow automation, analytics, governance and consumption. The growth opportunity is therefore less about replacing
ERP systems and more about expanding revenue per customer.
This matters because ERP vendors already sit inside mission-critical workflows. If vendors can attach AI to finance,
procurement, HR and supply-chain processes, they can expand wallet share without waiting for a full vendor replacement cycle.
This is especially important as the cloud migration process matures and the easiest migration cohorts have already moved. AI
becomes the next lever for growth acceleration.
The broader ERP market still provides a supportive backdrop, but AI must increasingly drive the next leg of growth.
Gartner expects continued ERP market growth through 2030, with SaaS ERP growing faster than on-premise ERP and small and
mid-sized companies representing an important growth pool. However, the investment conclusion is that cloud provides stability,
while AI must provide the next layer of upside.
The growth mix changes as AI adoption rises. Vendors may not always generate growth through more seats. In some
workflows, automation could reduce the need for human users. But vendors may offset this through premium AI features, usage
credits, workflow automation, governance modules, ecosystem marketplaces and higher retention. The quality of growth may
improve if revenue becomes more recurring, more usage-based and more tied to high-value workflows.
The bull case is that AI creates a new product cycle within ERP. AI can re-accelerate mature vendors by creating new
modules, improving attach rates, lifting ARPU and increasing retention. The rationale is that ERP has a large installed base and
many high-value workflows where automation can be attached progressively. Vendors do not need full autonomous ERP to
monetize the transition; they can monetize copilots, agents, governance and orchestration step by step.
The bear case is that AI may cannibalize parts of the existing model before it creates enough new revenue. Automation
can reduce manual effort, shrink some services work and pressure seat-based pricing. If AI capabilities commoditize quickly,
vendors may face higher R&D and compute costs without sufficient ARPU uplift. In that scenario, ERP remains durable but behaves
more like mission-critical utility software than a growth compounder.
GROSS MARGIN: TURNING ERP COMPLEXITY INTO SCALABLE SOFTWARE ECONOMICS
For ERP vendors, the gross margin question is not simply whether AI lowers costs; it is whether next-generation ERP
can turn historically complex, services-heavy workflows into more repeatable software-led delivery. ERP has always
carried a margin tension: the software itself is high margin, but deployment, customization, support, integration and upgrades
can be complex. Next-generation ERP can improve this equation if AI helps standardize configuration, automate testing, reduce
manual support, guide users inside workflows and make implementation more template-driven. In that sense, AI matters because
it can make ERP delivery more scalable, not because AI is valuable in isolation.
The positive gross-margin case is that ERP vendors use AI to reduce the cost-to-serve across the full ERP lifecycle. In
implementation, AI can help automate documentation, configuration support, testing and migration assistance. In live operations,
AI can reduce support tickets, guide users through complex processes and automate routine exception handling. In upgrades,
cleaner architectures and embedded intelligence can reduce the manual effort historically associated with ERP change cycles.
The margin benefit comes from reducing the amount of human effort needed to deploy, run and expand ERP, while increasing the
share of revenue captured through software subscriptions, premium modules and usage-based AI capabilities.
This is particularly important because ERP gross margins are affected by customer complexity. A clean, cloud-based
ERP customer with standardized processes should be easier to support and expand than a heavily customized legacy customer.
Next-generation ERP can therefore improve margins most where vendors combine cloud architecture, clean-core discipline, pre-
configured industry templates, embedded AI and strong partner execution. The point is not that AI automatically raises margins.
The point is that AI can help ERP vendors reduce the complexity tax that has historically surrounded ERP deployment and support.
The revenue mix also matters for gross margins. If AI-enabled ERP is monetized through premium editions, automation
modules, agent entitlements, governance tools and consumption credits, the incremental revenue should be more software-like
than services-like. That can lift blended gross margins, especially for vendors that attach AI to existing cloud ERP subscriptions.

Operating margins in next-generation ERP are likely to follow a J-curve because vendors must invest first before

term margin opportunity is attractive.
The first phase of the J-curve is the ERP reinvestment phase. Vendors need to invest in an AI-native product roadmaps,
clean-core migration tools, workflow agents, process orchestration, role-based permissions, auditability, monitoring, model
partnerships and customer enablement. They also need to help their ecosystems adapt, because ERP adoption depends heavily
on implementation partners, ISVs and services providers. These costs sit in R&D, sales and marketing, customer success and
partner enablement. The operating margin pressure is therefore not only an AI cost issue; it is the cost of repositioning ERP from
a system of record to a system of governed execution.
The second phase is the proof phase, where vendors must show that AI-enabled ERP creates measurable customer
value. This is critical because ERP buyers are unlikely to pay materially more for agentic capabilities without evidence of

differs from simpler software categories: customer trust, process control and auditability are central to monetization.
The third phase is the scaling phase, where repeatable ERP workflows can become a source of operating leverage. Once
vendors have reusable agents, workflow templates, governance modules and pre-configured industry processes, each additional
customer deployment should require less incremental effort. Sales motions can become more repeatable, support intensity can
decline, and partner delivery can become more standardized. This is the real ERP margin opportunity: not AI experimentation, but
productized workflow automation sold repeatedly across a large installed base.

THE RACE TO CONTROL ENTERPRISE EXECUTION
THE INVESTMENT DEBATE: WHO BECOMES THE ENTERPRISE EXECUTION LAYER?
The next phase of ERP competition is no longer about who has the largest system of record; it is about who can turn
that system of record into a trusted execution layer. That distinction matters. Traditional ERP value came from owning critical
transactions, standardizing business processes, and creating a reliable operational backbone. The next stage is more ambitious.
ERP platforms are now being judged on whether they can use AI agents, enterprise data, business rules, workflow orchestration,
and governance controls to help companies execute work more intelligently and safely.
This is why market positioning should be assessed differently from a normal ERP share-gain debate. A vendor may have
a large installed base, but that does not automatically make it a leader in Autonomous ERP. The relevant question is whether the
platform has the right combination of agentic capability, trusted data, governance, workflow breadth, ecosystem openness, and
monetization credibility. In other words, investors should ask a simple question: can the vendor move from assisting users to safely
executing meaningful work inside mission-critical processes?
Our scorecard is designed to separate AI visibility from AI value. We use six dimensions. Agentic capability carries the highest
weight at 25%, because the core test is whether the system can perform useful work rather than merely assist the user. Data and
semantic foundation carries 20%, because agents need trusted transactional data, process context, business rules, metadata, and
clean-core architecture before they can act. Governance and trust also carries 20%, because ERP touches financial, operational,
HR, procurement, and compliance-sensitive workflows where enterprises will not accept autonomy without permissions, audit
trails, approval checkpoints, explainability, and human control.
The remaining dimensions capture the commercial and strategic reach of the platform. Workflow breadth carries 15%,
because a broader footprint across finance, procurement, supply chain, HR, manufacturing, projects, and service workflows
makes the platform more credible as an enterprise execution layer. Ecosystem and extensibility carry 10%, because most large
enterprises operate mixed software estates and need APIs, developer tools, partners, third-party agents, and cross-platform
integration. Monetization and adoption credibility also carries 10%, because AI capability only matters financially if it drives usage,
retention, ARPU, premium bundles, workflow pricing, or customer adoption.
The ranking should be read directionally, not as a precise forecast. Our assessment combines qualitative judgment and
quantitative scoring. It should therefore be read as a directional framework, not as a precise measurement of future market share,
product maturity, or financial upside. Several inputs rely on subjective assumptions, particularly around agentic depth, governance
maturity, ecosystem openness, and adoption credibility. Small changes to these assumptions can meaningfully change the ranking.
The approach is intentionally hybrid because this market is still early, but it remains imperfect. It does not yet fully capture the
scale, pace, or effectiveness of each company’s ongoing AI investments, nor does it fully account for how quickly customers can
translate those investments into production use cases.
It is still early days, so points can and will change. AI maturating and adoption is relatively early and especially in enterprise
application software therefore the numbers and the ranking can and will change and therefore this really should be considered
as a directional indicator.

INVESTMENT TAKEAWAYS: PROCESS DEPTH VERSUS INTERFACE CONTROL
The three-year investment debate comes down to a clean strategic question: will the winning layer be the ERP process
layer or the agentic interface layer? If the winning layer is the trusted execution layer embedded inside mission-critical ERP
processes, SAP looks best placed. It has the deepest enterprise process semantics and the strongest claim to governed execution
in complex workflows. If the winning layer is the agentic front door through which users access and coordinate work across
applications, Microsoft looks like the most dangerous challenger.
Oracle sits between these two worlds as the strongest integrated-stack player. It may not be as open as Microsoft, and it
may not have SAP’s process depth in the most complex enterprise estates, but it has a coherent and credible closed-loop model.
For customers that are comfortable standardizing around Oracle, this can be a real advantage. Oracle does not need to be the
neutral control plane for everyone. It can still be a powerful winner inside Oracle-centric environments.
Workday and Sage are better understood as focused winners rather than broad-platform contenders. Workday can win in
people-centric and finance-led workflows where trust, data quality, and process consistency matter. Sage can win in practical mid-
market finance automation where simplicity and targeted productivity gains matter. Both can create value. But neither currently
screens as a broad enterprise execution platform in the same way as SAP, Microsoft, or Oracle.
What investors should watch
The first investor test is whether agentic capability moves from demos into production workflows. Basic AI assistants can
summarize, search, draft, and recommend. That is useful, but it is not enough to redefine ERP economics. The more important test
is whether agents can investigate exceptions, trigger workflows, prepare actions, route approvals, and execute bounded tasks
inside governed business processes. This is where the gap between AI marketing and AI value should become visible.
The second test is whether the vendor has the data and semantic foundation required for trustworthy autonomy.
Agents need more than access to data. They need business meaning. They need to understand customers, products, suppliers,
contracts, policies, exception rules, approval rights, and process dependencies. Vendors with stronger transaction data, cleaner
architectures, deeper process rules, and better semantic layers should have a better chance of converting AI into execution.
The third test is governance. ERP touches mission-critical workflows, and enterprises will not give autonomy to systems they
cannot control. Permissions, audit trails, explainability, approval checkpoints, autonomy ceilings, and human override rights are
not nice-to-have features. They are the adoption gate. In this market, governance is not a brake on innovation. It is the condition
that makes innovation usable.
The fourth test is ecosystem reach. Most enterprises will not be single-vendor environments. The winning platform needs to
work across mixed estates, connect to third-party systems, support partners and developers, and coordinate across applications.
This is why Microsoft’s ecosystem matters, why SAP’s BTP and Business Data Cloud execution matters, and why Oracle’s more
integrated model is both a strength and a constraint.

The fifth test is monetization. Investors should be careful not to confuse free AI features with financial upside. AI only changes

FINAL VIEW: FROM AI CLAIMS TO CONTROLLED EXECUTION
The ERP market is entering a more selective phase where AI should widen the gap between true execution platforms
and ordinary application vendors. Today, Oracle has the strongest current integrated-stack story. SAP has the deepest process
asset base but needs modernization and customer upgrading to Cloud to unlock it. Microsoft has the strongest agentic interface
and ecosystem, even if it does not always own the deepest ERP record. Workday is a high-quality specialist in HCM and finance.
Sage is a pragmatic mid-market finance automation story.
Over three years, SAP and Microsoft look like the two most strategically interesting names, but for very different
reasons. SAP has the stronger claim to the governed ERP execution layer. Microsoft has the stronger claim to the agentic front
door and orchestration layer. Oracle remains highly credible where customers accept an Oracle-centric model. Workday should
remain a premium vertical workflow compounder. Sage can create value through practical automation, but it is unlikely to become
a broad Autonomous ERP control plane.
The punchline for investors is simple: the next ERP cycle will not reward every vendor equally. The market should reward
platforms that turn AI into controlled execution, workflow ownership, measurable customer value, and monetization. It should be
more skeptical of vendors that market agents without production evidence, bundle AI defensively, lose the interaction layer, or fail
to control the cost of delivering AI at scale. The next ERP battle is not about who talks most convincingly about AI. It is about who
can make enterprise work happen safely, repeatedly, and profitably.

COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE: INCUMBENTS VS AI-NATIVE COMPANIES
THE COMPETITIVE DEBATE IS NOT ABOUT REPLACING ERP, BUT ABOUT CONTROLLING ENTERPRISE EXECUTION
The most important competitive battle is no longer who owns the system of record, but who controls the layer where
enterprise work gets orchestrated and executed.
Investor excitement around AI-native ERP companies has created a narrative that a new generation of vendors (e.g., Rillet,
Campfire, DualEntry) could disrupt established leaders such as SAP, Oracle, Microsoft, Workday, and Sage. While AI-native
companies are introducing genuinely new approaches to enterprise software, the reality is more nuanced. The competitive threat
is not that large enterprises suddenly abandon their ERP systems. Instead, the more credible scenario is that new AI-native vendors
and neutral AI platforms begin to capture the user interface, workflow orchestration, and execution layers that sit above traditional
ERP systems. Meanwhile, incumbents retain control of the underlying transactional core where critical business data, controls,
and governance reside.
This distinction is important for investors. ERP systems are deeply embedded in enterprise operations and are often intertwined
with financial controls, compliance requirements, audit processes, and industry-specific workflows. Full ERP solutions are
incredibly difficult to replicate and building the full depth and breadth needed to replace an ERP system would, even with AI
development tools, be expensive and time-consuming to build. These systems are not easily replaced. As a result, the competitive
landscape is likely to evolve through coexistence and gradual value migration rather than wholesale disruption.
WHERE AI-NATIVE COMPANIES HAVE A GENUINE ADVANTAGE
AI-native vendors benefit from starting with modern architectures specifically designed for automation and
autonomous workflows.
The strongest advantage of AI-native ERP vendors is that they are not burdened by decades of legacy technology.
These companies can build systems around agents, conversational interfaces, APIs, workflow automation, and event-driven
architectures from day one. AI is not an additional feature layered onto an existing platform; it is embedded into the core design
of the product.
This architectural advantage makes AI-native vendors faster and more flexible. They can innovate rapidly, deploy new capabilities
quickly, and optimize workflows around automation rather than around traditional software screens and manual processes.
Their strongest opportunities are concentrated in areas where large incumbents are structurally weaker. These include small
and mid-sized businesses, greenfield deployments without existing ERP systems (and lacking in complex ERP system of record
requirements), and highly specific business processes that generate significant operational friction. Finance close, accounts
payable automation, revenue recognition, procurement intake, inventory exceptions, and HR service delivery are examples
of workflows where focused AI-native vendors may deliver faster innovation and better user experiences than broader ERP
platforms.
Another advantage is that many AI-native companies are being designed as execution layers rather than as complete ERP
replacements. Instead of asking customers to rip out existing systems, they integrate into modern software environments and
automate high-value workflows on top of existing infrastructure. This lowers the barrier to adoption and allows customers to
realize benefits more quickly.
WHY INCUMBENTS STILL HOLD STRONG COMPETITIVE POSITIONS
Incumbents may be slower to move, but they control the data, processes, governance, and trust that enterprise AI
ultimately depends on.
While AI-native vendors enjoy architectural advantages, incumbents possess assets that are difficult to replicate. Enterprise ERP
is not simply a software application; it is the operational backbone of large organizations. Companies rely on ERP systems for
business rules, permissions, audit trails, master data, compliance controls, and workflow execution. These functions become even
more important as enterprises introduce AI agents that need to act autonomously.

This creates a structural advantage for incumbents. If AI agents need access to trusted data and governed workflows, the ERP
platforms that already manage those processes remain strategically important. Rather than making ERP less relevant, AI may

Incumbents also benefit from their massive installed bases, extensive partner ecosystems, long-standing customer relationships,
and proven compliance capabilities. Large enterprises, especially those operating in regulated industries, continue to prioritize
security, auditability, and governance when evaluating mission-critical software. These are areas where incumbents possess
decades of experience and credibility.
Importantly, incumbents also have multiple paths to monetizing AI. They do not need fully autonomous systems to generate new
revenue streams. Copilots, workflow automation, data services, governance capabilities, premium AI features, and agent-based
functionality can all be commercialized before fully autonomous ERP becomes a reality.
Lastly, while pulling data from ERP (or any complex system of record) is relatively easy and painless, updating that data from an
external third party system is not. Any mistakes can create data corruption and compliance issues. In addition, mixing vendors
increases the finger pointing and places far more pressure and responsibility on IT.
THE KEY WEAKNESSES FOR BOTH GROUP
AI-native vendors lack enterprise scale and trust, while incumbents are constrained by legacy complexity and
modernization challenges.
The main challenge facing AI-native vendors is scale. Most remain focused on a relatively narrow set of workflows, often
concentrated in finance and accounting. They generally lack the breadth of functionality required by multinational enterprises and
have not yet established the compliance, governance, and security credentials expected by highly regulated customers.
Incumbents face the opposite problem. Their biggest obstacle is not technology innovation but customer readiness. Many
enterprise environments remain heavily customized and fragmented after years of modifications. The process of creating a clean,
modern foundation for AI-enabled workflows can take years. As a result, the largest customers may also be the slowest to realize
meaningful AI benefits. Note that this data fragmentation problem also is a challenge for AI-natives who have to access and update
multiple systems of record.
There is also a strategic risk that value gradually migrates toward neutral orchestration layers. If users increasingly interact with AI
agents, workflow platforms, or data environments instead of directly interacting with ERP applications, part of the value creation
could shift away from traditional ERP vendors.
THE EMERGING BATTLEFIELD: THE AI EXECUTION LAYER
The most valuable position may be the platform that coordinates work across systems rather than the platform that
stores the data.
A new competitive layer is emerging across enterprise software. Rather than competing solely through functionality, vendors are
increasingly competing to become the execution and orchestration layer that coordinates actions across multiple applications.
This development creates opportunities for both camps.
For incumbents, success depends on leveraging their deep process knowledge, governance frameworks, and transactional
systems to become trusted platforms for AI-driven execution. SAP, for example, benefits from deep enterprise process expertise,
while Microsoft benefits from broad AI distribution and a horizontal orchestration ecosystem that spans a wide range of workplace
applications.
For AI-native vendors, the opportunity lies in delivering superior workflow automation, faster deployment, and better user
experiences. Their goal is not necessarily to replace ERP systems immediately, but instead to own the interaction layer where
business activities are planned, coordinated, and executed.
In practice, this suggests that the next phase of competition will be fought over workflow orchestration and execution rather
than over traditional ERP functionality alone.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR INVESTORS
The most likely outcome is coexistence, with incumbents retaining the core while AI-native vendors drive innovation
at the edges.
Our base case is not a rapid shift toward fully autonomous ERP. Instead, the next several years are more likely to bring supervised
agentic execution in selected workflows, stronger copilots, increasing automation, and growing orchestration capabilities across
finance, procurement, supply chain, and HR.
From an investment perspective, the strongest incumbent positions appear to belong to SAP and Microsoft. SAP benefits from
unmatched process depth and governance assets, while Microsoft combines a powerful AI ecosystem with broad enterprise
distribution. Oracle also remains well positioned within customers that are committed to the Oracle stack, while Workday and Sage
maintain strong positions within their respective domains.
The most credible threat from AI-native companies is not the replacement of enterprise ERP platforms. Rather, it is their ability to
win greenfield deployments, dominate selected high-friction workflows, and gradually capture the user interface, orchestration,
and monetization layers that sit above incumbent systems. For incumbents, the greatest long-term risk is not losing the ledger. It
is losing control of the workflow layer where a growing share of future value may be created.
Ultimately, the winners are likely to be the vendors that combine automation with trust. AI-native companies bring speed and
architectural simplicity, while incumbents bring governance, scale, and enterprise credibility. The competitive landscape is
therefore less a story of disruption versus survival and more a story of how value is redistributed across the enterprise software
stack.

HISTORY OF ERP: SIX DECADES AT THE CORE OF ENTERPRISE IT ECOSYSTEM
ERP has become a standard part of the business vocabulary, but its history stretches back much further than many people
realize. Over the past six decades, ERP systems have evolved through multiple generations of technology, expanding from basic
manufacturing tools into intelligent platforms, driven by AI, that support enterprise-wide decision-making (Exhibit 37).

In the 1960s, manufacturers developed the first Material Requirements Planning (MRP) systems. These early solutions were
designed to track inventory levels and coordinate production schedules. Because they ran on expensive mainframe computers and
required dedicated IT teams to maintain, they were largely limited to large manufacturing organizations with significant technology
resources.
During the 1970s, adoption of MRP systems broadened and the first software vendors emerged. Companies no longer had to build
these systems internally, although implementations remained complex and costly. At this stage, the software continued to focus
primarily on manufacturing activities, including bill-of-material (BOM) management, production scheduling, and inventory control.
The 1980s saw the introduction of Manufacturing Resource Planning (MRP II) systems. These solutions, including SAP, extended
beyond basic inventory management to include capabilities such as capacity planning, shop-floor scheduling, and demand
forecasting. For the first time, multiple departments involved in the production process could operate within a shared system,
improving coordination across manufacturing operations.
A major transformation occurred in the 1990s with the emergence of Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems. ERP
introduced the concept of a unified information architecture, centered around a single database shared across multiple business
functions. This foundation enabled organizations to integrate finance, human resources, procurement, sales, and manufacturing
into a common platform. The ERP model established during this period, including SAP and Oracle, remains the basis of many
enterprise systems today.
In the 2000s, ERP evolved into what became known as ERP II. These internet-enabled systems extended connectivity beyond
internal business processes and integrated with applications such as customer relationship management (CRM) and e-commerce
platforms. Cloud-based ERP solutions, including NetSuite and Workday, also began gaining traction. By reducing infrastructure
requirements and lowering implementation costs, cloud deployment made ERP systems accessible to a much broader range of
small and mid-sized businesses.
The 2010s brought another significant step forward as ERP systems started processing data in real time. Advances in machine
learning and the Internet of Things (IoT) enabled connected devices to feed operational data directly into ERP platforms. This
improved organizations’ ability to respond quickly to supply chain disruptions and enhanced predictive analytics capabilities.
In the 2020s, artificial intelligence became embedded throughout ERP platforms. This development completed the transition of
ERP from a system primarily used to record transactions into a platform that actively supports decision-making. AI can monitor
business performance, identify exceptions, and recommend actions. Generative AI further extends these capabilities by helping
users create reports, draft documents, and interpret complex datasets using natural language.
Taken together, this evolution reflects a broader shift in the role of ERP. What began as a manufacturing planning tool has
become the digital core of the enterprise, integrating business processes, providing real-time insights, and increasingly supporting
intelligent, AI-driven decision-making.
