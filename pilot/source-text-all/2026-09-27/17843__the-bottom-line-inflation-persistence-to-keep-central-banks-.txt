TOP STORIES OF THE UPCOMING WEEK
US NFPs likely rose moderately after the August jump  
US non-farm payrolls (NFPs) for August were stronger than expected, rising by a whopping 162k as most 
sectors of the economy recorded gains. Sizeable upward revisions to previous data lifted the three-month 
average payroll growth to around 70k, broadly in line with the pace of new hiring we deem consistent with 
the labour market remaining in equilibrium. The strength of the August NFP data partly reflected temporary 
factors, including an outsized increase in government payrolls, which is unlikely to be repeated this month, 
and probably some issues related to seasonal adjustment. Survey-based and other labour-market indicators 
generally point to a mixed picture for new hiring and little evidence of accelerated firing. We expect 
employment growth to have slowed meaningfully in September, and we forecast a moderate increase of 60k 
for payrolls. The unemployment rate has probably remained at 4.1%, but risks are tilted to the upside if 
participation recovers some ground from the current low level. The picture will likely remain one of cooling 
growth in nominal wages, with average hourly earnings rising at an annual pace of just above 3%. Given that 
productivity is increasing rapidly, the risk of second-round inflation effects through wages appears low.

Even after revision, core PCE inflation will probably remain too high for the Fed’s comfort  
Based on already published data for CPI and PPI, we estimate that the headline PCE deflator for August rose 
by 0.3% mom, while the core measure probably increased by 0.2% mom. Other conditions remaining equal, 
these monthly increases would leave the yearly growth rates for headline and core PCE prices at 3.7% and 
3.3%, respectively. However, the August PCE report will bring about important methodological changes as the 
Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) will adopt a different methodology to compute PCE prices for portfolio 
management fees, computer software and accessories, and legal services. We expect that the revisions will 
shave about 0.2pp off the yearly core PCE inflation rate, which is therefore likely to remain above 3%. This 
small change will not provide comfort to the Fed, which will likely maintain a hawkish stance amid solid 
economic activity, high energy prices and a labour market that is broadly in equilibrium. 
Eurozone inflation likely to have jumped 
We forecast a strong acceleration in eurozone headline inflation for September, to 3.8% yoy from 3.2%. This 
would be the highest level since summer 2023. Most of the acceleration is likely to have been driven by 
energy prices, which have continued to climb strongly due to a combination of rising prices for Brent and TTF 
gas, and surging refining margins, which have pushed retail prices for both petrol and diesel to record-high 
levels. Overall, we estimate that energy added about 0.4pp to the headline inflation rate. Core inflation 
probably rose as well, to 2.6% yoy from 2.4%, as the unusual weakness recorded in the prices of some holiday-
sensitive services in August has likely reversed, while core goods inflation points north amid indirect effects 
from the energy shock. The climb in headline inflation has further to go. We expect it to rise to 4% by year-
end. 
RBA likely to raise its policy rate again next week  
The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) will probably raise its cash rate target by another 25bp to 4.60% at its 
meeting on 29 September. A combination of higher oil prices, stronger-than-expected economic data and 
increasingly hawkish signals from RBA members, including Governor Michele Bullock saying that upside risks 
to inflation appear to be materialising, justify the move. Another rate increase at one of the two remaining 
meetings this year (3 November and 8 December) seems to be a concrete risk, largely depending on upcoming 
inflation figures and developments in the Middle East conflict. Still, the rate hike next week is well priced in 
with a likelihood of 95%, thus the positive impact on the Aussie dollar is unlikely to be big. 
VIEW UPDATE: NEAR-TERM OIL PRICE RISKS REMAIN ELEVATED 
The ongoing disruption in the Middle East continues to shape the near-term oil outlook, with uncertainty surrounding 
regional production, export flows and shipping routes remaining exceptionally elevated. While the restoration of key 
Saudi export infrastructure has improved the immediate operational backdrop, developments surrounding the UN 
General Assembly have highlighted both the existence of diplomatic channels and the persistence of significant 
geopolitical tensions, underscoring that the underlying conflict remains unresolved. We therefore continue to view 
the risk of sustained supply disruptions as material, with intermittent price spikes likely as long as geopolitical 
uncertainty remains elevated. Against this backdrop, we are raising our 4Q26 Brent forecast to USD 92/bbl from USD 
87/bbl, reflecting a more persistent geopolitical risk premium than previously embedded in our baseline scenario. 
Our medium-term outlook remains unchanged, however, and we continue to expect Brent to moderate towards USD 
78/bbl by mid-2027, as geopolitical risk premiums fade gradually and regional supply conditions recover.

WHAT WE ARE READING 
OECD Economic Outlook (Interim Report September 2026) › 
The core message from the OECD's latest outlook is that the global economy has 
proven more resilient than expected despite the Middle East conflict and the 
associated energy shock. Strong AI-related investment, ample oil inventories, 
additional supply from non-Gulf producers and government support measures 
have helped to cushion the impact on growth. 
The European Union’s automotive sector needs a plan, not undue protection › 
Benjamin Bjerkan-Wade, Ignacio García Bercero, Antoine Mathieu Collin, Ben 
McWilliams and Simone Tagliapietra of Bruegel argue that Europe's automotive 
industry faces a structural challenge from electrification, rising Chinese 
competition and weak domestic demand, but that policymakers are overly 
focused on protecting incumbent manufacturers instead of helping them adapt. 
Electability over ideology: Takeaways from the 2026 primaries › 
In this report, Elaine Kamarck and Jordan Muchnick of Brookings conclude that in 
the most competitive 2026 US congressional primaries, voters prioritised 
electability over ideological purity. Mainstream candidates generally 
outperformed candidates from the ideological wings of both parties, suggesting 
that many primary voters were focused on winning in November rather than 
making an ideological statement.
