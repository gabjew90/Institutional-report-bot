The abrupt halt to the relatively stable volatility backdrop that characterized most of 
the move higher in yields signaled a less fundamental quality to this week’s selloff. 
While the market likely needs a catalyst to reinvigorate duration risk appetite, US 
valuation and positioning measures are consistent with a building asymmetry 
towards lower yields. The initial window following the first Fed hike is typically 
consistent with front-end versus belly flattening. And although Fed pricing is 
stretched versus survey-based expectations, higher vol and hedge value 
considerations explain part of this gap, with the asymmetry towards hikes likely a 
near-term speed limit on front-end rallies. In a similar vein, we think that the recent 
underperformance of 5s on the European curve is overdone given the ECB’s 
comparatively low inflation tolerance. The idiosyncratic cheapening of OATs is not 
necessarily a harbinger of outperformance, but the extremity of the move combined 
with the distance to the elections tilt risks towards consolidation in the near-term. 
We expect the UK front-end to continue to trade largely as a function of energy 
prices, with upside likely to bring further flattening. Further out, uncertainty ahead of 
the Autumn Budget will likely limit scope for additional compression in Gilt risk 
premium until the fiscal picture becomes clearer. The JGB curve is one spot where we 
have greater sympathy for 5s to continue to lead in selloffs.

Potential TGA investment in repo gaining traction. Investment of TGA cash 
n
balances in repo has gained more traction as a potential way of reducing funding 
volatility since the May Quarterly Refunding. TGA swings are the largest source of 
volatility in reserves and consequently responsible for much of the volatility in 
funding costs. Investing excess TGA balances in repo could help neutralize the 
impact of TGA swings on funding, but as we noted in May, details around 
implementation will matter. Panelists at this week’s US Treasury Market Conference 
highlighted a few possibilities, noting that an excess cash position is available 
through most of the month, and Treasury could deploy more funding during tax 
season, or specifically when TGCR exceeds IORB. We think Treasury could also set a 
minimum threshold for cash on hand, above which it would readily deploy up to an 
announced amount when there is an economic incentive, i.e. TGCR > IORB. The 
direct economic benefits of this policy more broadly are likely small—TGCR has 
rarely exceeded IORB this year, meaning that any returns to lending are likely to be 
marginal. That said, ample reserve conditions have mitigated funding volatility, and 
the supply/demand backdrop could change, especially if coupon issuance begins to 
climb. A more meaningful benefit could be the reduction of risk premium through 
money market curves. Bills-OIS is sensitive to liquidity conditions, so another 
backstop-like source of liquidity could compress front-end borrowing costs. While 
many details remain to be ironed out, we think an approach that helps mitigate TGA 
swings should support a smoothing of repo volatility and accommodate an 
incrementally lower level of reserves.

Europe 
OATs unanchored. France remains a key focus within European rates despite only 
n
incremental information on the presidential race or macroeconomic outlook. In this 
context, we continue to think the recent idiosyncratic widening in OAT is 
disproportional compared to shift in deficit expectations or near-term political risk 
over the same period. This month’s idiosyncratic underperformance has now 
exceeded prior extremes. While moving beyond a specific political deadline has 
typically seen underperformance give way to outperformance, idiosyncratic OAT 
weakness has not necessarily anticipated subsequent tightening—the widening in 
June 2024 reflected convergence to fundamentals, for example (Exhibit 5). But we 
think the extremity of the move combined with the distance to the presidential 
elections are consistent with improved risk/reward in OATs, with risks skewed 
towards consolidation in the near-term. The clearest risk to this view is that ongoing 
increase in global yields deteriorates debt sustainability arithmetic for France. On 
this front, we note that the correlation between the 10y OAT-Bund spread and 10y 
USTs has also reached record highs, consistent with a heightened exposure to global 
spillovers (Exhibit 6). This suggests that global duration relief may be needed for 
OATs to stabilise.

Japan 
Shifting curve dynamics pressure 5s. The belly of the JGB curve continues to 
n
underperform alongside the global selloff in belly rates. More hawkish BoJ pricing 
has broadly helped stabilize the 10s30s curve but has also shifted the brunt of the 
cheapening pressure from macro conditions onto 2s and 5s. The signal from 
long-end curves and swap spreads suggest that cheapening is now less about fiscal 
risk premium and more about monetary policy expectations. Japanese officials’ 
repeat interventions in USD/JPY (actual and verbal) also continue to mean that rates 
will likely remain the release valve for macro pressures, both domestic and abroad. 
And with growing speculation that the BoJ would prefer not to “fall behind” the pace 
of Fed hikes or risk a weaker JPY, global pressures on front-end rates could have an 
outsized impact on Japan. Against this backdrop, we continue to think that paying 5s 
on the fly in Japan best captures these shifting curve dynamics. 
Australia and New Zealand 
Navigating the late stages of the RBA cycle. This week’s slightly weaker than 
n
expected Australian labor force report served as a reminder of the consequences of 
tightening policy, and provided some relief for the front-end of the AUD curve. That 
said, the unemployment rate only rose to RBA’s estimate of neutral, which is unlikely 
to shake the market’s confidence in further hikes ahead given the recent hawkish 
pivot. At next week’s meeting—where our economists expect a 25bp hike, in line 
with market pricing—focus thus should be on the extent of further tightening and 
the likely end point—current pricing implies about three hikes more than the 
endpoint of the most recent cycle, bringing policy to levels last seen before the GFC, 
when inflation was about a percentage point higher than today. We think this creates 
asymmetry towards the RBA under-delivering versus current policy pricing, even if 
the global backdrop for duration remains challenging in light of ongoing energy price 
volatility (we raise our YE26 10y forecast to 5.0% from 4.7% to reflect the updated 
policy path). In light of the more limited room for further tightening, we prefer 
receiving 5s on the 2s5s10s fly—which stands at the cheaper end of the last two 
years’ range—to outright longs to position for relief.
