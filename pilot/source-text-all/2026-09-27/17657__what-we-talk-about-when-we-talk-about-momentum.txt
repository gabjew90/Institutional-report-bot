What We Talk About When We Talk About 
Momentum
When investors point to momentum as a reason for stock returns, they 
could be misunderstanding what they are analyzing
September 23, 2026
Momentum is often lumped together with investment factors such 
as quality, growth, and value. But there is an important distinction. 
Those factors generally begin with an attribute of a company or 
stock. Quality describes whether the company has durable profits. 
Growth describes how quickly those profits (or the revenues that 
provide them) can compound. Value describes whether a stock is 
cheap relative to the profits the business generates.
Momentum begins somewhere else: with the market’s recent 
verdict. It reflects where capital has been flowing and which 
stocks have participated in that flow.
That makes momentum less a discrete company characteristic 
than a kind of meta signal: It captures the market’s response to 
information without necessarily identifying the information that 
elicited that response. A rising stock price may reflect improving 
margins, stronger execution, better capital allocation, or growing 
confidence in a company’s competitive position. It may also 
reflect sector leadership, country allocation, factor rotation, 
macroeconomic sensitivity, thematic enthusiasm, or simply 
greater risk appetite. Momentum tells us that something has been 
working before it tells us why it has worked.
That is both the value and the limitation of momentum. Investors 
often underreact to new information, and a rising stock price 
might be an early sign of improving fundamentals that investors 
have not yet fully recognized. But momentum can also capture a 
crowded theme or a regime-specific exposure that has little to do 
with the long-term merits of the underlying business. The signal is 
useful, but its contents are not stable.
This instability matters because the same label—momentum—
can describe very different portfolios at different times. In one 
period, a momentum-based portfolio may largely consist of 
profitable, high-quality growth businesses. In others, it may favor 
cyclicals, commodity producers, defensive-yield assets, or highly 
speculative companies. A portfolio may appear to be trying to 
harvest the same “factor” in each case, while the actual economic 
exposure changes substantially.

Momentum therefore requires interpretation. The important 
question is not whether a portfolio has positive or negative 
momentum exposure. It is what that exposure represents today.
For fundamental investors, this can be uncomfortable territory. 
Buying a stock simply because its price has already risen can feel 
like an abdication of judgment. After all, for such investors, the 
rationale for owning a stock is supposed to rest on an assessment 
of its cash flows, competitive advantage, management quality, 
capital allocation, or valuation.
That means that momentum often enters fundamental portfolios 
through the language of confirmation. A stock whose price is 
rising while earnings estimates improve and margins expand can 
be easier to own with conviction and to size more aggressively. 
The investment thesis appears to be gaining support. Management 
looks more credible. The market seems to be recognizing the 
opportunity identified by research.
That confirmation may be entirely justified. Price strength often 
accompanies genuine business progress. But it can also affect 
judgment. Conviction about a company may rise because the 
evidence has improved, or because the stock has performed well 
and the holding now feels validated by other investors. Those 
explanations are not always easy to separate.
Quantitative investors are more explicit about momentum 
exposure. They measure it directly and include it in their models. 
That transparency is valuable, but it carries a different risk: 
thinking of momentum as a stable premium rather than a 
changing collection of recent winners.
Because momentum’s composition shifts, a portfolio can 
accumulate exposure to a crowded regime trade without 
clearly identifying the underlying bet. The model still reports 
“momentum,” but the portfolio may in practice be concentrated in 
one theme, sector, macroeconomic sensitivity, or style of company. 
The factor name remains constant while the risk shifts.
That helps explain why momentum reversals can be so violent. 
Momentum strategies own what has been working. When the 
regime turns, yesterday’s winners can become today’s losers, 
with many portfolios attempting to exit similar holdings at the 
same time. 
Both fundamental and quantitative investors can therefore 
misread momentum. A fundamental manager may mistake a 
rising stock price for independent evidence that an investment 
thesis has strengthened. A quantitative process may mistake a 
changing regime exposure for a stable factor return. In each case 
the mistake is similar: treating momentum as though it tells us 
something intrinsic to the companies exhibiting it, rather than 
something about how the market has been reacting to them. 
That’s what distinguishes momentum from factors such as value, 
quality, and growth. Momentum can contain valuable information, 
including information about fundamental improvement that the 
market is still incorporating. But momentum does not explain 
itself. It tells us that the market’s view has been moving in a 
particular direction. Understanding why is a separate—and 
essential—task.
