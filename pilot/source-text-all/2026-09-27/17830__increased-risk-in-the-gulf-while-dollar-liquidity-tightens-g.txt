Conclusions
6


[Page 27]
Takeaways
•
Tighter USD liquidity is not only down to the Fed and the UST sell-off. It reflects moves across global central 
banks and government yields, with China being the exception.
•
Offshore USD liquidity is getting less help from the Gulf and China than in the past, for different reasons: the 
Gulf has a smaller trade surplus, and China’s capital outflows - consequence of its large trade surplus - are 
increasingly denominated in RMB. Hong Kong remains an important reservoir of offshore USD liquidity as 
clearly indicated by the low money market rates in HKD compared to USD.
•
We moderately raise our oil price forecast from USD 85/bbl to USD 90/bbl by year-end. More robust crude flows 
through the Strait of Hormuz from Saudi, escaping the new bottleneck from the East-West pipeline shows that 
Middle Eastern producers can be quite resilient, which calls for a moderation when projecting oil prices. 
However, limited tanker availability keeps flows below prewar levels, and China's recovering oil imports –
although only partially - put a floor under prices.
•
More structurally, China's energy basket is changing, especially its oil imports from the Gulf, which fell from 
41% of the total in 2022 to 25% by July 2026. The Gulf is becoming more dependent on China than China is on 
the Gulf, with relevant long-term implications.
•
Gulf borrowing from Asia has gone down a lot, especially since May 2026 as the conflict has become more 
entrenched.
•
Finally, so far, the Chinese economy seems quite insulated from the risk stemming from the Gulf, thanks to the 
very diversified oil imports, lower dependence on oil in the energy basket but also a very strong grip on the 
supply chain.
