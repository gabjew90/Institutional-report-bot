Transformational Innovation Opportunities (TRIO): Artificial Intelligence
 
Authors: Delwin Kurnia Limas, CFA, CIO Equity Strategist, UBS AG Singapore Branch; Kevin Dennean, CFA, CIO Equity Strategist, US Technology & 
Telecom, UBS Financial Services Inc. (UBS FS); Achille Monnet, CIO Equity Analyst, UBS Switzerland AG; Allen Pu, CFA, CIO Equity Strategist, UBS AG Hong

• Recent developments, including Oracle's Project Jupiter 
delays and weakness in the Zenith Arc financing, 
highlight increasing risks around power availability, 
permitting, project execution, and capital formation 
across the AI infrastructure ecosystem.
• We believe these challenges are primarily supply-side 
constraints rather than signs of weakening AI demand. 
While delays and cancellations are beginning to emerge, 
they remain manageable and are already incorporated 
into our conservative 2027 AI capex forecast of USD 1.2tr.
• We continue to favor a barbell strategy within 
technology, combining high-quality semiconductor and 
hardware beneficiaries of AI spending with mega-cap 
platforms and defensive tech which would be relatively 
more resilient if the AI capex boom fades.
Source: Shutterstock
 
Our view
Recent developments highlight growing risks around the 
industry's ability to deliver infrastructure on schedule, secure 
sufficient power capacity, and finance increasingly capital-
intensive projects.
Project Jupiter highlights growing AI infrastructure 
execution risks
A recent example is Oracle's force-majeure notice to an 
affiliate involved in developing Project Jupiter, a planned 
2.5GW data-center campus in New Mexico. The notice 
reportedly seeks to preserve Oracle's contractual position, 
including the ability to defer certain payments should 
power-related delays prevent the campus from becoming 
operational according to its current timeline. Importantly, 
neither party has indicated any intention to terminate the 
project, with both parties reiterating their commitment to 
the development. The announcement follows a separate 
Financial Times report that USD 18bn of debt tied to the 
project has been trading at distressed levels in secondary 
markets.
The challenges surrounding Project Jupiter appear largely 
linked to power infrastructure rather than AI demand. The 
project was initially designed around large-scale gas-fired 
generation, but efforts to secure the necessary pipeline 
infrastructure have experienced repeated delays. Oracle 
subsequently explored fuel-cell-based alternatives, although 
these solutions also require pipeline connectivity and remain 
subject to environmental approvals. In addition, the project's 
air-quality permit issue remains unresolved. These issues 
highlight a recurring theme across the industry: the primary 
bottleneck for next-generation AI campuses is increasingly 
access to power and permitting rather than demand for AI 
compute capacity.
We believe Project Jupiter serves as an important 
reminder that AI infrastructure deployments face substantial 
execution complexity as projects scale toward gigawatt-
sized campuses. While leading hyperscalers possess the 
balance sheet strength to absorb delays, slower deployment 
timelines can nevertheless delay revenue recognition, 
increase financing costs, and affect supplier order visibility.

Financing markets are becoming more selective
Alongside execution risks, we are also seeing signs that 
capital markets are becoming more discerning in their 
underwriting of AI infrastructure projects.
The recent underperformance of Zenith Arc is one of the 
first notable examples of stress in AI infrastructure financing 
outside the hyperscaler ecosystem. The USD 2.25bn bond 
issuance, which finances a large-scale data-center project 
fully leased to a major trading firm, has experienced a 
meaningful sell-off since issuance, with yields widening by 
about 150bps despite relative stability across many other AI-
related and data-center credit instruments.
The divergence suggests investors are reassessing risks 
associated with specific counterparties and project 
structures rather than questioning the secular AI investment 
thesis.
Many recent AI data-center projects have benefited from 
hyperscaler counterparties, investment-grade guarantees, 
or the balance-sheet support of large technology 
companies. By contrast, Zenith Arc relies on a single BB rated 
tenant and therefore carries greater tenant concentration 
risk, execution risk, and secondary-market liquidity risk.
As a result, we expect financing conditions to become 
increasingly bifurcated. Projects backed by hyperscalers and 
investment-grade counterparties should continue to enjoy 
relatively attractive access to capital, whereas developments 
targeting tenants without an explicit big tech credit 
backstop may face higher funding costs, greater equity 
contribution requirements, and slower debt syndication 
processes.
Investment implications
AI demand remains resilient, reinforced most recently by the 
emergence of consumer AI agents such as Meta Platforms' 
Muse, which have renewed investor confidence that AI 
adoption is expanding beyond model training into broader 
consumer use cases. Against this backdrop, hyperscalers 
continue to maintain aggressive AI infrastructure investment 
plans.
That said, recent developments highlight growing risks 
around the industry's ability to bring infrastructure online on 
schedule, secure adequate power and transmission capacity, 
navigate permitting requirements, and finance increasingly 
capital-intensive projects.
UBS Evidence Lab currently tracks 57GW of operational 
AI data-center capacity in North America, alongside a 
further 130GW of announced pipeline capacity. Within this 
universe, we identify nine projects impacted by delays or 
cancellations, representing roughly 7GW of affected initial 
capacity. Importantly, many of these projects were designed 
for multi-phase expansion, implying that the capacity at risk 
could rise to 17GW upon full build-out.
In context, the affected capacity represents 12% of current 
operational capacity and 5% of announced pipeline capacity 
in North America. In Europe, there are currently 17GW of 
operational capacity and 30GW of pipeline capacity, with 
around 6% and 3%, respectively, facing potential delay or 
cancellation risks.
While these percentages remain relatively modest, they 
reinforce our view that execution risk is becoming an 
increasingly important consideration alongside AI demand 
fundamentals. Notably, the emerging challenges are 
largely concentrated around power availability, permitting, 
transmission infrastructure, and project execution, rather 
than any deterioration in underlying AI compute demand.
For now, we view the risk to our AI capex forecasts as 
manageable. Our 2027 AI capex estimate of USD 1.2tr, up 
from USD 900bn in 2026, already incorporates conservative 
assumptions that a portion of the announced data-center 
pipeline will be delayed, resized, or ultimately canceled. 
Nevertheless, as AI infrastructure projects continue to scale 
toward multi-gigawatt campuses, we expect execution risk 
to remain a key area of investor focus and will continue to 
monitor developments closely.
From an investment perspective, we continue to favor a 
barbell strategy within tech. On one side, we prefer quality 
semiconductor and hardware companies that remain direct 
beneficiaries of AI infrastructure spending. On the other, we 
favor mega-cap technology platforms and defensive tech 
which would be relatively more resilient if AI capex boom 
fades.
Key risk statements:
Meta Platforms - Key risks include execution, regulation, 
and competition.
Oracle - Key risks include a slowdown in demand, 
execution failing to meet expectations, and slower AI 
adoption than anticipated.

Equity selection: An assessment relative to a benchmark
Equity selections in Equity Preferences lists (EPLs) are assessments made relative to a sector/industry, country/regional or 
thematic benchmark. The chosen benchmark is disclosed on the front page of each EPL. It is also used to measure the 
performance of the EPL owner(s). Including a stock in the EPL constitutes neither a view on its expected, standalone absolute 
performance nor a price target. Rather, EPLs are meant to support the UBS House View, with the stocks included in them 
selected for their superior risk/return profiles.
Our selection is based on an assessment of the company's fundamental outlook and valuation, the risks owning the stock 
entails, including material sustainability risks and the diversification benefits it provides in an investment portfolio, among 
many other factors. UBS GWM CIO‘s selection methodology enables wealth management clients to invest in a specific 
investment theme or focus on a sector/industry or country/region.
The lead strategist is responsible for the CIO view on a stock, while the EPL owner(s) is/are responsible for the stock selection 
within an EPL. Stocks can be selected for multiple EPLs. For consistency's sake, a stock can only be selected as either Most 
Preferred or Least Preferred, not both simultaneously. As EPL benchmarks differ, stocks do not need to be included on every 
list to which they could theoretically be added.
