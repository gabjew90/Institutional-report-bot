Americas Technology: Sizing the AI Economy Needed to Justify ROIC on 
Hyperscaler AI Capex
24 September 2026 | 4:30PM EDT
Against rising compute needs to match outsized and growing AI token consumption, 
the US-based hyperscalers have undergone a dramatic transformation around 
capital intensity to match this computational shift. These investments are being 
made against a combination of both real-time demand signals (in a current 
supply/demand imbalance) and multi-year compute needs of key customers. 
Throughout this year (and most notably with this past Q2 earnings season), we’ve 
noticed that this theme has become better understood – from earnings results and 
company commentary, investors have achieved greater comfort that both (a) the 
hyperscalers are earning a sufficient (if not outsized) return on prior-period capex 
(CY2023-25) through incremental revenues and operating cash flow and (b) that the 
hyperscalers are currently undergoing the next phase of capex spend (CY2026-27) 
against a compute-constrained and accelerating end demand environment 
(producing pricing levels for compute solidly above our assumed long-term baseline). 
As a result, the debate has shifted in the past few months toward a more forward 
outlook: how current period capex spend (across CY2026-27) might deliver a return 
in future (CY2028-30) periods (and how current ROIC earned on prior-period capex 
can be used as a baseline). In this report, we introduce a framework to analyze the 
necessary output from the AI economy in order to earn a baseline return on 
invested capital (“ROIC”) on the current levels of capex spend from the 
US-based hyperscalers. 
Our analysis in this report focuses on the revenue hurdles required to produce a 
15% annual ROIC on “Phase 2” (CY2026-27) AI-compute investments deployed 
by the US-based hyperscalers. Based on various assumptions (an average upfront 
cost of ~$42bn per Gigawatt (GW); a buildout mix of ~70% “compute” v. ~30% 
“shells”; conservative depreciation schedules; ongoing operating cost assumptions; 
etc.), the analysis implies that the six major US-based hyperscalers (Alphabet, 
Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle and SpaceX) must generate a combined ~$1.42 
trillion of cumulative CY2028–30 revenues (or ~$11.6 billion of annual revenue 
per GW) to clear the 15% ROIC hurdle. While we expect near-term returns to 
compress (as near-term results are muted by an accelerated capex cycle), we expect 
the attractive ROIC on current period capex to be better realized in operating results 
over the medium-to-long-term. Said differently, we frame the near-term return 
compression as a natural consequence of the massive upfront investment cycle 
rather than evidence of structurally poor AI economics.

Framing The Current State of Hyperscaler Capex (and Key Investor Debates) 
 
 
Capital intensity remains high as the US hyperscalers accelerate AI compute 
buildout. On the back of the demand curve for AI tokens (and corresponding demand 
for compute capacity), the capital intensity of the US-based hyperscalers has scaled to 
demonstrably higher levels than seen in prior compute cycles. One of the dominant 
themes at our recent Communacopia + Technology conference (link | link | link) was that 
the overall industry remains capacity-constrained and, as a result, the current pace of 
capital spending over the next 12-18 months is sustaining and aimed at closing this 
supply/demand imbalance. 
That said, this near-term capex spend is already well understood by investors. 
Having already been well signaled by the companies (through explicit current-period 
guidance and/or qualitative framing of investment priorities), we believe the near-term 
(CY26-27) capex spend levels are now well understood and priced into the stocks at 
current levels — since the start of the year, Visible Alpha Consensus Data capex 
estimates for CY26-27 for the five US-based hyperscalers that were public at the start of 
the year (GOOGL, MSFT, AMZN, META and ORCL) have risen by ~66% (or a combined 
~$750bn total increase for both years across the five companies). Even still, we remain 
broadly ahead of consensus Street estimates on CY27 capex estimates (and believe our 
above-Street estimates are more aligned with investor expectations).

Bottom-Up Views on AI Infrastructure Capex — A Semiconductor Perspective 
 
 
We present our view on the overall levels of AI infrastructure capex over the next 
three years, based on the latest guidance from the semiconductor supply chain.  
Over the past three years, the supply chain has been a reliable indicator of the direction 
of travel for AI infrastructure capex spending trends.  In general, bottom-up supply 
chain datapoints and company guidance are far from a perfect predictor of quantitative 
trends — especially in a supply-constrained environment that encourages customers to 
be more aggressive in their forecasts.  However, in the medium term we believe they 
remain a good indicator of spending trends.  On the demand side, we believe 
semiconductor investors are concerned about the potential “funding gap” between what 
AI infrastructure providers are spending on capex today relative to the level of 
present-day EBITDA/FCF they generate.  On the supply side, the most discussed risk is 
the amount of datacenter land/power/shell that will be available to satisfy the planned 
infrastructure capex — especially in 2028 and beyond.  
Here, we present our implied estimates for AI infrastructure capex and associated 
datacenter deployments, based on semiconductor company guidance and our own 
forecasts (Exhibit 3).  We estimate AI infrastructure capex of approximately $1.3 
trillion in 2027 (up 60% YoY) and $2.0 trillion in 2028 (up 42% YoY). In power terms, 
our analysis implies roughly 20 GW / 35 GW / 57 GW of AI datacenter deployments 
in 2026/27/28. Importantly, these figures represent AI-specific infrastructure 
investment and extend beyond traditional hyperscaler capex to include AI labs 
(including the capex beyond what is contemplated as part of hyperscaler spending), 
sovereign AI enterprises, and corporate/enterprise customers. 
We summarize these high-level datapoints below:

Introducing a Framework to Analyze US Hyperscaler ROIC and the Necessary Output from the AI 
Economy 
 
 
Below we provide a framework to answer this key question: What is the aggregate 
revenue that the US-based hyperscalers need to earn in order to achieve a sufficient 
ROIC on this “Phase 2” (2026-27) AI capex buildout? 
Our US hyperscaler ROIC framework can be summarized by a few key 
points/assumptions: 
Relevant Companies: This analysis covers the “US-Based Hyperscalers”, which we 
n
define as (in market cap order): Alphabet (GOOGL), Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon 
(AMZN), SpaceX (SPCX), Meta Platforms (META) and Oracle (ORCL). We believe 
this cohort of companies represents a sufficient proxy for AI-related 
infrastructure/compute buildout and capital intensity (as it relates to this debate). 
Defining “Phase 2” (2026-27) Capex: We only include capex related to “AI 
n
compute” over the two-year period that we define as “Phase 2” (CY2026-27) to 
measure the revenue generation needed on this near-term buildout. Importantly, we 
are isolating this 2-year capex phase and do not include a) revenue/EBIT generated 
in CY26-27 as compute comes online or b) ongoing capex spend (and resulting 
revenues or operating expenses on that additional capacity) in CY28 and beyond. 
The amount of “AI compute” capex spend in Phase 2 utilizes our actual 
company-specific capex forecasts and includes only AI-related spend to the extent 
relevant (e.g. GSe AWS capex for AMZN; GSe AI-segment capex for SPCX; etc.) – for 
companies where AI v. Non-AI capex can’t be easily estimated (e.g. GOOGL), we 
assume a flat ~85-90% of total company capex in CY26-27 goes toward AI compute. 
Finally, we categorize capex spend into two buckets, with a consistent % mix 
between the two: (1) “Compute” (servers/chips, networking hardware & equipment, 
etc.), or ~70% of 2-year capex, and (2) “Shells” (land, buildings and related 
infrastructure), or ~30% of 2-year capex.

n

and assuming a 10% inflation rate from those YE25 figures (we use 10% to be 
consistent with the rate at which META + AMZN have revised up their capex 
guidance, citing input costs as the primary factor); (b) a blended average input cost 
per GW framework from our US Semiconductors coverage team (based on an 
assumed mix of datacenter capacity by hardware type); and (c) reported / implied 
realized costs of AI compute infrastructure buildouts YTD from the companies.

ROIC Thresholds: We use a flat 15% annual return, as defined by annual net 
n
operating profit after taxes (“NOPAT”) earned in CY2028-30 as a % of average 
annual capex in CY2026-27. In Exhibit 8, we also provide a scenario analysis flexing a 
range of ROIC thresholds (between 0-30%). We note that the ROIC that the 
hyperscalers are currently seeing on capex spend (and what they are likely 
forecasting on this wave of capex) is almost certainly higher than this 15% threshold 
(see “How Have the Companies Framed ROIC?” section below). That said, our 
framework is designed to show the revenue needed to justify what we view as a 
reasonable (and likely conservative) 15% return threshold and address some investor 
concerns that the hyperscalers will realize an even lower (possibly negative) ROIC on 
current capex. 
Operating Expenses (EBIT-to-Revenue Bridge): We “gross up” the implied annual 
n
NOPAT in CY28-30 by an assumed 21% corporate tax rate (current US corporate 
income tax rate) to show the annual operating income (“EBIT”) needed. From EBIT, 
we add the assumed annual operating expenses on the GWs of compute built out to 
show the annual revenue needed to clear the ROIC hurdle. The operating expenses 
can be categorized in two buckets: (1) depreciation expense – we assume a useful 
life of 5 years / 15 years on the “Compute” and “Shells” assets purchased in Phase 2, 
respectively, and factor in the resulting annual depreciation expense on those assets 
and (2) non-D&A operating and maintenance expenses – we assume an annual 
cost to operate these datacenters (power, labor, etc.) of $836mm/GW (this 
assumption is based on Epoch AI estimates [link] as of YE25 with an assumed 10% 
inflation rate).

also, for the most part, only use capital expenditures related to the buildout of

Using Cloud Backlogs to Contextualize Forward Monetization Potential 
 
 
To help contextualize the output of the analysis above, we look at the three major 
US-based public cloud companies (AWS [AMZN], Azure [MSFT] and Google Cloud 
[GOOGL]) as a read on current demand backlogs and the infrastructure buildout that 
these companies are undergoing in response. Growing enterprise demand for both AI 
training and inference workloads is driving a sizable uptick in reported revenues and 
backlog commitments for all three CSPs — as of C2Q26, the three companies reported a 
combined backlog of ~$1.69trn (+152% YoY and ~1.5x since the start of the year), with 
management citing capacity constraints limiting the ability to serve strong AI-related 
demand and being a key reason for the increase in capex investments. 
 
Using the same framework of a 15% ROIC hurdle rate, the combined CY2026-27 capex 
for these three companies of ~$1.22trn would need to generate a cumulative ~$1.00trn 
of revenues in CY2028-30 (see Exhibit 12, Exhibit 13 and Exhibit 14 below for details). 
This required 3-year revenue total represents ~59% of the total current reported 
backlog of ~$1.69trn as of C2Q26 (compared to company disclosures of an 
expectation to realize ~1/2 of the reported backlog over the next 24 months and ~1/2 
thereafter). While some debate can be made about how the current backlog will be 
converted into revenues going forward (customer concentration risk; product/revenue 
mix; timing of revenue realization given longer-term contracts; etc.), this assumes no 
further growth of backlog commitments going forward (a likely conservative assumption 
given the steady DD%+ sequential backlog growth in recent periods).

How Have the Companies Framed ROIC? 
 
 
 Capacity constraints and accelerating demand driving short payback periods on 
current datacenter capex. 
On its 2Q26 earnings call, AMZN outlined how it has a clear line of sight into current 
n
period AWS/AI-related capex generating strong returns in the future: capex is 
bucketed into servers and networking equipment (a ~5-6 year useful life v. 
breakeven point of ~3 years, implying significant cash flow generation for 2-3 years 
post breakeven) and datacenter shells (30+ year useful life, implying the ability to 
support 5-6 cycles of server economics). Currently, capex spend begins ~2 years 
before a datacenter becomes operational (masking current period results against 
what is otherwise positive/attractive ROIC on AI-related investments). 
On its F4Q26 earnings call, ORCL disclosed that it sees steady-state ROIC in the high 
n
20s % for larger projects within its Infrastructure business (with potential upside 
from potential elongated GPU replacement cycles and/or initiatives such as 
“Bring-Your-Own-Hardware” that lower ORCL’s upfront capex spend). 
At the GS Communacopia + Technology conference, SPCX disclosed that it is 
n
currently deploying AI segment capex (on terrestrial datacenter capacity) against a 
one-year payback period. 
Datacenter infrastructure is being built with flexibility/fungibility in mind (reducing 
exposure to one single technology framework or utility). 
MSFT has previously framed its infrastructure investments as going toward building 
n
a “fungible fleet”, including flexibility in terms of geography and  hardware type and 
able to serve different types of workloads/utilities across the AI lifecycle.  
META has discussed how it builds out its datacenter capacity through a “staged’ 
n
approach, leveraging modular designs, timing of server orders (“as needed” / close 
to deployment) and leasing of additional 3P capacity (to fill gaps as 1P compute is 
deployed. 
Similarly, multiple companies have discussed innovation around datacenter 
n
architecture and design – for example, AMZN’s Modular Design enables it to easily 
retrofit existing infrastructure with new hardware in the future.  
GOOGL has outlined its ability to dynamically shift capacity between internal 
n
(Search, YouTube, Google DeepMind) and external (Google Cloud) use cases and 
direct workloads across geos and server types to optimize for high utilization rates. 
The outlook for future monetization potential (across multiple avenues) of 
compute capacity remains optimistic. 
The major public CSPs (GOOGL, AMZN, MSFT and ORCL) have raised pricing on 
n

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
