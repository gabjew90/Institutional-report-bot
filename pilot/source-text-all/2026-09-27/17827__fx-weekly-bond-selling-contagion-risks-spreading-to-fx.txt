USD: The building risk of fixed income selling spreading 
The US dollar has today retraced some of the gains recorded this week following some 
respite in fixed income markets with yields lower on some optimism that progress has 
been made toward the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. The retracement is modest 
reflecting an understandable level of scepticism – we have been here before. For this 
to extend we will need to hear some positive noises from Washington. The respite is 
welcome after what has been a brutal move in global bond markets, led by the US 
Treasury market. In our view this move has raised the danger level and risks of broader 
financial market instability is increased. That could have significant implication for FX 
market conditions as well.  
The recent US yield move is very significant and has caught a lot of the ‘fast money’ 
market participants off guard, resulting in notable losses and forced selling which has 
reinforced the move higher. Taking the period since the start of Jackson Hole in August 
(which culminated with Fed Chair Warsh’s hawkish speech), the 2-year UST bond yield 
has surged 72bps to yesterday’s close. That’s a move that took place over 21 days 
and is the largest 2yr yield jump over that same length of time since 3rd March 2023. 
On that occasion it was a big enough yield move to cause turmoil by bringing to the 
fore issues in the US regional banking sector. News of the collapse of Silicon Valley 
Bank (SVB) broke a few days later followed by Signature Bank and the news prompted 
a huge turnaround in the rates market as investors positioned for the Fed having to cut 
rates. The 2-year yield plunged 89bps in two days following the news. The US dollar 
had advanced over 4% in the month ahead of SVB collapse as US yields jumped and 
the dollar had fully retraced the move a month after the banking collapse. We are not 
suggesting further banking trouble now but the 2023 events do highlight the scale of 
the recent move has consequences for risk takers and the likely losses that have been 
suffered raise the risks of consequences going forward. There is now an increased 
incentive amongst market participants to cover those losses which raises the risk of 
profitable trades being liquidated to lock in profits. 
Strong risk appetite has been a consistent feature of financial market conditions, 
despite the geopolitical risks and the surge in energy prices. AI-related equities is an 
obvious area that could be vulnerable to a correction if yields remain elevated or extend 
further. The Nasdaq Composite did hit a new high this week but only just breaking 
above the June high before correcting lower. The Nasdaq is still basically flat relative 
to the close at the end of May. Possible signs that yields are starting to bear down on 
risk. There are substantial profit cushions there if there was a move to lock in profits to 
cover fixed income losses. Mag 7 is up 19% over the last three months versus 5% for

What Does the US Bond Rout Mean for FX? 
The US Treasury sell-off has intensified over recent weeks, pushing the 30Y UST yield to its highest level since 2004. 
Conventional market narrative suggests that such a dramatic rise in yields should provide significant support for the USD. Yet 
market participants remain divided over whether the recent move reflects stronger growth, persistent inflation pressures or fiscal 
concerns. In this report, we decompose the rise in long-dated UST yields into its underlying drivers and assess the implications 
for FX. While the recent repricing has been concentrated in the real-yield component rather than inflation expectations, 
understanding the source of higher real yields is equally important for evaluating the likely impact on the dollar. 
YIELD DECOMPOSITION 
The recent rise in UST yields has frequently been framed as a reflection of inflation concerns and fiscal deterioration. However, 
a decomposition of the 30Y UST yield suggests a different story. The 30Y UST yield has risen to 5.46%, its highest level since 
2004. While breakeven inflation has remained comparatively stable near the low 2% area, while most of the repricing has 
occurred through the real-yield component.  
Historically, inflation-driven yield increases have produced mixed FX outcomes because market participants flag weaker 
purchasing power and future monetary instability. In contrast, higher real yields increase the inflation-adjusted return available 
on US assets and have generally been associated with a stronger US dollar. The current selloff should therefore be viewed as 
a real-rate repricing event rather than an inflation repricing event.

The rise in UST yields in recent periods has been driven predominantly by higher real yields 
rather than inflation compensation. 
 
TERM PREMIUM  
While the decomposition clearly points to higher real yields as the dominant driver of the sell-off, this doesn’t mean the move 
reflects stronger growth expectations alone. Real yields rise due to expectations of stronger long-run growth and higher real 
interest rates, but they also rise due to elevated/rising term premiums, the compensation demanded by investors for duration 
risk. 
The recent rise in the 10Y ACM term premium suggests that investors are increasingly demanding compensation to hold long-
duration US Treasuries following concerns surrounding fiscal sustainability, elevated debt issuance and greater uncertainty 
around the long-run path of interest rates. Historically, growth-driven increases in real yields have tended to generate a 
stronger and more persistent USD response than increases driven primarily by term premium. This is due to tighter financial 
conditions and raise concerns about the attractiveness of US assets, creating a more ambiguous impact on the dollar.

The current yield backdrop is extraordinary by post-GFC standards. The US 30Y Treasury yield currently stands at; 99.98th 
percentile since 2010 and 92.8th percentile over the full available sample (2004 to present).The significance of this result is that 
yields have returned to levels last observed in the mid-2000s despite a considerably less favourable fiscal backdrop. Compared 
with the last period when the 30Y Treasury yield traded near current levels, federal debt and fiscal deficits are substantially 
higher today. That reality has understandably increased investor focus on fiscal sustainability and Treasury supply dynamics. 
However, our analysis suggests that the FX implications continue to be driven primarily by real-rate developments.

DXY SENSITIVITY 
To quantify the impact of higher real yields on FX, we perform a rolling regression analysis of DXY returns against changes in 
the US 30Y real yield. Current estimates suggest that a 10bp increase in the US 30Y real yield is associated with 
approximately a 0.25%-0.30% appreciation in DXY. Importantly, the estimated sensitivity remains modestly above its long-
run historical average. 
This result suggests that, despite growing market discussion around fiscal deficits, Treasury supply and term premium dynamics, 
the traditional relationship between higher US real yields and a stronger dollar has not materially broken down. While the market 
narrative surrounding Treasury yields has evolved, FX markets continue to respond primarily through the relative-return channel. 
Higher real yields increase the attractiveness of USD-denominated assets and continue to provide support for the dollar.

FX Implications 
 
• 
The recent Treasury sell-off has been driven primarily by higher real yields rather than inflation expectations, making it more supportive for 
the USD than a comparable inflation-driven yield increase. However, elevated term premiums suggest that a growing portion of the move 
is linked to duration risk and fiscal uncertainty. Historically, growth-driven increases in real yields have generated a stronger USD response 
than term-premium-driven increases. As a result, the positive USD implications of the current sell-off may be somewhat weaker than a 
simple real-yield decomposition would imply. 
 
• 
Nevertheless, the relationship between higher US real yields and a stronger dollar remains intact. Our rolling regressions suggest that DXY 
sensitivity to real-yield changes remains above its long-run average. 
 
• 
As long as US real yields continue to move higher, the balance of risks remains tilted in favour of USD strength, particularly against low-
yielding FX. However, an increasing share of the move driven by term premium rather than growth would argue for a more measured USD 
response than historical experience might otherwise suggest.
