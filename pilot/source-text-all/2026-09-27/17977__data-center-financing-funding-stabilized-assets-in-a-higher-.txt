Data Center Financing: Funding 
Stabilized Assets in a Higher 
Rate Environment
As interest rates continue to rise, financing stabilized data 
centers is proving more challenging. Data center operators can 
accommodate higher funding costs by lowering DSCR levels if 
the market allows, or tolerating lower returns. Hyperscaler-
backed issuers may also gravitate towards other credit channels 
such as the secured bond market.

Key Takeaways
With 5y rates over 100bps higher since the start of the year and as datacenter 
spreads leak wider, data center securitized credit supply is slowing. Higher rates 
make the economics of financing through the ABS space challenging, particularly 
if rates increase quickly.
This slowdown in supply will be transitory, in our view. Ultimately, given the 
amount of leased capacity that is due to energize in the coming years, a majority 
of those data centers will require debt financing.
Our analysis shows that an all-in cost of debt around 8% would slow ABS 
issuance under the current levels of LTV and DSCR. That said, issuers have a 
number of levers they can pull as rates move higher, primarily LTVs and DSCRs. 
Further, IRR targets can be malleable after taking into account meaningful 
completion premiums.
Taking higher rates into account against the higher GW forecasts, we lower our 
2027 forecast of datacenter securitized credit issuance to $40-50bn and hold our 
2028 forecast steady at $55-65bn. We now expect $120-$145bn of gross issuance 
in securitized credit data centers for 2026-2028.
We also continue to prefer colocation datacenter ABS over hyperscaler 
datacenter ABS, believing that the agility of the colocation providers to monetize 
elevated rental rates as leases are signed or renewed outweighs a potentially 
weaker structure in the market.
Higher rates should slow corporate DC issuance, though greater flexibility to 
renegotiate leases before stabilization may provide more room to adjust than in 
securitized credit. Lower-cost refinancing of existing deals will likely be more 
selective, favoring strong tenants or credit support and requiring flexibility across 
funding markets.

Cost of Capital Concerns: Funding a Stabilized Data Center 
in Securitized Credit Markets 
As Treasury rates rise and spreads grind wider in data center ABS, the funding 
backdrop ahead looks more challenging for data center securitized credit  issuers than 
it did even just back in 1Q26. At the start of the year, 5y A/A- rated data center ABS 
bonds were yielding around 5.5% at issuance; the first AAA-rated bond of the year funded 
at sub 5%. Today, with 5y rates over 100bp higher since January, funding costs on the 
AAAs are now above 6% (6.21% on the recent Vantage 2026-1, which priced at a spread of 
140 bp) and the As are approaching 7% (6.81% on the Vantage 2026-1, spread of 200 bp).

Higher funding costs have likely played a role in dampening securitized credit issuance 
this year. Supply in ABS and CMBS  slowed down in 2Q26 and 3Q26 to date, though it has 
not yet halted altogether. With $13bn ABS and $6.5bn CMBS brought to market so far this 
year, issuance is pacing in line with 2025. While in another asset class that may imply 
continued strength in a higher-rate environment, we think volumes coming in on par with 
last year is nothing if not an underperformance vs expectations. To be sure, across the 
fixed income complex data center-backed issuance has been very healthy, around $100bn, 
though primarily through credit channels (~$43bn in IG and ~$37bn in HY YTD) (see here). 
Given the current compute environment, it could be easy to argue that elevated funding 
costs can readily be passed through to the tenant; owners of power supply and 
interconnected data centers do have material pricing power. However, one important 
difference between assets financed in corporate credit and securitized credit creates a 
bigger challenge for securitized credit supply: the time between lease finalization and 
securitization. 
 In the HY corporate credit channel, assets can get funding within 1-2 quarters of the lease 
being signed  (with continued leeway to renegotiate contracts if the funding-cost 
expectations change). In ABS and CMBS, by contrast, stabilized assets generally have a 
much longer lag between contract signing and public financing, particularly for the 
hyperscaler-backed assets. We also think there is likely little room for contract re-

With the market currently pricing in elevated 5y rates over the medium term, and with 
data center ABS spreads more likely to move wider than tighter in our view, data 
center companies must grapple with persistently higher funding costs. As we show 
throughout this report, as cost of debt approaches 8%  issuers will be  forced to adapt 
either structurally or by tolerating lower returns. We think 8% marks a threshold at which 
supply will meaningfully slow, until such a time that new assets with higher rents and 
escalators can be securitized.
The speed with which rates rise matters due to this  lag between the time that the leases 
are signed and the underlying asset is securitized. As a consequence, we view any 
slowdown in supply as transitory, given the amount of energized capacity that is 
scheduled to come online through the end of this decade. In the meantime, we expect to 
see the data center operators adapt to this higher rate environment as follows:
1. Lower LTVs, and lower DSCRs: A trend that is already well underway, issuers will 
be able to raise less debt as interest rates rise to stay within the boundaries of 
their ABS master trust (or within the boundaries of what the market tolerates). 
However, there is plenty of latitude within existing trusts: as we show below, most 
outstanding master trusts have debt service coverage ratios (DSCRs) well above 
cash sweeps or other trigger levels, and so we would expect to see DSCRs come 
down.
2. Bonds issued at a discount: As in a previous cycle when rates rose quickly, deals 
were brought to the market below par to keep coupons tolerable for DSCR 
restrictions. 
3. Increased credit market issuance: The secured bond market has so far proved 
more tolerant of low DSCRs and high LTVs. The structures in credit are also more 
customized, with a wider array of terms, amortization schedules, backstops and 
guarantees. With more levers to pull, the credit markets could pick up some of the 
securitized credit supply, which for now we view as only for the hyperscaler 
campuses. 
We also believe that issuers may be willing to tolerate lower IRRs on a single deal for two 
reasons: 1) if rates fall and/or rents rise, they may expect to average into better returns

over time and can therefore tolerate a portion of the master trust financed at less-than-
ideal rates; 2) the construction-to-completion premium (over a 1.5-2y period) has been so 
significant as to offset the weaker returns once the asset is stabilized. Indeed, focusing 
myopically on one single deal risks missing the forest for the trees: an ABS deal is part of 
a broader portfolio funding strategy, and is  just one phase of the asset's useful life. The 
issuer's assumptions around long-term data center valuations and the forward interest 
rate environment will therefore play an important role in timing of issuance, even if cost 
of debt rises above 8%. 
In this report, we outline what it looks like for various types of data centers to securitize 
given restrictions on LTV and DSCR, and IRR targets. We also dig deeper into  how these 
companies may adapt to these interest rate pressures (see Adapting to a Higher Rate 
Environment ). Finally, in the next section we show that even in a higher rate 
environment, the quantum of GW that will operationalize in 2027 and beyond is still so 
large that it will necessitate more debt raises. 
 For each classification of data center, we assess two scenarios: 1) parameters are based on 
what the market currently tolerates for that type of shelf and 2) what we think 
theoretical limits are, either due to shelf parameters or rating agency limits. For simplicity, 
we are modeling a transaction in isolation;  we are not assuming there is a currently 
outstanding shelf, which would be more restrictive.  We assume that the equity sponsor 
(the parent of the issuer) is looking for at least an 8.5% IRR over the life of a 5y deal, 
based on our analysis of recent issuances. This 8.5% IRR is predicated on a constant cap 
rate over a five-year period, though we note that if the issuer has more bullish estimates 
of valuation they may be expecting a higher IRR, likely in excess of  10%. We assess the 
sensitivity to cap rate levels as well. (Our assumptions for LTV, DSCR, and cap rate are 
based off of existing shelves; see Appendix: An Overview of Financing Stabilized Assets 
with Data Center ABS & CMBS   for more). 
Funding Economics: Hyperscaler Shelves
Hyperscaler shelves have the most consistent and predictable rents, with leases signed for 
long durations (e.g. 10-15+ years) with contracted annual rent escalators –  on average 
about 2.0% per year, based on our estimates of currently outstanding hyperscaler shelves. 
We note that the pricing power of today could ultimately lead to new contracts being 
signed at rent escalators of 3% or more, as we are seeing come through the corporate 
credit space, but for now we assuming escalators in-line with currently outstanding 
shelves.  If the lease is triple-net, as new hyperscaler leases increasingly are, then all 
operating costs, including power, security, property taxes, insurance, and maintenance 
expenses, are passed through to the tenant in addition to base rent.

Current Market Conditions
We make four assumptions about our model data center, based off of levels that are 
comparable to current market conditions: 
1. The market will tolerate a Class A2 LTV up to 65% and a shelf LTV of up to 75%.
2. The market will tolerate a Class A2 DSCR down to 1.60 and a shelf DSCR down to 
1.25. 
3. The data center campus is valued at a 5.65% cap rate, the current universe average 
for hyperscaler shelves, holding steady over 5y. 
4. AANOI grows by 2% per annum, in line with many annual rent escalators. 
The unlevered return of this model data center is around 7.75%, meaning that, should cost 
of debt rise above that level, debt financing will be dilutive to IRR. Below a 7.75% funding 
cost, debt financing is accretive to the equity holder. 
As rates rise and debt service costs increase, the issuer is forced to cut LTV as the DSCR 
becomes the binding constraint. With a cost of debt at 6.5%, just a little bit higher than 
where current A2 deals are currently yielding from the hyperscaler class (~6.5%), the 
issuer would be limited to a ~50% LTV for the A2 class.  Rates need to fall by ~150bps 
before the issuer can tolerate a 65% LTV on the Class A2, or the issuer needs to set a 
lower coupon and issue at a discount to maintain DSCR limits. A 100bp increase in cost of 
debt to 7.5% forces the issuer down further to 45% LTV in our scenario of Class A2 DSCR 
restrictions.

Our analysis is sensitive to our cap rate assumption. If we hold fixed a  7% cost of debt at a 
60% LTV  with our 2% annual rent escalator, then we find the issuer can tolerate some cap 
rate expansion over the 5y period, though not too much. We are unsure what these 
companies are modeling for themselves, given the current rate outlook and the larger 
thematic backdrop for supply and demand of compute, not to mention their ability to add 
data centers with higher rent escalators to the master trust if they sign new leases. We 
believe they could be quite bullish on the medium-term valuations of their assets, but the 
higher-for-longer rate environment should counterbalance some of this thematic uplift. 
The more bullish their cap rate assumptions, the higher the debt financing rate they can 
tolerate.

At the Limit
What the market currently tolerates is different than what the rating agencies and 
structures ultimately tolerate. Even if the status quo is that LTVs max out at 65% on Class 
A2, most are allowed to issue up to 70%, with no restrictions on Class B. As rates rise, we 
think DSCR levels are likely to fall to accommodate higher interest costs while maintaining 
similar levels of leverage. 
We make four assumptions about our model data center, based off of levels that we think 
test the bounds of legal and/or rating agency restrictions: 
1. The issuer can bring paper with a Class A2 LTV up to 70% and shelf LTV up to 
85%.
2. The issuer  will tolerate a Class A2 DSCR down to 1.35 and a shelf DSCR down to 
1.10.
3.  As before, the data center campus is valued at a 5.65% cap rate and held flat over 
the 5y time horizon.
4. AANOI grows by 2% per annum, in line with many annual rent escalators. 
In this scenario, the issuer has quite a bit more bandwidth on both the Class A2 and across 
the stack to issue into the higher-rate environment. A 6.5% cost of debt now allows for up 
to a 60% LTV, while a 7% cost of debt accommodates up to a 55% LTV (vs 50% each in our 
previous scenario). This additional LTV gives the issuer more breathing room from the IRR 
perspective. We highlight in a darker shade of green the opportunities for issuance that 
are now tolerated in this scenario that were not in the prior.

Funding Economics: Colocation Shelves
Colocation data centers are operated very differently than hyperscaler data centers. For 
one, there are many more tenants in any single asset, with dozens of companies operating 
out of the same data center. For another, leases tend to be shorter, with most signing <5y 
leases that they frequently renew. Lastly, the type of lease itself can vary: where 
hyperscalers might sign a triple-net lease, other companies could be signing a modified 
gross lease, where only the power costs get passed through to the tenant and not 
insurance, taxes, and other opex.  Or they may have a different structure altogether in 
which they pay for a bundle through a service agreement which includes the space, power, 
connectivity, and all other expenses. For a deep dive on the colocation sector see 
Communications Infrastructure: Digital Real Estate - Initiating Coverage of US Data Center 
REITs. 
As a consequence, the rent revenue over the life of the ABS deal and beyond is not known 
for certain, and neither are the other costs, leading to less certainty of the NOI margin. 
Thus, annual revenue growth is an estimate rather than a known contracted outcome 
which creates more uncertainty but can lead to greater upside if rents continue to rise. At 
the moment, with power so scarce the direction of travel for rents is one way: JLL finds 
that since 2020, data center rental rates are up nearly 70% or on average 9% per year, 
with vacancy rates nearly down to 1% and pre-commitment rates at 95%. CBRE recently 
also found that the first half of 2026 saw 3-10MW deployment rates rising 8.3%, while 
smaller loads of 500kW-3MW saw rents rise 7.9%. 
We model a faster annual revenue growth of 2.5% for colocation data centers (vs 2% for 
hyperscalers), based on modeling of the colocation data center shelves in Intex. There are 
a number of reasons this could be more conservative than recent data points (e.g. from 
CBRE) suggest: tenants may be grandfathered into lower renewal rate contracts, or third-

allow for variations over a longer time horizon. 
Current Market Conditions
We make four assumptions about our model data center, based off of levels that are 
comparable to current market conditions: 
1. The market will tolerate a Class A2 LTV up to 61% and a shelf LTV of up to 68%.
2. The market will tolerate a Class A2 DSCR down to 1.70 and a shelf DSCR down to 
1.60.
3. The data center campus is valued at a 6.50% cap rate, the current universe 
average for colocation shelves, which we hold steady over 5y.
4. AANOI grows by 2.5% per annum, in line with modeled growth projections.
The unlevered return of this model data center is just over 9%, meaning that should cost 
of debt rise above that level, debt financing will be dilutive to IRR. Below a ~9% funding 
cost, debt financing is accretive to the equity holder – this is in fact more cushion than the 
hyperscaler data center because of the higher NOI growth. 
Yields on Class A2 colocation data center bonds currently sit around 6.8%, higher than 
both the hyperscaler and mixed business shelves. At 7% funding cost, the issuer is 
restricted to a 50% LTV in our model; a 100bps rise in rates pushes the issuer down to 
45% LTV while an improvement of at least 75bps is necessary before the issuer can once 
again bring a 60% LTV instrument.

Our third classification of data center shelves – "Mixed Business" – securitize both 
enterprise colocation and hyperscaler business within the same shelf. We often cannot be 
sure what type of workload a hyperscaler or other tenant is doing within a data center 
(e.g. cloud, AI inference, AI training) though we believe that most of the hyperscaler leases 
within the mixed business shelves skew towards cloud. 
In some ways, these mixed business shelves offer strengths of the prior two categories: 
they can be anchored by a long-term lease from a high-quality company, allowing for some 
rent stability, but a diversity of tenants offsets  the single-name exposure risk and allows 
for some capitalization on increasing rent rates. We find that these shelves have on 
average the tightest cap rates at 5.35%, about 30bps inside of the hyperscalers, and they 
price on top of the hyperscaler shelves. The shelves in our ABS data set also have the 
highest modeled annual rent growth at 3%. These features combine to an unlevered 
return of 8.5% for the asset. 
Current Market Conditions
We make four assumptions about our model data center, based off of levels that are 
comparable to current market conditions: 
1. The market will tolerate a Class A2 LTV up to 60% and a shelf LTV of up to 70%.
2. The market will tolerate a Class A2 DSCR down to 1.75 and a shelf DSCR down to 
1.50.
3. The data center campus is valued at a 5.35% cap rate, the current universe average 
for mixed business shelves, which we hold steady over 5y.
4. AANOI grows by 3.0% per annum, in line with modeled growth projections.
The unlevered return of this model data center is just around 8.5%, meaning that should 
cost of debt rise above that level, debt financing will be dilutive to IRR. Below an 8.5% 
funding cost, debt financing is accretive to the equity holder.
Yields on the Class A2 mixed business shelves currently sit around 6.6%. At a 6.5% yield 
on Class A2, we think the issuer here maxes out at a 45% LTV – in large part because we 
restrict the mixed business shelf at a higher DSCR based on the lowest observed level in 
this category. (On average, colocation shelves have the highest Class A2 DSCR, at around 
2.15, vs hyperscaler and mixed business at 1.95. But we use here the most lowest  of the 
group to signal what the market will allow, which is 1.6 for hyperscalers, 1.7 for colocation, 
and 1.75 for mixed business).

What can issuers do if rates rise and spreads continue to widen? First steps we think will 
be to flex around the LTV and DSCRs, but as we have shown above, at some point rates 
become too restrictive to continue to issue within the tolerated parameters of the typical 
ABS trusts. If issuers are forced to adapt to rates in excess of  8%, either because the 
underlying base  rate continues to climb and/or because spreads widen for the asset class, 
we think they can pursue a number of strategies and/or tolerate weaker IRRs, as we lay 
out below. 
1. Issue at a discount: First, a strategy already undertaken is to set a lower coupon and 
issue below par so that the yield clears the market while reducing debt service  – this 
keeps  monthly cashflows contained in tolerable DSCR ranges, but requires some 
assumption by the issuer that come time to refinance, the data center will have 
appreciated sufficiently in value. Given the supply/demand imbalance of compute today, 
and evidence in recent years of rising rents and asset valuations, we think this is an 
assumption that many data center developers are willing to make. 
Of course, if rates stay persistently higher for longer, we would expect that data center 
companies will take these higher funding costs into account when negotiating new leases. 
In an environment where compute is scarce, these companies have significant pricing 
power and should be able to negotiate better lease terms to account for the higher 
interest rates. The main challenge, then, is in the transition period into a higher rate 
environment, as we can see most clearly in Exhibit 33 between 2021 and 2023. To 
account for higher rates than  anticipated, bonds were issued at significant discounts to 
par. 
We have less visibility on when, exactly, lease terms are struck with no further room for 
negotiations. Raising financing for construction for a hyperscaler tenant generally requires 
an anchor tenant. We think it is possible that, prior to stabilization, lease terms could still 
be finalized if funding costs are more punitive than anticipated. However, once the asset is 
operational and the contract has been signed, with rent escalators already determined, we 
believe the issuer has limited ability to increase rents to account for a higher cost of debt.

4. Issue instead into the credit markets: Other markets have thus far tolerated higher 
LTCs and lower DSCRs, which we think could prove beneficial for ABS issuers in a higher-
for-longer rate environment. We dig deeper into the structures in the credit market in the 
section Implications for the Corporate Credit Market  , noting that the amortization 
features in most credit deals makes comparison more challenging. That said, most deals 
that have come through the IG and HY corporate markets have DSCRs that begin around 
1.10-1.20  given that cashflow certainty is provided by the backing lease (which tends to be 
15y with a 3% rent escalator). The one non-amortizing secured credit deal has an average 
DSCR over the 10y bond term of 1.30, still well below where the ABS market is today. 
The credit markets are also tolerating higher leverage: nearly all of the deals in credit have 
a construction component, so loan-to-cost (LTC) (rather than loan-to-value) is the better 
metric; LTCs average around 85-90% but can be as high as 97% in cases where 
amortization is full over the life of the bond and the underlying tenant credit quality is 
very high.  In the one non-amortizing deal, we believe LTV was ~90%. In addition, the depth 
of the credit markets should be attractive to data center operators, as well as the speed

low DSCRs given the difference in business model.

Leased Capacity Projections and Securitized Credit Supply 
Forecast
The impossible-to-ignore reality is that the quantity of data centers coming online 
through the end of the decade is very high – far  more than has come online so far this 
decade. Estimating the amount of data centers that will require debt financing is 
challenging, primarily because estimates for the amount of data centers (both online and 
under construction) vary between data providers. Often data from third-party data 
providers differ from what companies individually report, if they report at all, both in 
terms of timing and in capacity. However, all agree: the pace of new additions will remain 
elevated over the coming years, and we believe that most of those that are leased out will 
be backed by debt. 
Our Global Thematics Research team estimates that data center power in the US will 
grow meaningfully through the end of this decade, based on their bottom-up analysis of 
computing power associated with chip sales. They forecast that US data center power 
could grow from nearly 70GW in 2026 to 101GW in 2027 and then to 149GW 2028; 
however, there is a significant risk of a 30-40% shortfall given myriad constraints that 
developers continue to face with respect to securing new power access. (Their analysis

Given the relentless pace of data center construction, the natural question is why, 
then, securitized credit supply has so far lagged our expectations, and how supply will 
look in the years to come.  
The starting point for issuance forecasts in ABS & CMBS is first and foremost these 
capacity numbers: how much new capacity in MWs is there even to securitize in any given 
year? We think the data above show that there will be no shortage of new capacity next 
year and in 2028 that could be financed through the ABS or CMBS markets. 
The second question is how much of their capacity do the issuers actually want to fund 
through securitization? A diversity of funding sources is good for a company that will 
heavily depend on debt financing for growth. These companies are sure to explore the 
many avenues at their disposal – securitized, unsecured and secured credit, broadly 
syndicated loans, and private credit – to build a wide buyer base and ensure continued 
capital market access. The corporate credit markets are deeper and broader, allowing for 
faster execution, bigger size, and better liquidity. However, we do think some avenues will 
be more natural for  certain types of assets than others, and expect that multi-tenant, 
multi-campus sites will continue to find a primary home in securitized products.

Implications for the Corporate Credit Market  
Higher Treasury rates could also slow issuance of new secured data center-backed 
paper in corporate credit (a contrast from core hyperscaler issuance, which we see 
being more immune to rates). The slowdown in some cases should be temporary –  
especially for HQ tenants –  as lease economics can be improved, but it does 
introduce  a bigger lag in the supply evolution than it would have otherwise. Also, 
higher rates add to other supply-side constraints that are building, with potential 
shortages in power, people (work force), and politics. 
Another interesting debate is what happens to prior HY deals for data centers that 
are 1) close to completion/stabilization (4Q25 vintages), or 2) those in which debt 
has been issued, but construction is still some ways off. Stabilizing the assets 
might  have been enough for issuers to achieve lower cost of capital a few months 
ago, but is no longer a given for all borrowers. If rates stay high/rise further, we 
think that deals with higher-quality tenants and appropriate deleveraging through 
amortization may have the ABS take-out option that could result in lower coupon 
payments. 
From a metric standpoint (<70% LTV and >1.35x DSCR), we think corporate deals 
can meet the requirements to be refinanced in ABS, but if benchmark Treasury 
rates stay higher for longer, it is unlikely that the securitized credit market will 
offer materially lower rates to these borrowers. We also note that our current LTV 
methodology, based on capex and contract value, is likely to evolve: we still do not 
have enough of a  track record history to differentiate the issuers in the space, but 
we expect more LTV dispersion as time goes by. 
It also remains unclear if all the first-time issuers that have come to  market in the 
last ~12 months will transition into long-term data center developers and 
operators. Accordingly, some might look to stay in the corporate credit market to 
preserve optionality on their assets. This is especially true if power and political 
challenges limit the growth potential of these companies, which has been 
predicated on their ability to expand their portfolio of data centers. 
All things considered, particularly for the HY market, we now see less self-
adjustment in terms of supply and takeout. Asset quality is becoming increasingly 
important: cleaner deals with high-quality tenants and appropriate structures may 
still be able to access longer-term refinancing through alternative markets, such as 
loans and IG. By contrast, deals with lower-quality tenants or structural challenges 
– whether operational, such as permitting or power-access issues, or financial, such 
as limited amortization or weaker covenants – face a narrower range of refinancing 
options.

As in securitized credit, data center supply in the corporate credit market should be 
impacted by higher  rates that affect asset-level IRR economics. Compounding the rate 
environment,  political backlash regarding AI is increasing and developers are facing

Relative to securitized credit though, corporate credit deals can better adjust higher 
rates as  it is  plausible for the issuers to try and renegotiate the leases before 
stabilization to achieve better economics. Given the shortage of power and surging need 
for compute capacity, we think negotiating leverage currently lies with the powered-shell 
providers (PSPs). There is usually a lag between when leases are announced and financing 
comes to market, which could allow for renegotiation (such lag has varied since the 
beginning of the year, but we think it stays within 3 to 6 months in most cases). 
Importantly, and compared to securitized credit, this renegotiation happens before the 
lease would commence. So, purely based on higher rates, corporate credit issuance

By running a sensitivity analysis on the key drivers of IRR for powered shell providers, we 
estimate that returns are most sensitive to the following factors: 1) lease term; 2) 
construction cost; 3) starting lease revenue; 4) annual rent escalator; 5) construction 
period; and 6) margins (in case the lease is not triple-net). DCSR and debt coupon have 
lower impact on the IRR vs these six factors, so tenants can, in theory, help improve 
economics of certain deals so that IRRs clear the cost of capital hurdle by adjusting lease 
tenor, lease revenues, margins and escalators.  Construction cost and construction period 
are outside of the scope they can control, in our view. 
If we assume that the required equity return hurdle is  equal to the cost of capital, we 
find that less deals will clear the bar with cost of debt at 9% or higher ( Exhibit 44 ).

readily available. A number of these companies issued converts and therefore are already 
subject to dilution risk. Simply said, if projects are not funded with debt, there is a real risk

Refinancing Past HY Deals: Potential Avenues
Higher rates raise uncertainty around what future funding and refinancing paths for 
legacy HY deals could look like. HY project finance deals have been raised pre-
construction to finance the build-out of the actual shells. Accordingly,  credit spreads at 
new issue compensate investors for construction risk ( Exhibit 42 ), which is more front-
loaded in the initial years of the term of the bond. The leases on the other hand that pay 
the rent to service this debt are typically longer dated, which suggests that this debt will 
need to get refinanced at some point beyond the non-call period. 
When rates were lower, one could make the case that post construction and asset 
stabilization, existing expensive HY debt could be taken out at cheaper levels, with the 
ABS market being one potential outlet (apples to apples ABS deals were printing at ~6% 


[Page 25]
M
 Foundation
Morgan Stanley Research
25
when HY deals were getting done at ~8%). But the rate sell-off, especially if it goes 
further starts to complicate this thesis, at least on the surface.  
We think there is a path to lower cost of capital for certain HY issuers once 
construction is completed (see more details in here), but such path is now  more 
selective and might require flexibility across funding markets. To the extent that 
construction risk rolls off, there should be spread compression, and if this  exceeds any 
increase in Treasury yields, issuers could still refinance at a stable or even lower all-in cost 
of capital. However, this might be more selective. Observing trading levels since mid-
July, we think this refinancing is more feasible for deals with either high-quality 
tenants or strong credit support from high-quality counterparts. As such deals 
approach stabilization, we expect the differential between the project-finance bond and 
the tenant's unsecured bond of similar duration will narrow from current levels (which 
range from ~90bp to ~150bp). For projects with lower-quality public tenants, we expect 
differentials to be impacted by tenant performance and standalone credit profiles.
The ABS market has been assumed to be one potential refinancing outlet for data 
center backed corporate deals,  and we think that still holds even in a higher-for-longer 
rate environment, but with a smaller tailwind from lower cost of debt. Our 
understanding, based on data from select deals, is that there is generally a clear path for 
transactions to meet the <70% LTV and >1.35x DSCR requirements. Importantly, DSCRs in 
corporate credit are lower than in ABS because of the amortization features that exist in 
the vast majority of IG and HY deals that does not yet exist in ABS. 
We introduce a basic framework for LTV calculation, and find that most deals should 
be in the 40-50%  LTV range. For now, the corporate credit market underwrites deals on 
LTC (loan-to-cost) instead of LTV (loan-to-value), with a basic approach to LTV being the 
sum of total capex and total lease contract value. Under such an approach, most of the 
deals would have LTVs of <35%. We make the following adjustments:
• We assume a 10% premium on total capex following construction completion.  
We understand how 10% might be conservative, but we think this is the minimum 
completion premium afforded to data centers based on our work in Exhibit 34 , 
assuming a $15mm/MW construction cost for that asset. Other variables, such as 
overall scarcity, location, and other considerations could result in a higher 
premium. 
• We bring the total contract value to present value.  The annual escalator certainly 
helps adjusting revenue overtime, but as investors have limited access to the 
leases between the issuer and the tenant, a discount for a potential early 
termination or reassignment, not to mention the overall uncertainty, might make 
sense. Bringing the 10 to 15-year leases to present value using the debt coupon as 
discount rate  results in net present value of the contracts equivalent to ~60% of 
the total contract face value. In our view, this is another conservative assumption.

Appendix: An Overview of Financing Stabilized Assets with 
Data Center ABS & CMBS  
In securitized products, stabilized data centers can be financed either via an ABS or a 
SASB CMBS deal. As we have written before, the collateral differences are effectively 
non-existent: there is no clear trend in either market in terms of the types or sizes of data 
centers that back an ABS over a CMBS deal. The primary difference, in our view, is that an 
ABS has exposure to a dynamic pool of collateral and a SASB deal has static exposure: in 
ABS, data centers are part of a mastertrust and so the collateral pool backing a deal may 
change over time as assets are added to or subtracted from the trust and new deals are 
issued. In SASB, there is no such flexibility.
Historically, SASB data center deals were primarily floating rate and followed a 3+1+1 
structure – a 3y initial term with two one-year extension options. More recently, deals 
have skewed fixed rate and have mirrored the general design of an ABS, with the 5y IO 
period followed by an amortization should the deal not refinance. As the market coalesces 
around this one structure, we focus our analysis on this type of instrument. However, 
similar analysis could be repeated for a floating rate structure.
In ABS, bonds are interest-only until an anticipated repayment date (ARD) – generally five, 
but sometimes seven or more rarely ten years after issuance. If the deal does not 
refinance by ARD, the bond enters into a period of rapid amortization (which, importantly, 
does not on its own constitute an Event of Default), with all excess cashflow diverted to 
pay down principal sequentially. In many shelves, interest from subordinated classes is 
paused to repay down senior classes. To date, no data center deal has failed to refinance 
by its ARD, but there have been instances of failure to refinance in other corners of the 
market like triple-net lease.
The mastertrusts are evaluated on and restricted by two key metrics: the trust's Loan-to-
Value (LTV) and the Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR). On the former, often the 
threshold is that the Class A2 LTV cannot exceed 70%. On the latter, restrictions are a bit 
more nuanced: prior to ARD, if the Class A2 DSCR falls below a certain level (for example, 
1.35), then the deal will start trapping cash. A further decline to ~1.2 will cause the deal to 
amortize until the condition is cured. New deals cannot be issued from an existing trust 
that would breach those levels. 
In practice, shelves are well away from their limits. Below, we calculate a simplified DSCR 
for each shelf of just the AANOI divided by the relevant interest payments, excluding the 
maintenance capex and servicing fees, which are paid senior to debt cashflows and run ~1-
2% of AANOI. Shelves with the lowest DSCRs tend to be hyperscaler-backed (our 
category "Predominantly Hyperscaler") where leases are long term, locked-in, and often 
triple-net. This reduces the possibility for AANOI fluctuations over the life of the deal as is 
possible in colocation shelves where leases can be significantly shorter (e.g., 1y) and more 
noise is possible.  
Shelf LTVs are similarly low: the highest shelf Class A2 LTV (again, in a hyperscaler shelf 
ECORE, which has cashflows contracted and basically fixed until 2037) is ~67%, below the 
threshold of 70%. On average, Class A2 LTV sits around 55% while shelf LTV is a touch

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)

in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, 
as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment 
speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the 
MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even 
though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any 
premium paid.
