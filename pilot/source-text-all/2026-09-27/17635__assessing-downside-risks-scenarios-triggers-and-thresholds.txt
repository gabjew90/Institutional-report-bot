Executive Summary
1.	Backdrop of Rising Risks
The equity market rally this year has occurred despite 
rising interest rates, elevated geopolitical tensions and 
persistently high energy prices, raising questions about the 
sustainability of current market buoyancy.
2.	Scenario Analysis Framework
Scenario analysis provides a useful framework for helping 
us think about the potential outcomes. A key factor over the 
next 12 months will be the evolution of events in the Middle 
East and the global economic repercussions.
3.	Base Case (70% Probability): Fragile Status Quo
The most likely scenario sees the Middle East conflict 
neither resolved nor escalating significantly. This least-worst 
outcome points to moderate global growth, gently declining 
inflation, and equity markets buoyed by robust earnings. 
Central banks are expected to pause rate hikes next year, 
with long bond yields stabilising at around current levels.
4.	Downside Scenario (20% Probability): Escalation Risks
An escalation in the Middle East could trigger oil price 
spikes, a sell-off in risk assets, more hawkish central banks, 
and higher yields. If fiscal tightening also occurs in the US 
and Europe, global growth could falter, with oil-importing 
regions facing recessionary pressures.
5.	Upside Scenario (10% Probability): Rapid Resolution
A swift end to Middle East hostilities and falling energy 
prices could spark a rally in risk assets, lower bond yields, 
and a reacceleration in global growth, though higher interest 
rates would eventually follow.
6.	Key Downside Triggers
•	Oil Price Shock: An increase in the oil price to over $125 per 
barrel for a sustained period could threaten global growth, 
especially for net energy importers like Europe. Every $10 
rise in oil prices may cut global growth by 0.1–0.2% over the 
subsequent 12 months and lift inflation by 0.2–0.4%.
•	Monetary Policy Overtightening: While rising rates often 
coincide with strong markets, central banks risk over-
tightening due to policy lags. A flattening yield curve, 
especially if the 10-year/3-month spread drops below 
10bps, would be a warning sign.
•	Earnings Collapse: Strong earnings, underpinned by AI 
investment and large government deficits, have supported 
markets. Risks therefore include slowing AI spending or 
fiscal consolidation, both of which could hit corporate 
profits.
7.	Other Risks on the Horizon
Additional threats include US mid-term elections, further 
geopolitical shocks (e.g., China-Taiwan, Russia-Ukraine), and 
potential instability in private credit markets.
8.	Conclusion: Vigilance Required
While the base case remains constructive, investors are 
urged to monitor oil prices, monetary policy signals, and 
corporate earnings closely. Preparing for downside risks is 
essential in today’s uncertain environment.

(i) Higher Oil Price.
One of the surprises this year is that higher oil prices have so 
far had no discernible impact on either aggregate activity or core 
inflation (i.e. ex-food and energy). However, at some point that will 
change. It is therefore important to think about at what level oil 
prices start to become a problem.
A crude rule of thumb is that every $10 increase in the oil price 
reduces global growth by about 0.1% to 0.2% over the following 12 
months while increasing headline inflation by 0.2% to 0.4%. 
A separate heuristic claims that the stall speed growth rate of the 
global economy is about 2.5%1 - if the global economy is expanding 
by less than 2.5% per annum that is consistent with recessionary 
conditions in much of the world.
According to the IMF, global GDP is expected to grow by about 
3.0% this year and 3.4% next year. If growth were to decline by 
between 0.5% to 1.0% over the next twelve months that would see 
it approach the stall speed identified above.

Introduction
In the three years to 23 September 2026, MSCI World returned 
over 80%, equivalent to more than 20% per annum. Furthermore, 
some sectors - such as technology and communication services – 
experienced outsized returns over this period, indicating a degree 
of market concentration. Against a background of rising interest 
rates, heightened geopolitical risks and elevated energy prices, it is 
legitimate to ask for how much longer markets can remain buoyant.
Scenario Analysis
Performing a scenario analysis is a useful approach to help us 
think about possible outcomes. It provides a framework around 
which to form views of the world and allows people to impose 
their own opinions on top if they wish to adjust, for example by 
changing the probabilities or tweaking the narrative.
Our latest scenario analysis considers what happens in different 
states of the world based in large part around events in the 
Middle East. The full table is provided in the Appendix with a brief 
summary provided here for convenience:
-	 Base Case (70%). In our base case the situation in the 
Middle East remains without resolution but also without major 
deterioration for the foreseeable future. Whilst this is not the best 
outcome for either the Iranian regime or the US administration, it is 
uncomfortably acceptable for both. The risk for the Iranians is that 
the US authorities become impatient and actively pursue regime 
change. The risk for the US is that Iran steps up its attacks on 
shipping and oil infrastructure in the region, causing the oil price 
to spike. Given these risks, the current fragile status quo could 
be considered the least-worst outcome. In such a scenario, we 
foresee decent but not rampant global growth and equity markets 
supported by robust earnings. We would expect inflation to decline 
gently towards target, central banks to be close to the top of the 
rate hiking cycle and long bond yields to decline from the top of 
their recent ranges.
-	 Downside (20%). The next most likely outcome in our opinion 
is that the situation in the Middle East deteriorates. Iran could 
miscalculate and launch a series of successful attacks that cause 
havoc in the region, thereby pushing the US into a more forceful 
response. We would anticipate a spike in the oil price, a sell off 
in risky assets, more hawkish central bank rate expectations and 
a shift higher in yields across the curve. The downside would be 
compounded further if at the same time we saw enforced fiscal 
consolidation across the US and Europe as governments exhaust 
their spending potential. Global growth would be challenged, with 
some parts of the world – notably those that are net oil importers 
– facing recessionary conditions.
-	 Upside (10%). The least likely outcome is one in which growth 
reaccelerates significantly. This could happen if there is rapid 
resolution to the war in Iran that is accompanied by a swift and 
sharp decline in energy prices. Such a situation would likely be met 
with more dovish rate expectations, declining long bond yields and 
a rally in risky assets. Growth expectations would be expected to 
reaccelerate and that would eventually lead to an increase in rate 
expectations but with a lag.

An important area of the economy that would be impacted by 
rising rates is the housing market, since higher borrowing costs 
negatively impact affordability. So this is another signal we will 
be watching closely for any signs of a slowdown. It is notable that 
several housing markets around the world have been stagnant

(iii) Earnings Collapse.
One of the reasons why equity markets have been so buoyant this 
year is because earnings growth has been strong, supported by 
multiple factors including the vast amount of Artificial Intelligence 
(AI) investment spending and enormous government budget 
deficits. A risk to markets, therefore, is that one or both of these 
factors goes into reverse.
Gartner estimates that AI infrastructure spending will be about $1.5 
trillion this year and that there will be an additional $1.2 trillion 
AI-related spending on things like software and services.2 This is 
out of total global GDP of about $120 trillion. Moreover, Gartner 
forecasts that next year these expenditures will rise by about one 
third.
One company’s expenditure is another’s revenue so the scale of 
this spending is of huge benefit to the global corporate sector as 
a whole. It is also notable that an increasing proportion of these 
expenditures are being funded by debt issuance; five of the largest 
hyper-scalers have issued $130 billion in debt this year so far, 
about one fifth of treasury issuance at similar maturities. Should 
the cost of issuing debt rise or the market appetite for absorbing 
the debt diminish, this would pose a threat to AI-related spending.
It is also possible that a correction could come from a simpler 
cause. Analysts tend to extrapolate from the past into the future. 
This ignores cyclicality and it is likely that some part of the outsize 
increase in AI-investment is cyclical and will therefore slow down 
is heavily dependent on imported energy. Secondly, oil intensity 
in GDP production is declining over time as technology becomes 
more energy efficient and as non-fossil fuels become more widely 
used. Thirdly, the oil price is used here as a simple proxy for 
energy prices in general. However, some countries and sectors 
are disproportionately impacted by changes in the prices of other 
energy inputs, such as natural gas or diesel. These factors also 
need to be taken into consideration.
(ii) Overtightening of Monetary Policy.
It is a commonly held view that tighter monetary policy is bad for 
equity markets. However, this view has little empirical support. In 
fact, it is more normal for equity markets and interest rates to be 
rising at the same time. This is because central banks normally hike 
into economic strength which is typically an environment that is 
good for corporate earnings.

However, it is also common for central banks to overdo the amount 
of tightening that is necessary. This is related to the variable lag 
between monetary policy being implemented and its effect on the 
economy. A hike in rates today will affect the economy over time, 
with a peak impact at some indeterminate point in the future. 
Central bankers therefore typically keep policy tight until there is 
hard evidence that it needs to be eased and it is very difficult to 
anticipate when that will be necessary.
There is therefore a risk in the current environment that the world’s 
central banks continue to hike rates in the face of inflation that is 
stubbornly above target up to the point at which economic activity 
and inflation both start to fade. Normally this is asymmetric: 
economies expand consistently over time then experience a brief 
but painful correction.
One of the indicators to pay attention to in this regard is the slope 
of the yield curve. The academic work in this field typically uses 
the spread between the 3-month and the 10-year Treasury yield. 
With the exception of 2023-24 - which was arguably distorted by the 
scale of Covid stimulus – this yield spread has been an excellent 
indicator of US recessions. When the 10-year yield has declined 
below the 3-month yield a recession has very often following within 
the following 12-months.

AI corporate spending
At the same time, government budget deficits are unusually large 
given where we are in the economic cycle. While these deficits add 
to the stock of debt and create potential problems in the future, 
they have also provided a major support to growth and hence to 
corporate profitability. Should there be pressures from the market 
or politically for governments to rein in spending, that would be a 
headwind to corporate profitability, potentially to the tune of $1 
trillion dollars or more . 
Early warning signals to look out for with regards to corporate 
profits therefore include:
-	 News that AI-related spending is slowing down
-	 Any signs of fiscal consolidation
Summary
Scenario analyses are useful tools for helping us think about 
different states of the world. In our base case the status quo 
persists for the time being with robust but not rampant growth and 
decent equity market performance.
However, this view is not without risk. This report has identified 
three potential sources of risk: (i) oil prices (ii) rates (iii) corporate 
profits. There are undoubtedly other risks, including the impending 
mid-term US elections, the possibility of another spike in 
geopolitical risk e.g. China invading Taiwan, a deterioration in the 
Russia-Ukraine situation, and potential for a fallout in private 
credit that spills over into the economy and markets more broadly. 
Whilst none of these is in our central view, it is nonetheless 
prudent to consider what could go wrong before the event. As the 
saying goes, by failing to prepare, you are preparing to fail.

Iran
Iran increases the frequency of attacks 
on ships and tankers transiting through 
the SoH and also on the US and other 
countries in the region. Traffic through 
the SoH grinds to a halt. The US 
retaliates militarily and economically 
but is ineffective in deterring the Iranian 
leadership. Iran can cheaply and easily 
disrupt the flow of oil and other goods 
through the SoH while it becomes 
increasingly difficult for the US to defend 
itself and the region more broadly. Other 
countries reluctantly get dragged in and 
there is increasing talk of US boots on the 
ground, for example in taking control of 
strategic locations such as Kharg Island. 
The status quo persists, in which there is 
an uneasy de facto truce between the US 
and Iran with occasional flare ups. There 
is no permanent ceasefire but nor is there 
meaningful escalation. Iran continues to 
use the SoH as a point of leverage with the 
US while the US blockade remains in place. 
However, ships and tankers sneak through, 
limiting the impact on the global economy. 
From time-to-time Iran is forced to adopt 
a more conciliatory tone as sanctions take 
effect and hit the economy. Conversely, 
there are some periods during which Iran 
feels more emboldened and increases 
its attack frequency on US assets in the 
region and other Middle Eastern countries.
Sanctions bite more quickly than expected, 
forcing the Iranian leadership to the 
negotiating table to make a deal. The 
SoH reopens and vessels start to move 
freely once again through it. The ceasefire 
holds and the US and Iran move towards a 
more permanent agreement that includes 
granting Iran access to some nuclear 
materials for domestic energy production.

Ukraine
Putin loses patience and, sensing that 
Ukraine is low on supplies of key military 
hardware, takes the opportunity to 
intensify Russia’s military presence in 
Ukraine with more tanks, more soldiers 
and more bombings. There is little 
response from NATO.
Russia and the Ukraine remain stuck in a 
stalemate that sees Russian forces make 
very slow gains at relatively high cost 
at the same time as Ukraine becomes 
increasingly successful with its drone 
attacks.
The success of a growing number of 
Ukrainian drone attacks encourages Putin 
to seek a diplomatic solution. An uneasy 
truce is negotiated.
Political 
backdrop
Despite President Trump’s best efforts to bring the situation in the Middle East to a negotiated conclusion, the Iranian government 
has not played ball. If the war remains unresolved by the time of the mid-term elections in early November, it will likely be a 
contributory factor creating headwinds for the Republicans.  Even if an amicable solution were reached today, there is insufficient 
time for the full benefit to be felt for the mid-terms. One consequence is that the Trump administration is likely to try to implement 
other measures to encourage voter support. However, the options are limited.
Financial 
markets
Markets move into risk off mode in 
response to the increased uncertainty 
and the associated drag on the global 
economy. Some countries experience 
recession. Equity markets correct by 10%-
20% and the yield curve inverts as central 
banks initially hike rates to mitigate 
against rising inflationary pressures. Credit 
spreads widen.
Most of the time markets remain focussed 
on factors other than geopolitics although 
occasionally risk metrics spike when there 
is a deterioration in the Middle Eastern 
situation. Long bond yields stay elevated 
but do not break out to the upside. The 
short end of the curve moves in line with 
central bank actions. Equities move higher 
in response to ongoing robust earnings 
growth at the same time as rotation 
continues.
Markets breath a sigh of relief and 
there is a healthy rally in risk assets. 
Equity markets rebound. Credit spreads 
tighten. Bonds experience a modest 
rally as inflation risks subside and rate 
expectations soften.
Oil price
Oil and gas prices quickly move higher by 
10% to 20% in response to the heightened 
risks. Prices then stay elevated and 
grind higher over time as the situation 
continues to deteriorate.
Energy prices initially remain rangebound, 
with the oil price levelling off in the range 
$80 - $100 per barrel. During periods when 
more traffic starts to move through the 
Strait of Hormuz oil prices recede towards 
the lower end of the range and rise 
towards the top end when there is a spike 
in attacks.
Oil and gas prices recede as the risk 
premium reduces. However, it takes three 
to six months for prices to return to 
where they were before the war started 
due to the need for countries to rebuild 
inventories and the fact it takes time to 
transport oil and gas from the Middle East 
to the importing countries.
Precious 
metals/FX
Gold trades higher,  within the range 
of $5,000/oz to $5,500/oz but with the 
possibility to go higher the longer the 
situation persists. The CHF is also a 
beneficiary as part of the risk off trade.
Gold stays in the current broad range 
($4,000 - $5,000/oz). We also expect the 
USD to remain rangebound against the 
majors and on a trade weighted basis.
The gold price trades lower with the range 
of around $3,500/oz to $4,500/oz, as 
lower rates are offset by the diminished 
geo-political risks. The USD resumes its 
downtrend; however the CHF is weaker 
against the GBP and EUR.
