However, we increasingly see the building blocks of an investable nuclear

frameworks, foreign capital, and hyperscaler engagement converge around a new 
nuclear compact. We believe the market will underwrite the nuclear trade if all 
stakeholders come to the table and agree on a framework to share development 
risks and employ creative financing arrangements. In any scenario, we still believe 
load growth from data centers, electrification, onshoring, coupled with the desire 
for firm, clean power circles back to new nuclear, where first development 
(domestic or abroad) will spark quick followers.
•

programs. States have also paved the way with ambitious goals (NY) and 
funding frameworks tied to restarts (MI). South Korea and Japan’s trade

Sell-Off Obscures Meaningful Improvements
Nuclear equities traded favorably through most of 2025 on accelerating “power 
scarcity” narratives tied to data centers and AI, but the group has largely contracted in 
2026 (down +50% on average YTD). Broader power and energy-adjacent names have 
also experienced pressure, yet nuclear has de-rated more sharply, in our view. 
Momentum unwind and data center ramp concerns stand out as key culprits, but we see 
these headwinds as transitory ultimately, secondary to the structural need for clean, 
reliable, firm generation. Despite the sell-off, we see an increasingly improving 
backdrop for nuclear, reflected in more creative financing and risk-sharing across 
federal, state, and corporate channels. We see these factors enabling additional nuclear 
development financing and brinigng meaningful tailwinds to the nuclear sector. 
Moreover, CEG’s partnership with GOOG to expand capacity at operating PJM nuclear 
units represents another encouraging data point, sending CEG shares ~15% higher 
(same day trading). While the CEG/GOOG deal represents an uprate rather than a new 
build, it reinforces a key point: investors are willing to underwrite nuclear when 
market structure and counterparties de-risk the outcome.

•

catalyze US power-exposed equities, including advanced nuclear peers like OKLO,

What Drives a Recovery
•
Forming a new nuclear compact. While we see an increasingly supportive policy 
backdrop conducive to new nuclear builds, supported by foreign capital as well via 
South Korea’s recent $120bn commitment, we believe a share re-rating will require 
more than federal and state support, and will instead depend on firm commitments 
and risk sharing amongst nuclear stakeholders. Hyperscalers continuing to 
financially support development, such as the XE JDA with AEP and Amazon, helps 
build momentum and market confidence. Ultimately, we believe the market can 
underwrite the nuclear trade should all stakeholders (hyperscalers, EPC, supply 
chain, developers, utilities) come to the table and agree to a framework that shares 
development risks. 
•
Lessons learned from Vogtle, and why now is different. Southern Company’s 
Plant Vogtle (Units 3 and 4) is often considered a cautionary tale by nuclear skeptics 
given the cost overruns and delayed construction timeline, but we see significant 
benefits for “next-of-a-kind” projects that apply the lessons Vogtle and the Chinese 
large scale reactors taught. While the two Vogtle units exceeded initial budget plans 
meaningfully, cost overruns were largely attributed to construction execution rather 
than the nuclear design itself, suggesting that the final ~$15,000/kW build-cost for 
Units 3 & 4 reflects the ceiling, not the standard. In fact, an MIT study cites $6-
7,000/kW in potential cost savings on second-of-a-kind nuclear, reflecting first-time 
learnings that can be implemented at a next project site (see 2Identifying Savings to Get to “Next-of-a-Kind” NuclearFigure 3Federal Grants and Loans). Inflation 
likely erodes some of the $6-7,000/kW in potential savings, but even partial savings 
can meaningfully drive down levelized costs when paired with productivity 
efficiencies that truncate the construction timeline.

Learning Curves, Lead Times and LCOEs
Sentiment toward nuclear has recently shifted, but we believe market confidence will 
recover as new projects deploy across the US and abroad, driven primarily by 
demonstrating FOAK learnings and efficiency gains that lower unit economics. To 
support this view, we take a deeper look into new nuclear economics, presenting a 
levelized cost of energy (LCOE) analysis that illustrates the path from first-of-a-kind to 
Nth-of-a-kind. We view Plant Vogtle’s estimated cost (~$15,000/kW) as a worst-case 
scenario ceiling rather than a de facto benchmark, and we expect lessons learned to 
drive significant improvements in the next build. Although no new large-scale project 
has been announced yet, we believe growing policy support from the DOE and DOC 
will help establish a commercial framework, with international initiatives appearing 
promising as well. Using our interactive LCOE model, we map a multi-scenario bridge 
from true FOAK (including cost overruns), to realistic NxtOAK, to mature and blue-sky 
NOAK outcomes, drawing on work from MIT, NYSERDA and other experts. A NOAK 
LCOE approaching ~$100/MWh might look ambitious today, and a blue-sky NOAK of 
$81-87/MWh even more so, but we see a near-term path to NxtOAK LCOE $113-124/
MWh, which could effectively prove the large-scale nuclear investment case and entice 
meaningful following projects.

Lessons Learned: Going from FOAK. . .
•
FOAK worst case driven by one-time factors (incomplete design) compounded 
by non-repeating factors (COVID). Our analysis estimates the unsubsidized 
LCOE of the most recent large-reactor US builds (Plant Vogtle Units 3 and 4) at ~
$211/MWh under today’s Energy Dominance Finance program (or ~$250/MWh at 
50/50 debt/equity capitalization). These levels land higher than initially projected 
and well-above alternative baseload resources, explaining why market participants 
did not immediately follow up with subsequent builds despite rising power demand. 
With a now completed design (effectively only MSD% completed at Vogtle start) 
and the very low likelihood of another impending worldwide pandemic, we view the 
next reactor build as having materially lower risk and this FOAK data point as 
ultimately a particularly weak proxy for repeat-build economics, especially under a 
more disciplined contracting framework and overall supportive financing policy.
•
Vogtle set the ceiling, not the standard. Significant cost overruns and timeline 
delays marred the Vogtle development, but we believe current nuclear skepticism 
fails to appreciate the meaningful progress made from lessons learned in the 
2023/2024 Units 3 and 4 COD. Instead of seeing Units 3 and 4 as the high-water 
mark for large-reactor development, some investor feedback frames the ~$15,000/
kW build-cost and implied >$200/MWh LCOE as the industry standard and 
therefore the set price of second-of-a-kind nuclear, very incorrect in our view. We 
disagree and assert that Plant Vogtle misteps conversely provide a road map on 
where future projects can reduce costs.

Identifying the cost-outs post-Vogtle. According to a 2024 MIT study, a next-of-a-
kind build on a brownfield site can avoid a significant portion of Plant Vogtle’s cost 
overruns by applying lessons learned from Units 3 and 4.  By layering in additional 
efficiency and productivity gains, a project sponsor could realize the full $6-7,000/
kW in potential savings the study identifies. Scenarios 1 and 2 of our LCOE model 
conservatively focus on the standalone cost overruns; our “blue-sky” case 5 applies 
the full savings, shown further in the note. The study attributes Vogtle’s roughly 2x 
cost overrun to supply chain gaps, declining labor productivity, NRC licensing 
hurdles, and EPC changes, which together drove an outsized indirect cost profile. 
Assuming full cost overruns — consistent with FOAK costs at Vogtle Units 3 and 4 
— results in a ~$211/MWh unsubsidized LCOE under current federal funding 
programs. Reducing cost overruns by roughly 50% improves the unsubsidized 
LCOE to $178/MWh. We view this improvement as encouraging, though still 
conservative, given that the construction timeline remains unchanged at 10 years and 
capital costs are not assumed to improve beyond the ~50% reduction in cost 
overruns.

. . . To NOAK Depends on a Successful “NxtOAK”
•
Getting to NOAK. . . We estimate the blue-sky levelized cost of energy for nth-of-
a-kind nuclear at approximately $105/MWh unsubsidized and $81-87/MWh 
subsidized with a 30-40% investment tax credit. However, recent inflation does 
present an offset versus expected cost reductions. The blue-sky estimate (case 5) 
reflects the full ~$6,600/kW in potential savings cited in the MIT study, resulting in 
a sub-$90/MWh LCOE (subsidized) that would easily compete with other firm 
power resources when pricing in nuclear’s clean power premium. However, 
assuming full cost savings appears ambitious near-term, implying an impressive ~
$7,400/kW in build-costs vs. Vogtle’s estimated ~$15,000/kW. As well, the MIT 
study specifies that the $6-7,000/kW savings represent a brownfield site, not 
greenfield. Considering the limitations of new nuclear on brownfield sites, we 
present an alternative greenfield NOAK case that we believe investors can more 
comfortably underwrite vs. our blue-sky. We assume a project sponsor reduces 
capital costs at a greenfield site marginally to ~$14,000/kW vs. FOAK’s ~$15,000/
kW cost profile. We then apply significant learnings and efficiency gains in our 
analysis, represented in a shortened construction timeline — down to 6 years versus 
~10 years at FOAK — and a lower equity hurdle rate of 10% vs. our 12% FOAK 
assumption. Notably, these improvements drive the LCOE to $105-115/MWh 
subsidized, marking a significant improvement from FOAK and a not-too-distant 
delta from our blue-sky scenario.

Methodology
•
We conduct a simplified NPV analysis to forecast a range of large-scale nuclear 
LCOEs across several parameters that we broadly characterize as FOAK, NxtOAK 
(next-of-a-kind), and NOAK, with particular focus on cost overrun risk. In our 
analysis, we frame five unique cases: 1) FOAK with overruns meant to replicate 
Vogtle Units 3 and 4, 2) an “in-between” FOAK case with just half of the overruns 
from Case 1, 3) NxtOAK, which assumes no cost overruns and modest realization of 
the ~$7,000/kW savings identified by the MIT report, 4) greenfield NOAK, with 
modest savings but a meaningfully shortened construction timeline, and 5) blue-sky 
NOAK, capturing the full $6-7,000/kW in cost savings. Across these instances, we 
sensitize our LCOE model across debt financing, construction timeline, tax credit 
recognition, and several fixed and variable cost levers. We model a set of cases 
where cost overrun risk shapes first of a kind execution, and step this down over 
subsequent cases as Vogtle learnings and carefully managed project execution drives 
cost overruns and build-time lower. In our two NOAK cases, we tether to no cost 
overrun risk, and leverage learnings from previous deployments as a driver to the 
upside to typical cost savings for our blue sky scenario.
•
We ground our analysis in academic literature and federally funded studies. Notable 
work in the field includes research from MIT nuclear engineer Koroush Shirvan 
(2022, 2024), the recent NYSERDA analysis (2026), Lazard LCOE analysis (2026), 
and DOE’s Liftoff report (2024). We marry takeaways across these analyses to form 
our own proprietary view on where nuclear LCOEs might land as we transition from 
early-stage deployments focused on rectifying lessons learned from Vogtle Units 3 
and 4, to strong execution and supply chain redundancy in nth-of-a-kind 
deployments.
