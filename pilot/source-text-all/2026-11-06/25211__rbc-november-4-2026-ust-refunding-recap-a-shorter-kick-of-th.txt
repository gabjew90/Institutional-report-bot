UST Refunding Recap: A shorter kick of the can 
Treasury made no changes to coupon auction sizes at today’s Quarterly Refunding Announcement (QRA), but there was a 
slight change in their long-held forward guidance. Despite some market chatter about potential long-end issuance cuts at 
today’s QRA, Treasury held auction sizes steady as we expected. However, there was a tweak to the now-standard forward 
guidance, which has been a part of refunding announcements since February 2024. Prior versions had said Treasury anticipates 
holding auction sizes unchanged for “at least several quarters”, but today’s version dropped the “at least”. This does tighten up the 
timeframe for potential changes and is consistent with the fact that both primary dealers and the Treasury Borrowing Advisory 
Committee (TBAC) generally believe increases in coupon sizes will be warranted by the end of the fiscal year. For reference, the 
final QRA of FY 2027 is three quarters away (August 4, 2027). We see a high probability that this guidance is dropped altogether 
(or fundamentally altered) in the next refunding statement. 
• 
The market’s view on what this guidance is trying to communicate seems more two-directional now – but we see it 
entirely in terms of coupon increases. Since the “several quarters” has been part of the statement, it has generally been 
viewed by the market as guiding on the timing of coupon increases. But as we wrote in our preview, the discussion this quarter 
was much more two-directional, with many suggesting that changes to this guidance could open the door to long-end auction 
cuts. We see today’s changes much more in terms of increases, especially given the TBAC discussion and primary dealer 
survey referenced above.  
• 
Markets took the announcement largely in stride. The long-end did underperform today, but the belly-led rally was much 
more a function of the oil selloff which in turn followed JD Vance and President Trump’s acknowledgments that Republicans’ 
underperformance in yesterday’s mid-terms was largely driven by the Iran War. If markets were at all disappointed in the lack 
of cuts, the back end certainly didn’t show it – 20s underperformed slightly on the curve and a post-QRA dip in long-end swap 
spreads evaporated almost immediately.

We still see increases focused in the belly/front-end but are now pushing those back from May 2027 refunding to 
August 2027. We still have relatively low conviction on the specific timing though given our lack of insight into Treasury’s 
reaction function around bill supply. We also don’t see those increases being a “twist”, where Treasury increases front-end 
coupons while simultaneously cutting back-end coupons. Given the expected financing needs in coming years, all auction 
sizes are likely headed higher, or at very best staying constant.

The debt limit may also impact the timeline for coupon increases. The debt limit is likely to become binding again early 
next year, with the Treasury potentially operating under “extraordinary measures” through the end of FY27. On a very 
preliminary basis, we would expect the debt limit x-date may once again be a somewhat binary outcome between July/August, 
or late-October (depending on whether Treasury can last to the September 15th corporate tax date). Treasury has historically 
not increased coupon auctions while the debt limit is binding. As such, the increases we are currently penciling in for August 
2027 may have to wait until November or later. 
• 
Treasury added another line about market outreach on supply/demand dynamics, but we don’t see much signal there. 
Aside from dropping “at least” Treasury also added that they will, “…engage with a variety of market participants to better 
understand evolving Treasury supply and demand dynamics. In considering any changes, Treasury will be guided by the 
objective of financing the government at the lowest cost over time.” That second sentence, in particular, seems like a truism, 
and we didn’t take much directional signal from this. 
The auction cycle for the 7-year benchmark will change from monthly to quarterly. In other words, it will mirror 10s/20s/30s 
(one new issue each quarter with two re-openings) rather than 2s/3s/5s (with one new CUSIP per month). This change has been 
under discussion in some form since February 2023.  The change will go into effect with the March 2027 auction. We don’t expect 
this change to fundamentally alter the total amount of issuance, but fewer, larger CUSIPs should provide some benefits in the form 
of smoother settlement, reduced fails, and increased netting benefits. 
The combination of recent bill auction increases (which have surprised us to the upside) and Treasury’s initial financing 
estimates released on Monday have led to some shifts in our near-term bill forecasts. This was entirely a shift in the expected 
timing of our cash flows and cash balance assumptions rather than a change in overall deficit levels. There was also a very large 
$33bn outflow from HHS related to prescription drugs on October 30th that we had not been expecting (which does go part of the 
way towards explaining our surprise in the magnitude of the recent bill increases). Having shifted our cash balance forecasts to be 
more in line with Treasury’s and pulling some of the outflows we had penciled in for Q1 CY2027 into the current quarter, we have 
pushed out the next bill supply cuts to late November. We agree with Treasury’s assessment that bill supply will need to be ramped 
back up in January. As noted above, we would expect the debt limit to become binding around mid-March, which does cloud the 
outlook for bills beyond that point. We are not currently penciling in debt limit related paydowns but will be adding those in as the 
situation develops. Note that Treasury would be able to continue issuing bills somewhat normally, perhaps for several months, 
under “extraordinary measures” so any required paydowns will likely be focused in late spring/early summer.

TBAC’s presentations to Treasury were focused on AI/capex-related issuance and the drivers of investor demand for 
derivative duration vs. cash UST. The first found “no clear evidence” that AI/capex-related issuance was “crowding out” UST. 
This generally aligns with our views, as we have been characterizing this issuance as merely a marginally exacerbating factor in the 
UST selloff this year. The second serves as a good primer on why some investors prefer derivative duration exposure. But it also 
noted some ideas that could drive more end user demand for UST, including lessening balance sheet constraints, changing 
accounting and tax treatments, and encouraging greater buildout of repo capabilities, among others. Those presentations are both 
worth a look and can be found here and here.
