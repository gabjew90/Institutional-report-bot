THE TD COWEN INSIGHT
As a follow-up to our 2025 report, we present a deep dive analysis on supply / demand and
cost structure across the uranium, conversion, enrichment, and recycling steps in the nuclear
fuel cycle. We are bullish on uranium supply / demand and the equities with Cameco and
Denison Mines our top picks.
Our Thesis
We see a constructive backdrop for uranium mining and conversion, while we see uncertainty
across enrichment and recycling.
What Is Proprietary?
Our cost analysis for uranium covers 97% of global supply, and we see higher prices needed to
justify new mine development. Our enrichment analysis aggregates ~14MM SWU of expansion
plans among US/European companies, equivalent to a ~60% increase in regional supply, and
our cost analysis suggests higher SWU prices may be needed to justify attractive returns.
Finally, we present cost estimates and a sensitivity analysis for various recycling pathways
and see recycling conventional light water fuel potentially cost prohibitive without significantly
higher uranium prices while recycling fast reactor fuel could be viable but requires a fleet of
fast reactors.
Financial & Industry Model Implications
Higher mine development costs support our recently upgraded uranium price assumptions
and correspondingly higher cash flows and valuations for our uranium coverage. Potential
new enrichment supply and high/uncertain capital costs create uncertainty around the long-
term outlook for returns. While recycling looks challenging today, if global nuclear capacity
is going to achieve aspirations, fuel availability may become limited, creating an obvious
economic incentive for recycling.
What To Watch
Price evolution of uranium, conversion, and enrichment services. Progress on new nuclear
capacity in North America and Europe through uprates / restarts and new-builds as a leading
indicator for fuel demand. Further refinement of enrichment capacity plans, how much
actually gets built, how much is retired, and how much backlog enrichment companies have
accumulated.
Stock Conclusions
We're most bullish on uranium pricing and highlight Cameco and Denison Mines as top
uranium picks. We initiated Centrus and Oklo at Hold owing to uncertainty over returns on
enrichment investments and the viability of reactor / recycling costs, respectively. We continue
to view GE Vernova as having the most commercial progress among public company reactor
designs and call out X-energy as our preferred pure-play reactor technology company.

Portfolio Managers' Summary 
This report provides an overview of the nuclear fuel cycle, covering mining, 
conversion, and enrichment as well as existing and future recycling pathways. We do 
not cover fuel fabrication as there are currently no pure play investments in the 
public markets.  
We find nuclear capacity growth requiring replacement uranium supply, supporting 
our upwardly revised uranium price deck. Conversion and enrichment have become 
increasingly strategic and constrained. We see conversion entering a deficit later this 
decade. While enrichment is currently tight, a material amount of capacity expansion 
has been announced, though not all expansion plans are definitive. Recycling, 
particularly with fast reactors, could be an attractive long-term pathway; however, 
processing costs are unproven, and a closed-loop system requires a transition to a 
fleet of fast reactors.   
Against this backdrop, we favor Cameco and Denison Mines for exposure to uranium 
prices and in the case of Cameco also conversion and enrichment.  
Along with this report, we have initiated coverage of Centrus and Oklo, both rated 
Hold. Centrus is the only publicly traded pure play uranium enrichment company with 
established technology pursuing commercial capacity expansion today. Oklo is the 
only publicly traded company actively developing a sodium fast reactor with plans to 
use pyroprocessing for recycling, which, if successful, could be highly disruptive.  
While not related to the fuel cycle analysis in this report, X-energy is our favored pure 
play nuclear technology company. 
Mining - We outline an increasingly constructive uranium market in which visible 
global reactor growth, life extensions, restarts, government support, and fuel-cycle 
security concerns are driving higher long-term requirements, while concentrated 
production, operating constraints, depleted mobile inventories, and lengthy 
greenfield development timelines limit the supply response. Although near-term utility 
coverage may temper contracting urgency, rising unfilled requirements and the need 
to secure diversified replacement supply point to a structurally tight market through 
the balance of the decade, supporting our decision to raise our long-term uranium 
price forecast to $100/lb from $95/lb in our recent preview note (link). 
Conversion – Conversion is becoming an increasingly strategic and constrained 
segment of the fuel cycle as global nuclear demand grows. Conversion markets have 
tightened significantly since 2022, driven by renewed utility contracting, geopolitical 
concerns around Russian supply, and limited spare capacity, resulting in a multi-year 
bull market for conversion services. Global conversion demand is projected to rise 
from approximately 66,000 tU in 2026 to 82,500 tU by 2030 and potentially 145,000-
200,000 tU by 2040. Existing primary capacity remains concentrated among a 
handful of suppliers, and secondary inventories continue to decline. While we believe 
markets are balanced for the next few years, we expect conversion deficits to emerge 
later this decade, unless meaningful new capacity is added, with potential 
contributions from projects such as Springfields and UEC’s proposed UR&C facility. 
Enrichment – Uranium enrichment is a critical step in nuclear fuel manufacturing, 
and while global supply is greater than demand, Russia accounts for 43% of this 
supply and is being prohibited in several markets. Furthermore, national and energy 
security aspirations, particularly in the US, create an opportunity for new investment. 
While growing demand from nuclear capacity expansions could further tighten the 
market, a significant amount of new supply is in planning phases or under 
construction, which, along with high capital cost and a competitive landscape 
dominated by state owned enterprises, could eventually challenge returns.

Our analysis aggregates a total of ~14MM separative work units (SWU) of gross 
enrichment capacity plans in the US and Europe, equivalent to ~60% of current 
regional capacity with startup spread from mid-late 2020s into the 2030s. While a 
material figure, new volumes will be needed to displace Russian imports, offset 
attrition, and meet growing nuclear generation capacity, some of which uses higher 
enriched fuel like HALEU. Still, with so many variables, it is difficult to say whether the 
market will face an over- or undersupply. 
Limited enrichment capacity for high assay low enriched uranium (HALEU) used in 
advanced reactors appears to be driving a significant premium to low enriched 
uranium (LEU) currently. We expect a premium to continue over time due to higher 
handling costs; however, how much of a premium remains unclear.  
While the fate of enrichment prices may be uncertain, the input is only a small portion 
in the overall cost of manufacturing nuclear fuel and thus unlikely to materially 
change operating costs for nuclear power plants. 
Recycling - Recycling nuclear fuel could dramatically expand utilization of the 
world's uranium resource without the need for conversion and enrichment, though 
our analysis shows costs are unproven and may be uneconomic at current nuclear 
fuel prices. Furthermore, concerns around weapons proliferation present a challenge. 
However, if the world is going to significantly increase nuclear generation capacity, 
strains on resource availability may build support for recycling.  
Our recycling analysis draws two primary conclusions. First, recycling conventional 
reactor fuel (light water) is inefficient and expensive, only slightly increases utilization 
of the uranium resource, and is only economically viable if uranium prices increase 
~4-6x. Second, recycling with a fleet of fast neutron reactors running in a closed-loop 
system could enable fuel costs below current fresh uranium while significantly 
reducing long-lived radioactive waste. However, processing costs are unproven, and 
creating a fleet of fast reactors would require a massive investment and shift in 
technology preference.

Uranium Top Picks 
Cameco Corp. (CCO-T, CCJ-N) – Cameco—our top pick in the uranium space—is a 
vertically-integrated pure-play investment in the nuclear sector and best positioned 
among the uranium miners to benefit from growing demand, with Tier-1 uranium 
mining assets capable of delivering into what is expected to be widening longer-term 
deficits. The company also plays a leading role in refining and conversion (link to our 
conversion site tour note) and has exposure to enrichment through its 49% stake in 
Global Laser Enrichment. The company's 49% stake in a large reactor developer 
provides further vertical integration with the addition of reactor builds and fuel 
services. 
Denison Mines Corp. (DML-T, DNN-N) – Recently chosen as our top SMID-cap pick, 
Denison offers differentiated exposure to the uranium market through its high-grade 
Athabasca Basin development portfolio, led by its 90%-owned Wheeler River project. 
Construction is underway at Phoenix, which is expected to become Canada’s first 
commercial-scale in-situ recovery uranium mine and is targeted for first production 
by mid-2028, positioning Denison to deliver a sizeable source of low-cost supply into 
an expected period of widening uranium deficits. The company also retains longer-
term growth through the conventional underground Gryphon deposit, while its 22.5% 
interest in the McClean Lake joint venture provides exposure to existing processing 
infrastructure and near-term production from the SABRE mining method. 
Uranium Energy Corporation (UEC-N) – UEC is an Americas-focused diversified 
uranium company, which recently transitioned from developer to producer status 
with the start-up of Christensen Ranch in Wyoming and Burke Hollow in Texas. The 
company hosts a range of assets across Texas, Wyoming, and the Athabasca Basin 
including a minority stake (5%) in the Wheeler River and a wholly owned interest in 
Roughrider. We see a path forward for UEC to produce 5Mlbs by the end of the 
decade. In addition to its pipeline of uranium assets, UEC is advancing its wholly 
owned subsidiary Uranium Refining and Conversion Corp, as the company seeks to 
become vertically integrated and play an important part in the U.S. nuclear supply 
chain. The conversion services plant will have a design capacity to produce 
~10,000tU per year as UF₆, representing a substantial share of U.S. demand. Over 60 
professionals are dedicated to the project, and site selection remains a key focus, 
while engineering work progresses. A Class IV cost update is expected to be released 
to the market in mid-2027. 
NexGen Energy (NXE-T, N) - NexGen’s advanced-stage Arrow development project in 
the Athabasca Basin of Saskatchewan has the potential to be among the largest and 
lowest-cost mines in the world. Construction is now underway, with completion 
targeted in H2/2030 (we assume first production in 2031). At full production, NexGen 
expects to produce 30Mlbs U3O8 (11.5ktU). We expect the development of Arrow will 
have a meaningful impact on the supply/demand forecast towards the end of this 
decade and into the early next decade. Given its Tier 1 status and importance to the 
overall market, we believe NexGen could attract M&A interest from larger industry 
players. 
IsoEnergy (ISO-T) - IsoEnergy provides exposure to a large, diversified portfolio of 
exploration and development assets focused primarily on Canada's Athabasca 
region and Australia. We expect the shares to re-rate higher as the company 
executes its exploration plans across its large portfolio, including along the trend at 
its flagship Hurricane deposit in Saskatchewan, which is among the highest-grade 
projects in the world. ISO also owns a 33% interest in DISA Uranium Corporation, a 
U.S.-based uranium technology and development company that aims to combine its 
uranium assets in Utah with its proprietary HPSA processing technology to create a 
U.S. conventional uranium platform.

Uranium State of the Union: Demand Visibility Improves While Supply 
Execution Remains the Constraint 
The uranium story has strengthened over the past year since the publication of our 
AOTC in 2025, prompting us to recently raise our long-term uranium price to 
$100/lb. Nuclear growth has moved beyond policy ambition toward construction, life 
extensions, restarts, and government-backed procurement, while the supply 
response remains concentrated, operationally fragile, and slow to permit. Near-term 
utility coverage has delayed a full contracting cycle, but it has not solved the 
widening requirement for replacement volumes later this decade. We now see a 
higher clearing price as necessary to mobilize greenfield supply and raised our long-
term uranium price forecast to $100/lb from $95/lb on October 22, 2026. 
The market has evolved materially since the post-Fukushima surplus. Production 
discipline by Kazatomprom and Cameco (e.g., the 2018-2022 suspension of McArthur 
River), the drawdown of mobile inventories, and financial demand from physical 
uranium vehicles helped rebalance the market. Russia's invasion of Ukraine then 
exposed the strategic importance of conversion and enrichment, not just mined 
supply. The result is a more segmented fuel cycle where uranium is available in the 
ground, but security of origin, conversion capacity, enrichment capacity, and 
delivery timing increasingly determine what utilities can actually procure. 
Spot pricing has moved higher in two stages since our last Ahead of the Curve 
report in October 2025. The initial rally to roughly $82/lb reflected renewed investor 
buying, a thin pool of readily available material, and fresh supply concerns after 
Cameco reduced McArthur River guidance and Kazatomprom moderated its 2026 
production outlook. Prices then consolidated as utility participation remained 
selective and improved producer output reduced near-term urgency before 
strengthening again into the high-$80s by mid-2026. The second leg has been 
supported by physical-fund purchases and market speculation leading to a price 
spike to $100/lb in early 2026, increased on- and off-market contracting, continued 
producer discipline, and rising long-term prices as we inch closer to eventual 
replacement-rate contracting. In our view, the move has been driven less by an 
immediate shortage of reactor inventory than by a repricing of replacement supply in 
a market where mobile inventories are scarce and major producers remain unwilling 
to add uncontracted volumes aggressively.

Reactor growth is now visible in committed projects. WNA reports that the global 
fleet totals approximately 441 operable reactors and 404 GW, with roughly 80 
reactors (representing 87 GW) under construction and another 123 reactors 
(representing 112 GW) planned. China remains the anchor, with 38 units under 
construction and 43 planned, but growth is broadening through India, Turkey, Egypt, 
Eastern Europe, and selected Western projects. Importantly, the demand outlook also 
benefits from life extensions and restarts, which generally carry less execution risk 
than greenfield construction. 
A useful rule of thumb is that each operating GW of conventional nuclear capacity 
requires approximately 0.4-0.5Mlbs of U3O8 annually for reloads. Fuel intensity 
varies by reactor type, capacity factor, burn-up and enrichment assumptions, but the 
current fleet provides a reasonable anchor: the 2026 Red Book estimates 398 GW of 
operating capacity required 64,500tU, equivalent to approximately 168Mlbs U3O8, or 
0.42Mlbs/GW annually. On the broader WNA fleet definition, current 2026 
requirements are approximately 68,920tU, equivalent to roughly 179Mlbs U3O8. New 
reactors also require roughly 1.5Mlbs/GW for initial cores, implying approximately 
130Mlbs for the 87 GW under construction and 168Mlbs for the 112 GW planned, 
before delays and project attrition. 
The demand impact from new reactor construction is meaningful before 
accounting for ongoing reload requirements (50GW AP1000 example). By 2040, the 
IAEA estimates roughly 50GW at the midpoint of new deployments net of retirements 
in North America and Europe. As illustrated below, achieving 50 GW of installed 
nuclear capacity using specific AP1000 load inputs would require ~70Mlbs of U3O8 for 
initial core loads, based on roughly 1.4Mlbs/GW, followed by an additional 20+Mlbs 
annually once the fleet is operating. New builds create a sizeable upfront call on 
uranium inventories, while each incremental gigawatt adds recurring demand that 
must be supplied over the reactor’s operating life. We replicate this same scenario for 
conversion and enrichment requirements in their respective sections later in this 
report. While this deployment scenario only captures AP1000s, SMR deployments 
may offer additional requirements. See page 43 for fuel cycle assumptions on 
AP1000, X-energy's Xe-100, and Oklo's Aurora.

The 2025 World Nuclear Fuel Report already marked a major upward reset in 
expected nuclear capacity and uranium requirements. Subsequent developments 
reinforce that direction with the 2026 Red Book, a joint report by the Nuclear Energy 
Agency and the International Atomic Energy Agency, estimating 441 commercial 
reactors were operational at the start of 2025 and required approximately 64,500tU 
annually. Furthermore, the report projects 2050 requirements of roughly 84,800-
143,900tU. The Red Book also highlights the central tension in our outlook where 
identified resources recoverable below costs of $260/kgU ($100/lb) U3O8 are 
approximately 8.1MtU (~21Blbs), representing just over 100 years of supply at current 
levels of demand. At face value, the metric suggests an abundant amount of 
uranium, but we highlight two caveats: 1) mine development may typically require 
10-20 years, meaning geological abundance does not equate to timely supply; and 2) 
the cost of extraction does not fully capture the true cost of production, investment 
hurdle rates, project risk, etc. For this reason, we form higher conviction for uranium 
prices to continue trending higher as the supply-demand framework reveals an 
increasing need for greenfield projects, which can only be unlocked through higher 
incentive pricing.

2025 WNA report: 
• 
Reference demand scenario: reactor capacity reaches 449 GW in 2030E 
and 746 GW in 2040E, including 49 GW of SMRs. Uranium requirements 
increase from 67,300tU (175Mlbs U3O8) in 2024 to 150,525tU (391Mlbs) in 
2040E. 
• 
Upper demand scenario: reactor capacity reaches 492 GW in 2030E and 
966 GW in 2040E, including 110 GW of SMRs. Uranium requirements rise to

•

552 GW in 2040E. Even this case implies uranium requirements of 107,300tU 
(279Mlbs) by 2040E, materially above current mine supply.

Supply update: higher output does not eliminate the structural gap 
Kazakhstan and Canada remain the key swing suppliers. Kazatomprom produced 
13,291tU (34.6Mlbs) on a 100% basis in H1/26, up 9% y/y, and maintained 2026 
guidance of 27,500-29,000tU (71.5-75.4Mlbs U3O8). However, this remains below the 
previously contemplated return to full subsoil-use agreement capacity, and 
management continues to prioritize value over volume. Separately, Cameco 
maintained attributable 2026 guidance of 19.5-21.5Mlbs despite temporary logistics 
disruptions in Saskatchewan; H1/26 Canadian output declined 5% y/y to 10.1Mlbs. 
Incumbents can grow, but output remains exposed to operating constraints, and 
neither producer is front-running uncovered utility demand. 
The broader project pipeline is improving, but timing and scale remain uncertain. 
Brownfield restarts and expansions should provide the bulk of incremental supply 
through the late 2020s, while many greenfield projects still require permits, financing, 
offtake support, and lengthy construction. Secondary supplies are also less 
dependable than in the prior cycle as commercial inventories have been drawn down 
and geopolitical fragmentation has reduced the fungibility of material. We therefore 
view headline production growth as necessary but insufficient to close the modeled 
balance. 
Fuel-cycle security is pulling demand forward 
The U.S. ban on Russian low-enriched uranium is reaching its deadline with waivers 
expiring at the end of 2027, creating a hard cutoff from January 1st, 2028. This 
primarily tightens conversion and enrichment, but it also supports uranium demand 
through enrichment overfeeding (while SWU supply is tight), inventory rebuilding, and 
the need to secure non-Russian origin material. Western enrichment additions are 
advancing, yet deployment is measured in years, and utilities must contract well 
before new capacity is available. We see this as a durable source of contracting 
urgency rather than a one-time trade disruption. 
Government support is also becoming tangible. In June 2026, the U.S. Department 
of Energy issued a conditional commitment for US$17.5bln of loans to finance long-
lead equipment for up to ten AP1000 reactors across five sites, representing roughly 
11 GW of potential capacity. When viewed from a lens of grid expansion and the 
bolstering of a long-term nuclear supply chain, the initiative materially improves the 
probability, timing, and supply-chain readiness of a U.S. large-reactor buildout, 
incrementally pulling demand forward. 
Our Supply/Demand Model 
We continue to forecast a tight uranium market through the balance of the decade. 
Our upward revision to our price forecast is not predicated on an immediate squeeze 
in spot uranium; utility inventories and near-term coverage can continue to moderate 
contracting urgency. Rather, a real price of $100/lb starting in 2030 better reflects 
the price required to advance a sufficient portfolio of new mines, compensate for 
permitting and execution risk, and secure diversified supply before requirements step 
up in the 2030s. The principal downside risks are reactor delays and a faster-than-
expected supply ramp; upside risks include stronger utility contracting, additional 
restarts and life extensions, underperformance by major producers, and accelerated 
AI-driven baseload investment. The forward curve for uranium, as reported by UxC, 
also supports our long-term price forecasts, with UxC reporting uranium prices of 
$104/lb in three years and $111/lb in five years forward, after accounting for 
inflation.

The Cost of Producing Uranium 
Our cost analysis for uranium covers 97% of global supply, which we believe is unique 
and differentiated versus many of our peers, and we see higher prices needed to 
justify new mine development. 
We estimate the 2025 weighted average cash cost at $21.65/lb based on our 
analysis (Figure 4). We compiled our 2025 global uranium cost curve based on 
available data for 29 major global uranium mining operations. The dataset covers 
almost 162Mlbs of U3O8 production, representing ~97% of total global mine supply. 
In-Situ Recovery (ISR) operations account for 59.2% (~95.8Mlbs) of supply, while 
conventional mining methods represent 40.8% (~65.9Mlbs). 
We estimate ISR mines have a weighted average cash cost of ~$19/lb (Figure 5) and 
conventional operations have a weighted average cash cost of ~$25/lb (Figure 6). 
C1 cash costs tell only one part of the picture. We estimate capex per pound of 
production in the range of $9-13/lb, resulting in AISC of $31-35/lb before factoring in 
royalty costs and other taxes. We expect costs to increase in 2026 across the industry 
due to higher input costs including diesel and sulphuric acid, but these costs should 
be manageable given the margin at today's spot prices.

Term uranium prices remained firm throughout the period, increasing modestly to

discussions with companies under coverage, utilities are feeling a sticker shock on 
pricing and seem reluctant to contract meaningfully. Meanwhile, uranium producers

activity will recover but when the recovery will take place. Utilities continue to 
contract below replacement levels and recent historical purchasing trends, 
suggesting current procurement levels are unlikely to be sustainable over the longer 
term. As uncovered requirements continue to build, we believe utilities will ultimately 
need to increase their purchase volumes in a more meaningful way, particularly for

Part 2 – Conversion (Craig Hutchison) 
Conversion is becoming an increasingly strategic and constrained segment of the 
fuel cycle as global nuclear demand grows. Conversion markets have tightened 
significantly since 2022, driven by renewed utility contracting, geopolitical concerns 
around Russian supply, and limited spare capacity, resulting in a multi-year bull 
market for conversion services. Global conversion demand is projected to rise from 
approximately 66,000 tU in 2026 to 82,500 tU by 2030 and potentially 145,000-
200,000 tU by 2040. Existing primary capacity remains concentrated among a 
handful of suppliers, and secondary inventories continue to decline. While we believe 
markets are balanced for the next few years, we expect conversion deficits to emerge 
later this decade unless meaningful new capacity is added, with potential 
contributions from projects such as Springfields and UEC’s proposed UR&C facility. 
Conversion is the process by which uranium is converted into a usable form that can 
be used for heavy water reactors or as an interim step ahead of enrichment for light 
water and advanced reactors. In the nuclear fuel cycle, conversion represents ~5% of 
total fuel cost. 
Figure 14 - Conversion in Nuclear Fuel Cycle 
 
 
 
Source: TD Cowen, Solstice Advanced Materials 
 
Conversion for CANDU heavy water reactors: Cameco's Port Hope facility in Ontario 
is the only conversion facility in the western world that converts UO3 to UO2 (natural 
uranium) to be used in CANDU reactors. The process involves adding nitric acid and 
ammonia to UO3, which is fed into a special kiln operating with a reduction 
atmosphere. The resulting ceramic-grade UO2 powder, which contains about 0.7% of 
the U-235 fissile isotope, can be formed into pellets for insertion in fuel bundles. 
Conversion for light water reactors: Light water reactors require enriched UO2 with 
U-235 concentrations between 3-5%. Therefore, uranium must first be converted to a 
gas, UF6, before it can be enriched. There are two main types of conversion 
processes: wet and dry. The wet conversion process is employed by Cameco in 
Canada, Orano in France, Lanzhou in China, and Seversk in Russia. During the wet 
conversion process, UO3 is reduced to uranium dioxide (UO2) then fluorinated to form 
uranium tetrafluoride (UF4). UF4 is exposed to gaseous fluorine (F2) at elevated 
temperatures to produce uranium hexafluoride (UF6). UF6 is the only uranium 
compound that can exist as a solid, liquid, or gas, depending on temperature and 
pressure. Liquid UF6 is transferred into steel cylinders where it cools and solidifies and 
can then be transported to enrichment plants. 
Under the dry conversion process, which is employed at Solstice's Metropolis 
conversion plant in the United States, U3O8 is first converted into an impure UF6 and 
then refined using a two-stage distillation process.

How much converted uranium does each reactor need: Theoretically, a modern 
third-generation 1,000-MWe light-water reactor, such as the AP1000 or EPR, requires 
about 170 tU of converted uranium annually, which is equivalent to roughly 250 
tonnes of UF₆ feed per year. Actual uranium requirements can vary significantly by 
reactor design and operating characteristics, with annual consumption ranging from 
approximately 170 tU to 250 tU per year. Key factors that affect converted uranium 
consumption include fuel burnup, thermal efficiency, tails assay, and operating 
strategy. 
Example buildout of conversion demand for 50GW of AP1000s - By 2040, the IAEA 
estimates roughly 50GW at the midpoint of new deployments net of retirements in 
North America and Europe. Using the conversion requirements for initial core loads 
and reloads for a typical AP1000, we modeled a 10-year deployment scenario with 5 
GW of annual additions, consistent with the IAEA forecast. We assume conversion for 
each initial core begins three years before reactor startup, with total conversion 
demand spread evenly across that period, while reload demand begins one year 
after deployment. No additional deployments are assumed past year 10. Over the 
course of the 50GW initial buildout, ~26,000tU of conversion capacity would be 
required just for initial-core load outs. Conversion requirements would total ~8,600tU 
by year 11, representing a 13% increase over current demand levels alone. We 
replicate this same scenario for uranium and enrichment requirements in their 
respective sections later in this report. While this deployment scenario only captures 
AP1000s, SMR deployments may offer additional requirements. See page 43 for fuel 
cycle assumptions on AP1000, X-energy's Xe-100, and Oklo's Aurora.

Conversion prices enter a multi-year bull market: Between 2017 and early 2022, 
both spot and term conversion prices generally remained below $25/kgU, reflecting 
sufficient inventories, subdued utility contracting activity, and excess conversion 
capacity. 
Starting in March 2022, conversion prices rose rapidly as utilities accelerated fuel 
procurement, geopolitical tensions reshaped fuel sourcing strategies (Russia's 
invasion of Ukraine), and conversion facilities approached full utilization. Spot prices 
increased more sharply than long-term prices, trending towards $100/kgU in January 
2025 as buyers competed for a limited uncommitted capacity and near-term supply. 
Term prices also moved higher, which reflected a structurally tighter conversion 
supply.  
While spot prices subsequently retraced from peak levels, both spot and term prices 
remained well above historical levels in 2026, with spot prices continuing to 
command a moderate premium over long-term prices.

are projected to total ~66,000tU, which can be covered by global licensed primary

principal source of converted uranium, is produced by a small number of dedicated 
conversion facilities. The industry is highly concentrated, with five key players: 
Cameco and Solstice in North America, Orano in West and Central Europe, Rosatom 
in Russia, and CNNC in China.

Secondary conversion supply: Secondary supplies of converted uranium are derived 
predominantly from commercial and government inventories, with additional 
contributions from enrichment-related sources such as underfeeding and tails re-
enrichment, as well as reprocessed nuclear fuel materials. 
Historically, secondary supply played a critical role in balancing the conversion 
market, contributing as much as half of global UF₆ supply from 2011 to 2017. As 
demand for nuclear fuel strengthened, secondary inventories were increasingly 
depleted while conversion facilities ramped up production, resulting in a growing 
dependence on primary supply. According to World Nuclear Association, secondary 
UF₆ supply is expected to decline from roughly 7,500 tU in 2026 to 1,500-2,500 tU by 
2035, suggesting a progressively tighter conversion market and diminished inventory 
support over the long term.

Conversion markets – New Primary Capacity Addition 
If new conversion supply is not added later this decade, inventories will continue to 
fall and market deficits will grow, creating a bottleneck in the nuclear supply chain. 
Springfields: One opportunity to help address the growing supply shortfall is the 
potential to restart Springfields operation (49% Cameco/51% Brookfield) in the U.K. 
The operation historically produced 5,000tU of UF6 until 2014 when it was placed on 
care and maintenance. The owner received grant money from the U.K. government in 
2023, when the company explored the viability of adding a new conversion line 
capable of converting reprocessed uranium and freshly mined natural uranium.  
UEC U.S. Uranium Refining & Conversion Corp: Another potential source of primary 
conversion capacity growth is UR&C, Uranium Energy Corp.'s proposed conversion 
platform. As part of the company's strategy to build a vertically-integrated nuclear 
fuel cycle business, UEC plans to develop UR&C into one of the largest conversion 
facilities in North America, with a production capacity of 10,000 tU in UF6 per year.  
While the project is at an early stage, it has achieved an important preliminary 
licensing milestone after receiving docketing by the U.S. NRC and is currently working 
on a potential site selection process. A dedicated team of 63 people from Fluor are 
currently working on the process flow sheet and development considerations. UEC 
plans to update the market with a Class IV estimate in mid-2027. Given the early-
stage nature of the design and lack of site selection/permitting, we do not expect the 
UR&C conversion facility to enter production this decade. 
Raven-Flint Nuclear: Raven-Flint is another early-stage U.S. conversion project that 
aims to build the United States' second uranium conversion facility. The company 
recently achieved several key milestones, including NRC docketing of its license 
application and selection into the U.S. Department of Energy's Nuclear Energy 
Launch Pad program at Idaho National Laboratory, which provides access to 
technical expertise, facilities, and regulatory support to help advance the technology 
toward commercial deployment.  
A key technological differentiator of Raven-Flint, supported by successful laboratory-
scale production, is its ability to produce UF₆ without the use of elemental fluorine 
gas, a highly corrosive and hazardous reagent that materially increases capital 
investment, process complexity, and operational risks. Management noted that this 
proprietary production method, known as the Corvus Route, could reduce 
construction capital costs by more than 30% while eliminating reliance on HF 
electrolyzers sourced from outside the United States.  
Raven-Flint plans to construct Torch, a first-of-a-kind pilot conversion facility at Idaho 
National Laboratory capable of producing 500 tU of UF₆ annually. Torch is intended 
to demonstrate the Corvus Route at pilot scale and support broader efforts to expand 
domestic U.S. fuel-cycle infrastructure and reduce reliance on foreign conversion 
capacity.

Part 3 – Enrichment (Marc Bianchi) 
Uranium enrichment is a critical step in nuclear fuel manufacturing, and while global 
supply is greater than demand, Russia accounts for 43% of this supply and is being 
prohibited in several markets. Furthermore, national and energy security aspirations, 
particularly in the US, create an opportunity for new investment. While growing 
demand from nuclear capacity expansions could further tighten the market, a 
significant amount of new supply is in planning phases or under construction, which, 
along with high capital cost and a competitive landscape dominated by state-owned 
enterprises, could eventually challenge returns.  
Our analysis aggregates a total of 14.2MM separative work units (SWU) of gross 
enrichment capacity plans in the US and Europe, equivalent to ~60% of current 
capacity with startup spread from mid-late 2020s into the 2030s. While a material 
figure, new volumes will be needed to displace Russian imports, offset attrition, and 
meet growing nuclear generation capacity, some of which uses higher enriched fuel 
like HALEU. Still, with so many variables, it is difficult to say whether the market will 
face an over- or undersupply. 
Limited enrichment capacity for high assay low enriched uranium (HALEU) used by 
advanced reactors appears to be driving a significant premium to low enriched 
uranium (LEU) currently. We expect a premium to continue over time due to higher 
handling costs; however, how much of a premium remains unclear.  
While the fate of enrichment prices appears uncertain, the input is only a small 
portion in the overall cost of manufacturing nuclear fuel and thus unlikely to 
materially change operating costs for nuclear power plants.

Overview – What Is Enrichment? 
Nuclear power requires a sustained chain reaction of fission accomplished with fuel 
made from heavy metal actinides on the periodic table. Uranium is the preferred 
heavy metal given its naturally-occurring abundance and presence of the U-235 
isotope, which is fissile, meaning it can undergo fission. However, naturally-occurring 
uranium has just 0.7% U-235, and most reactors require a higher concentration to 
sustain fission. The process of increasing the concentration of U-235 is known as 
enrichment.   
The most common nuclear reactors are light water reactors (LWR) and use uranium 
fuels enriched to 3-5% U-235, known as Low Enriched Uranium (LEU), with some 
efforts to enrich up to 10%, known as LEU+. Higher enrichment rates can offer longer 
time between refueling, more efficient plants, and higher burnup rates (less waste).  
Many advanced reactor designs utilize higher enrichment levels up to 20% U-235, 
known as High Assay Low Enriched Uranium (HALEU). Enrichment above 20% is 
Highly Enriched Uranium (HEU) and used in defense applications.  
The process of creating enriched uranium fuels starts with natural uranium (U3O8), 
which is mined and milled then converted to uranium hexafluoride gas (UF6), which is 
then enriched and then converted to UO2 powder for fuel fabrication.  
Currently, uranium is enriched with gaseous centrifuge technology; however, an 
alternative laser enrichment is under development but not yet commercial. Since the 
same enrichment technology can be used to create weapons-grade enriched 
uranium (HEU), the industry is highly regulated and largely government controlled. 
Centrus Energy is currently the only privately owned (publicly traded) company in the 
commercial enrichment market, while BWX Technologies is developing enrichment 
for the National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA). General Matter is a private 
company based in the U.S. that recently filed a license application with the Nuclear 
Regulatory Commission (NRC) with plans to produce LEU, LEU+, and HALEU by 2030. 
Utilities generally procure uranium from miners and contract for conversion, 
enrichment, and eventually fuel fabrication services.  
Enrichment capacity is described in Separative Work Units (SWU), which is a 
standard unit of measurement representing the effort required to separate U-235 and 
U-238. The amount of SWU required to produce 1kg of Enriched Uranium Product 
(EUP) depends on the desired U-235 assay (concentration), assay of the UF6 feed, 
and contractually agreed tails assay (remaining uranium that is not enriched). For 
example, 1kg of LEU with a product assay of 4.4%, feed assay of 0.71%, and tails 
assay of 0.23% would require ~9kg of UF6 and ~7 SWU. By comparison, 1kg of HALEU 
with a product assay of 17.5% and with the same feed/tails assays would require 
~36kg of UF6 and ~37 SWU. Urenco has a useful SWU calculator on their website.  
In practice, however, feed for higher enrichment levels may already have some 
enrichment done. For example, the feed to make HALEU will likely be LEU, which is 
already enriched up to 5%, so the corresponding SWU required to convert LEU to 
HALEU will be lower than if the feed were natural uranium. Still, the total SWU needed 
to enrich from natural uranium to HALEU should be the same.

Demand Outlook and Sensitivity 
The absence of Russia from some markets should tighten the supply / demand 
balance, but demand is set to grow as well. This growth is first driven by nuclear 
restarts and brownfield expansions (uprates) in the U.S. and eventually new 
construction in Europe, the U.S., and elsewhere. A trend toward higher enrichment 
levels for conventional reactors (LEU+) could also add to SWU demand.  
While material demand increases in new nuclear within the U.S. are still many years 
away and timing/magnitude is still uncertain, restarts and uprates can contribute 
sooner and are more tangible. Currently, we count ~2.25GW of planned restarts 
between Palisades, Crane (TMI-1), and Duane Arnold and a further ~1.7GW of uprate 
announcements. The NRC also tracks a list of expected applications for uprate 
totaling 2.4GW between 4Q26 and 2032. We also note the DOE's UPRISE (Utility 
Power Reactor Incremental Scaling Effort) initiative targets 5 GW of nuclear capacity 
in the U.S. by 2029 through uprates and restarts. 
Over the past 20 years, the average SWU per GWh of nuclear generation in the U.S. 
has been ~17.8 with the nuclear fleet running at an average capacity factor of 91%. 
More recent capacity factors have averaged 92-93%. Assuming a 92% capacity 
factor with 17.8 SWU / GWh, each 1GW of nuclear capacity in the U.S. would add 
143K SWU to demand, and 5GW would add ~700K SWU. Based on our estimates, new 
AP1000s may be around a similar ~140K SWU/GW range. For restarts and newbuilds, 
the SWU requirements could be temporarily higher for initial fuel loadings.

business primarily involves brokerage of enrichment services, with the buildout of

do not yet reveal much about the business of enrichment. However, Urenco and 
Orano, while state controlled, do disclose financial results.

from enrichment services. Orano's business involves the entire fuel cycle and 
recycling, and while the company does report a "front end" segment, this also 
includes uranium mining and conversion. 
Urenco's results show a few interesting things. First, the revenue per SWU (unit price)

Enrichment for National Security 
Besides commercial power reactors, enriched uranium is also critical for national 
security missions with Highly Enriched Uranium (HEU) needed for submarine and 
aircraft carrier propulsion and LEU for tritium production used in nuclear weapons 
(aka nuclear deterrent). Enriched uranium for these purposes must be unobligated, 
meaning it must be produced entirely from U.S. sources by U.S. companies. This 
requires the entire supply chain of mining, conversion, enrichment, and associated 
equipment be U.S. sourced. Currently, Urenco and Centrus' DOE HALEU 
demonstration are the only enrichment capacities in the U.S., and neither qualifies as 
unobligated: Urenco, obviously as a non-U.S. company, and Centrus as their 
centrifuge uses foreign-sourced carbon fiber. Centrus has identified a U.S.-sourced 
supplier for their planned development at Piketon, which is expected to qualify the 
capacity as unobligated.   
In August 2026, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) published a report 
outlining enriched uranium supplies and demands and potential challenges for the 
National Nuclear Security Administration's (NNSA) enriched uranium supply goals. 
Current national security missions are supported by domestic stockpiles that last 
through the 2040s. In response to this dwindling inventory, NNSA is supporting two 
gaseous centrifuge enrichment technologies: large centrifuge, which is the 
technology used by Centrus, and small centrifuge or Domestic Uranium Enrichment 
Centrifuge Experiment (DEUCE), which the NNSA has awarded a sole source contract 
to BWXT in 2025 to construct a pilot. In total, NNSA expects to require 1.5MM SWU 
per year by the 2050s to support LEU for tritium and HEU for naval propulsion.

FAQ 
Why do most conventional reactors run on 5% LEU and only recently are starting to 
run on higher enrichment levels (LEU+) or even exploring advanced reactors with 
HALEU? 
Most conventional reactors use 3–5% LEU because light-water moderation slows 
neutrons into a thermal spectrum where U-235 fissions efficiently, allowing large LWR 
cores to sustain a chain reaction without higher enrichment. Early gaseous-diffusion 
enrichment was also expensive, reinforcing an industry framework built around low-
assay fuel.  
LEU+ and HALEU are gaining relevance as newer fuel and reactor designs target 
more efficient and higher burnup rates, longer cycles, smaller cores, or neutron 
spectra with less-effective moderation, all of which place greater needs on fuel that 
has a higher concentration of fissile material.

What is SWU underfeeding and overfeeding, and how does that change 
supply/demand dynamics for uranium? 
One thing to consider on SWU calculations is that there are SWU uranium 
underfeeding and overfeeding dynamics that will come into play and may affect 
calculations. In our scenarios, we assume a simple 0.22–0.23% tails assay. The tails 
assay measures the concentration of U-235 remaining in the depleted uranium 
stream after enrichment. However, strained SWU supply may lead to uranium 
overfeeding, increasing uranium requirements and tails assay while reducing SWU 
intensity. Conversely, additional SWU supply may encourage underfeeding, reducing 
uranium requirements and tails assay while increasing SWU intensity. 
What are the key differences between laser enrichment and gaseous centrifuge 
enrichment? 
Both laser enrichment and gaseous centrifuge enrichment raise U-235 from natural 
uranium's ~0.7% to reactor grade levels, but the physics differs. Gaseous centrifuges 
spin UF6 gas at very high speeds so that the slightly heavier U-238 drifts outward. The 
mass difference is small, and each machine achieves a small enrichment gain, so 
thousands of machines are linked into cascades. 
Laser enrichment instead exploits the slightly different light absorption of the two 
isotopes. Tuned lasers selectively excite U-235, which are then separated from the 
unexcited U-238. Centrifuge enrichment is commercially proven and modular, with 
costs driven by capital and electricity. Laser enrichment has not operated on a 
commercial scale, so its potential cost advantages remain unknown. Laser facilities 
also raise proliferation concerns and draw extra regulatory scrutiny.  
Can existing LEU enrichment capacity be converted to produce LEU+/HALEU? What 
additional equipment, licensing, and investment would be required? 
LEU and LEU+/HALEU production are technically quite similar because they use the 
same centrifuges, which represent most of the equipment cost, so the CapEx 
difference is marginal. The primary physical difference is how the centrifuges are 
connected and arranged within the cascade, so transitioning capacity mainly 
requires changes to piping and cascade configuration rather than entirely new 
enrichment technology. 
The more significant differences are regulatory. HALEU production requires 
additional NRC authorization, including a license amendment, along with enhanced 
Category II safeguards and security due to the fuel's higher U-235 concentration. 
Operators must also demonstrate that the facility can safely control criticality risks 
and properly account for, protect, handle, and store the material.

Part 4 – Recycling (Marc Bianchi) 
Recycling nuclear fuel could dramatically expand utilization of the world's uranium 
resource without the need for conversion and enrichment, though our analysis shows 
costs are unproven and may be uneconomic at current nuclear fuel prices. 
Furthermore, concerns around weapons proliferation present a challenge. However, 
if the world is going to significantly increase nuclear generation capacity, strains on 
resource availability may build support for recycling.  
Our recycling analysis draws two primary conclusions. First, recycling conventional 
reactor fuel (light water) is inefficient and expensive, only slightly increasing 
utilization of the uranium resource and only economically viable if uranium prices 
increase 4-6x. Second, recycling with a fleet of fast neutron reactors running in a 
closed-loop system could enable fuel costs below current fresh uranium while 
significantly reducing long-lived radioactive waste. However, processing costs are 
unproven, and creating a fleet of fast reactors would require a massive investment 
and shift in technology preference.  
Along with this report, we have initiated coverage of Oklo Inc. (OKLO) with a Hold 
rating and $26 price target. Oklo is the only publicly traded company actively 
developing a sodium fast reactor with plans to use pyroprocessing for nuclear fuel 
recycling, which, if successful, could be highly disruptive. 
Overview 
Besides the well-known attributes of nuclear fission, like clean firm power at scale 
and an attractive safety record, there are two aspects that we believe are less well 
understood: 1) the process uses just a small portion of the initial fuel resource (5-
20%); 2) fission not only generates energy but breeds more usable fuel, some of 
which is consumed inside the reactor and some of which is left over when fuel is 
removed. Both attributes mean fuel can be recycled to extract more energy from the 
world's uranium resource.  
Currently, recycling is limited and almost all activity involves spent fuel from 
conventional light water reactors, which is not very efficient or cost effective. Fast 
neutron reactors, however, are much better for recycling, particularly when they are 
fed with recycled fuel from fast reactors, creating a "closed-loop" system. This is 
because fast reactors breed more usable fuel and can more effectively cause fission 
in a wider spectrum of elements and isotopes. 
However, this closed loop requires a fleet of fast reactors producing spent fuel, which 
does not exist today. Thus, the first fuel loadings for a closed-loop system would need 
to be with fresh enriched uranium or recycled light water fuel before building up a 
sufficient inventory of used fast reactor fuel to then recycle.  
With plenty of low-cost natural uranium and enrichment capacity, there has not been 
enough economic incentive to pursue recycling, particularly with fast reactors where 
the closed-loop solution requires a technology shift and massive investment away 
from conventional light water reactors. Back-end waste management costs also 
remain relatively low, further limiting the economic case for recycling solely as a 
waste solution.  
Our analysis considers three recycling pathways: 1) recycling spent light water fuel to 
be used in light water reactors, 2) recycling spent light water fuel to be used in fast 
neutron reactors, and 3) recycling spent fast reactor fuel to be used in fast reactors.

Oklo (OKLO) and recently public Newcleo (NWCL) are currently the only public 
companies pursuing light water to fast reactor recycling and eventually fast 
reactor to fast reactor recycling, while state-owned Orano is the only major 
recycler of light water fuel.  
Orano has recycled fuel at commercial scale using the more mature aqueous 
reprocessing and MOX pathway. Newcleo plans to leverage the same 
established pathway to manufacture fuel for fast reactors, while Oklo is pursuing 
fast reactor fuel recycling through pyroprocessing, which has been 
demonstrated but not deployed commercially. Newcleo’s approach offers 
potential applications in existing reactors while leveraging an established 
process, whereas Oklo argues that pyroprocessing is more proliferation resistant 
and potentially simpler for fast reactors because the recycled material does not 
need to be separated into a pure plutonium stream. We examine the nuances of 
both approaches throughout this report. 
 
Why Can Nuclear Fuel Be Recycled? 
Nuclear energy is produced through fission, a process in which a heavy atomic 
nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into smaller nuclei, releasing large amounts of 
energy and additional neutrons. When enough of those neutrons trigger subsequent 
fissions, a self-sustaining chain reaction is created and can be harnessed to generate 
electricity. 
The heaviest metals in the periodic table, particularly the actinides, contain large 
nuclei that can participate in fission. Some actinide isotopes are fissile, meaning they 
can readily sustain a chain reaction, while others are fertile, meaning they can 
absorb neutrons and be converted (bred) into fissile fuel. This distinction is important 
because fertile isotopes can significantly expand the amount of energy ultimately 
extracted from a given natural resource base. 
Self-sustaining reactors require a sufficient inventory of fissile material. Most 
conventional reactors rely on a relatively high concentration of fissile fuel, while some 
advanced reactors can be designed to make greater use of fertile material and 
recycled fuel. 
Three actinide elements are discussed as nuclear fuel resources: uranium, plutonium, 
and thorium. Uranium is the foundation of today's nuclear industry because it occurs 
naturally in recoverable concentrations and contains the fissile isotope uranium-235. 
Plutonium is an excellent reactor fuel but needs to be produced inside reactors 
through neutron capture in uranium-238. Furthermore, plutonium carries proliferation 
risk as it is directly weapons-usable. Thorium is more abundant than uranium but is 
fertile rather than fissile and must first be converted into uranium-233 before it can 
be used as fuel. 
Natural uranium consists primarily of fertile U-238 (99.3%) and fissile U-235 (0.7%). 
For most reactors, the proportion of fissile U-235 must be increased (enriched) to 
sustain the fission reaction. Most conventional reactors use uranium enriched to 3-5% 
U-235, known as Low Enriched Uranium (LEU), while many advanced reactors use 
enrichment up to 20% U-235, known as High-Assay Low Enriched Uranium (HALEU). 
Inside the reactor, some neutrons are absorbed by fissile U-235 causing fission, while 
other neutrons are absorbed by fertile U-238, which decays into fissile plutonium, 
known as breeding. This bred plutonium can then fission, releasing more neutrons 
and more energy. Fun fact: ~1/3 of the energy from conventional reactors comes 
from plutonium that was bred inside the reactor.

Nuclear fuel is encased in fuel assemblies, which contain a bundle of fuel rods, 
structural supports, and coolant passages. The actual fuel (uranium or recycled 
heavy metal) is formed into small ceramic cylinders that are stacked inside fuel rods. 
A conventional reactor will have hundreds of fuel assemblies inside the core. 
Over time, reactor fuel loses effectiveness because the atoms that readily undergo 
fission are gradually consumed and replaced with byproducts that absorb neutrons 
without producing useful energy. At the same time, years of exposure to radiation 
and high temperatures degrade the fuel assembly. Eventually, the fuel can no longer 
efficiently sustain the reactor's chain reaction and must be replaced. Most 
conventional reactors replace 1/3 of their fuel every 12-24 months with each fuel 
assembly staying inside the reactor for about 5 years. 
Despite being considered "spent", depending on reactor type and burnup, the fuel 
still contains roughly 80-95% of its original heavy metal inventory while the remaining 
5-20% is largely fission products and no longer valuable. Most of this remaining 
heavy metal material is fertile U-238, and a smaller portion has been transformed into 
fissile transuranic (TRU) elements, including plutonium and minor actinides, with 
some residual U-235, which can be extracted to make recycled fuel.

These fissile elements in spent fuel can play a similar role that U-235 plays in 
conventional uranium fuel: providing the fissile material needed to sustain the chain 
reaction. However, unlike natural uranium, which has a consistent concentration of 
~0.7% U-235 that must be enriched, depending on the reactor design, spent fuel will 
have varying concentrations of fissile material, which should have a significant 
impact on recycling economics.  
 
What Determines Spent Fuel Inventory 
The number of neutrons and how they are managed is a defining characteristic of 
different reactor designs and is a determinant of the composition of fresh and spent 
nuclear fuel. Spent light water reactor fuel has a low level of remaining fissile 
material, while fast neutron reactors have a high level. 
Conventional light water reactors (LWR) are thermal or slow neutron reactors and 
are the most common reactor design using ordinary water to slow neutrons to a 
thermal equilibrium, making them more likely to cause fission. While the water does a 
good job slowing neutrons, it also absorbs neutrons requiring a higher proportion of 
fissile U-235 to increase the chances of fission and sustain the reaction, i.e., enriched 
uranium. Furthermore, while LWRs do breed plutonium that is consumed inside the 
reactor, the water's neutron absorption significantly restricts plutonium breeding. 
Alternatively, a reactor can be designed to operate with a fast neutron spectrum. In 
these fast reactors, neutrons are not intentionally moderated or absorbed. Fast 
neutrons have high kinetic energy that can induce fission in a broader range of heavy 
elements and isotopes; however, fast neutrons have a lower probability of fission 
requiring a fuel with higher fissile content. The higher neutron count also means fast 
reactors are efficient at breeding plutonium from fertile U-238. As a result, spent fast 
reactor fuel can have a significantly larger inventory of fissile plutonium and TRU 
than a thermal reactor. This feature of fast reactors is the key to extracting more 
energy from uranium resources and the closed-loop fuel cycle.  
Fast reactors can be configured in three ways: burner, break-even, and breeder. 
Break-even and breeders maintain or increase their inventory of useful heavy metal in 
spent fuel, which can be part of a closed-loop fuel cycle. 
Burner fast reactors are designed to consume more transuranic (TRU) material than 
they create. The fresh fuel for a burner is typically rich in plutonium and other 
recycled actinides, with relatively little fertile uranium compared to a breeder design. 
The core is configured to maximize fission of plutonium and minor actinides while 
minimizing breeding, often by reducing the amount of fertile U-238 available to 
absorb excess neutrons. As a result, the spent fuel contains less plutonium and TRU 
than the fresh fuel, while the proportion of fission products increases. The reactor 
effectively converts long-lived actinides into shorter-lived fission products, reducing 
the overall waste burden. 
Break-even fast reactors are designed to produce approximately as much fissile 
material as they consume, operating with a conversion ratio near 1.0. Fresh fuel may 
contain HALEU, fissile plutonium, and TRU with fertile material or a combination of 
both. The core geometry and fuel loading are configured so that neutron capture in 
fertile U-238 creates roughly enough new fissile plutonium and TRU to replace the 
fissile material destroyed by fission, although the isotopic composition changes and 
fission products accumulate. In effect, the reactor maintains its fissile actinide 
inventory while producing energy.

Breeder fast reactors are designed to create more fissile material than they consume. 
Fresh fuel typically consists of a fissile driver fuel surrounded by substantial fertile 
material, such as U-238 blankets. The design maximizes utilization of surplus 
neutrons by capturing them in fertile material and converting U-238 into plutonium. 
As a result, the spent fuel contains more total plutonium and TRU than the fresh fuel, 
despite having generated electricity. After reprocessing, the excess fissile material 
can be recovered and used to fuel additional reactors, allowing the system to expand 
its fuel inventory over time. 
For both break-even and breeder reactors, if the initial fuel loading is HALEU, the 
spent fuel will still have a meaningful amount of residual U-235 that can be useful 
fissile material for recycled fuel. Over time through recycling trips, the residual U-235 
is consumed and the dominant fissile material in spent fuel becomes plutonium and 
TRU.

Considering the limited uplift in uranium utilization, relatively low cost of fresh 
uranium fuel, and that conventional MOX recycles plutonium but not most minor 
actinides, LWR-to-MOX recycling can be viewed as only a partial fuel-cycle solution. It 
effectively allows plutonium to make one additional pass through a reactor but falls 
short of the repeated actinide recycling envisioned in closed fast-reactor fuel cycles. 
Recycling of spent fuel for use in a fast reactor, particularly utilizing pyroprocessing 
to extract the actinides, is much more appealing as it could lead to a closed fuel 
cycle but is still in pre-commercial stage. Russia is currently the only country 
operating anything close to an integrated commercial fast reactor recycle program. 
The Russian program uses PUREX to extract plutonium from conventional reactor fuel 
and create full MOX core for their BN-800 fast reactor, which is viewed as a bridge to 
a closed fuel cycle program using the BN-1200 design. Russia is still just testing fuel 
assemblies with minor actinides. Prior to the current Russian effort, France operated 
Phénix and Superphénix fast reactors in a similar manner, utilizing PUREX derived 
MOX but not pyroprocessing TRU. The French fast reactor program was ultimately 
wound down due to a combination of operational challenges, weak economics in a 
low-uranium-price environment, and declining political support. 
 
Recycling Costs – Closing the Loop 
Since LWR-to-MOX for LWR is largely mature, primarily conducted by the French 
government, and we do not see any new commercial pathway, our discussion of 
recycling will focus on fast reactors. 
A major driver of fuel recycling costs will be how much spent fuel must be processed 
to extract the desired quantity of fissile material, plutonium, or TRU. As figure 47 
above shows, these quantities are multiples higher in fast reactors than LWRs. 
Recycling economics can be viewed similarly to ore grade in mining. LWR spent fuel 
is abundant but dilute in the fissile material required for new fast-reactor fuel, making 
it the low-grade ore of recycling. Fast-reactor spent fuel is far richer in reusable 
plutonium and transuranics, making it the high-grade ore. While spent fast reactor 
fuel is superior in this regard, without an operating fleet of fast reactors, virtually all 
spent fuel available today is from LWR and will be higher cost. To ultimately achieve 
a closed-loop fuel cycle, fast reactors will need to initially be fueled with fresh 
uranium (HALEU) or recycled LWR. The spent fuel from these fast reactors will, over 
time, produce TRU on a ~1:1 ratio such that ~1kg of spent fuel plus a small amount of 
makeup material (depleted uranium) can produce ~1kg of fast reactor fuel. 
The recycling process must recover the valuable uranium and transuranics, meaning 
the TRU that includes plutonium and minor actinides such as neptunium, americium, 
and curium, while separating out fission products, which have no value. The 
recovered material must then be rebalanced with makeup material to hit the right 
fuel recipe for the target reactor. Finally, it must be refabricated into qualified fuel 
rods, pins, pellets, or metal fuel assemblies, depending on the reactor type and fuel 
form.  
Pyroprocessing is a high-temperature recycling technology that uses molten-salt 
electrochemistry to recover plutonium together with other actinides from spent fuel. 
Unlike conventional PUREX reprocessing, which separates plutonium into its own 
stream, pyroprocessing typically produces a mixed actinide product. This approach 
aligns well with fast reactors, which can consume a much broader range of recycled 
actinides. Furthermore, by not separating out plutonium, pyroprocessing creates less 
of a proliferation concern.

The limitation today is commercial maturity. Pyroprocessing has been studied and 
demonstrated at smaller scales, and electrorefining has been used at Idaho National 
Laboratory (INL) for Experimental Breeder Reactor-II (EBR-II) fuel treatment, but the 
process has not yet been deployed as a large-scale commercial fuel supply system 
for a fleet of fast reactors. 
Since pyroprocessing has not been developed in any commercial way, costs are 
largely unknown and will vary between facilities, with total capacity and utilization 
the major swing factors. The primarily real world datapoint is EBR-II fuel reprocessed 
using pyroprocessing at INL at $212,440/kgHM (heavy metal), a figure heavily 
influenced by the small throughput capacity of the facility and the small amount of 
feedstock.  
In 2020, the DOE presented a study that estimates pyroprocessing costs of between 
$1,850 and $4,250/kgHM, with remote refabrication estimated in the range of $1,052 
and $2,850/kgHM. We note that the DOE study does not reference any real 
commercial reprocessing facilities, which makes the estimate ranges low confidence, 
and the study explicitly mentions the refabrication range in particular as difficult to 
pinpoint. The DOE study was focused on spent LWR fuel; however, the higher 
concentration of TRU within spent fast reactor fuel should translate to a higher cost 
per kgHM. 
Oklo has announced plans for a $1.68B first-phase advanced fuel center in 
Tennessee, with the initial facility intended to recycle spent light water fuel into fuel 
for fast reactors. The project is one of the most visible U.S. efforts to commercialize 
pyroprocessing-based fuel recycling, although the facility remains in the planning 
and licensing stage, with commissioning and initial production targeted in the early 
2030s. Given the low fissile inventory in spent LWR fuel, we'll be closely watching how 
the economics of this project evolve.

FAQ 
Below is a collection of FAQs we encountered during our analysis: 
Why hasn't nuclear fuel recycling ever gained widespread commercial traction? 
Recycling has been demonstrated technically, but commercial adoption has 
remained limited by high capital intensity, costly recycled-fuel fabrication, 
safeguards and security requirements, waste-handling complexity, and weaker 
economics than fresh fuel. Historical projects reinforce the execution risk. The U.K.’s 
THORP facility ultimately closed after demand declined, while major U.S. 
reprocessing and MOX projects were halted or discontinued after regulatory 
requirements, costs, and delays increased substantially. France remains the clearest 
commercial example but has remained the exception rather than the global model. 
What is the cost structure for recycling LWR fuel for LWR reactors (i.e., LWR-to-
MOX), and how does that compare to fresh LEU? 
While Orano currently performs LWR recycling at scale, cost figures of the operation 
are not publicly available. However, we do have cost estimates from Japan’s 
Rokkasho complex, which provides the most visible modern benchmark for 
commercial-scale LWR to MOX recycling infrastructure. Based on stated costs, 
capacity, a 40-year operating life, and 80% utilization, we estimate costs of 
approximately $4,000/kgHM for reprocessing and a further approximately 
$4,000/kgHM for MOX fabrication, before discounting. While project-specific factors 
contributed to cost overruns and delays, Rokkasho illustrates that both reprocessing 
LWR spent fuel and recycled-fuel fabrication of MOX are multi-thousand-dollar-per-
kgHM activities. 
Using those datapoints, the reprocessed MOX fuel at an equivalent enrichment of 
~4.5% would be $36,000/kg. This compares with current prices of LEU at 4.5% with 
fabrication to be around ~$4,300/kg.

Will there be pushback on pyroprocessing because of proliferation concerns? What 
is the historical record? 
Proliferation concerns are central to any discussion of reprocessing since the process 
involves recovering material that can contain plutonium usable in nuclear weapons. 
Traditional aqueous reprocessing, particularly PUREX, raises the clearest concern 
because the process can separate plutonium into a distinct product stream. Even 
when that plutonium is reactor-grade and intended for civil MOX fuel, a separated 
plutonium stream still requires stringent safeguards, material accounting, physical 
protection, and international oversight.  
Pyroprocessing is often viewed as more proliferation-resistant than traditional PUREX 
because the process can keep plutonium mixed with other transuranics rather than 
producing a clean, separated plutonium stream. The resulting mixed actinide product 
is less directly attractive than separated plutonium and would generally require 
additional processing before misuse. Proliferation-resistant does not mean 
proliferation-free, however. Critics argue that plutonium could still be separated 
through additional processing, meaning pyroprocessing facilities would still require 
stringent safeguards, material accounting, physical protection, and regulatory 
oversight. 
Perhaps the strongest historical example of proliferation pushback on recycling is the 
Barnwell Reprocessing Plant in South Carolina. The plant was an aqueous 
reprocessing facility based on the PUREX process. The project was essentially 
completed but never operated because U.S. policymakers concluded that 
commercial plutonium separation posed unacceptable proliferation risks. The 
broader U.S. reprocessing industry and breeder-reactor programs were also 
significantly curtailed by the same policy shift in the late 1970s.

Can disposal costs be avoided with recycling?  
Disposal costs are an important offset in the recycling debate, but current assumed 
disposal costs are unlikely to close the economic gap for LWR reprocessing on their 
own. A once-through fuel cycle still requires storage, packaging, transport, and 
eventual disposal, with some industry estimates putting total back-end costs for 
direct disposal at ~$600/kg. Recycling can reduce some waste volumes and recover 
usable material, but it also creates separate waste streams and adds reprocessing, 
safeguards, security, and fabrication costs. As a result, avoided disposal costs may 
improve the recycling case at the margin, but current disposal-cost assumptions do 
not appear large enough by themselves to make recycling broadly cost competitive 
with fresh LEU or even HALEU under current fuel-price assumptions. We do note that 
spent HALEU fuel would have higher disposal costs than the current costs of the 
spent fuel today.  
What companies are exposed to nuclear fuel recycling? 
In terms of the Western market, the notable companies exposed to nuclear fuel 
recycling are Oklo (OKLO), Newcleo (NWCL), and Orano (private). Orano currently 
has the only commercial scale recycling operation and leverages the aqueous and 
MOX processes.  
How does long-lived radioactive waste compare between spent LWR and spent Fast 
Reactor recycling programs? 
Spent fuel from LWRs contain fission products plus plutonium and other long-lived 
elements such as americium. Most fission products decay within a few hundred 
years, though a few persist longer. The plutonium and related elements are the real 
problem since they take ~100,000 to 300,000 years to decay to the radiotoxicity of 
natural uranium ore. In a once-through cycle, all the material goes to disposal. 
Fast reactors can burn these long-lived elements instead of discarding them. If nearly 
all of them are recovered and recycled, the waste's radiotoxicity falls to ore levels in 
~300 to 1,000 years instead. However, these numbers are largely forecasted since 
fast reactor recycling has only been demonstrated at small scale. 
What is the sensitivity analysis for all Recycled Fuel scenarios? 
Uranium and enrichment costs are important drivers of nuclear fuel cost. As these 
costs increase, recycling becomes more economically viable. This benefit is greatest 
for fast reactors, which can extract more energy from recycled material and require 
less new uranium. Recycling spent fast-reactor fuel may also be less costly than 
recycling spent LWR fuel.

VALUATION METHODOLOGY AND RISKS
Valuation Methodology
Metals & Minerals
Our valuation methodology may include two approaches: a relative value approach or an
intrinsic value approach. Our relative value approach relies upon utilizing a blend of P/NAV
and EV/EBITDA multiples, which we consider as the most relevant metrics. Our intrinsic value
approach utilizes discounted cash flow (DCF) methodology as well as our sum-of-the-parts NAV
analysis, which values a company based on its mineral resources and selected balance sheet
items. Mine plan, capital costs, and operating cost assumptions applied in our DCF are based
on published technical reports, our best estimates using our experience and observations from
other projects and information publicly disclosed by management.
We make investment recommendations on certain early stage, pre-revenue companies based
upon an assessment of their asset quality and upside potential, technology, probability
of market success, and the potential market opportunity, balanced by an assessment of
applicable risks. Such companies may not be assigned a price target.
Electrical Equipment:
We derive a year end price target using a discounted cash flow model with the discount
rate equal to the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). We derive our WACCs from our
estimates of the risk free rate, market risk premium, the company’s unlevered beta, the cost
of incremental borrowing, and market values of debt and equity. Our terminal value assumes
growth equivalent to the risk free rate. We make investment recommendations on certain
early stage, pre-revenue companies based upon an assessment of their business model,
technology, probability of market success, and the potential market opportunity, balanced by
an assessment of applicable risks. Such companies may not be assigned a price target.
Clean Energy:
Our valuation methodology is based on a discounted cash flow analysis.
We make investment recommendations on certain early stage, pre-revenue companies based
upon an assessment of their business model, technology, probability of market success,
and the potential market opportunity, balanced by an assessment of applicable risks. Such
companies may not be assigned a price target.
Investment Risks
Metals & Minerals
Technical Risk – Mining projects are all subject to a certain level of technical risks depending on
the nature of the project.
Permitting and Political Risk – Mining projects are subject to the geopolitical risks in their
respective jurisdiction. Project permitting can also be adversely affected by the jurisdiction's
political climate and social issues.
Financial & Forecast Risk – The company's future financial performance is dependent on a
number of assumptions, including metal prices, foreign exchange rates, operating costs, and
capital expenditure levels. The company may require additional funding beyond our forecasts
and there can be no assurance that these funds will be available, or at an acceptable cost.
Environmental Risk – Risks assessed include water and waste recycle rates, electricity and
energy from renewables, emissions and energy intensity, scope 1 & 2 & 3 emissions, greenhouse
emission targets, reclamation and tailings targets, environmental incident counts and
environmental certifications, and preventative practices and monitoring.

and operating a mine. Some of the key elements to assess include workplace safety metrics,
community investment, economic value generated and distributed, procurement from local
