The Commodities Feed: Oil plunges
following US midterm elections
Energy markets are under significant pressure following the Democrats’
clean sweep in the US midterms as hopes grow that the political shift
could accelerate efforts to bring the war with Iran to an end
Energy – Middle East optimism grows following US election
Oil markets sold off aggressively yesterday, with ICE Brent settling 8.7% lower on the day and
below $95/bbl. Selling pressure has only continued in early morning trading today. The
outcome of the US midterm elections, with the Democrats winning both the House and Senate
has increased hopes that we could see a quicker end to the war in Iran. Given the Democrats’
control Congress, there will likely be more scrutiny over the war, while funding it could become
more difficult. The outcome of the elections means there could be more pushback from the
Republican Party, too. Yet the new senators and members of the House start in January,
leaving room for President Trump to escalate if he wants. Trump has suggested there are no
imminent plans to strike Iran.
How all this plays out is still highly uncertain. Therefore, the degree and speed to which the oil
market has sold off seems to be overdone. One would think Trump might want to come away
with some accomplishments from this war. Does he go about that through renewed strikes or
striking a deal? Or by continuing to put pressure on Iran with the ongoing maritime blockade?

In the lead up to the midterms, all signals pointed towards Trump resuming strikes. This is less
clear now.
In the absence of escalation, the oil market is likely to continue to slowly give back the risk
premium it built in recent months, with oil flows from the Persian Gulf holding up well. This risk
premium could disappear even quicker if there’s some sort of agreement between the US and
Iran. Fundamentally, the oil market is looking increasingly softer, reflected in the weakening in
the physical market as well as timespreads.
Inventory data from the EIA yesterday showed that US commercial crude oil inventories fell by
865k barrels over the last week. When including releases from strategic reserves total crude,
inventories fell by 1.26m barrels. Meanwhile, changes on the refined product side were even
more supportive, particularly for middle distillates. Despite refineries increasing their utilisation
rates by 1.7 percentage points over the week to 94.5%, distillate fuel oil inventories still fell by
3.57m barrels. This took total distillate stocks below 100m barrels for the first time since 2003.
Gasoline inventories also edged lower by 222k barrels.
European gas prices also sold off yesterday amid hopes the US midterms outcome could lead
to a quicker recovery in LNG flows. TTF fell by more than 3.8% and pressure has continued this
morning, leaving  it trading around the EUR85/MWh level. However, fundamentally, the
European gas market remains tight. Storage has started drawing with the heating season now
underway. EU storage stands at 74% full. Where we come out the other side of winter is
largely dependent on how much of a recovery we see in Persian Gulf LNG flows this winter, if
any. And, of course, given the tighter market, weather is going to be even more important
than usual through the winter.
Metals – Gold surges back above $4,000/oz
Spot gold jumped more than 3% yesterday, climbing back above $4,000/oz following a
Democratic sweep in the US mid-term elections. Expectations of legislative gridlock, lower
Treasury yields and a weaker dollar are supporting the move.
Meanwhile, central bank buying continues to provide underlying support, with official-sector
demand remaining strong in September. Central banks added 61 tonnes during the month,
according to the World Gold Council, while third-quarter purchases reached 228 tonnes (-7%
year-on-year). Poland and China continued to lead purchases, reinforcing the structural
demand story for gold.
Despite the rebound, the near-term outlook remains challenging for the precious metal.
Inflation concerns continue to support expectations for tighter monetary policy. But much
depends on developments in the Middle East and what they mean for energy markets.
