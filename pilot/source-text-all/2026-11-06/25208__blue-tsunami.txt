Introduction  
The midterm elections have turned into a Blue Wave. Democrats have gained a majority in both 
the House of Representatives and the Senate. This election outcome means a shift from unified 
Republican government to divided government with a Democratic majority in the House of 
Representatives and the Senate and a Republican President. 
While earlier in the year President Trump claimed that he did not care about the midterms, it was 
obvious this was to be taken with a grain of salt. In fact, more recently Trump has been 
campaigning extensively. He told his supporters to pretend he is on the ballot. The midterms 
matter because Congress has the power of the purse and while the US President has considerable 
executive powers, a Democratic House of Representatives and Senate can deprive the President of 
the funds needed for his ambitions in the last two years of his term. 
Since markets already priced in a divided government, the impact of the election result on longer-
term interest rates has been muted. In fact, as we showed in Midterms and Markets, a Blue Wave 
was the most likely outcome (more than 60%) according to prediction markets and the Democrats 
winning the House of Representatives had a larger than 90% probability. The 10-year US treasury 
yield lost just a few basis points overnight, which is consistent with pricing out the small 
probability that the Republicans were going to hold on to their majorities and provide an

Blue tsunami will lead to gridlock 
In yesterday’s midterm elections, the entire House of Representatives was on the ballot, and 35 
out of 100 Senate seats. The Republicans have lost their majorities in the Senate and the House of 
Representatives in a Blue Wave that was larger than expected. Even traditionally red states and 
districts turned blue on Election Day. The Democrats gained solid majorities in both the House of 
Representatives and the Senate (Table 1).

Now that the Democrats have managed to get a majority in the Senate and the House of 
Representatives, the current balance of power (Table 2) will change when the new Congress 
convenes on January 3, 2027. This means that there will be a shift in the policies that will be 
proposed in Congress.  
However, the President will veto the policies that he doesn’t like and the Democrats – despite the 
impressive Blue Wave – have not gathered the votes needed to overturn Trump’s veto. They do 
not have a 2/3 majority in either chamber of Congress to overturn a presidential veto. This 
would require 290 seats in the House of Representatives and 67 seats in the Senate. A substantial 
number of Republicans would have to defect and face a MAGA backlash in the next election. 
Since MAGA is still a major force in the Republican party, a defection would not be without 
consequences. This could deter moderate Republicans from reaching across the aisle.  
Bipartisan initiatives would probably have a better chance if actually backed by President 
Trump. However, this would probably require some restraint on the Democrats’ desire for 
investigations of the Trump administration. Otherwise, any spirit of bipartisanship may fade 
rapidly. 
Therefore, Democratic policy initiatives may not get far, despite the solid majorities. Only 
bipartisan proposals could make it, but there is very limited common ground between the two 
parties these days. Therefore, we are likely to see gridlock in legislation.    
Faced with an unsupportive Congress, President Trump is likely to focus on ruling through his 
executive powers. He is also likely to test how far he can stretch these powers. This means that 
especially in foreign policy, trade policy and immigration policy, he may continue his current 
course.  
In contrast, since Congress has the power of the purse, fiscal policy will become a major 
challenge. But this is true for both parties. Gridlock is the most likely outcome. At least this should 
limit additional government spending and tax cuts, but we don’t expect any serious effort to get 
the US public debt under control. The public debt trajectory may not get another major impulse, 
but the trajectory will still be upward-sloping. 
Gridlock may also lead to potential brinkmanship related to government shutdowns and the 
debt limit in 2027.

Congress vs Trump 
What are the tools of opposition that the Democrats will have, starting in January? The Senate and 
the House of Representatives have different non-legislative powers.  
The House of Representatives is tasked with congressional oversight and impeachment. This 
means they can start investigations into the Trump administration and even impeachment 
proceedings.  
The Senate is responsible for approving presidential appointments, such as cabinet members, 
judges and Fed governors. This means that the Democrats will be able to block Trump’s 
appointments. For the Fed, a Democratic Senate will prevent Trump from getting loyalists into the 
Board of Governors if one of the current governors is forced (Cook) out or steps down voluntarily 
(Powell).  
The Senate also judges whether impeachment by the House of Representatives should lead to the 
President’s removal from office. Still, a two-thirds majority in the Senate is needed to remove the 
President from office after impeachment by the House through a simple majority, so conviction is 
unlikely. This would require a substantial number of defecting Republican senators. 
Meanwhile, these investigations are likely to undermine any cooperation between President 
Trump and the Democratic majority in Congress. 
At the same time, it should be noted that gridlock also implies that the One Big Beautiful Bill Act 
(OBBBA) will remain valid. President Trump can simply veto any attempt to overturn elements of 
his signature bill. This means that the stimulus from OBBBA remains in place. 
Lame ducks in a hurry 
It is important to note that the newly elected Congress does not convene until January 3. So, 
during the lame duck period the Republicans still have some time for legislation. However, 
Congress has only about 24 legislative days left in session. Moreover, the December 11 deadline 
to avert a government shutdown may complicate matters. The Republicans only have a slim 
majority in the House of Representatives at the moment and the deliberations about the 
continuing resolution needed to avert the shutdown will cost precious time. However, the 
remaining legislative days in session do give the Republicans a window to push through a number 
of priorities.

Blue tsunami across the oceans 
What’s important for the rest of the world is that the Blue Tsunami in US politics may force 
President Trump even further into international politics. As is often the case with US presidents 
who lose their majority in Congress in the midterms, President Trump is likely to focus on foreign 
and trade policy as domestic and fiscal policy is gridlocked. This means that he can focus on 
outstanding trade policy issues. This includes the pending Section 301 investigations on global 
excess capacity, digital services taxes, and the struggles for supply chain dominance and 
technological dominance with China. All three issues will also affect the EU. Meanwhile, existing 
tariffs are likely to remain in place as far as they hold up in court and the President vetoes 
legislation by the Democrats. 
Conclusion 
The US midterms elections have turned into a Blue Tsunami. This means a shift from unified 
Republican government to divided government with a Democratic majority in the House of 
Representatives and the Senate. President Trump is likely to focus on ruling through his executive 
powers. He is also likely to test how far he can stretch these powers. This means that in foreign 
policy, trade policy and immigration policy, Trump may continue his current course. In contrast, 
fiscal policy is likely to end up in gridlock and potential brinkmanship related to government 
shutdowns and the debt limit. For the rest of the world, this means that President Trump could be 
even more focused on foreign and trade policy than before.
