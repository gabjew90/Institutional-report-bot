A «blue wave» sweeps across the coun-
try, delivering a clear rebuke to President 
Trump. The Democrats now control both 
houses of Congress. The White House 
loses its legislative initiative. Trump re-
tains broad executive powers in trade 
and foreign policy and resorts to vetoes 
and executive orders on domestic policy.
Political disputes are shifting to the bud-
get and the debt ceiling. Fierce fiscal bat-
tles loom, whilst major spending pro-
grammes or tax rises are set to be vetoed 
by the President.
The reaction of the financial markets re-
mains limited. The focus is shifting back 
more strongly to fundamentals. In the 
background, the risk of government 
shutdowns and tensions over the debt 
ceiling continues to smoulder.
Manuel Ferreira, Chief Strategist Christian Brändli, Senior 
Economist, North America
 
 Two years after the Republicans’ landslide victory in the 
presidential election, US voters have pulled the emer-
gency brake. The Democrats have not only recaptured 
the House of Representatives, but have also overturned 
the Republicans’ previous majority in the Senate. In the 
House of Representatives, the Democrats have secured 
more than 250 of the 435 seats. In the Senate, they have 
made gains even in Republican strongholds and will now 
hold a solid majority of at least 54 of the 100 seats (see 
chart). The main drivers of this turnaround are nation-
wide opposition to President Trump and concerns about 
the cost of living, which many voters link to the govern-
ment’s tariff policy and the consequences of the military 
conflict with Iran. For the remaining two years of Donald 
Trump’s term in office, this result marks a turning point. 
The White House is losing its legislative initiative.

With a majority in both chambers, the White House and 
the Republicans are losing control of the legislative pro-
cess. The Democrats can block judicial appointments and 
cabinet appointments in the Senate. Trump is therefore 
likely to make personnel changes swiftly so that the Sen-
ate, which is still dominated by the Republicans, can con-
firm them by the end of the year. With their double ma-
jority, the Democrats gain significant bargaining power 
vis-à-vis the Trump administration, as they set the agen-
da in Congress and draft legislation. However, without 
cross-party compromises, the legislative process remains 
largely deadlocked, as 60 votes are required in the Sen-
ate for most bills. Furthermore, President Trump can veto 
any bill passed by Congress, and such a veto can only be 
overridden by a two-thirds majority in both chambers. By 
taking control of the House of Representatives, the Dem-
ocrats also gain control of key investigative committees 
and could initiate a third impeachment proceeding 
against the President. However, a conviction in the Sen-
ate is unlikely in the absence of a two-thirds majority. 
For his part, Trump will largely govern domestically 
through executive orders.
Fiscal policy battles
Political disputes are centred on the federal budget, de-
fence spending and the debt ceiling. Whilst the Demo-
crats could use the budgetary process to pass fiscal meas-
ures on spending, taxes or the debt ceiling by a simple 
majority, Trump’s veto continues to force cross-party 
compromises. The spending authorisation, which expires 
on 11 December 2026, is a matter of urgency. Given the 
divisions and narrow majorities, it is uncertain whether 
the outgoing Congress will reach an agreement, which is 
why a short-term extension until early 2027 is likely. A 
second key point of contention is the debt ceiling, which 
is expected to be reached next spring and will need to be 
raised by autumn 2027 at the latest. The Democrats will 
use these levers to demand concessions on social and 
economic issues (including healthcare and the cost of liv-
ing, as well as health insurance subsidies). In a possible 
compromise, the White House could demand higher de-
fence spending but, in return, would agree to a propor-
tional increase in other expenditure. Overall, federal ex-
penditure is likely to rise by only a few tenths of a 
percentage point of GDP. More extensive programmes 
or new taxes would be vetoed by the President. The gov-
ernment deficit is therefore likely to remain at around 
6% of GDP in the short term, resulting in a largely neu-
tral fiscal impulse next year.
Trump retains room for manoeuvre on tariffs and Iran
Despite losing power in Congress, Trump retains consid-
erable executive leeway in trade and foreign policy. 
Through national security clauses, the government can 
continue to impose or adjust tariffs without a congres-
sional resolution. With the annual USMCA review, the 
tariff truce with China running until 10 January 2027, 
and ongoing negotiations on trade agreements, numer-
ous trade issues remain in the hands of the executive. 
Following the election, the government could also pub-
lish the findings of the investigation into industrial over-
capacity – which was launched in parallel with the inves-
tigation into forced labour – and raise tariff rates once 
again. Whilst the Democrats will attempt to scale back 
individual existing tariffs – for example, those on Canada 
or certain product groups – as part of budget negotia-
tions, a fundamental departure from Trump’s tariff poli-
cy is not to be expected. The same applies to the Iran 
conflict: the strategic direction remains primarily in the 
hands of the executive. At most, the House of Represent-
atives can exert pressure through targeted cuts to the 
defence budget or symbolic votes.
Hardening stances on AI regulation
Another contentious issue is the regulation of artificial 
intelligence. Whilst there is growing cross-party aware-
ness of security and risk considerations, the hurdles to 
comprehensive regulatory legislation remain high. The 
requirement for 60 votes in the Senate, as well as the 
possibility of a presidential veto, make far-reaching reg-
ulations unlikely. The Trump administration continues to 
advocate a cautious, industry-led approach. Most Repub-
licans also reject strict regulations, pointing to competi-
tion with China and the economic benefits of AI invest-
ment. The industry is likely to be receptive to this course 
of action, as uniform minimum standards across the 
country could reduce legal uncertainty and prevent a 
growing patchwork of state-level regulations. Overall, 
the election result is unlikely to significantly slow down 
the current AI investment cycle. It is more likely that the 
Democrats will launch investigations into major technol-
ogy companies to scrutinise AI development, security 
practices and data collection more closely.
Financial markets in the new balance of power

cratic majority in Congress, as it is associated with more 
regulation and higher taxes. Furthermore, Big Tech and 
traditional energy companies are coming under increas-
ing political pressure to justify their actions. However, 
President Trump can use his veto to block unwanted reg-
ulatory initiatives and fiscal U-turns over the next two 
years. The market reaction is therefore likely to remain 
limited, particularly as the focus will quickly shift back to 
fundamentals following the midterm elections. Here, 
positive earnings momentum and a robust economy con-
tinue to dominate the picture. Market sentiment is also 
significantly shaped by inflation, developments in the 
Middle East and the issue of AI, which are largely inde-
pendent of the political backdrop. Nevertheless, unwel-
come surprises in trade and foreign policy remain possi-
ble, as the President still has considerable scope for 
manoeuvre in these areas. Lurking in the background, 
too, is the risk of fiscal policy battles, which could lead to 
further episodes of government shutdowns and tensions 
surrounding the debt ceiling.
Equity markets
The «blue wave» will, in principle, do little to alter the 
existing market drivers. The environment remains fa-
vourable for risk-bearing investments. Furthermore, giv-
en the intact growth environment and the recent broad-
ening of the market, a cyclical recovery is possible.  The 
performance of global equities will be driven by eco-
nomic momentum and global monetary policy. The 
former remains supportive overall, whilst the latter is, for 
the time being, slightly restrictive due to the risk of infla-
tion. With a Democrat-dominated Congress, however, 
disagreements between the President and Congress are 
likely to increase, making further growth impetus less 
likely and potentially leading to higher volatility. Regula-
tion-sensitive sectors such as healthcare, energy and ma-
terials could face headwinds, as government interven-
tion becomes more prevalent. Political initiatives in 
favour of renewable energy, on the other hand, could 
increase.
Bond markets
A more critical stance by a Democrat-dominated Con-
gress towards fiscal expansion could have a positive im-
pact on the development of US Treasuries and, conse-
quently, on the global yield curve. However, given the 
structural conditions – persistent deficits, rising govern-
ment bond issuance, an increased term premium and 
waning foreign demand – any recovery is likely to be 
short-lived. The medium-term view that the yield envi-
ronment will remain volatile still stands. Regardless of 
the election result, the long end of the US yield curve re-
mains the most vulnerable to ongoing fiscal and supply-
side pressures compared with the rest of the world.
US dollar
The short-term impact of a Democrat-dominated Con-
gress is likely to be moderate. The dominant influencing 
factors remain the Federal Reserve’s policy, US growth 
data and AI-driven capital flows – all of which are cur-
rently supporting the dollar. In the medium term, how-
ever, the headwinds predominate: Structural fiscal con-
cerns, the diversification of currency reserves and waning 
foreign demand for US Treasuries are creating head-
winds. A political stalemate alone cannot eliminate 
these. We should therefore expect a gradual rather than 
an abrupt weakening.
