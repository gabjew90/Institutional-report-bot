Rates Spark: US midterms and
expanding European government bond
concerns
Tuesday will be nothing more than the day ahead of a much bigger day
– Wednesday, by which time we'll have guidance on the midterm
implications, and we'll have the US refunding announcement. In Europe,
political jitters in Belgium highlight that France is not the only one
struggling to consolidate its finances
Along with the results of the US midterm elections, the Treasury's refunding operations loom large
The midterms and refunding loom large on Wednesday
Two things for Treasuries to get their teeth into in the coming days. First, the signals from the
midterm elections. Expect this to be an issue for trading on Wednesday, as projections emerge
through Tuesday night. The basic market discount is for the Democrats to take the House, and
maybe the Senate. The only big surprise would be if the Republicans held on to both, which
would be a huge surprise, as it would, in fact, suggest an endorsement from the US electorate
for the war, tariffs and the current immigration policy, among other contentious issues that
have emboldened the left and caused right-leaning independents to swing left. In all
probability, this would be bond negative, on the theory that it's more of the same until
disproven. The more probable blue sweep should have little market impact, as it should be
broadly discounted.

The second big thing for Wednesday is the refunding announcement. At the previous one,
there was a clear intimation of no change to the rhythm of issuance. And the questions posed
to primary dealers a few weeks back did not suggest anything big or imminent in terms of
change. However, there has been some growing talk of the refunding including a reduction in
long-end issuance. More likely, if the Treasury were minded to do so, it would be an indication
that it has the flexibility to do so going forward. Either that, or no change to the issuance
profile, is probable. We don't think the Treasury needs to panic here by changing tack on
issuance. Swap spreads are well-behaved. Better to leave such a policy for a time when there is
a material need to tighten swap spreads.
European bond spreads will remain in focus for some time, and
it’s not just France
The European government bond space has also moved back into focus at the start of the
week, although France has fared better than one might have feared. In an unscheduled review,
Fitch lowered the outlook on France’s A+ rating to negative, citing weaker growth, higher
borrowing costs and a wider deficit since the August review. Nonetheless, the 10y OAT/Bund
spread still tightened by around 4bp against the backdrop of a broader tightening in
government bond spreads at the start of the week, which seems to have been driven by some
easing of geopolitical tensions, with oil prices coming off their highs. To be fair, France has
been trading at levels more akin to a BBB name for some time already, suggesting spreads are
already wider than pure fundamentals would imply.
There was one important exception to the tightening trend. In contrast to the broader trend,
10y Belgian government bond spreads widened by up to 4bp versus Bunds. The backdrop was
that the prime minister tendered his resignation after his coalition failed to agree on a budget.
The King did not immediately accept the resignation and called on coalition parties to find a
compromise. The King will receive the prime minister next on 6 November. The headlines
underscore that it is not only France that faces the challenge of consolidating its finances. In
the summer, the Belgian central bank forecast a deficit of 5.3% for this year and 5.5% in 2027.
Tuesday’s events and market view
Investors will be looking forward to the US midterm elections as the main political
event of the week. In terms of data, the US will release the JOLTs job openings data for
September ahead of Friday’s official payrolls data.  In the absence of the data releases,
the focus in Europe will be on a busy schedule of ECB speakers, including Kocher,
Moulin, Nagel and Sleijpen.
In primary markets, Germany auctions 5y bonds (€5bn) and Austria sells 4y and 9y
bonds (€1.73bn in total). The UK auctions 10y green gilts (£3.25bn).
