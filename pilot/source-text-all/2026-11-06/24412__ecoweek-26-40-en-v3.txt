US MIDTERMS: THE COST OF LIVING IS NOW A PIVOTAL ISSUE

In the United States, growth is holding up, unemployment remains low and consumer spending shows no sign of slowing. 
On paper, that should flatter the president’s party ahead of the midterms. Yet, Republicans could lose control of Congress. 
The rise in the cost of living, which weighs on households far more than macroeconomic aggregates suggest, could be one 
of the reasons. On top of persistent inflation, rekindled by the war in Iran, there is also a slowdown in wage growth and 
rising housing costs. The election could hinge on affordability, yet whichever party wins will lack the means to address it. 
A change in the majority in Congress will nonetheless have an impact on the country’s economy.
On 3rd November, Americans will elect a new Congress for the next 
two years. With Republicans currently in control of the White House, 
the House of Representatives and the Senate, the balance of power in 
Washington could shift. Polls suggest that the House, at least, could 
swing to the Democrats. Beyond the new balance of power, the vote 
will reflect how voters view the way the country is being run. Here, we 
focus on the cost of living.
INFLATION: THE INCUMBENTS’ BURDEN
The incumbent party goes into the election with two handicaps. 
The first is structural: no matter who holds the White House, the pre-
sident’s party almost always loses seats in midterm elections. The 
second stems from the combination of robust but uneven growth 
and persistent inflation, which is undermining household confidence. 
Pre-election polls1 confirm this assessment: the economy and the cost-
of-living top voters’ worries.
Inflation has overshot the Fed’s target every year since 2021. Prices are 
up 28% over five years: 31% for housing (not including mortgage rates, 
which have more than doubled), and 29% for food. This shock is felt all 
the more keenly as it is the most severe in forty years and follows on 
from decades of low inflation2. Furthermore, it hits the lowest-income 
households the hardest. In addition, the election comes after an ener-
gy-driven rise in inflation. Energy accounts for the entire gap between 
the latest CPI reading (3.7% y/y in September) and the last figure re-
corded before the conflict in the Middle East started (2.4% y/y).
Fuel has made things worse. The rise in the price of a gallon (46% 
between February and October) caused by the Iran war — which is 
unpopular even amongst Republican voters3 — is an aggravating factor. 
In 2026, inflation only slightly crossed the ‘attention threshold’, the 
3.6%y/y rate beyond which households start to regard it as problema-
tic4. Fuel is a recurring, unavoidable expense whose price is directly 
observable, unlike many items in the CPI basket. This results in a subs-
tantial gap between perceived inflation and measured inflation.
Inflation must be viewed alongside the other drivers of households’ 
well-being. Today, wages, payrolls and the housing market are moving 
the wrong way for most Americans.
IT'S NOT JUST PRICES
The 2022 midterms suggest caution in reading the polls. Democrats 
defied expectations of a ‘Republican wave’, retaining control of the Se-
nate and losing the House by just a few seats, even though inflation 
averaged 8% that year. This reminds us that inflation must be viewed 
alongside the other drivers of households’ well-being. Indeed, how 
people experience the cost of living depends on the interplay between 
prices, wages and housing. In 2022, wages, payrolls and the housing 
market were all growing faster than in 2026. Today, all three are mo-
ving the wrong way for most Americans.
Wage growth is slowing just as inflation picks up again. In September 
2026, average hourly earnings were up 3% y/y in nominal terms, the 
slowest rate since 2018, and now below inflation. Real wage earnings 
are therefore falling (-0.7% y/y in September, with six out of nine mon-
ths showing a decline in 2026). The rise in the cost of living is thus 
compounded by a net loss of purchasing power. The Atlanta Fed reports 
that wage growth is lower for the bottom quartile of earners (the 25% 
of employees with the lowest pay) than for the top quartile (the 25% 
with the highest pay): 3.8% y/y vs. 4.4% on average since late 2024, 
following 10 years in which the gap favoured the bottom quartile. Top 
earners also benefit most from the wealth effect linked to stock mar-
ket valuations. This explains the resilience of aggregate consumption. 
However, this rests on a relatively narrow base, mostly households 
earning more than USD 125,000/year5. Meanwhile, a falling saving rate 
(3.8% in September 2026, down 1.4pp y/y) suggests households are 
drawing on their savings to offset price rises. A large share of the po-
pulation is therefore excluded from the growth that has been observed.
Beyond wages, there is the question of employment prospects. 
The unemployment rate remains low and consistent with full employ-
ment. Yet the labour market looks stagnant: low firing despite repeated 
shocks and low hiring despite solid growth. Payroll growth (an average 
of 68,000 per month since January) is well below that of the previous 
decade (nearly 200,000 per month between 2011 and 20196). Com-
bined with a workforce that is no longer growing due to an ageing 
population and a restrictive immigration policy, this is enough to keep 
the unemployment rate low and the labour market looking healthy.

But households are likely to be suffering from a lack of opportunities, 
and consequently from a lack of prospects for wage growth that would 
help them to increase their purchasing power. The Conference Board’s 
labour differential, a gauge of perceived job availability, says as much: 
outside the pandemic, it is at its lowest since 2015.
Housing is the area that has deteriorated the most. The rise in 
mortgage rates that began in 2022, from under 4% to over 7%, has 
dampened the property market. This is true on the demand side, as 
expected, but also on the supply side. The sharp rise in interest rates 
is deterring homeowners with low-interest mortgages from selling, as 
they would have to borrow again at current rates. This ‘lock-in effect’ 
prevents prices from falling despite weaker demand. The lock-in effect 
is also affecting tenants, as the decline in the availability of housing is 
driving up rents. Housing lies at the heart of the disconnect between 
the macroeconomic picture and voters’ perception of it, as they face a 
multifaceted rise in the cost of living. That rise is also driven by credit: 
monthly repayments on a home of the same value have risen shar-
ply. This additional cost, which inflation figures do not capture7, erodes 
households’ purchasing power in the property market.
A CONGRESS POWERLESS ON THE COST OF LIVING
The next Congress will, in all likelihood, have little room for ma-
noeuvre. Neither party is within reach of the thresholds required to 
override the filibuster (which allows 41 out of 100 senators to block 
a bill from coming to a vote) and the presidential veto (two-thirds of 
both chambers).
Affordability is likely to be a key issue in the election, but the winners 
will lack the tools to address it. The Democrats’ agenda relies notably 
on the reintroduction of schemes that were abolished or scaled back 
under the current administration (Affordable Care Act tax credits, Me-
dicaid). Even if Congress passed them, they would be subject to a pre-
sidential veto. There is no question of direct support for households or 
a restrictive fiscal policy.
Whether inflation falls will depend on two factors: how the conflict in 
Iran unfolds and the effectiveness of monetary policy. The labour mar-
ket should hold its current balance: robust growth (see our EcoFlash) 
is supporting demand, whilst failing to alleviate supply-side pressures, 
and the unemployment rate could continue to fall. Household senti-
ment will continue to depend on how prices develop. As for housing, 
it is unlikely to improve in the short term. With the 10-year Treasury 
yield still at 4.8% at the end of 2027 (BNP Paribas forecast), 30-year 
mortgage rates would, at best, remain around 7%.
THE OTHER ECONOMIC STAKES OF THE VOTE
Historically, divided governments have brought legislative stability, 
which is viewed favourably by the markets. But polarisation has in-
creased in recent years and is likely to feature prominently in the bud-
get vote and the debt-ceiling debate. The debt limit, in particular, could 
be reached as early as Q1 2027, depending on the Treasury’s borrowing 
requirements. The current administration, meanwhile, has taken major 
decisions (on tariffs, immigration and Iran) without involving Congress, 
a practice that is likely to continue.

Risks to the deficit remain tilted to the upside. According to our base-
line scenario, it is expected to remain stable at 6.3% of GDP in 2027, 
but the post-election balance of power could add to the cost of the war 
in Iran and rising debt-service costs. In particular, interests could align 
in favour of increased spending should the Democrats gain control of 
both houses of Congress. Avoiding, or ending, a shutdown could require 
a trade-off involving mutual concessions from the presidency on key, 
popular aspects of the Democratic agenda (such as healthcare), and 
from the Democrats on defence.
The AI sector is exposed to the vote. The economic stakes are high 
given how much the associated investment cycle and the wealth effect 
contribute to US growth. However, at this stage, the risk is not legisla-
tive. As with the cost of living, sweeping legislation is unlikely, all the 
more so with a president holding a pro-AI stance, and who holds the 
right of veto. The risk lies rather in sentiment and further out. A Demo-
cratic sweep could lead to increased congressional activism on regu-
lation, on concentration in the AI industry, and on the knock-on effects 
on the cost of living (data centres and electricity) and on jobs. Putting 
these issues on the agenda could create a climate conducive to tighter 
regulation in the medium term and weigh on the sector’s valuations. 
Furthermore, the mid-term elections will see 36 governors’ seats up 
for election. These governors have extensive powers (regarding autho-
risations and incentives) when it comes to the establishment of data 
centres in their states.
Finally, the midterms will matter for monetary policy. The Senate 
confirms, by simple majority, the president’s nominees to the Fed’s 
Board of Governors. Consequently, should the Senate come under De-
mocratic control, President Trump would need a consensus pick to re-
place Governor Powell from 2028 — or to fill any other seat that might 
become vacant during the next Congress.
*****
Whatever the outcome of the election, disinflation and, more broadly, 
relief from the cost of living, depend less on Congress than on the 
White House and, to a lesser extent, the Fed. The key factor lies in the 
resolution of the conflict in Iran, coupled with an effective monetary 
policy to enable a sustainable return to price stability.

ADVANCED ECONOMIES
In brief. Data releases confirmed resilient to strong growth and 
above-forecast inflation in most advanced economies, with France lag-
ging behind and earning a rating outlook downgrade from Fitch as a 
result. Fed, ECB and BoJ remained on hold, as expected, and on track 
for December hikes. Oil continues to fluctuate between escalation fears 
and hopes for a return to the negotiating table. The tech sell-off in 
equity markets intensified despite strong earnings, as markets balk at 
large capex plans. This helped bond yields ease from recent highs.
EUROZONE
Resilience confirmed despite tightening credit. GDP expanded by a 
stronger-than-expected 0.4% q/q in Q3 (0.2% ex-Ireland), with Spain, 
Germany and the Netherlands contributing to the bulk of non-Irish 
growth. We increase our growth forecast to 1.6% in 2027 (+0.1pp), 
driven by further improving sentiment in manufacturing. Bank lending 
conditions tightened further in Q3 for businesses, especially SMEs, 
driven by France and Germany, while corporate and housing-loan de-
mand weakened. The ECB kept its deposit rate at 2.5% and signaled it 
was firmly on track for a (likely final) December hike. President Lagarde 
also confirmed she would step down early, on February 7, setting off 
the succession race. EU leaders are expected to decide jointly on the 
three Board members departing between January and May (President, 
chief economist, and market operations), likely at their mid-December 
meeting. Official candidates to succeed Lagarde are BIS Head Pablo 
Hernandez de Cos and former Dutch central bank Governor Klaas Knot.
To watch next week: October flash CPI (Wednesday).
France: Another growth setback, with silver linings
GDP contracted by 0.1% q/q in Q3 as a sharp drop in aeronauti-
cal production hit exports and investment. However, services out-
put grew by a robust 0.3% q/q, and household consumption also 
grew more strongly than forecasted (+0.4%). While 2026 will ine-
vitably be knocked down (to 0.3%), we forecast 0.8% for 2027. 
Nevertheless, Fitch issued an unscheduled rating outlook downgrade 
to negative territory, on grounds that fiscal, growth and ad-
justment prospects have deteriorated since its August review. 
A new review could be concluded as early as February. Harmonised 
inflation accelerated to 3.8% y/y in October, with both energy and 
non-energy contributing. Meanwhile, producer price inflation accele-
rated to 8.2% y/y in September. Insee’s index of household confidence 
fell to 80, matching its 2013 all-time low, driven by a decline in ex-
pectations of its own financial situation. Private payroll employment 
declined by 26,000 over Q3 (-0.1%). However, in previous quarters, the 
decline in payroll employment was more than offset by the increase in 
self-employment (not released until November 27).
To watch next week: September industrial production (Thursday); Sep-
tember foreign trade (Friday).
Germany: Growth holds up as inflation slows
GDP grew by 0.2% q/q in Q3, with consumption rising slightly but in-
vestment declining and export growth slowing. HICP inflation fell from 
3.3% to 2.8% in October following energy-tax cuts and fuel discounts, 
but core inflation also slowed, to 2.1%. Business confidence (Ifo survey) 
reached its highest level in more than three years, led by manufactu-
ring, which is back in positive territory for the first time since 2023. 
By contrast, consumer confidence deteriorated to its lowest since May, 
due to growing concerns about inflation, which are weighing on consu-
mers’ spending intentions.
To watch next week: September retail sales (Monday); September fac-
tory orders (Thursday); September industrial production (Friday).
Italy: Stagnant growth meets energy-driven inflation surge
GDP was flat in Q3, missing expectations, as positive domestic demand 
was offset by net exports. Economic sentiment improved, but HICP in-
flation jumped from 4.2% to 5.6% in October, its highest level since 
September 2023, mainly because of regulated energy prices; core in-
flation also rose, to a more moderate 2%. More positively, economic 
sentiment rose to 100.2 in October 2026 (from 99.4 in September), 
despite the Eurozone decline. The improvement was driven by services 
and construction. The Italian cabinet approved a budget focused on 
middle-class tax cuts, financed partly by levies on banks, energy and 
gambling companies. x
UNITED KINGDOM
A cautious budget buys breathing space. The budget offered 98 small 
cost-of-living alleviation measures and £4.1 billion in household ener-
gy cost relief, while only modestly increasing taxes, including a tem-
porary rise in the bank surcharge. Its short-term stance is slightly ex-
pansionary, while major structural decisions on defence, pensions and 
welfare have been postponed. The official budget watchdog, OBR, cut 
its 2027 growth forecast to 1%, and firms’ inflation expectations conti-
nued to rise. UK gilts outperformed and equities advanced after the 
budget. GBP rose to a six-week high against the dollar and a 19-month 
high versus the euro as markets price a near-certain Bank of England 
rate hike (to 4%) at this week meeting. We agree but, unlike market 
pricing, we do not expect it to hike further.
To watch next week: Bank of England interest-rate decision (Thursday).
UNITED STATES
Fed awaiting more information despite strong growth. The Federal Re-
serve held its target range at 3.75–4.0%, with two members voting for a 
25 bp increase, as Chair Warsh highlighted stronger growth, favourable 
employment conditions and inflation that remains too high. Q3 GDP 
accelerated to 3.4% annualised, powered by resilient consumption and 
a 12.7% jump in business investment. Despite jobless claims remaining 
near multi-decade lows, compensation costs adjusted for inflation de-
clined 0.4%, marking the second consecutive quarter in which labour 
cost growth trailed price increases.
To watch next week: Midterm elections (Tuesday); October ISM ma-
nufacturing (Monday) and non-manufacturing (Wednesday); Q3 non-
farm productivity (Thursday); October non-farm payrolls (Friday).
JAPAN
BoJ on hold with underlying inflation “consistent with the 2% tar-
get”. The Bank of Japan kept its policy rate at 1.25%, with one dissent 
in favour of a hike. Its outlook judged underlying inflation to be 
consistent with the 2% target, a major shift in its assessment, although 
Governor Ueda mentioned risks on both sides in his remarks, stres-
sing in particular the risk of inflation overshooting the target. It signals 
continued gradual monetary adjustment, with a next hike expected in 
December. Alongside the rate hold, the BOJ announced it would near-
ly triple the pace of ETF sales starting in January, to approximately 
JPY 860 billion per year. Japanese equities shrugged off the announ-
cement.
To watch next week: Minutes of the September Bank of Japan meeting 
(Thursday); September wage growth (Friday).

EMERGING ECONOMIES
ASIA
China: Monetary policy reform and no growth signals
The PBOC disclosed a 1.25% overnight reverse-repo rate and an-
nounced that the overnight rate would become its principal policy 
lever, starting 2nd November. It also pledged to develop cross-border 
renminbi payments and introduced a liquidity backstop for non-bank 
financial institutions. The 5th Plenum of the 20th Central Committee of 
the CCP focused on party discipline and crackdown on corrupt cadres, 
without broader policy signals. Chinese equities had their worst month 
since 2016, falling 8.5% in October, as doubts over AI earnings deepe-
ned investor pessimism. The PMIs published on 30th October suggest 
economic activity is losing momentum; in the manufacturing sector, 
the RatingDog PMI fell slightly to 52 (from 52.1) while the official PMI 
fell more sharply and dropped below 50 (49.3 vs. 50.1 in the previous 
month).
South Korea and Taiwan: AI boom lifts growth even higher
In Korea, GDP grew by 1.0% q/q and 3.4% y/y in Q3, supported by boo-
ming semiconductor exports linked to global AI demand. The expansion 
was uneven, however, as private-consumption growth slowed marke-
dly. In Taiwan, GDP surged by 3.2% q/q and 14.4% y/y in Q3, beating 
expectations and accelerating from the previous quarter as technology 
firms expanded capacity to meet strong demand. 
EMEA
Poland: Lower inflation gives monetary policy some room
Headline inflation declined from 4.0% to 3.5% in October as electricity, 
gas and transport fuel prices rose more slowly, helped by a temporary 
fuel-price cap. Rates are likely to remain unchanged in the near term, 
although renewed zloty weakness means a hike cannot be ruled out.
Saudi Arabia: Reserves fall as regional pressures intensify
Official reserve assets declined by 4.4% m/m in September to 
SAR 1.75 tn, their lowest level since December, with foreign-currency 
assets recording particularly sharp monthly and annual declines.
Türkiye: Pressure on lira and FX reserves
The lira headed for its largest monthly fall in 15 months and cen-
tral-bank reserves came under renewed pressure. This is the conse-
quence of the warning by MSCI that Türkiye could lose its emer-
ging-market status if measures to improve transparency and market 
supervision following the Fund scandal are deemed insufficient.
LATIN AMERICA
Brazil’s post-election rout: stocks and real plunge, yields surge. Bra-
zilian markets, expecting a Flavio Bolsonaro victory, sold off sharply 
after Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva won a fourth presidential term. The Ibo-
vespa fell 14% Monday — its largest daily drop since COVID-19 — and 
ended the week down 18%. The real fell over 11% intraday and ended 
the week down 8% against the euro. Meanwhile the yield on Brazil’s 
10-year government bonds rose 250 bp to 15.5%, reaching its highest 
level in over a decade. The selloff reflects fiscal concerns over pledges 
to raise the minimum wage and Bolsa familia benefits and to offer free 
public transport on weekends and bank holidays. Finance Minister Da-
rio Durigan’s pledge to seek a higher 2027 primary surplus, without a 
new target, did little to reassure markets.
COMMODITIES
The oil market continues to fluctuate between escalation fears and 
hopes for a return to the negotiating table. Brent prices were down by 
5% over the week, reaching USD 109/b on Monday. Tensions on physical 
supply continue to decline. Ditto for diesel crack spreads. On the back 
of geopolitical tensions, lower-than-requested EU stock level and spe-
culation over a cold snap in Europe in November, European gas prices 
(TTF) recorded another strong weekly rise and have breached the 
EUR 100/MWh for the first time since Dec 2022). The Asian gas bench-
mark (JKM) is following the same trend but at a slower pace (4.6% w/w 
to USD 32 /MBtu, equivalent to around EUR 96.3 /MWh).
