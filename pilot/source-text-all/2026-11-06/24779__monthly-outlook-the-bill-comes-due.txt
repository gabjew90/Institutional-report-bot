The bill comes due 
For years, governments behaved as if debt were tomorrow’s problem, but this year’s inflationary 
energy shock seems to have sped up tomorrow’s arrival. 
Global yields may have eased back a little over the past month, but the spotlight on public 
finances stays. That does not mean a new debt crisis is imminent. Governments generally borrow 
for long periods, major economies retain market access, and Europe has stronger backstops than 
it did in 2010. But the era in which every shock could be placed on the public balance sheet at 
negligible cost is over. Debt accumulated in the years of “free money” is gradually being 
refinanced at today’s rates. The bill comes slowly, but it does come. 
France is where markets are reading it most carefully. Its debt ratio is not uniquely high, but its 
direction is uncomfortable: weak growth (confirmed again last week), a large primary deficit and a 
political system struggling to produce credible consolidation. Italy carries more debt, but it runs a 
primary surplus and currently enjoys greater political stability. That captures the essence of debt 
sustainability. It is not simply about how much you owe. It is about interest rates versus growth, 
the primary balance and, crucially, whether investors believe politics can deliver adjustment. 
And, as we argue here, politics is usually the trigger. Fiscal numbers deteriorate gradually; 
confidence can disappear quickly. Higher risk premia raise interest costs, forcing tougher fiscal 
choices that can weaken growth and intensify political resistance. Markets can turn manageable 
arithmetic into a reflexive problem. France is not there yet, but investors are less willing to wait 
indefinitely for politicians to choose between higher taxes, lower spending and broken promises. 
And while Paris burns, people also take to the streets in many other European cities, to protest a 
variety of things ranging from high cost of living and housing affordability to lack of proper 
education and high defence spending. Nor is this French or even European exceptionalism. The 
US combines reserve-currency privilege with uncomfortable deficits – and higher gasoline prices 
may well lead the political landslide this week. Japan is discovering that monetary normalisation 
matters when debt approaches 200% of GDP. Brazil faces punishing real rates. In short, this is a 
global beauty contest with many unattractive candidates. 
Energy is accelerating the reckoning. Renewed escalation around the Persian Gulf threatens oil, 
refined products and LNG flows. Our energy forecasts point to higher prices and hence 
inflationary pressure across economies, complicating central banks’ job and keeping upward 
pressure on yields. Meanwhile, higher energy bills squeeze households and corporate margins. 
Stagflation never makes fiscal arithmetic easier. 
And another voracious borrower has entered the room: AI. The investment boom requires data 
centres, chips, grids, cooling systems and vast amounts of power, much of it financed before 
uncertain future revenues arrive. Recent stress at several neoclouds and smaller hyper scalers, 
resulting in higher credit risk premia and the pulling of an IPO, suggests markets are not 
questioning demand for computing power so much as the leverage used to supply it. There may 
be a shortage of chips, but there certainly is no shortage of demand for capital. 
That creates a global tug of war. AI, defence and infrastructure investment support activity, 
including in the US and Europe. But they also compete with governments for savings, energy and 
physical resources, potentially keeping long-term yields higher. At the same time, an energy shock 
that undermines real incomes and margins could eventually do the opposite by weakening 
growth. Increasingly, the price of capital sits at the intersection of all these forces. 
The danger, then, is less a sudden replay of the euro crisis than a slow squeeze in which interest 
payments crowd out investment, defence and social spending. And that is where bond markets 
meet ballot boxes. Governments can postpone choices, obscure them or blame external shocks. 
They cannot repeal arithmetic. The bond vigilantes may not yet be kicking down the door. But 
they have found the address.

Fixed Income 
Higher inflation, not necessarily more rate hikes? 
Inflation risks have not lessened. The wars in the Middle East and Ukraine continue to push energy 
prices higher, as our energy strategists outline elsewhere in this monthly outlook. However, 
there’s still no evidence that energy inflation is turning into something more broad-based and 
persistent.  
Energy inflation can fluctuate sharply, and that can have a big impact on headline inflation rates. 
However, underlying inflation dynamics generally change to a much smaller extent. So, the 
inflation peak will be higher than we –and central banks– had foreseen for next year, but that also 
amplifies the base effects that will drive disinflationary pressures in 2028. 
Indeed, ECB President Lagarde acknowledged that energy prices now exceed the central banks’ 
baseline forecasts, but she added that policymakers do not see greater risk of second-round 
effects than they did in September. This suggests that policymakers expect that the energy prices 
shock will increasingly impact growth. Lagarde pointed to the deteriorating consumer sentiment 
as one example. 
The ECB also expects that tighter financial conditions will weigh on the growth outlook. The Bank 
Lending Survey indicated that credit standards had tightened in the third quarter, and Lagarde 
noted that the rise in long-term interest rates had resulted in a decline in loan demand amongst 
corporates and households. 
Let there be no mistake, price stability remains central bankers’ key concern, and they will act if 
necessary. But inflation is no longer the only risk they must worry about. High energy costs and 
high interest rates are increasingly undermining the growth outlook, and high rates are 
increasingly feeding concerns about debt sustainability – especially in highly indebted countries. 
A key question is whether an economic slowdown is sufficient to lessen inflationary pressures. 
Less energy demand does not take the sting out of the price increases that are dictated by risks 
and disruptions to energy supplies, but weaker growth may limit the indirect and second-round 
effects of energy prices on the broader inflation rate.  
However, if weaker growth doesn’t lessen inflation, monetary policy will not be very effective 
either – at least not without causing much more economic pain. Policymakers prefer to avoid that, 
unless underlying inflation dynamics indicate that a stronger response is necessary to avoid that 
high inflation becomes entrenched. 
Debt sustainability is becoming a more pressing concern 
European rates have declined substantially from the end-September peaks, but not before the 
global grind higher could spark investor concerns about debt sustainability. In fact, the parallel 
flattening of the EUR curve and widening of Eurozone spreads suggest that those fiscal concerns 
are a key factor behind the decline in long-term rates through most of last month. 
Unsurprisingly, the focus is on countries with high debt ratios – especially if that coincides with 
political uncertainty. The French debt ratio is not exceptional: there are countries with higher 
debt/GDP ratios within the Eurozone and outside of it. But that is compounded by a high primary 
deficit and political gridlock as candidates for the 2027 presidential race start their campaigns. 
This is not an immediate debt crisis. France has market access and the higher interest rates add to 
France’s debt servicing only gradually as maturing debt needs to be refinanced. However, if the 
deficit is not reined in, interest payments could eventually crowd out other spending. 
The required fiscal effort is not impossible, but markets are questioning France’s political will to 
implement the necessary budget cuts. If France fails to take corrective steps, further loss of fiscal

Moreover, if investors were to lose all confidence in fiscal policy, long-term problems can quickly

Investors’ risk aversion did affect higher beta sovereigns, but contagion to other countries has 
been limited so far. And Europe has created institutions to arrest any spillovers before they can 
truly materialise. The ECB can use the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI) to counteract 
unwarranted, disorderly market dynamics. However, the odds that the instrument will be activated 
are low.  
Crucially, if the ECB were to use the TPI, we argue that this will be to shield other countries’ debt 
markets from unwarranted fallout of the French political impasse. The central bank may be less 
willing to support French yields until the country adopts a sound fiscal plan. If the ECB were to 
step in, they would erode politicians’ incentives to agree on a budget – not only for the coming 
year: it would also set a precedent for future budgets and other countries.  
So, fiscal problems must be resolved by politicians, not the central bank. Further spread widening 
may be necessary to force a budget compromise. Through strikes and protests, the French 
population has undermined efforts to cut spending on social security. In a way, they have 
managed to position themselves at the top of the pecking order. Spread widening is bond 
investors’ only way to reassert their status as senior creditor.   
The problem is currently most pressing in France, but rising interest rates require many 
governments to cut their primary deficits. So, the focus on debt sustainability reduces 
governments’ ability to support economic growth – at least in the near term.  
Since the need for budget cuts is inversely related to the level of interest rates, the ECB’s hiking 
cycle will arguably have more impact on the real economy than the previous cycle did. That could 
apply to the economy more broadly: to the extent that debt sustainability concerns are adding to 
economic uncertainty and risk aversion in markets, any additional rate hikes may lead to stronger 
tightening of financial conditions.  
EUR rates at a turning point? 
Major geopolitical developments cannot be ruled out after the US midterm elections, but a larger 
energy price shock is not a sufficient condition to warrant significantly stronger policy tightening. 
A further tightening of financial conditions and the potential impact on economic activity may 
dampen the need for rate hikes. So, there is still a big hurdle for the ECB to hike as far as money 
market pricing for 2027 implies. However, as long as inflation risks remain to the upside, the ECB 
will not push back against these expectations with strong conviction.  
These upside forces are partly offset by concerns about French debt sustainability. We believe that 
these concerns are unlikely to lessen before the French election cycle has concluded. Crucially, 
there should be a positive correlation between higher yields and fiscal concerns. That implies that 
higher yields would lead to safe haven flows, which, in turn, dampen the effect of higher yields.  
We believe that this limits how fast and how far rates can rise from here. We forecast rates to be 
broadly stable around current levels through the end of the year, after which long-term rates can 
gradually come down a bit if it turns out that the ECB has concluded its hiking cycle. However, 
risks remain to the upside as long as the inflationary backdrop isn’t visibly improving.

Foreign Exchange 
EUR/USD: politics more than economics?   
The sharp acceleration in the widening of the 10 year OAT – Bund yield spread through 
September and into early October corresponded with a drop in the value of the EUR. Rather than 
move out of OATs into other Eurozone assets, contagion fears appeared to prompt investors to 
move away from the single currency. While Moody’s decision on October 23 to keep France’s 
credit rating unchanged brought some relief to OATs, the EUR failed to see much respite. A drop 
in October ECB rate hike expectations certainly weighed on the single currency last month. In our 
view, however, it is not just the change in rate hike expectations that has prevented the EUR from 
recovering ground. Political concerns have been rising to the fore. In our view, European political 
risks are likely to keep the single currency on the defensive going forward.   
There may be some near-term respite for the EUR if a French budget is in place by the end of the 
year. That said, uncertainties connected with Europe’s move towards the far right could increase 
pressure on the EUR next year. Le Pen is the front runner in next spring’s French Presidential 
election. For the past decade or so, her National Rally party has appeared more moderate in order 
to attract voters from a wider base. This has meant that policies such as Frexit have been 
abandoned. That said, Le Pen has talked about measures such as rejecting the primacy of EU law 
over migration policy and about a reduction in the country’s contribution to the EU’s budget. 
These uncertainties are compounded by the strengthening of the far-right in Germany. The AfD 
supports a significant renegotiation of EU treaties.  
Political uncertainties for Europe, fears over the impact of high energy prices and elevated 
concerns about Russian hybrid warfare all suggest a difficult outlook for the EUR. While the single 
currency is likely to see some short-term relief if a French budget is passed this year, we see 
limited scope for a sustained recovery and forecast a move to EUR/USD1.10 on a 3-month view.  
EUR/GBP – cleanest dirty shirt  
It has become second nature in GBP markets to worry about the UK’s high level of debt and the 
fiscal headroom (or lack of) that the Chancellor has to work with. The latest forecasts from the 
OBR suggest that the Chancellor has cut the fiscal headroom to just GBP15 bln during 2029/30 
from GBP23.6 bln in the March forecast. Chancellor Healey has defended the reduction in the 
headroom as a measure needed to absorb some of the impact of the Iran war and a buffer to be 
used when needed. A reduction in the fiscal headroom is not good news, but crucially this is a 
self-imposed discipline. Compared with some other G7 countries, and most notably France, at 
least the UK has a budget consolidation plan in place.  
There are many concerns about whether the UK’s medium-term budget deficit reduction plan is 
achievable. The tax burden has already risen, high inflation could increase the spending demands 
of some government departments, and the experience of former PM Starmer suggests that 
welfare reform will be tough for the Labour government particularly given the ageing population. 
At the same time Healey has announced that defence spending will rise to 3% of GDP by 2030. 
That said, the recent protests in France illustrate that the UK is better positioned than some of its 
peers. The IMF recently described the UK as a country where action has been taken ‘decisively’ to 
reduce the debt and the deficit. The UK’s fiscal position is far from enviable, but at least it has an 
improving primary deficit which should provide some reassurance for GBP markets.  
In RaboResearch’s view, the market continues to price in too much BoE policy tightening. Our 
central view is that following a November rate hike that the Bank is unlikely to move more than 
once more this cycle. If the market unwinds rate hike speculation, GBP is at risk of being 
undermined into 2027. That said, due to the headwinds facing the EUR, we forecast EUR/GBP at 
0.84 on a 3-month view.

JPY: turning a corner? 
The JPY is the second best performing G10 currency in the half year to date after the NOK. 
Granted, significant amounts of FX intervention in favour of the JPY have been a notable factor in 
supporting it this year. Even so, this performance raises the question as to whether the JPY has 
turned a corner. Indeed, last month the question of whether the JPY benefitted from some safe 
haven inflows, for the first time in a while, was debated. 
Since the BoJ ended its QQE policy in 2024, its balance sheet has shrunk by almost 18%. In effect 
this has stripped JGB prices of their insulation from market forces. Higher inflation in Japan and 
fears about the reflationary biases of PM Takaichi have pushed JGB yields higher. Worries about 
Japan’s high debt/GDP ratio appeared to focus the broader market’s attention on Japan’s fiscal 
issues. However, not all Japan’s debt metrics are poor. The end of QQE may have led to a modest 
increase in the percentage of Japanese government bonds owned by foreign investors. However, 
any change will have been small. Compared with other G7 countries, a high percentage of 
Japanese government debt is owned by domestic investors. Since domestic bond investors 
appear to be less reactive to ‘bad’ fiscal news, this implies that the JPY should be afforded 
protection from Japan’s fiscal concerns. In addition, compared with other G7 nations, projections 
for the country’s primary deficit are better than average. 
In view of the increased costs of debt maintenance, budget processes are likely to remain in the 
spotlight. That said, if Japan’s economy can continue to recover and if the primary budget balance 
remains in check, the market’s fears about Japan’s fiscal situation should be contained. Indeed, 
historically the high proportion of JGBs owned domestically was a factor that encouraged safe 
haven inflows into the JPY. These factors, combined with the likelihood of another 25-bps rate 
hike from the BoJ at the December policy meeting suggests a strong potential for the JPY to hold 
its own in 2027 vs. other G10 currencies. We retain a 3-month forecast of USD/JPY155.00.

Energy Markets 
Crude Oil & Refined Products 
Oil markets are shifting from expectations of a short-lived shock towards prolonged disruption. 
After trading mostly between $99 and $110/bbl since 10 September, December Brent closed at 
$116.72/bbl on 29 October, its highest close since 29 April. The full-year 2027 swap has risen to 
$92.41/bbl, signalling expectations that tight supply, elevated transport costs and geopolitical risk 
will persist well into next year. 
Improving physical flows have not brought lower prices. TankerTrackers estimates Middle East 
crude exports averaged 17.8 mb/d over the 28 days ending 19 October, against 19.06 mb/d 
before the war; the seven-day average reached 19.08 mb/d. Saudi exports through Yanbu account 
for approximately 3–4 mb/d. 
The “Hormuz Shuttle” helps sustain exports: tankers transit along Oman’s coast under US air 
cover, with identification systems switched off, then transfer cargo offshore. However, this 
workaround remains costly, vulnerable and insufficiently permanent to represent normalisation. 
UK Maritime Trade Operations reported 32 attacks in October, versus 16 throughout September, 
with underreporting suggesting greater exposure. 
Persian Gulf–China tanker rates have reached $1.4 million per day, compared with $40,000 
normally, adding approximately $13.5/bbl to crude costs. Physical Brent and Shanghai oil 
consequently command premiums of $10–25/bbl over futures. Insurance and bunker fuel 
compound the burden, with total supply-chain costs estimated by the IEA at up to $37/bbl. The 
issue is not simply whether oil flows, but the cost and fragility of delivery. 
Our base case remains renewed Middle East escalation after the US midterm elections on 3 
November. We assume the US, Israel and a broader Arab and European coalition are likely to 
attack Iran and the Houthis while defending Saudi Arabia. Strikes could target nuclear facilities, 
military assets and governing capacity, potentially damaging critical infrastructure. Fighting could 
continue at varying intensity until end-Q1. 
Retaliation could threaten Gulf energy facilities, ports, airports, desalination plants, 
communications infrastructure and potentially Egypt and the Suez Canal. Vulnerable energy assets 
include Fujairah and the ADCOP bypass pipeline, Qatar’s Ras Laffan LNG plant, Saudi Arabia’s 
East–West Pipeline and Ras Tanura terminal. Wider regional conflict or an Iranian land incursion 
would amplify supply risks. 
Improved crude flows have not stopped inventory depletion. Global oil stocks are drawing by 
approximately 2–2.5 mb/d, while refined products face a further approximately 4 mb/d deficit. 
Nearly 12% of global refining capacity is offline across the two wars, making diesel and marine 
fuel oils the principal concerns. Both could reach new inventory lows over the next ten weeks. 
The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve holds 283 million barrels, its lowest level since 1982, following 
authorisation of another 40-million-barrel release. China drew approximately 55 million barrels 
from storage in September as imports fell to 7.6 mb/d. Each withdrawal reduces the buffer 
available against further disruption. Inventories are a savings account, not a permanent supply 
solution.  
We raised our 2027 refined-product forecasts. RBOB–WTI cracks are expected to average 
$38.75/bbl, and HO–WTI cracks $95/bbl. NY Harbor ULSD rises to $4.64/gal and RBOB to 
$3.30/gal. Brent–Gasoil cracks average $76/bbl, implying European ICE gasoil at $1,385/mt. 
We raise Brent to $120/bbl in both Q1 and Q2 2027, reflecting likely infrastructure attacks, 
elevated shipping and insurance costs, and tanker disruption lasting at least through the first half.

Natural Gas and European Power 
European and Asian gas prices face renewed upside pressure in November and December. Our 
outlook assumes an imminent escalation in US military action against Iran, with retaliatory attacks 
threatening already-fragile Gulf LNG exports and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Recent 
improvements in cargo movements should therefore not be mistaken for a durable normalisation 
of supply. Further disruption to LNG infrastructure, loadings or tanker passage could reduce 
available volumes, while higher freight and insurance costs would raise the delivered cost of 
surviving cargoes. For the remainder of 2026, geopolitical risk and the reliability of deliveries will 
matter more than the prospect of abundant future supply. 
Europe enters this period with gas storage below recent seasonal norms, leaving a smaller buffer 
against interrupted LNG deliveries or a cold spell. This does not imply that Europe is about to run 
out of gas, but it increases the price sensitivity to any deterioration in supply as heating demand 
rises. Europe must continue attracting flexible Atlantic Basin cargoes, while Asian buyers face 
more direct exposure to curtailed Qatari exports. Renewed disruption would intensify competition 
for those cargoes, supporting both TTF and JKM and increasing the risk of sharp price spikes. 
Temporary price declines following successful shipments are unlikely to prove lasting if the next 
escalation again interrupts flows. 
Reflecting these risks, our revised Q4 2026 forecasts stand at €79/MWh for TTF and 
$25.40/MMBtu for JKM. These are quarterly averages rather than ceilings, as a wider conflict or 
additional damage to LNG infrastructure could push prices materially higher during November 
and December. A credible de-escalation could trigger a rapid retreat in the security-of-supply 
premium, potentially before physical deliveries recover. Nevertheless, sustained relief requires 
dependable loadings and passage, not simply a short-lived improvement in sentiment. 
Higher gas costs also underpin an elevated European power-price outlook through year-end. As 
the winter generation mix becomes more dependent on dispatchable supply, expensive gas feeds 
into electricity prices when gas-fired plants set the marginal price. Renewable generation can 
provide temporary relief, but periods of low wind output leave power markets more exposed to 
the gas rally. The November–December outlook is therefore one of high prices and pronounced 
volatility, with the greatest upside risk when weaker renewable output coincides with stronger 
heating demand and renewed LNG disruption. 
The coming LNG supply wave does not resolve this year-end tightness. Around 40 million tonnes 
per annum of non-Qatari export capacity could become available during 2027, followed by 
roughly another 50 million tonnes in 2028, progressively reducing dependence on Gulf supply. 
However, capacity additions are not the same as immediately available cargoes, and their broader 
price impact should emerge from 2027 onwards as projects ramp up. Our medium-term view of 
lower gas prices remains intact, but it offers little protection against a cargo lost in November or 
December. The near-term outlook remains tighter and potentially more expensive first, with 
meaningful supply-led relief later.

Eurozone 
Stumbling on as fiscal fault lines widen 
Growing 0.4% q/q in the third quarter, the eurozone economy again proved more resilient than 
expected. But the headline flatters the underlying picture with Ireland’s 5.5% increase accounting 
for almost half of that. Among the larger member states, divergence remained considerable: Spain 
and the Netherlands grew by 0.6% q/q, Germany eked out 0.2%, Italy stagnated and France 
contracted by 0.1%. So, while the eurozone continues to defy the headwinds, growth is hardly 
broad-based. Q3 was better than expected, but several of the forces supporting activity are either 
temporary or concentrated in a narrow range of sectors. We have therefore raised our 2026 
growth estimate to 1.0%, from 0.9%, but we cut our 2027 forecast to 1.1%, from 1.2%.  
Defence and infrastructure spending is cushioning weak private demand, particularly in Germany, 
while the global AI investment boom is benefiting European producers of semiconductor 
machinery, electrical equipment and other specialised industrial inputs. Information and 
communication services have also performed well. But for now, AI, defence, and infrastructure 
appear to be raising the floor under activity rather than lifting the economy onto a stronger trend. 
Meanwhile, corporate margins are under pressure, confidence remains subdued, and hiring is 
losing momentum. The Q3 data therefore confirm that the eurozone can absorb a setback, but 
they do not establish that the next one will be equally easy to digest.

The energy shock is rising – again 
The energy shock has regained impetus after the summer. Europe is better prepared than in 2022: 
firms have adjusted sourcing and hedging, and household balance sheets are generally stronger. 
But these buffers delay the impact rather than eliminate it. Higher oil and gas prices, together with 
more expensive transport, insurance and logistics, will increasingly feed into consumer prices, 
business costs, and real incomes. Our updated energy assumptions add around 0.4 percentage 
points to our 2027 inflation forecast, which we have raised to 3.9%. We expect inflation to fall 
back to 1.8% in 2028 as energy prices ease and base effects reverse.  
The shock remains smaller than in 2021-2023, and we expect significantly less passthrough into 
wages and core inflation this time, reflecting weaker demand, tighter financial conditions and a 
cooler labour market. Even so, the timing is uncomfortable. Higher energy bills will reduce real 
wage growth, while firms have less room to absorb cost increases in already-squeezed margins.

with higher long-term yields, tighter bank lending conditions and rising sovereign borrowing

How do you say “debt sustainability” in French?  
France has become the clearest illustration of the eurozone’s changing fiscal landscape. Its 
problem is not simply high debt, but the combination of a sizeable primary deficit, weak growth 
and political gridlock. Italy has a higher debt ratio, but its primary surplus and greater political 
stability currently give markets more comfort, while Spain benefits from stronger growth. 
Germany faces no immediate sustainability problem, but its shift towards much higher defence 
and infrastructure borrowing could lift borrowing costs across the eurozone.  
These differences underline why debt 
sustainability is purely a matter of debt ratios. 
Investors will increasingly focus on the 
differential between interest costs and growth, 
primary balances and refinancing needs, but 
also on whether governments can credibly 
adjust when required. That is where economics 
and politics start to interact. Higher yields 
worsen the fiscal arithmetic and may force 
consolidation, which in turn could weaken 
growth and further undermine debt dynamics.  
Europe’s institutional safety nets (ECB, ESM) are 
much stronger than during the sovereign debt 
crisis. A slow deterioration, in which rising 
interest costs gradually crowd out investment, 
defence and social spending, looks considerably more plausible than a new debt crisis. Still, this 
comes at an awkward political moment. Electoral uncertainty in Spain, France’s 2027 presidential 
election, and growing political fragmentation in Germany are complicating policymaking just as 
Europe tries to deliver an ambitious strategic-autonomy agenda. It is thus no surprise that the EU 
aims to agree on a 2028-2034 budget before year-end, and to fill several high-ranking vacancies 
at the ECB to safeguard continuity beyond the French elections. But this doesn’t ensure stability. 
The eurozone may continue to defy the pessimists, but the burden of proof is shifting. In the 
coming months, expect investors to scrutinise not just growth, inflation and fiscal fundamentals, 
but increasingly the ability of governments to make credible choices under pressure.

The Netherlands 
Dutch economic growth was 0.6% in 2026Q3 
Last week, Statistics Netherlands released its first GDP estimate for 2026Q3, showing growth of 
0.6% q/q. This follows relatively strong growth of 0.3% in 2026Q1 and 0.7% in Q2, highlighting the 
Dutch economy’s resilience to the global shocks, including geopolitical shocks, it has faced since 
early 2020. 
Net trade was the largest contributor to growth in 2026Q3, alongside inventory building (see 
figure 1). Inventory building usually correlates strongly with imports, suggesting that underlying 
net trade was even stronger than the official figures indicate. Household consumption and 
government expenditure also contributed substantially to the q/q GDP growth. Housing 
investment was the only drag, while business investment rose only slightly. 
The 0.6% q/q growth exceeded our expectation of around 0.2. We had anticipated more modest 
growth given potential growth of about 0.3-0.4% per quarter, ongoing geopolitical unrest and the 
already strong growth in previous quarters. This positive surprise will lead us to raise our forecasts 
for both 2026 and 2027. We now expect annual growth of 1.8% in 2026 (previously 1.5%) and 
1.3% in 2027 (previously 1.1%). 
The Netherlands has outperformed many other large European economies (see figure 2). Since 
the start of global unrest in 2020Q1, the Dutch economy has outgrown the Euro Area overall, 
particularly France and Germany. Only Spain has grown faster, but its labour market offered 
substantial potential for growth. By contrast, Dutch unemployment was already low and has 
remained relatively stable. 
Manufacturing main driver of higher production 
Manufacturing was the main driver of production growth in the third quarter. Value added in this 
sector rose by 2.7%, contributing 0.3 percentage points to overall GDP growth, following an 
already substantial contribution in 2026Q2. According to Statistics Netherlands, Machinery 
Manufacturing accounted for a large share of the sector’s strong q/q growth. 
This is unsurprising, as ASML and its ecosystem appear to be thriving as never before. It also helps 
explain net trade’s strong growth contribution: manufacturing, particularly machinery 
manufacturing, is highly export oriented. 
IT, Agriculture, Financial Services and Business Services also grew substantially, while Trade, 
Transportation and Hospitality, and Construction performed relatively weakly.

United Kingdom 
Reset 2.0 
The EU-UK summit on 20 November should finally put more flesh on the bones of the reset. We 
expect to see a deal on agri-food trade, emissions trading, youth mobility and energy 
cooperation. But the more interesting question is what comes next. Prime Minister Andy Burnham 
has argued that the status quo is “not good enough”, and with that he opened the door to a 
broader discussion about the UK’s long-term relationship with Europe. 
We continue to see the current agreements as a minimum viable reset, which reduces frictions in 
selected sectors. An SPS agreement would pull Britain back into the EU's regulatory orbit for food 
and agriculture, while closer cooperation on emissions trading and electricity would deepen 
integration in energy markets. However, looking forward, the UK’s desire to be included in “Made 
in Europe” increasingly links market access to industrial policy, economic security and supply-
chain resilience. Adding this all up, we have the UK gradually reintegrating into EU policy 
frameworks. 
That creates geopolitical trade-offs that Westminster has hardly begun to debate. The closer the 
UK moves towards Europe's increasingly protectionist industrial strategy, the greater the likely 
pressure to align with its tougher approach towards China, including on EVs, subsidies, 
procurement and investment screening. That will invite Chinese retaliation. The US presents a 
different problem. A UK anchored more firmly in European supply chains may actually further US 
objectives around China and economic security, but not necessarily on US terms. It would also 
narrow the UK’s ability to offer Washington regulatory concessions in any future trade 
negotiation, although little is expected on that front. 
Beyond the 20 November Reset, we therefore expect the debate to shift quickly towards a more 
structured EU-UK relationship, potentially encompassing defence, energy, technology, finance and 
economic security. As we discuss here, we expect all options to be on the table. But the biggest 
constraint may ultimately be domestic politics. Brussels will want reciprocity and, crucially, 
evidence that any new settlement can survive a change of government. The more ambitious 
options may therefore require a fresh electoral mandate, with Prime Minister Burnham vocally and 
actively making the case for a new EU-UK settlement.  
Budget 
The Autumn Budget was uneventful. The Chancellor largely coloured within the lines, with 
measures that were too small to meaningfully change the economic outlook. The fiscal headroom 
of around £15bn was broadly in line with market expectations. This avoided an adverse reaction in 
the gilt market. Under the bonnet, however, the public finances look less comfortable. Higher 
interest rates are pushing up debt servicing costs by about £14bn, and rather than offsetting this 
through higher taxes or lower spending, the government decided to borrow more now and make 
the fiscal offset after the next general election. While this is within the lines of the fiscal 
framework, we don’t think it is very credible. In that sense, the Budget kicks the harder choices 
down the road and effectively bets that the energy and interest rate shock will fade.  
For the Bank of England, meanwhile, the immediate implications are limited. Fiscal support only 
slightly cushions households from higher energy costs, while the energy measures have a small 
impact on the inflation profile. We think this restraint is by design: a large fiscal intervention could 
have added to inflationary pressures, created market unrest, and would have forced the MPC to 
respond more aggressively. By keeping the fiscal package modest, the Chancellor gives the Bank 
room to raise rates as gradually as possible.

United States 
Midterms and the economy 
Affordability remains a key issue in the US elections. President Biden’s popularity and Harris’s 
presidential election chances were badly hurt by years of excessive inflation. At the last FOMC 
meeting, Chair Warsh stressed that the Fed has been overshooting its 2% inflation target for close 
to 6 years. When President Trump took office in January 2025, CPI inflation was 3.0%. The most 
recent reading (September 2026) was 3.7%. At the same time, unemployment has risen from 4.0 
to 4.2%. Therefore the misery index – which adds the inflation and unemployment rates – has 
risen from 7.0% to 7.9%. In the past we have used the misery index to illustrate the erosion of 
popular support for the government, from the United States to the Middle East. This time does 
not seem to be different, if we look at the opinion polls in the US.  
What makes things worse this time around, is the cumulative nature of six years of excessive 
inflation. Those who do their own shopping cannot help but notice the enormous price increase 
between then and now. Therefore, it is not surprising that President Trump’s approval rating has 
been falling and the Republicans are not doing well in the polls. Although the largest part of the 
cumulative price increase took place under President Biden’s watch, last year’s tariffs and this 
year’s war have added two inflationary impulses to the economy, delaying the return of inflation 
to 2%. What’s more, the second inflationary shock has been particularly visible in gasoline prices, 
which rose from $3.23 per gallon in the fourth week of January 2025 to $4.41 last week, an 
increase of almost 37%.

Mexico 
Losing appeal? 
CPI inflation in Mexico has been relatively moderate compared with trends elsewhere, largely 
reflecting the IEPS and its role in suppressing distillate prices. Headline CPI inflation registered 
3.4% y/y and 0.42% m/m in May. The year-over-year measure has nevertheless risen for the past 
two months. At 3.4%, it remains meaningfully above Banxico’s 3% target, although within the 
Bank’s tolerance band. The monthly increase was the largest since March. 
But the underlying picture is more nuanced. Core CPI inflation remains elevated at 3.75% y/y, 
above its 2024 lows, but has been trending lower since March, suggesting limited evidence of 
energy-price pressures feeding through to core inflation. Services inflation has remained 
persistent since 2022 but is steadily declining. It registered 4.27% y/y in September, its lowest 
level since early 2022. Goods inflation, meanwhile, remains above its 2024 lows, but has declined 
through most of 2026, registering a comparatively subdued 3.21%.  
However, food is back in focus. Agricultural prices have been highly volatile: after falling in June, 
they rose for three consecutive months, with September increases of 1% m/m and 2.8% y/y. Much 
of the inflation was concentrated in tomatoes (30% m/m) and onions (23% m/m), due to 
environmental factors. While Banxico can do little to ease the price of produce, they can try to 
ease the strain on Mexican consumers’ wallets by further containing inflationary pressures in other 
components.

Brazil 
Fiscal policy: election is over, and fiscal uncertainties abound 
We see gross public debt at 83.4% of GDP in 2026 and 87.2% in 2027. President Lula was re-
elected for a fourth term with 51.8% of valid votes, but will start his new administration facing a 
more conservative Congress and significant fiscal challenges. Campaign pledges still need to be 
incorporated into the 2027 budget, further limiting discretionary spending and leaving little room 
for expenditure-based consolidation. A more fragmented Congress could not necessarily limit the 
scope for expenditures expansion, and may also constrain the approval of structural reforms, 
suggesting that fiscal consolidation will remain insufficient to stabilize public debt. Regarding the 
latest fiscal data, fiscal accounts did improve in September, with the central government recording 
a BRL 4.8 billion primary surplus, bringing the 12-month deficit to BRL 51.6 billion, or 0.4% of 
GDP. However, the result was largely supported by BRL 12.8 billion in extraordinary revenues from 
canceled court-ordered debt payments. Net revenues increased 13.6% y/y in real terms, also 
supported by higher oil-related revenues, while expenditure rose 2.4% y/y amid continued 
pressure from mandatory spending. On tax reform, the Federal Court of Accounts proposed an 
11.3% reference rate for the new federal CBS tax from 2027, above previous estimates, partly 
reflecting tax exemptions and differentiated regimes introduced in the reform. The final rate will 
still be determined by Congress, with the proposal reinforcing the trade-off between preserving 
tax revenues and maintaining sectoral exemptions. 
Real activity: high-frequency data reinforce the loss of momentum 
We forecast GDP growth at 1.7% in 2026 (from 1.8% before) and at 1.5% in 2027 (from 
2.2% before). Recent data continue to point to a gradual loss of momentum in 26Q3, despite 
some improvement in the overall activity indicator. The IBC-Br increased 0.3% m/m in August, 
following a 0.3% decline in July, although its statistical carryover points to a contraction in 26Q3. 
Sectoral data were less favorable, with industrial production falling 0.6% m/m, while retail sales 
remained broadly stable and services declined 0.1% m/m. The weakness was particularly visible 
across more interest-sensitive segments, reinforcing evidence that restrictive monetary conditions 
and elevated household indebtedness continue to weigh on domestic demand. More importantly, 
in October, the Congress passed Lula’s shorter workweek bill, and various sectors now join white-
collar sectors with a workweek of 40 hours, taking effect already in 2027. Given decades-long 
productivity stagnation, we estimate this new labour legislation should cut 0.7p.p. off GDP growth 
in 2027. The labour market remains resilient, although signs of moderation are gradually 
becoming more visible. The unemployment rate remained close to historical lows at 5.2% in 
September, while real average income and the real wage bill increased 3.6% and 4.7% y/y, 
respectively. Resilient labour income should continue to provide some support to consumption in 
the near term, but weaker employment dynamics, restrictive financial conditions and fading fiscal 
and credit stimulus should contribute to a gradual slowdown in activity over the coming quarters. 
Inflation: inflation rebounds, while upside risks remain 
We revised our CPI inflation forecast to 5.2% (from 5.1%) by end-2026 and to 4.6% (from 
4.1%) by end-2027. Headline inflation accelerated in September, rising 0.82% m/m (August:  
-0.32% m/m), while the annual rate increased to 4.6% y/y from 4.2% previously. The higher 
reading was mainly driven by the reversal of the temporary Itaipu electricity bonus, while food, 
fuels and airfares also contributed positively. The composition was somewhat less favorable, as 
core measures increased and services inflation remained elevated, reinforcing our view that 
services disinflation should remain gradual amid resilient demand and labor market conditions.

increasingly affected by a combination of supply-side and policy-related cost pressures. Food

China 
Fifth plenum politics 
During the last days of October, Beijing hosted the 5th plenum which was different from a regular 
5th plenum. The reason is that Xi Jinping skipped the third plenum in 2023 and now the 5th 
plenum will discuss topics that are normally reserved for the 6th plenum. This means that this 
year’s 5th plenum will discuss issues related to Party governance. Moreover, this meeting could 
very well be the last meeting of the Party’s Central Committee before next year’s 21st Party’s 
Congress.  
The Party’s Central Committee has 376 seats, but a record number of members have been 
expelled from the committee due to various allegations ranging from corruption to nepotism. This 
left the committee with about 300 members while an additional 33 members were purged during 
the 5th plenum. Therefore only 279 members filled the seats, leaving the largest number of empty 
seats in Chinese history. Of the 33 members who have been ousted, 16 were from the military 
indicating a continuous overhaul of China’s military top brass and further centralization of power. 
During Xi Jinping’s third term, a considerable number of cabinet ministers, generals and other 
(provincial) officials have been sacked. Consequently, Xi Jinping will need to assign many new 
members for the Central Committee during next year’s Party congress. To make things more 
concrete: during Xi Jinping’s consecutive terms, the average tenure of provincial party leaders has 
declined significantly over time. It is also likely that no less than five of the seven members that 
currently make up the Politburo Standing Committee (the supreme organ of state power of the 
People's Republic of China) will (need to) retire if the Seven Up, Eight Down norm still holds.  
Finally, the Central Military Commission will also need to be replenished. At the start of the 20th 
National Party Congress there were seven members, now only Xi Jinping and Zhang Shengmin are 
left. Therefore, this 5th plenum might provide important clues about future leadership. The plenum 
also adopted a document that calls for greater discipline, stronger anti-corruption efforts and 
tighter internal controls. Still, it will likely take a while before more details regarding the 5th 
plenum will be made public.

Australia 
Further to go 
In our outlook for October, we said that the Reserve Bank of Australia now views interest rates as 
restrictive and was likely to be in “fine tuning mode”. We revised our forecasts later in the month 
after the Q3 CPI inflation report surprised to the upside and several RBA officials made overtly 
hawkish public comments. Our updated our forecast projected a 25bp increase to the Cash Rate 
Target in November, to 4.85%. The prospect of renewed hostilities in the Middle East, and the 
associated upward pressure on oil prices, poses further upside risks for the Cash Rate Target. 
This leaves the RBA in something of a bind. Board members will want to reassure markets that the 
RBA is a credible inflation fighter but avoid overtightening and risking recession. Economic activity 
slowed in the second quarter of the year, but not by as much as the RBA had expected. 
Meanwhile, unemployment (Figure 1) and the inflation rate have both risen faster than expected. 
Together, these outcomes suggest that monetary policy has not been as restrictive as the RBA 
previously thought and that the Phillips Curve has shifted upwards, at least in the short run. This 
implies a higher unemployment rate for any given level of inflation. RaboResearch estimates the 
non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU) to be 5%.
