In this monthly report, we outline our proprietary risk premium for 27 developed and  
23 emerging markets in the Americas, Europe, Africa, Asia, Australia and New Zealand.  
◼ Investor focus is shifting from the pace of earnings growth to the sustainability of that 
earnings growth. Markets with the strongest upgrades to earnings expectations, particularly 
Korea, Taiwan, and the US, have generally outperformed. However, the recent negative market 
reaction to in-line earnings results suggests that investor focus is shifting from the pace of earnings 
growth to the sustainability of that growth. 
◼ The key valuation question is: what growth is already in the price? By inverting the valuation 
framework and estimating the long-term growth rate implied by current market prices, we assess 
whether market expectations are realistic. Our analysis suggests that growth expectations remain 
demanding in Taiwan, Japan, and Mexico, while Korea, Brazil, Indonesia, and several peripheral 
euro-area markets embed less aggressive assumptions. 
◼ Cyclically elevated earnings distort valuation signals. Current 2027E earnings forecasts in 
several markets appear well above historical trend levels. Korea, Taiwan, and the US screen as being 
closest to cyclical earnings peaks, raising the risk that valuations may appear attractive primarily 
because current earnings expectations are unusually elevated. 
◼ Margin of safety- the devil is in the detail. Spain, Italy, and Korea appear best placed to absorb 
earnings downgrades while still delivering an 8% return. However, Korea’s required 2027E EPS 
remains around 3.5 times its latest reported earnings and significantly above trend earnings.

Editorial 
This year, market performance has been highly divergent across countries. A similar divergence 
is evident in the earnings outlook for the next two years, with some markets expected to deliver 
only modest earnings growth while others are projected to post exceptionally strong gains, 
despite already having experienced robust earnings growth in recent years. More recently, 
however, several of the markets with the strongest earnings expectations have reacted negatively 
to earnings releases, even when reported results were broadly in line with consensus forecasts. 
As a result, the market debate is increasingly shifting from the pace of earnings growth to the 
sustainability of that growth over a longer horizon. 
Given the long-duration nature of equities, near-term earnings have a meaningful impact on 
intrinsic value. However, valuations become significantly more sensitive when investors assume 
that elevated earnings growth can be sustained well into the future. In this publication, we 
examine the impact of exceptionally high earnings growth on valuations and assess the earnings 
growth expectations currently embedded in market prices.

highlights the shift in earnings trajectories relative to a year ago. Korea's current earnings 
trajectory is approximately five times the level expected a year ago, while Taiwan's is nearly 
double. In contrast, the US earnings trajectory is around 30% higher than it was a year earlier. 
Despite equity market gains over last year (14% for the US and ~86% for Korea and Taiwan), the 
cost of equity has increased significantly. In Korea, the cost of equity currently stands at around 
12%, up from approximately 5.5% a year ago and close to its highest level in recent history. 
Similar trends of higher cost of equity is visible in Taiwan and the US. This means that these equity 
markets offer significantly better value if current consensus estimates fructify.

The key question is what growth is in the price? When there is significant uncertainty around 
the future trajectory of growth, it is useful to invert the valuation framework to estimate the long-
term growth rate ("g") implied by current market prices. Rather than calculating fair value based 
on assumptions for the cost of equity and long-term growth, we assume that the market should 
deliver a specified level of return. Given the cross-country nature of our analysis, we assume an 
8% annualized return (in local currency as well as in USD) across all equity markets. Using a 
constant-growth model, we then derive the implied long-term growth rate required to generate 
this return. This allows us to assess whether the growth expectations embedded in current 
market prices are realistic. 
In the left-hand chart below, we calculate the implied long-term growth rate ("g") required to 
generate an 8% local-currency return and compare it with the current long-term growth 
assumption used in our model. Markets where the required implied "g" is below our current 
estimate of "g" possess a greater margin for earnings disappointment, as a lower level of long-
term growth is already sufficient to justify current valuations. Across emerging markets, most 
countries, with the exception of China and Taiwan, exhibit an implied "g" below our current 
estimate. Within developed markets, the peripheral euro-area countries and the UK appear to 
offer the greatest margin for earnings disappointment. 
When we examine the required long-term growth rate ("g") from a common-currency return 
perspective, Taiwan, Japan, and Mexico appear to have limited scope for earnings 
disappointment, as current valuations already embed relatively demanding growth 
expectations. In contrast, markets such as Korea, Brazil, and Indonesia require only a modest 
level of growth from current levels to deliver reasonable returns. Among developed markets, the 
peripheral euro-area countries appear better positioned on this measure, with valuations 
implying less demanding long-term growth assumptions.

What if the current earnings are extremely elevated? The purpose of the above analysis is to 
avoid making multiple assumptions about the longer-term growth trajectory. However, as the 
methodology is anchored to the current earnings base, it may understate or overstate implied 
growth depending on the market’s position in the earnings cycle.

the extent to which 2027E and 2028E consensus EPS forecasts could be reduced while allowing 
the cost of equity to remain at or above 8%. Our analysis suggests that the peripheral euro-area 
markets, particularly Spain and Italy, along with Korea in EM Asia, have the greatest capacity to 
absorb earnings downgrades while still delivering an 8% return. 
However, Korea’s required 2027E EPS remains approximately 3.5 times its latest reported EPS. As 
highlighted earlier, a sharp upward shift in the earnings trajectory can overstate the apparent 
margin of safety. The results should therefore be assessed in the context of the earnings cycle 
rather than viewed in isolation.

Risk premium methodology at a glance 
Our dividend discount model. We use a 4-stage dividend discount model to calculate the equity 
risk premium, which gives us the necessary flexibility to model a wide range of earnings 
trajectories across developed and emerging markets. We use the following methodology to 
forecast earnings: 
1) Period 1 (from Year 1 to Year 3): consensus median earnings forecast (IBES).  
2) Period 2 (from Year 4 to Year 6): earnings to grow at a rate equal to 10Y average earnings 
growth (k). 
3) Period 3 (from Year 7 to Year 15): earnings growth declines linearly from k to g (perpetual growth), 
g being the long-term economic growth equivalent to the 10-year average nominal GDP growth rate. 
4) Period 4 (From year 16 onwards): during this period, earnings grow perpetually at a rate g 
(calculated as above). 
The 4 stages of earnings growth  
Source: DataStream, SG Cross Asset Research/Global Asset Allocation 
Calculation of equity risk premium: The equity risk premium is simply the excess return offered 
by equities over the risk-free rate. The expected return from equities (internal rate of return) 
is the discounting rate at which the present value of all future dividends is equal to the current 
market level. The earnings growth as projected above is multiplied by the dividend payout ratio. 
To remove temporary fluctuations in the payout ratio, we use the 10-year average payout ratio.

Comparing IG and HY corporate credit with equity and 
government bonds 
 
Using our proprietary equity risk premium methodology, we compare the relative 
attractiveness of credit with equity. To compare equity with credit, we first calculate the 
internal rate of return for the equity market. The internal rate of return for equities is the 
discounting rate at which the present value of all future dividends is equal to the current market 
level. We use a 4-stage model to forecast future dividends. 
How to compare credit with equity? We deduct the yield offered by IG/HY corporate credit from 
the internal rate of return for the relevant equity market. We use the yield on the Barclays IG and 
HY index for IG and HY corporate credit in the US. Similarly, we use IBOXX Europe corporate bond 
index and Barclays Euro HY index for IG and HY corporate bonds in Europe. 
Excess return (equity) = Internal rate of return of equity – Bond yield of IG/HY index 
How do we decide if credit is rich or cheap relative to equity? We look at the current excess 
return offered by equities (internal rate of return on equity – bond yield) in historical context. If 
the excess return offered by equities is above the long-term average level, we rate equity as 
attractive and vice versa. 
Comparing IG and HY corporate credit with government bonds: We calculate the excess yield 
offered by IG and HY corporate credit relative to government bonds by deducting the government 
bond yield from the yield on relevant IG and HY corporate credit indices respectively.  
Credit risk premium = Bond yield of IG/HY index – 10Y government bond yield 
We look at excess return offered by IG/HY corporate credit in historical context to decide whether 
IG or HY bonds are rich or cheap relative to government bonds.

Backtesting our ERP model for country 
allocation 
Our methodology: For the purpose of this analysis, we chose to invest across equity and 10y 
government bonds of four developed market countries: the US, the UK, Germany and Japan. 
Broadly, there are three scenarios, the details of which are below: 
Country allocation as well as equity/bond allocation is static (portfolio 1 to portfolio 3):  
In this case, allocation ‘Wi’ to various markets is determined as below: 
Wi = 1/N, where N= # of applicable assets. 
For equally weighted equity-only (portfolio 1) and equally weighted bond-only portfolios 
(portfolio 2), N is equal to 4. For all the assets, equally weighted equity/bond portfolio (portfolio 
3), N is equal to 8. 
Country allocation is static but equity/bond allocation within each country is dynamic 
(portfolio 4): Each country (US, UK, Germany and Japan) is allocated 25% of the portfolio. We 
define a parameter which is the equity risk premium per unit of 1-month realised volatility (ERP/1-
month realised volatility). Using the above parameter, we define a synthetic indicator calculated 
as the percentile value of ERP/1-month realised volatility (since 1990). Allocation to equity and 
bonds within each country depends on the level of the synthetic indicator.  
Equityi = (ERP/1-M Realised Volatility) percentile Rank of ith country 
Bondi = (1-Equityi) 
Portfolio performance is improved further by making the country allocation dynamic as 
well. In this scenario, the capital of a particular country is allocated based on the ratio of its cost 
of capital and the sum of the cost of capital of all four countries. Allocation to equity and bonds 
within a country is done using the percentile rank of the synthetic indicator discussed in the 
previous section. 
Allocation to ith country = Cost of Capital of ith country / Sum of cost of capital
