No single playbook for Federal Reserve interest rate hikes 
Football season is underway, and coaches are once again turning to their familiar playbooks. The plays may not 
change much from season to season, but the game plan does, shaped by the opponent, field position, and 
circumstances of each game. We think investors face a similar challenge with Federal Reserve (Fed) rate-hike 
cycles. Although each cycle may feature familiar forces, relying on the same playbook every time can overlook the 
unique conditions shaping each cycle. 
The Fed has been one of many focal points for investors in 2026, and the path of monetary policy is likely to 
remain in focus heading into 2027. Although the basic mechanical actions of the Fed repeat cycle to cycle, the 
market outcomes do not. Through the last six completed hiking cycles, leadership rotated among developed 
markets DM ex-U.S. (DM), emerging markets (EM), and commodities, showing investors should not rely 
automatically on any single asset-class to outperform others. 
We believe the key for investors is to avoid getting caught up in the headlines or concentrating their portfolio on a 
rate-hike or rate-cut strategy. Instead, we favor approaching each cycle independently, much like a coach relies on 
players with different strengths to prepare a team for varying game conditions. For investors, that means utilizing 
multiple asset classes that can play different roles as market conditions change, helping position portfolios for a 
range of outcomes a new rate cycle may bring.  
Rates alone do not determine portfolio returns 
The Federal Open Market Committee sets the target range for the federal funds rate, which influences interest 
rates across the economy, notably the prime rate and the rates on credit cards and auto loans. Raising the target 
generally increases borrowing costs and slows demand, while lowering it makes borrowing cheaper and can 
stimulate spending and investment. 
Investors may be tempted to expect higher rates to weigh on bond returns while constraining upside for equities, 
and that may be the case, if the bond market does not believe that policy makers will raise rates enough to tame 
inflation. But the answer to that question and other conditions that shaped the last cycle may not define the next, 
and history suggests that the direction, timing and sizes of the rate increases collectively influence asset-class 
outcomes. 
Asset-class leadership varied considerably across the past six completed hiking cycles (see Table 1). EM led during 
three periods, commodities during two, and DM during one. U.S. stocks, meanwhile, generated positive returns in 
all six cycles, illustrating that Fed tightening has not necessarily coincided with negative equity returns. 
The range of outcomes within individual asset classes was also substantial: 
• 
U.S. stocks: approximately 3.0% to 20.1% 
• 
U.S. bonds: approximately -4.3% to 7.0% 
• 
Commodities: approximately -4.8% to 35.6% 
• 
DM ex. U.S.: approximately -4.2% to 18.3% 
• 
EM: approximately -18.8% to 65.8%

Although the direction of Fed policy was similar across each period, the direction and magnitude of the policy rate 
changes were not. Past cycles show that these determinants of Fed policy can be difficult to predict, making 
diversification based on an investor’s time horizon and risk tolerance important when preparing for a range of 
possible outcomes. 
Investment implications: Diversification is preparation, not prediction 
For investors, we believe another tightening cycle should not automatically trigger a retreat from risk assets or a 
major shift in portfolio strategy. History shows that rising rates can coexist with positive investment returns, 
although results have varied considerably across asset classes. That history reinforces the importance of investors 
being selective in their investment process. 
Within equities, continued economic and earnings growth should help offset the valuation pressure from higher 
rates. We favor U.S. large-cap equities, particularly high-quality companies with durable earnings and strong 
balance sheets that are well positioned for elevated borrowing costs and narrower market leadership. Abroad, we 
favor EM over DM, supported in part by their exposure to the AI investment theme. Within fixed income, we favor 
U.S. short-term fixed income, where attractive yields provide meaningful income without the added interest-rate 
risk of longer maturities. With persistent inflation likely to prompt additional Fed rate hikes, we see little reason to 
reach for longer dated maturities. 
Real assets provide another source of diversification, particularly as we expect inflation pressures to broaden into 
early 2027. We favor commodities, which can benefit from rising food and energy prices and help portfolios 
navigate an environment where inflation remains elevated. 
Ultimately, in our view, investors do not need to predict which asset class will outperform or exactly how markets 
will respond to higher rates. Just as a coach prepares for different situations rather than relying on a single play, 
investors can build flexibility into their portfolios to prepare for whatever the next tightening cycle brings.

Financials’ role in the AI buildout 
The AI investment boom is typically viewed as an Information Technology story but building the infrastructure to 
support AI requires tremendous amounts of capital. We believe that creates an important, and sometimes 
overlooked, role for the Financials sector. 
The numbers help illustrate the opportunity. J.P. Morgan estimates that capital spending by the five largest U.S. 
hyperscalers will reach $697 billion in 2026.1 Those investments extend well beyond computer chips to data 
centers, power infrastructure, and other physical assets needed to support growing demand for computing 
capacity.  
Financial companies are helping connect that investment with capital. On October 26, Bank of America, Citigroup, 
and Morgan Stanley joined Blackstone in announcing a $63 billion financing structure supporting AI computing 
capacity for Anthropic. The transaction combines bank financing with capital from Blackstone, illustrating how 
traditional banks and alternative asset managers can participate alongside technology companies in the AI 
buildout.2 
While we remain neutral on Financials, we believe this broadening investment cycle creates several potential 
opportunities. Large banks can benefit from lending, debt underwriting, and other capital-markets activity, while 
alternative asset managers can deploy private capital into data centers, infrastructure, and credit. Blackstone, for 
example, has identified AI-related infrastructure as a major investment theme and says demand for its QTS data-
center business has grown significantly. 
AI investment carries risks, including uncertainty around future demand, technology changes, and the enormous 
amount of capital being committed. Still, we believe the financing needs created by AI represent another way for 
Financials to participate in the investment cycle, even if they are not developing the technology themselves.

De minimis tax may be an issue for some munis 
Rising rates, record issuance, weaker reinvestment flows, and tax-loss harvesting pushed September’s Bloomberg 
Municipal Bond Index return to -4.36%, its worst month since 2008, according to Bloomberg. Investors may 
encounter more discount bonds, which often have lower coupons and longer duration than premium bonds, 
increasing their sensitivity to rising rates. 
Although municipal bond interest is generally federally tax-exempt, appreciation on bonds purchased at a market 
discount may be taxable under the de minimis rule. The rule applies to bonds purchased at a market discount 
exceeding a quarter point for each year remaining until maturity, potentially taxing the discount as ordinary 
income rather than capital gains. For a $100 par bond with 10 years remaining, the allowance is $2.50 (10 years × 
$0.25 per year), producing a threshold price of $97.50 ($100 − $2.50). Buying below $97.50 may generate ordinary 
income at maturity; selling earlier could produce both ordinary income and capital gains. 
Duration measures price sensitivity to interest-rate changes. Lower-coupon bonds generally have longer duration 
because more value is concentrated in the final payment. A five-year-duration bond would be expected to move 
about 5% opposite a 1% rate change. Lower-coupon bonds therefore have less price cushion and may fall below the 
de minimis threshold as rates rise. Investors that are concerned about the de minimis tax rule can look to swap into 
or add premium coupon bonds and should also consult their tax advisor.

Macro – Discretionary: Navigating shifting currents 
Macro - Discretionary hedge fund sub-strategies are managed by portfolio managers that make active decisions 
based on their views of the economy, interest rates, government policy, and geopolitical events. These strategies 
often have the potential to perform well during periods of market uncertainty when traditional stock and bond 
investments face challenges. 
Historically, Macro - Discretionary sub-strategies have provided valuable diversification benefits during volatile 
market environments. As shown in Chart 3, during several recent periods of heightened uncertainty and equity 
market declines, these strategies generally held up much better than global stocks, helping provide stability within 
a diversified portfolio. Importantly, Macro - Discretionary sub-strategies have not only performed well during 
difficult markets. Over the three years ending September 30, 2026, the sub-strategy generated an annualized 
return of 11.4%, despite a period in which equity markets also delivered strong returns.3

With uncertainty around economic policy, interest rates, and global events remaining elevated, investors may 
increasingly benefit from strategies that can diversify traditional equity and fixed-income exposure. Macro - 
Discretionary sub-strategies can potentially play that role by providing differentiated sources of return and 
improving portfolio diversification. That flexibility may be especially valuable following an unusually strong run for 
equities. If the S&P 500 Index remains on track and finishes 2026 with a double-digit total return, it would mark 
four consecutive calendar years of double-digit gains. By comparison, the market has achieved five straight years of 
double-digit returns only once since 1928, from 1995 through 1999. That historic streak ended in 2000 as the 
technology bubble began to burst. 
Given the significant influence that economic, policy, and geopolitical developments have had on markets in recent 
years, we continue to view Macro - Discretionary sub-strategies favorably. We believe they remain well positioned 
to capitalize on evolving market opportunities while potentially helping reduce overall portfolio volatility when 
paired with traditional stock investments.

Risk considerations 
Each asset class has its own risk and return characteristics.  The level of risk associated with a particular investment or asset class generally correlates with the level 
of return the investment or asset class might achieve. Stock markets, especially foreign markets, are volatile.  Stock values may fluctuate in response to general 
economic and market conditions, the prospects of individual companies, and industry sectors.  Foreign investing has additional risks including those associated with 
currency fluctuation, political and economic instability, and different accounting standards. These risks are heightened in emerging markets. Small- and mid-cap 
stocks are generally more volatile, subject to greater risks and are less liquid than large company stocks. Bonds are subject to market, interest rate, price, 
credit/default, liquidity, inflation and other risks. Prices tend to be inversely affected by changes in interest rates. High yield (junk) bonds have lower credit ratings 
and are subject to greater risk of default and greater principal risk. Municipal bonds offer interest payments exempt from federal taxes, and potentially state and 
local income taxes. Municipal bonds are subject to credit risk and potentially the Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT). Quality varies widely depending on the specific 
issuer. Municipal securities are also subject to legislative and regulatory risk which is the risk that a change in the tax code could affect the value of taxable or tax-
exempt interest income.  Although Treasuries are considered free from credit risk they are subject to other types of risks.  These risks include interest rate risk, 
which may cause the underlying value of the bond to fluctuate.  The commodities markets are considered speculative, carry substantial risks, and have experienced 
periods of extreme volatility.  Investing in a volatile and uncertain commodities market may cause a portfolio to rapidly increase or decrease in value which may 
result in greater share price volatility. Real estate has special risks including the possible illiquidity of underlying properties, credit risk, interest rate fluctuations and 
the impact of varied economic conditions. 
Sector investing can be more volatile than investments that are broadly diversified over numerous sectors of the economy and will increase a portfolio’s vulnerability 
to any single economic, political, or regulatory development affecting the sector. This can result in greater price volatility.   
Alternative investments, such as hedge funds, private equity/private debt and private real estate funds, are speculative and involve a high degree of risk that is 
appropriate only for those investors who have the financial sophistication and expertise to evaluate the merits and risks of an investment in a fund and for which the 
fund does not represent a complete investment program. They entail significant risks that can include losses due to leveraging or other speculative investment 
practices, lack of liquidity, volatility of returns, restrictions on transferring interests in a fund, potential lack of diversification, absence and/or delay of information 
regarding valuations and pricing, complex tax structures and delays in tax reporting, less regulation and higher fees than mutual funds.  Hedge fund, private equity, 
private debt and private real estate fund investing involves other material risks including capital loss and the loss of the entire amount invested. A fund's offering 
documents should be carefully reviewed prior to investing. 
Hedge fund strategies, such as Equity Hedge, Event Driven, Macro and Relative Value, may expose investors to the risks associated with the use of short selling, 
leverage, derivatives and arbitrage methodologies. Short sales involve leverage and theoretically unlimited loss potential since the market price of securities sold 
short may continuously increase. The use of leverage in a portfolio varies by strategy. Leverage can significantly increase return potential but create greater risk of 
loss.  Derivatives generally have implied leverage which can magnify volatility and may entail other risks such as market, interest rate, credit, counterparty and 
management risks.  Arbitrage strategies expose a fund to the risk that the anticipated arbitrage opportunities will not develop as anticipated, resulting in potentially 
reduced returns or losses to the fund.
