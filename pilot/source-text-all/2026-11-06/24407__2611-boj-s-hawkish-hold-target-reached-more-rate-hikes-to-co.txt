BOJ’s hawkish hold: target reached, more rate hikes to come 
Hawkish holds in Japan and the US keep inflation and rates firmly in focus 
By Naomi Fink, Chief Global Strategist and Chief Economist 
30 October 2026 
As expected, on 30 October the Bank of Japan (BOJ) kept its policy rate on hold at 1.25%. The 8-1 vote—with board 
member Hajime Takata dissenting by voting for an immediate hike—may be interpreted as modestly more hawkish 
compared with September’s 7-2 split, when dissenters voted against a rate hike.   
Hawkish dissent: The vote split is marginally more hawkish in that September’s dovish dissenters appeared to have 
accepted the current policy rate, while known hawk Takata argued in favour of a stepped-up pace of accommodation 
removal to bring rates more in line with “neutral”.  Looking ahead, the BOJ Outlook Report reaffirmed that it will “keep raising 
rates in response to the economy and prices”.  
Inflation consistent with target, wary of overshoot: Importantly, the BOJ confirmed in its October Outlook report that 
“underlying CPI inflation (is) at a level that is generally consistent” with the 2% price stability target. It warned that inflation 
expectations and corporate pricing behaviour could push inflation above target.   
Good conditions to normalise risk asset holdings: Another hawkish signal was the central bank’s announcement that 
it would double the pace of ETF disposals as of 2027, although the rate of reduction, at an annual 0.1% of stock market 
trading value (or roughly 860 billion yen on a book value basis), would still take 43 years to complete. The BOJ emphasised 
current buoyant market conditions as a driver of the change, and also offered a caveat that sales may be adjusted or 
suspended depending on future financial conditions. 
Forecasts: upgrade to 2027 inflation more significant than growth 
The BOJ raised its FY26 growth forecast modestly from 0.6% to 0.7%, while lowering its FY27 outlook from 0.8% to 0.7%. 
More notably, it upgraded its FY27 core CPI median forecast from 2.4% to 2.6%, reinforcing concerns about upside risks 
to its 2% inflation target.  
Both inflation drivers and risks appear inflationary: The BOJ explicitly identifies the possibility of inflation expectations 
and underlying inflation moving above target (which has now been reached). Energy costs, yen depreciation and AI-
related demand were identified as potential sources of upside pressure. Below, we illustrate the potential for additional 
pass-through, although transmission is not automatic.

Overall, the BOJ’s October decision broadly supports our expectations for ongoing tightening, which, at the time of writing, 
remain modestly below expectations for future hikes priced into the OIS market.  In September, the median forecast of the  
Amova Asset Management Global Investment Strategy Committee (GISC)  was for two additional hikes from the BOJ in 
the forthcoming 12 months, taking the policy rate to 1.75% by September 2027.  
From GISC to FOMC: still over-weighting the US rate path? 
Dollar/yen’s immediate moves after the 30 October BOJ policy decision have been limited, which may indicate that the pair 
remains more influenced by expectations regarding the US economy and the Federal Reserve (Fed)’s corresponding rate 
trajectory. On 28 October the Fed delivered its own hawkish hold (voting 10-2 to keep rates at 3.75% to 4%), similarly 
emphasising upside inflation risks. Our own analysis indicates a rise in policy message alignment from the Fed, with an 
increase in inflation emphasis.  Despite the repeated mention of a belief that productivity may keep inflation contained, Fed 
Chair Kevin Warsh explicitly acknowledged a need to refine his own understanding of US productivity via the upcoming Fed 
Productivity and Jobs Taskforce on 16 November.

As mentioned in the GISC, the ongoing risk-tolerant expansion in global markets is disproportionately reliant on the trifecta 
of robust nominal US growth, extrapolation of currently buoyant earnings expectations and benign financing conditions, 
which encompass the widespread risk tolerance that we believe underpins the “carry trade”.  With time, we expect tighter 
overall financial conditions to reduce the degree of accommodation buoying risk assets. 
Appendix: Bayesian FOMC model update 
The Fed kept rates on hold in October while retaining a hawkish message. We offer updates on specific criteria of interest 
identified in August. 
1. Policy-message alignment strengthened the most–Jackson Hole increasingly looks institutional, not personal: 
October’s Federal Open Market Committee meeting reinforces the view that Warsh’s Jackson Hole framework is 
increasingly reflected in actual Fed communication and decision-making. While this strengthens our confidence that 
the two are aligned on monetary policy, it is not proof that the Committee has adopted every part of Warsh’s economic 
interpretation. 
2. Inflation emphasis moved materially higher–the Fed is not using the productivity story to relax the inflation 
constraint: This importantly reinforces the first factor of interest. The meeting and press conference are jointly read as 
a hawkish pause, reinforced by the messaging at the press conference. Growth is tolerably strong (our employment 
sensitivity indicator remains little changed), but inflation remains the binding policy constraint.

3. Productivity conviction rose, but important questions remain: Warsh and the Fed appear more convinced of a 
durable supply-side improvement, but what they mean by “productivity” is still somewhat unclear.  At times, the FOMC 
has used “productivity” to refer to “labor productivity” while at other times it seems closer to “production”.  Current 
production can indeed reflect more capital and strong demand, but neither establishes a durable acceleration in total 
factor productivity (which includes gains from technological innovation).   
The bigger inconsistency would be the failure to address the possibility that if indeed productivity was rising in any 
durable way, the r-star could also be higher. That would point to a higher, not lower, neutral rate.  So, the inconsistency 
here is that Warsh invokes the supply-side upside, but has not publicly closed the loop on the neutral-rate 
implication. 
4. Less forward guidance strongly confirmed: We had fairly high confidence to begin with that Warsh was not a fan of 
forward guidance, so the modest reinforcement of conviction is not surprising. 
5. Balance sheet and governance views remain weakly identified: These are mostly non-results.  October did not 
reveal much about longer-run balance sheet preferences or how Warsh actually governs the Committee. 
— Bottom line: confidence in alignment on inflation fighting is rising, but the productivity story has unresolved 
holes
