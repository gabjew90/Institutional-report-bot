November 3, 2026 
— The economist Hyman Minsky warned, in essence, that “stability is destabilising”. He 
argued that a lengthy period of financial stability could be problematic, as it induced 
risk-taking and complacency amongst investors, laying the foundation for the next 
crisis. For example, lenders relax their terms and borrowers are more willing to extend 
themselves, as nobody wants to miss out. This dynamic makes economies inherently 
prone to financial panics and boom-bust cycles. 
— But since the 2008 crisis, and the subsequent sovereign crisis in the Eurozone, we 
have yet to see another financial crisis happen on a scale that had a lasting market 
and macroeconomic impact. There have been clear moments of concern, as in early 
2023 with the regional bank turmoil, but nothing that has caused a lasting impact. So 
this led us to ask why. 
— In this paper, we examine why the post-GFC era has seen fewer financial crises. 
Explanations include the reforms made after 2008 to avoid a repeat, the lower 
proportion of financial assets held by the banking system, as well as the speedier 
reaction of policymakers when wobbles have occurred. We then consider a few 
candidates that could cause the next crisis, including AI vulnerabilities, private credit, 
and stretched fiscal positions. 
— Ultimately, financial crises haven’t been abolished, but the reforms of recent years 
have made it harder for shocks to propagate through the traditional banking system. 
Moreover, policymakers have become far more inclined to intervene when initial signs 
of stress have emerged.

Section 1: What is a financial crisis, and what causes them?
There isn’t a single technical definition of a “financial crisis”, and they come in many 
different types. Commonly, we think of banking crises like 2008, but they can also 
encompass currency/balance of payments crises, debt crises and other forms too. 
Given currency and sovereign crises have been more common in emerging markets 
(the UK's ERM crisis in 1992 and the euro crisis being the two most notable DM 
occurrences), historical analysis in developed economies has tended to focus 
heavily on banking crises. Non-bank funding or market-liquidity crises are often not 
categorised separately in the literature, but they have become more common in 
recent years. This includes the "dash-for-cash" stress at the onset of the Covid 
pandemic in March 2020 and the 2022 LDI crisis in the UK.
But for all the different types of financial crisis, there are common themes between 
them. Often, they are accompanied by asset bubbles that burst in a non-linear 
fashion. We generally see a severe tightening in credit conditions, which in turn 
flows through into a broader economic contraction. Frequently, there are 
contagion-like dynamics that can be difficult to stop. And in the modern world, they 
usually require policymakers to step in, stabilising the situation with interventions.
At risk of oversimplification, we note four factors that determine the emergence and 
the severity of a financial crisis. The first three are well-established in the financial 
literature, sometimes pooled together into a build-up and a crisis phase 
(Brunnermeier & Oehmke 2013), while the fourth has become increasingly 
important over the past 15-20 years:
1.
 Underlying imbalances. Bubbles and financial imbalances build during 
the run-up phase, leaving accumulated risks that materialise when the 
crisis erupts. This is the build-up typically described by the early phases of 
Kindleberger's model of financial crises.
2.
 Trigger. This is the shock that turns an existing vulnerability into clear 
financial stress. These are often very difficult to predict and we mostly leave 
these outside the focus of this report.
3.
 Amplification and financial system fragility. Various factors determine 
whether a shock propagates through the financial system, and the 
intensity of that shock. These include leverage, liquidity and maturity 
mismatches, interconnectedness, and the concentration of exposures. 
Other channels in the financial system can then amplify the shock, 
including bank runs, fire sales and margin calls, and negative net worth 
effects, sovereign–bank loops etc.
4.
 Intervention/policy response. This has been increasingly important in 
recent decades as the size and scope of policy response to crises has 
increased. The outright size can vary, and intervention can occur across 
several dimensions, ranging from direct intervention in financial markets, 
to counter-cyclical macroeconomic policy that mitigates the impact on the 
real economy.
Clearly, these factors are not independent and the interaction between them is what 
determines the severity of a financial crisis.

One important aspect is the joint role that underlying imbalances and amplification 
play. While theoretical models such as Diamond & Dybvig highlight the potential for 
a bank run that triggers a broad crisis, empirical research shows that in practice, it 
is exceedingly rare for a bank run to lead to bank failure in the absence of an 
underlying solvency issue (Correia, Luck and Verner 2026). In other words, the 
presence of amplification channels alone does not lead to a crisis.
Conversely, in the absence of amplification, it is possible to get significant 
turbulence in asset prices, but without a financial crisis. For instance, a bear market, 
wider credit spreads, or even a recession do not in and of themselves imply there 
is a financial crisis. This was seen clearly with the bursting of the dot com bubble, 
a severe equity decline but not a financial crisis, though it did cause a recession. At 
the same time, in the chart below, we can see several US equity bear markets that 
took place outside of recessions. On other occasions, however, a “Minsky 
Moment” can then happen, where even a seemingly minor event can trigger a high-
profile incident that cascades across the financial system.

Section 2. Why have financial crises been more or less likely 
in certain periods of history?
1. Pre-WWII: More frequent financial instability, albeit under a very 
different system.
Financial instability was a recurrent feature of the pre-WWII era. During the 19th 
and early 20th centuries, there were frequent panics that often led to major slumps. 
For instance, the Panic of 1873 sparked a period often known as the “Long 
Depression”, which extended into the 1890s for many countries.
In part, financial panics occurred more frequently because policy operated under 
very different settings. For instance, the Gold Standard as the monetary system 
constrained a countercyclical policy response and transmitted deflation 
internationally, while falling prices increased real debt burdens. Moreover, the 
Federal Reserve wasn’t established until 1913, and the lack of an effective lender of 
last resort meant there was a private intervention during the US Panic of 1907. The 
first wave of globalisation also brought new vulnerabilities. Between 1870 and 
1913, many emerging economies accumulated debts denominated in gold or 
foreign currency, leaving them exposed when capital inflows reversed or exchange 
rates weakened.
The biggest and most famous crisis occurred in 1929, when the Wall Street Crash 
of that year sparked the Great Depression. The length and depth of that crisis helped 
to stimulate the study of modern macroeconomics, with huge efforts to prevent a 
repeat over the years ahead. Indeed, the failures of the response to the Great 
Depression played a large role in the much swifter response to the 2008 financial 
crisis, helping to avoid a second Great Depression.
That experience established much of the modern financial safety net that exists to 
this day. For instance in the US, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation was 
established in 1933. The US and many other countries came off the Gold Standard 
as well in this period. There were also regulatory reforms, and the Glass-Steagall Act 
separated out commercial from investment banking.
2. 1944-1971: The Bretton Woods era coincided with fewer financial 
crises
Around the end of WWII, economic policymakers sought to create an international 
monetary system for the post-war era. The relative financial calm that followed 
reflected a combination of restrictions on international capital movement, tightly 
regulated domestic financial systems, and a system of fixed exchange rates that 
aimed to provide stability and prevent the competitive devaluations that occurred 
in the 1930s. This saw exchange rates pegged against the US Dollar, which in turn 
was convertible to gold at $35 per ounce.
At the same time, capital controls helped preserve monetary policy flexibility, while 
maintaining the system of fixed exchange rates. These controls had several 
downsides but they also insulated countries from “sudden stop” crises that can 
occur, when capital rapidly flees a country. Moreover, capital controls prevented 
the rapid buildup of cross-border systemic leverage. So it was more difficult to 
generate a global financial crisis in the same way.

Alongside capital controls, there were also domestic reforms in the aftermath of the 
Great Depression, which sought to avoid a repeat. As mentioned earlier, the US 
Glass-Steagall Act of 1933 separated commercial banking from investment 
banking. Another example of the era’s regulated financial system in the US was 
Regulation Q, which imposed maximum interest rates that commercial banks 
could pay on deposits. That meant depositors could not search for yield and cause 
a run on deposits as easily. Another rationale was that with institutions not 
competing for deposits, it would avoid a compression in the margin between the 
rates banks paid on their lending and borrowing, which would disincentivise a push 
into riskier investments.
Finally, economic growth was also stronger in this period, with the period between 
WWII and the 1973 oil shock often described as the “Golden Age”. This played a role 
in avoiding financial crises too, as faster growth helped keep debt default rates 
down, avoiding the kind of “trigger” events above that might spark a financial crisis. 
Coupled with financial repression, strong growth also helped to bring down the 
high post-war public debt burdens.
Over time however, the peg between the dollar and gold found itself under growing 
strain. The economist Robert Triffin identified the “Triffin dilemma”. Supplying the 
world with dollar reserves resulted in outstanding dollar liabilities around the world 
that exceeded the gold reserves in the US, weakening confidence that dollars could 
ultimately be redeemed for gold. Conversely, restricting dollar supply to protect 
those reserves and preserve convertibility risked depriving the system of the 
liquidity needed to support trade and growth.
These tensions intensified in the late 1960s amid expansionary US policies, 
including spending on the Vietnam War and Great Society programmes. The 
continued drain in US gold reserves eventually led the US to suspend the 
convertibility of the US Dollar into gold, effectively ending the Bretton Woods 
system. Initially, there were attempts to bring some kind of fixed-exchange rate 
system back, but the world of fiat currencies (with no link to a precious metal) 
persists to this day.

3. 1971-2008: Financial crises become more frequent after the 
breakdown of Bretton Woods to the Global Financial Crisis
After the breakdown of the Bretton Woods system, financial crises became a more 
regular occurrence. While the end of the gold convertibility did not by itself trigger 
future crises, it opened the door for wider financial liberalisation, with the resulting 
rise in capital mobility and looser domestic financial regulation then helping to 
facilitate a global build-up of imbalances.
The 1980s and 1990s were a period of frequent crisis events. The transition away 
from capital controls meant that capital could flow more freely across borders. 
However, this could also cause a “sudden stop” if investors suddenly pulled their 
capital out of a country, contributing to bank runs and currency collapses. 
Moreover, many emerging markets borrowed in foreign currencies like the US 
Dollar, meaning that a depreciation in their own currency would see this USD-
denominated debt surge in local currency terms. This meant it was a particular 
problem if US interest rates were increasing, as it became harder for many 
emerging markets to repay their debts. This was a contributing factor behind the 
Latin American debt crisis in the early 1980s. And the rate hikes also had domestic 
ramifications, being a contributing factor behind the Savings and Loan crisis in the 
United States.
With capital flowing more freely, that also played a role in the 1994 Mexican Peso 
Crisis, and the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis. A sudden reversal in capital flows led to 
crisis and ultimately recession in many countries. The following year, the Russian 
financial crisis then took place, and fears grew in the United States about the wider 
ramifications, as the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) came 
under pressure. Indeed, this period saw 75bps of pre-emptive rate cuts from the 
Federal Reserve.
This volatility also saw the Fed proactively seek to step in and smooth out the 
economic volatility. This was broadly successful, but in the process also helped 
engender complacency and a further buildup of leverage. Indeed, after the dot com 
bubble burst, the federal funds rate was cut to just 1% in mid-2003, where it 
remained until mid-2004.
This period saw clear optimism that another Great Depression was highly unlikely. 
For instance, Nobel Prize-winning economist Robert Lucas argued in his 2003 AEA 
Presidential Address that “macroeconomics in this original sense has succeeded: 
Its central problem of depression prevention has been solved, for all practical 
purposes, and has in fact been solved for many decades.”
However, as we now know, the 2008 financial crisis occurred, driving the biggest 
global downturn since the Great Depression of the 1930s. It turned out that Minsky 
had been correct, and a lengthy period without a crisis had once again helped to 
induce complacency, laying the foundations for a repeat.

Section 3: The Post-2008 world: Why have we not had 
another big financial crisis?
Since the Global Financial Crisis of 2008, we have thankfully avoided a major 
financial crisis. Despite clear moments of stress, most notably around the collapse 
of SVB in March 2023, we have not seen a similar wave of bank collapses on that 
scale. There are several components to this.
First, post-GFC deleveraging cleared out risks in the system, with countries and 
sectors that had excess indebtedness before the GFC experiencing pronounced 
declines in leverage.
Second, there have been reforms explicitly designed to avoid a repeat of 2008. 
After the crisis happened, there was a clear recognition that gaps in regulation had 
exacerbated the crisis, leading to effects that cascaded across the financial system.
For instance, the Basel III reforms required banks to hold more equity, with a 
minimum requirement for Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital. They also 
introduced the liquidity coverage ratio, which requires banks to hold enough high-
quality liquid assets to ensure they can cover their net cash outflows under a 30-day 
stressed scenario. In addition, the net stable funding ratio was brought in, requiring 
banks to have sufficient stable funding that exceeded the required amount of stable 
funding for a one-year stressed scenario.
At the national level, further reforms were also introduced. In the US, the Dodd-
Frank Act was introduced, with measures including the Volcker Rule that restricted 
banks from engaging in proprietary trading, and required the Federal Reserve to 
perform an annual stress test of large institutions. Meanwhile in the European 
Union, which also experienced a sovereign debt crisis, reforms included a “Single 
Supervisory Mechanism”, whereby banking supervision was brought under the 
ECB, ensuring harmonised regulatory standards.

Third, the share of financial assets held by banks has fallen since the 2008 crisis. 
This hasn’t eliminated risk, but commercial banks are funded by retail deposits that 
can quickly fly. By contrast, non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs), e.g. hedge 
funds, are not as prone to these bank run dynamics. For instance, a private credit 
closed-ended fund is not one where investors can simply redeem their funds on 
demand.
We can see this shift in the data. Looking at the FSB’s group of 21 plus the Eurozone, 
we can see that the share of assets held by banks has fallen from a peak of 46% in 
2008, to 38% in 2024. By contrast, the share held by NBFIs has risen from 43% to 
51%.

Fourth, the lessons of 2008 have meant that when the financial system has had 
wobbles since, policymakers have acted with incredible speed to stop the 
turmoil.
This was most obvious when the Covid-19 pandemic began. Whilst a pandemic 
was very different to a financial crisis, there was initially huge turmoil in financial 
markets. Indeed, the S&P 500 fell by more than a third in just over a month, a decline 
at a speed and scale unseen since the Great Depression. Meanwhile, US HY credit 
spreads soared to levels last seen around the financial crisis, and at the height of the 
turmoil, there was a huge spike in real yields, despite the economy entering a 
recession.
Against this backdrop, central banks wasted no time in acting. For instance, the Fed 
delivered an emergency 50bp rate cut, followed by another 100bp rate cut. 
Moreover, they swiftly resumed Quantitative Easing, and announced the 
establishment of multiple new facilities to ensure the smooth functioning of credit 
markets. These included a Primary Market Corporate Credit Facility, to support new 
bond and loan issuance, along with a Secondary Market Corporate Credit facility, 
which offered liquidity for outstanding corporate bonds. Meanwhile, a third facility, 
called the Term Asset-Backed Securities Loan Facility, was set up to enable the 
issuance of asset-backed securities. Similarly in Europe, the ECB set up a new 
Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme (PEPP), which ultimately had a total 
envelope of €1.85tn.
Whilst the pandemic caused a huge economic shock, the swift action meant it did 
not translate into a financial one, and the brief tightening in credit conditions was

A similar pattern was seen during the regional banking turmoil of March 2023. This 
was a financial panic, which initially began with the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank. 
However, this began to spread, and Signature Bank also failed. Then in Europe, 
Credit Suisse came under investor scrutiny and saw large deposit outflows, which 
culminated in a purchase by UBS that included guarantees from the Swiss 
government. This coincided with huge market moves, with the 2yr Treasury yield 
posting its biggest daily decline in over 40 years, with a 61bps decline on March 13.
However, policymakers swiftly moved to stop any contagion. The US Treasury, 
Federal Reserve and the FDIC released a joint statement stating that all deposits at 
SVB would be protected, and that the Federal Reserve would provide additional 
funding to ensure banks could meet the needs of depositors. Meanwhile, the Fed’s 
balance sheet briefly expanded again, despite the fact it had begun the process of 
quantitative tightening. These actions ultimately meant the crisis proved contained 
and did not lead to a wave of further bank collapses in the manner of 2008.

Fifth, policymakers have also responded to recent shocks with huge fiscal 
transfers, meaning that debt has migrated from private towards sovereign 
balance sheets.
Whilst global debt has risen slightly over the last decade, if you dig deeper into the 
numbers, that’s entirely driven by a rise in government debt. If we compare the 
statistics for Q2 2026, relative to Q2 2016, there’s actually been a decline in debt for 
households, financial corporations, and nonfinancial corporations. The only one of 
the four segments that’s gone up in the last decade is government debt.
In relative terms, the transformation is even bigger if we look back to 2007, before 
the Global Financial Crisis kicked off. Back then, government debt was the smallest 
of the four categories, but because of the GFC and the pandemic, it’s now become 
the biggest of the four categories.
That in turn has led to separate issues, with government bond yields at multi-year 
highs, alongside rising net interest spending. So the fiscal space has become 
limited. However, public balance sheets don’t face the same kind of system-wide 
run that a bank would.

Overall, the post-2008 experience offers three key forward-looking lessons. First, 
stronger bank balance sheets and the shift towards non-bank finance suggest 
future stress may originate outside banks. As such, historical analyses centred on 
banking crises may provide a less complete guide. Second, policymakers have 
become increasingly effective at containing liquidity pressures and amplification. 
And third, the shift in debt towards sovereign balance sheets has cushioned private-
sector shocks but narrowed the capacity for future intervention.
Section 4: What are potential candidates for the next crisis?
This section considers some of the current risks in the financial system and whether 
they could be sowing the seeds for the next financial crisis. This section is not 
designed to be a comprehensive list of financial risks, but rather an assessment of 
three important areas where risks have risen in recent years.
1. Could AI spark broader vulnerabilities?
A huge driver of the current equity bull run has been the growth in AI-related stocks. 
This report won’t explore the debate as to whether this is a bubble, but examine 
whether a correction could eventually propagate into a systemic financial crisis. As 
noted earlier, a reversal in the investment cycle need not cause a crisis in itself, and 
the bursting of the dot com bubble also did not lead to a broader financial crisis.
Nevertheless, there are issues that could spark vulnerabilities. First, the rapid rise 
in AI capex raises questions about whether the investment will pay off. The AI build-
out is on course to exceed the scale of past technological investment cycles, which 
have often involved overinvestment even when the technologies ultimately proved 
transformative. Second, funding is shifting from predominantly operating cash 
flow towards external capital, and rising debt issuance in particular. Third, greater 
equity-market concentration increases exposure to a reassessment of technology 
valuations. Together, these developments make a correction a clear risk.
To assess the potential for systemic stress, we apply the framework in Greenwood, 
Hanson, Shleifer and Sørensen (GHSS). This associates heightened crisis risk with 
the combination of a strong run-up in asset prices and excessive credit growth, 
estimating a 45% probability of a crisis within 3 years if the two thresholds in Figure 
11 are met. On a backward-looking basis as of 2025, an economy-wide assessment 
finds that the equity-price increase meets the framework’s “red zone” test, but 
business credit growth has been considerably more subdued, given the slowdown

renewed rise in valuations.
While the GHSS framework gives a useful quantification of the potential risks, its 
indicators also warrant some qualification, which we judge to come in opposing 
directions:
n
On the one hand, there is a risk that aggregate credit measures may 
understate vulnerabilities when borrowing is highly concentrated. Circular 
financing, leases and off-balance-sheet structures complicate the picture, 
and while we incorporate the recent rise in payables in our leverage metric 
to capture some of this risk, it may well be underestimated. Moreover, 
realised interest costs remain below current market borrowing rates after 
the lengthy period of ultra-low rates, so leverage trends alone may miss the 
pressure from rising debt-service costs.
n
On the other hand, the price-based metric used by GHSS to assess excess 
asset price growth (the 3-year real rise in equity prices) may overstate 
valuation excesses given the strong rise in earnings. Indeed, P/E multiples 
have already seen a sizeable correction since late 2025, which was most 
pronounced for software stocks, and currently there is little evidence of 
overly stretched tech valuations, especially when compared to the dot com 
boom.
The risk of stress amplification is also likely to depend on the nature of any AI 
correction. A downgrading of the AI capex cycle due to slower growth in AI 
demand, financing constraints or supply bottlenecks may result in weaker 
valuations, leading to negative wealth effects and slower capital expenditure. 
However, while this would create an economic headwind, it need not produce a 
systemic crisis. And while parallels to the dot com bubble may well be relevant, 
currently there are factors that suggest downside risks would be more moderate. 
For instance, operating cash flow still plays an important role in financing the AI 
buildout, whilst there has been a limited run-up in borrowing and much less 
stretched valuations relative to the late-1990s.
Our assessment therefore remains that an AI correction is a clearer risk than an AI-
led systemic crisis at this point. For these risks to become more material, it would 
need leverage to keep building but AI returns to eventually disappoint, raising the 
likelihood of credit events and amplifying the investment slowdown through the 
real economy. We will focus on this interaction between debt-service pressures and

2. Risks from higher rates and private credit
In the previous section we noted that modest economy-wide debt growth doesn’t 
necessarily imply modest credit risk when debt-service costs are rising. As existing 
debt reprices or is refinanced, borrowers can face greater pressure even without 
additional borrowing. This is a key feature of the debt cycle, whereby vulnerabilities 
accumulate during the expansion, and rising rates (often amid a monetary 
tightening due to an overheating economy) can then expose them. These risks are 
relevant in the context of the current rate hiking cycle, especially as much of the 
catch up in debt service costs is yet to play out (see chart below).
With banking-sector risks having moderated since the GFC, the rapid expansion of 
private credit and its opaque structure have understandably drawn attention as a 
possible source of the next crisis. Parallels with the subprime credit boom have 
been periodically made. However, we think these should not be overstated. Even 
though the dollar size of the US private credit market is similar to that of subprime 
around 2006-07, the market must be assessed relative to the economy (which has 
doubled in nominal terms). Moreover, the slower build-up, restrictions on 
redemptions and lower risks of direct wealth effects all suggest much more 
moderate amplification risks than in 2008.
There are already signs that the private-credit growth cycle is slowing (see chart) 
and our credit strategists have noted warning signs including weaker new inflows 
into BDCs, increasing payment-in-kind interest and prospective refinancing 
pressures. That said, defaults remain limited thus far so these should not be seen 
as evidence of an imminent systemic event.

Whether more systemic risks could emerge will depend on issues of solvency, 
opacity and capacity for intervention.
n
First, while redemption gates can limit immediate cash outflows and 
reduce the need to sell illiquid loans under pressure, they do not restore a 
borrower’s ability to service debt or absorb underlying credit losses. This 
distinction between liquidity protection and solvency is therefore central to 
judging whether a slowing credit cycle remains orderly.
n
Opacity remains a concern, both in financing structures and in the 
distribution of exposures among end-holders, including insurers and other 
non-banks. This can make losses harder to identify and potential 
amplification harder to assess.
n
Policymakers’ success in containing liquidity pressures may not translate 
into an effective response to credit events - liquidity provision alone cannot 
repair impaired balance sheets. Extending support to opaque non-bank 
exposures raises difficult questions about eligibility, loss allocation and the 
threshold for intervention.
Quite aside from private credit, the fact that we are back in a rate hiking cycle from 
the major central banks also adds to the current risks. Indeed, higher rates bring 
down inflation, in part because they tighten financial conditions. But a 
consequence of this is that it often reveals vulnerabilities in the financial system, 
and makes a crisis more likely. For instance, it was a contributing factor behind the 
2023 regional banking crisis, as banks had growing unrealised losses on their 
securities portfolio when rates began to rise again. So even if higher rates don’t 
directly cause a crisis, they can often be a key contributor. Indeed, hiking cycles 
have regularly coincided with some kind of shock, as the experience of recent 
decades demonstrates.

3. Stretched fiscal positions and possible NBFI amplifiers
As discussed earlier, the migration of debt towards sovereign balance sheets has 
helped cushion private-sector shocks, but this has come at the cost of higher fiscal 
risks. Higher public debt and sovereign yields have limited the room for fiscal 
stimulus, while persistent inflation has limited the room for monetary stimulus. This 
makes another round of coordinated macroeconomic support harder to deliver.
The issue with high public debt is that it if fiscal sustainability is questioned, that can 
raise sovereign borrowing costs, which can amplify existing private-sector 
vulnerabilities via a tightening in financial conditions and slower growth.
The financial risk posed by sovereign stress will also depend on the financial 
amplification channels. These were clear in the European sovereign crisis with the 
bank-sovereign loop, but the changing structure of sovereign markets suggests 
that the sovereign–NBFI feedback loop may now warrant increasing attention. This 
was visible with the 2022 LDI crisis in the UK, while a more recent concern is the run 
up in leveraged hedge fund positions in sovereign bond markets, with hedge funds’ 
sovereign bond exposures more than doubling since 2022 (see chart below). As 
noted also by the IMF, this reflects greater issuance and a rising share of sovereign 
debt held by hedge funds.
The lessons from Black Monday, LTCM and the LDI crisis offer reassurance that 
intervention can contain market stress originating outside the banking system. But 
they are not a guarantee that future liquidity shocks will remain economically 
benign. The greater scale and interconnectedness of non-bank finance mean that 
we can’t simply rely on historical examples today.
Looking forward, a combination of sovereign repricing, leveraged financial 
amplification and a more constrained policy response risks proving harder to 
resolve than past episodes.

Conclusion
Whilst a major financial crisis has been avoided since the GFC and the subsequent 
sovereign crisis, the problem of financial instability has yet to be permanently 
solved. On the one hand, reforms to the financial system and the swift actions of 
policymakers have helped to prevent a repeat. But as we know from Hyman 
Minsky’s warning, lengthy periods of stability can often lay the foundations for the 
next crisis, even if these take place slowly. Indeed, risks often shift rather than 
disappear.
Looking forward, there are several vectors of risk worth paying attention to. But 
financial crises often start in areas where people are paying little attention, or risks 
are assumed to be contained. Indeed, it is the areas where risk is underappreciated 
which often prove the most vulnerable.
As such, predicting where the next crisis might arise is inherently difficult. But the 
topic is a vital one, given the long-lasting damage that financial crises can cause 
when they do occur. Indeed, with the fiscal space for sovereigns increasingly 
constrained today, the margin for a future policy error is limited.
