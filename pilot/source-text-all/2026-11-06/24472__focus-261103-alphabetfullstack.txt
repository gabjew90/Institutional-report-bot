AI assistants are challenging Google's dominance in Search. That story is now well known. Far

Four layers, one owner
A full-stack strategy means owning every layer needed to 
deliver AI, from the chip to the end user, rather than renting 
some of them. There are four: chips, cloud, models and 
distribution. Most of Alphabet's rivals own two or three 
and rent the rest. Alphabet owns all four.
Source: Syz assessment based on company disclosures, October 
2026
The value lies in how each layer feeds the next. TPUs give 
Google Cloud cheaper compute, since Google pays no 
margin to an outside chip supplier. Google Cloud trains 
and serves Gemini in data centres Alphabet owns, so it 
keeps the margin OpenAI pays to its compute providers. 
Gemini then reaches users through Search, Android and 
Chrome, which gave the Gemini app one billion users at 
a much lower acquisition cost than its rivals. Usage at 
that scale gives the TPU the volumes that lower its cost, 
and Google Cloud resells to outside clients the chips and 
models Alphabet first built for itself.
This is why the full stack is Alphabet's answer to the 
threat on Search. Alphabet earns from AI directly, through 
Search and Gemini, and indirectly when a rival pays it for 
compute. Several TPU clients compete with Google in 
models or cloud. Sundar Pichai, Alphabet's CEO, treats 
the TPU as a platform, like Android or Chrome, open to all. 
Even when a user picks another assistant, part of the AI 
spending can still end up at Alphabet.
What is a TPU, and how does it 
compare?
A TPU (tensor processing unit) is a chip Google designs 
only for the maths behind AI models, mostly large matrix 
multiplications. An Nvidia GPU started life as a graphics 
processor and is a far more general tool. The TPU gives up 
that flexibility in exchange for lower cost and energy use 
per calculation, once thousands of chips work together 
on the same model.
Source: Syz assessment based on company disclosures. 
Performance claims are company-reported.
Google began designing the TPU around 2013 for its own 
needs and opened it to Google Cloud clients in 2018. For 
years, it was a cost line that helped Search, YouTube and 
later Gemini run more cheaply.
That changed over 2025 and 2026. Google now sells 
complete TPU systems, installs them outside its own data 
centres and counts rival AI labs among its clients. The 
TPU now appears in three places in the accounts: in Cloud 
revenue, where system sales were a "meaningful contrib-
utor" in Q3; in the USD 561bn backlog; and in inventory. 
An internal tool is turning into a product that investors can 
value on its own.
The TPU, an essential building block
The TPU is not the advantage on its own, but it is the layer 
rivals find hardest to replicate, for two reasons.
Cost comes first. Google pays no margin to an outside chip 
supplier, and the 8th generation launched this year offers 
up to 2.7x better training performance and 80% better 
inference price-performance than the last. That matters 
most for Search. If an AI answer earns less than a page 
of links, the cheapest server wins as usage shifts. Alpha-
bet CEO Sundar Pichai added that selling TPUs outside 
Google raises volumes, and that in semiconductors scale 
"directly translates into better economics".
The second is a new source of revenue. Alphabet no 
longer rents only cloud access. It now sells complete TPU 
systems, with networking, storage and software included. 
Thomas Kurian, CEO of Google Cloud, counts more than 
100 significant TPU customers, served in Google Cloud, 
in their own data centres, at Oracle and through Crux AI, 
a joint venture with Blackstone. Clients include Anthropic, 
Apple, Meta, Citadel Securities and Jump Trading.

What it brings to Google Cloud
Google Cloud is now a business of more than USD 120bn 
of annualised revenue, with USD 11.1bn of operating profit 
in Q3 2026 alone.
Source: Alphabet earnings releases, Q3 2025 to Q3 2026; Q4 2025 
and Q1 2026 Cloud figures via Nasdaq/Zacks and Eastern Progress 
reports, rounded
The Cloud backlog reached USD 561bn, of which just 
over 55% should turn into revenue within 24 months. 
TPU system sales contributed to growth, but Google 
Cloud Platform services made up the vast majority of Q3 
revenue. The margin has two limits. It will face headwinds 
in Q4 as rented capacity ramps up, and it excludes the 
USD 7.7bn of shared AI costs booked at Alphabet level.
The cost in cash
Alphabet pays for its full stack before it earns from it. In Q3 
2026, Alphabet generated USD 42.3bn of operating cash 
flow but spent USD 51.4bn on capex, leaving free cash 
flow of minus USD 9.1bn. Excluding the USD 5.2bn EU fine, 
it was still about minus USD 3.9bn. Trailing twelve-month 
free cash flow fell to USD 19.7bn.
The largest item is capex. Guidance for 2026 is USD 
195–205bn, and management already expects a signifi-
cant increase in 2027. About 65% goes to servers and 
networking that wear out in six years, so depreciation, up 
49% in Q3, will keep rising for years. Behind capex sit USD 
924bn of future purchase commitments and USD 152bn 
of leases not yet started, although some contracts allow 
lower volumes.
The TPU business adds a cash cycle of its own. A signed 
contract enters the backlog; Google then buys compo-
nents, builds the systems and holds them in inventory, 
which jumped from USD 2.4bn at the end of 2025 to USD 
13.3bn. Cash comes back only on delivery and billing, 
mostly in 2027.
Management calls this a different cash cycle rather than 
traditional capex, and components are bought only once 
a contract is signed. In a ramp-up, however, every new 
contract consumes cash before it returns any.
Hard to copy, but less profitable than 
Search
This is the heart of the matter. Search is a near-monopoly 
that sets its prices through an auction and runs on infra-
structure built long ago. Cloud and TPUs are a different 
kind of business. The margins look close on paper, with 
36.2% for Cloud against 40.7% for Google Services in Q3. 
But Cloud competes with AWS and Azure, sells to a few 
large AI clients that can negotiate or build their own chips, 
and needs heavy new capital for every dollar of profit. Its 
margin also excludes USD 7.7bn of shared AI costs. The 
same dollar of profit is worth less when it comes from 
Cloud than from Search, and the market will not pay the 
same multiple for it.
Source: Alphabet earnings releases, Syz Research
Capital intensity has nearly doubled. Capex rose to 40% 
of revenue in Q3 2026, up from 23% a year earlier. 
Revenue grew by 25%, while capex increased by 114%. 
An increasing share of growth is now driven by Cloud and 
TPUs, which require far more capital than Search ever did.
Buybacks have stopped. Alphabet repurchased no shares 
in the first nine months of 2026, compared with USD 
39.8bn in the same period a year earlier. Dividends are 
now the only remaining form of direct shareholder returns. 
For many investors, buybacks formed a significant part of 
the investment case for owning the stock.
Search still funds most of the build-out, but its growth has 
slowed while capex keeps accelerating.
Management promises returns on invested capital well 
above its cost of capital, and much of the TPU inventory 
should turn into revenue in 2027. Neither shows up yet in 
the cash flow statement. Until it does, the full stack pro-
tects Alphabet's strategic position more than it answers 
the market's demand for cash. Management is walking a 
tightrope.

For two decades, Search paid for everything Alphabet wanted to build. With Search now under pressure, the full stack is
