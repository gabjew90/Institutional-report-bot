Shifting Tails Around a Benign Baseline 
 
 
Implied rates volatility in both the US and Euro area reached cycle lows around the start 
of the year, settling below our estimated fair value range in both markets. AI disruption 
concerns in February followed by the onset of the US-Iran war saw that reverse sharply, 
with vol subsequently overshooting estimates of fair value. Across a range of measures, 
we currently see a meaningful risk premium embedded across the surface in both USD 
and EUR (Exhibit 1 & Exhibit 2). And despite local pockets of stress, overall the year so 
far has seen above-average vol-selling returns, making short-vol strategies a bright spot 
amid the selloff in duration. 
 
In the US, near-term rate uncertainty primarily reflects the push-and-pull between 
elevated energy prices and AI-driven growth concerns. Upside energy risks are helping 
to keep the inflationary tail alive across major markets and have been a central factor in 
rates pricing for much of the year, driving the longest stretch of positive rates/vol 
correlation since 2021. We think as long as worries about an energy spike persist, energy 
volatility will be an important driver of day-to-day rates swings. 
That said, the shift in energy-related supply risks to AI-driven demand factors as the 
prominent driver of yields recently has already dampened sensitivity to oil prices. We 
had previously shown that the impact of a hiking cycle on implied volatility depends on 
policy follow-through; with 1y1y pricing up approximately 100 bps from summer lows, 
we think the upward push on implied vol from rising yields is largely exhausted under 
likely policy outcomes. 
Our baseline—falling inflation, stable growth, and subsiding rate uncertainty as the Fed 
delivers fewer hikes than priced—points to lower implied vol over the next year (Exhibit 
3). This baseline should bode well for vol-selling also in 2027, and we recently updated 
our toolkit for benchmarking returns on short-vol strategies to better time the entry for 
such trades. 
While renewed geopolitical escalation and a further energy spike is a risk, we think there 
should be constraints on further vol upside. In light of the higher starting levels for yields 
and evidence of a speed limit to rates selloffs imposed by concerns about the impact of 
higher borrowing costs, we are doubtful that a supply shock alone would push implied

Instead, we think downside risks present the larger potential catalyst to higher volatility 
from here. Fears of an AI slowdown have turned the rates/vol correlation sharply 
negative and sidelined energy prices, likely making rate rallies—rather than selloffs—the 
primary driver of higher near-term volatility (Exhibit 4). We think there is value to owning 
6m to 1y expiries on 2 to 5y maturities to hedge against these risks, with lower strikes 
typically offering a good balance between premium outlay and average return. 
Overall, we view the deeper tails of growth scares–especially if subsequently borne out 
by weaker data–as the larger vol event than energy upside. 
We think this asymmetry can also be better reflected in skew. Throughout most of the 
year, payer/receiver skew was tilted towards higher yields, reflecting upside risks to 
inflation from energy shocks and cyclical support from the AI buildout. Yields have 
indeed tended to rise more than implied by the forwards throughout the year, but in line 
with the shift in recent weeks, we see less justification for such a tilt to higher yields, for a 
few reasons (Exhibit 5). 
First, the more balanced slack backdrop and higher starting level for rates should reduce 
the risk of the Fed falling behind the curve and needing to deliver aggressive catch-up 
hikes as in 2022. Second, beyond the shift from the Fed, markets continue to price 
upside risk to the number of hikes implied by survey-based estimates. Skew notably 
shifted towards receivers multiple months before the final hike during the last cycle. 
Third, with yields rising about 100 bps over the past year, tighter financial conditions 
have begun to materialize. Further, the latest phase of the selloff saw higher yields 
become a clearer headwind to risk assets. While that doesn’t preclude rates rising above 
such levels in the future, yields that are on the high side of recent history typically reduce 
the market’s assumed asymmetry to further selloffs, similar to the effect we had shown 
for the zero lower bound during rallies.

It is through the lenses of the two tail risks—energy price risks and the AI trade—that we 
also view the clearest implications for volatility from the midterm elections. Typically, 
markets price a vol premium above fundamentals in the run-up to midterm elections 
that dissipates once results provide clarity, irrespective of government composition 
(Exhibit 6). While policy shifts can durably alter the macro outlook, our economists see 
limited fiscal implications from the outcome. Instead, the current vol premium likely 
largely reflects post-election geopolitical escalation risks and/or concerns about 
increased regulatory pressure on the AI buildout. 
In terms of other risks, history suggests limited impact from the potential path of 
negotiations on the debt ceiling, which we estimate is likely to bind in 1Q27. And even 
though trade uncertainty has not dissipated, we think markets’ playbook around tariffs 
has shifted. Recent trade tensions with Canada suggest that absent a full-blown 
re-escalation and across-the-board tariffs, vol markets are likely to look through 
headline risk. 
In Europe, we see parallels of a baseline of normalizing inflation and hike expectations 
consistent with a meaningful decline in volatility and lower vol premium. However, tail 
risks linger. First, energy risks, especially around gas going into the winter, continue to 
skew the risks around inflation to the upside, which would likely see markets price 
further ECB tightening, given the ECB’s comparatively lower inflation tolerance. Second, 
renewed sovereign stress in France could also fuel volatility, with the potential of a 
significant tightening of financing conditions adding to uncertainty around the policy 
rate path. 
That said, we think current levels of implied volatility now embed clear premia versus 
macro fundamentals. And while this year’s rise in implied volatility represents about 70% 
of the 2022 spike, yields and gas prices have only covered about 30 and 10% 
respectively of their 2022 rise (Exhibit 7). At the same time, we think the sovereign 
spread widening has introduced a speed limit to ECB tightening and led to a resumption 
of safe haven flows, suggesting core yield selloffs and spread widening are unlikely to be 
simultaneous sources of vol.

We think these dynamics broadly limit further volatility upside. Full compression of the 
vol premium may require clarity on the gas outlook and extent of right-tail risks—and 
thus visibility into the severity of the winter. But over a longer horizon, gradually easing 
inflation pressures should provide sustained support for volatility normalization as the 
range of outcome around policy rates narrows. Ultimately, the conclusion of ECB 
tightening followed by eventual gradual, non-recessionary rate cuts projected by our 
economists suggests scope for a return at least some of the way towards late-2025 
volatility lows. We think this creates asymmetry and opportunities for vol-selling from 
attractive valuations (Exhibit 8); we think selling payers is a reasonable way to fade 2027 
hike pricing. 
The reduction in hike risks under our baseline should also meaningfully shift the shape of 
the vol curve. Typically, the tail curve tends to steepen as markets gradually reduce their 
tightening expectations without pricing cuts. This tendency holds especially following 
substantial FCI tightening, when markets either shift towards pricing some front-end 
relief (in which case the level of vol subsides, too) or the tightening spreads further out 
the curve. 
And while falling-rate environments can also trigger tail curve inversion, this is 
dependent on the speed and shape of the move. Rallies of about 50-200bp/quarter are 
most clearly consistent with tail curve inversion. Instead, more gradual declines (as our 
baseline implies) tend to see a reduction in vol premium around front-end yields, 
whereas sharp ‘recession-type’ rallies inject vol premia further out the curve (Exhibit 9). 
Crucially, the curve dynamics matter: only bull steepeners tend to invert the tail curve, 
whereas parallel rallies generate broader vol premium across the surface. A gradual 
reduction in hike premium from current levels would thus be consistent with a modest 
steepening of the tail curve (Exhibit 10), even though the emergence of deeper tail risks 
poses risks to relative value curve trades.

On the micro front, convexity hedging poses contained risks in renewed selloffs, as 
index-level convexity stands close to zero Because approximately 95% of outstanding 
mortgages remain locked in below prevailing rates, our mortgage strategists estimate 
that yields would need to decline by at least 75–150 bps before refinancing channels 
reopen meaningfully. Peak gamma exposure in UST futures options also centers around 
current yields, and total open interest been reduced, suggesting these micro drivers are 
likely to play a smaller role for now. Concurrently, elevated global yields and stricter 
regulatory constraints have brought new Formosa bond issuance to a near-complete 
standstill over the past several years. Under our baseline yield projection, both call 
redemptions and primary issuance activity will remain subdued. Consequently, we do 
not anticipate a return of structural dealer vega supply to suppress ultra long-dated 
implied volatility.

Selling Options - Investors who sell calls on securities they do not own risk unlimited loss of the security price less the strike price. Investors who sell

price and the strike price, depending on whether the option is settled by physical delivery or cash-settled. Investors who sell puts risk loss of the strike 
price less the premium received for selling the put. Investors who sell put or call spreads risk a maximum loss of the difference between the strikes less 
the premium received, while their maximum gain is the premium received.
