The correlation multiverse – one index, many markets 
 
◼ The S&P 500 arguably looks calm, but the stocks beneath it are not. Earnings surprises, sector 
rotations and sharp single-name moves are all highly visible events, but index volatility has been 
subdued. The first indication that something has changed is that the share of stock-return 
variation explained by the broad market factor has fallen close to the bottom of its post-2016 
range. At the same time, the S&P 500 has broken into far more return-based clusters. 
◼ These clusters are not loose collections of unrelated stocks. Correlation between the stocks in 
each cluster remains strong, as before. What has changed is the links between 
clusters: correlation here has fallen sharply. The index is quiet not because stock-level risk has 
disappeared, but because risk is increasingly localised and separate groups are cancelling out one 
another’s moves. 
◼ Average correlation hides more than it reveals. It blends pairs that still move together within 
clusters with pairs across clusters that increasingly do not. That matters because index variance 
depends on the full weighted pattern of co-movement, not one headline number. 
◼ The clusters also differ from the labels investors usually rely on. Return-based clusters show 
limited overlap with macro and fundamental groupings and cut across the sector map. Sectors 
remain useful descriptions of businesses, but they are a weak guide to which stocks are currently 
moving together. 
 
The market is splitting into more clusters, with fewer meaningful links between them and greater isolation as a result

A quiet index, an active market 
The S&P 500 is calm because the market beneath it is no longer moving as one. Single-stock 
risk remains active as moves across clusters are increasingly cancelled out at index level. 
In 1933, Fritz Zwicky found that visible matter could not explain how a galaxy cluster held 
together. The same broad idea applies here: the index is not wrong, but it hides a change in the 
structure beneath it. Diversification explains why constituent moves can cancel one another out. 
It does not explain what has changed to make that cancellation so strong. The more useful 
question is how the market’s internal structure has shifted. 
The index whispers, even as the stocks are shouting 
 
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/Equity Quant 
The previous charts show the outcome. The next two sections separate its two components: more 
groups and weaker links between them. 
One market is becoming many 
The market is becoming less one-factor driven and is breaking into more return-based 
groups. PC1 now explains close to the lowest share of S&P 500 variation since 2016, while 
the cluster count under our base specification has risen from roughly 25 to 66. 
We group stocks using daily returns. Pairwise correlations are estimated over a rolling 252-day 
window, converted into a distance measure and joined through hierarchical clustering. Stocks 
that move together sit closer and are more likely to fall into the same cluster. The grouping 
therefore comes from how stocks move, not from labels set in advance. We use average linkage 
and a fixed distance threshold. Sensitivity tests using alternative thresholds, windows and 
linkage rules preserve the broad rise in fragmentation, although the exact cluster count changes.

The clusters still hold. The links between them do not 
Correlation within clusters remains firm, while correlation between clusters has fallen 
sharply, leaving the index quieter without a similar fall in stock-level risk. 
The clusters still hold, the links between them do not 
 
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/Equity Quant 
A single average correlation hides more than it reveals, blending pairs that still move together 
within clusters with pairs across clusters that increasingly do not, describing neither group 
particularly well. To connect the cluster map to the index, we split covariance into two 
groups: pairs inside the same cluster and pairs across different clusters. The raw total can be 
distorted by the changing number of pairs as cluster count rises. We therefore focus on average 
weighted covariance per eligible pair, which gives a cleaner comparison of co-movement inside 
and between clusters. As we can see in the chart below, the between-cluster covariance has 
remained relatively stable, while within-cluster covariance has gone up, indicating that risk is 
increasingly concentrated within clusters rather than transmitted between them. Taken together 
with what we saw earlier – the correlation between clusters is lower while that within the clusters 
is strong – we see further evidence that the linkage between clusters is responsible for lower 
realised index variance.

Groupings matter 
Return-based clusters are not sector, macro or fundamental groups under another name. 
They also form tighter groups than these traditional classifications. 
ARI1, compares different methods of grouping stocks, allowing for overlap that could occur by 
chance. A value near zero means the overlap is little better than chance; a higher positive value 
means the two maps place more of the same stock pairs together. Return-based groupings differ 
materially from fundamental groups and overlap only partly with macro groups.

Are clusters the new sectors? 
Return-based clusters are not sectors under another name. Sector labels remain 
economically useful, but they do not capture today’s dependence structure. 
Comparing clusters and sectors, we see that ARI remains positive but low: knowing a stock’s 
sector explains only a small part of its return cluster.

Different, yes. But how useful is it? 
Sectors remain useful economic labels, but return-based clusters provide a clearer map of 
current dependence. 
A sector can contain several groups that move in different ways. Information Technology, for 
example, contains software, hardware and semiconductor stocks that have responded 
differently as the AI theme has developed. The conclusion is simple: stocks in the same return 
cluster move together more consistently than stocks that merely share a sector.

The risk match: clusters versus sectors 
In our in-sample tests, portfolios spread across return clusters carry lower realised 
volatility than portfolios spread across sectors. Recent dependence therefore contains 
portfolio information not captured by sector labels.

spreading it across sectors. We compare the two approaches at the latest snapshot using the 
same number of equally weighted stocks. Clusters and sectors are fixed on the same date, and

The cluster map therefore adds information beyond sectors, in our portfolio simulations. The 
diversification benefit depends on the market remaining fragmented. The main risk is therefore 
a reconnection of the groups that are currently moving separately. 
Watching gravity return 
A credible reversal should appear across more than one dimension: common market 
movement, network structure and volatility intensity. No single signal is likely to be 
decisive. 
◼ Commonality: a rise in PC1 and between-cluster correlation 
◼ Topology: stronger connectivity between clusters accompanied by a collapse in the number of 
overall clusters 
◼ Intensity: higher single-name volatility accompanied by higher correlation between clusters.
