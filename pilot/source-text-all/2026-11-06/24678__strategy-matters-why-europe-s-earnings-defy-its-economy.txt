1. Upgrading our Earnings Forecasts 
We raise our STOXX 600 EPS growth forecasts to 18% for 2026, 7% for 2027 and 5% 
for 2028. This upgrade, from 15% to 18% in 2026 and from 5% to 7% in 2027, reflects 
the results of our updated earnings framework, with direct contributions from Financials 
and Commodities (Exhibit 1). 
Our forecasts are slightly below bottom-up analysts’ consensus, which expects EPS 
growth of more than 20% in 2026, 9% in 2027, and c.10% also in 2028. Excluding 
Commodities, consensus still expects earnings to grow 15% in 2026 and 12% in 2027, 
while the median company is expected to deliver around 10% in both years according to 
consensus estimates. Underlying earnings growth therefore remains healthy, even as 
headline growth starts to slow in 2027. 
 
Over the past year, earnings growth has been the principal driver of European equity 
returns, helping the market absorb higher bond yields, renewed energy pressures and 
subdued domestic economic growth. This raises a broader question: why are European 
earnings proving more resilient than the domestic economy would suggest?

while STOXX 600 earnings growth was 2.4%. A wider gap has opened this year. Our 
economists are now expecting nominal GDP growth of around 3.3% in 2026 (and 2.8%

Sector Composition 
Differences in sector composition in the Euro area help explain why listed earnings 
decouple from domestic GDP growth. GDP measures value added across the domestic 
economy, whereas aggregate market earnings reflect the profits of a concentrated 
group of large listed companies, many with significant international operations. 
Exhibit 2 compares sector shares of Euro area gross value added (GVA) with their 
weights in EURO STOXX earnings and market capitalisation (we use the Euro Area in both 
cases, but we think the conclusions would be largely similar based on STOXX Europe). 
National Accounts activities are mapped to the closest comparable ICB sectors. The 
largest gaps are in Financials, Energy and Technology on one side, and Industrials, Real 
Estate and Retail on the other. Financials account for 33% of earnings but only 10% of 
domestic activity, and Energy contributes 9% of earnings with almost no direct weight in 
the economy (petrol forecourt sales would count as retail). Technology also has a much 
larger weight in the listed market than in the economy, representing 4% of economic 
activity but 11% of earnings and 14% of market capitalisation. By contrast, Industrials, 
Real Estate and Retail represent c.45% of the economy but 15% of listed earnings. 
 
The distinction is even clearer when we subtract each sector’s share in the economy

positive side: earnings exceed their weight in the economy by more than 20pp, followed 
by Energy at 9pp and Technology at 7pp. At the other end, we see that Real Estate, 
Retail and Industrials are underrepresented by 13pp, 10pp and 8pp, respectively.

consensus expects more than 100bp of expansion, from around 8.7% to 10.1%. Our

changes in growth momentum, which we expect to remain broadly stable over our 
forecast horizon. We think maintaining already elevated margins is more plausible than

Appendix 
 
 
 We update the methodology first outlined in European earnings dissected (October 2016) 
and in Yearnings for earnings (November 2018). Our top-down models relate annual 
STOXX 600 EPS growth to a set of macroeconomic and market variables using 
multivariate regressions. The aggregate model is estimated from 1990 to 2025 with our 
principal dependent variable as annual STOXX Europe 600 EPS growth. We also estimate 
separate models for STOXX 600 earnings excluding Financials and Commodities. Our 
sector models cover Banks, Energy and Basic Resources. Our building-block framework 
uses annual sales growth and the annual change in margins for the STOXX 600 excluding 
Financials. 
Explanatory variables 
Sales-weighted GDP growth measures real GDP growth across the regions in which 
n
STOXX 600 companies generate revenues, using prior-year geographic sales shares 
as weights. Euro area GDP enters the Banks model separately because domestic 
activity is more relevant to loan growth and credit conditions. 
Brent and copper are measured as year-on-year changes in annual average prices. 
n
Brent enters the headline and Energy models, while copper is the principal 
commodity variable for Basic Resources. EUR/USD enters the sales and margins 
models and captures the translation effect from overseas revenues, while Euro area 
core inflation is included as a measure of nominal pricing growth. 
The capacity-utilisation gap measures Euro area capacity utilisation relative to its 
n
historical median. Conditional on GDP growth, high utilisation implies less spare 
capacity and less scope for further earnings expansion. For Banks, we introduce the 
Euro area primary balance as a share of GDP instead, to capture the fiscal stance, 
which is not fully reflected in current GDP growth; positive readings indicate fiscal 
tightening, while negative readings indicate fiscal stimulus. The coefficient should be 
interpreted as a historical cyclical association rather than a causal effect of fiscal 
policy on bank earnings.
