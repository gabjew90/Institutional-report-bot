• Democrats have secured majorities in both chambers of 
Congress and achieved pivotal state-level gains, including 
in the key data center market of Texas, raising concerns 
about a more restrictive environment for AI infrastructure 
development. We believe these pressures will smooth 
and extend the buildout into the late 2020s and 2030s, 
rather than destroy secular demand.
• Fears that tighter regulation, slower permitting, and 
grid constraints will delay data center expansion 
drove investors out of power producers and electrical 
equipment suppliers, hurting our strategy’s relative 
performance despite gains elsewhere in the market.
• Despite market pressures, we believe tighter oversight 
will extend, rather than derail, the AI infrastructure 
cycle. With several beneficiaries of the AI power buildout 
now trading near pre-boom valuation levels, we believe 
market expectations have become overly pessimistic. At 
the same time, multi-year backlogs, committed projects, 
and well-capitalized customers support our conviction 
that the sell-off offers an attractive risk-adjusted entry 
point.

Our view in summary
The outcome of the midterm elections—Democratic 
majorities in the US House and Senate, as well as 
pivotal down-ballot swings (such as the gubernatorial 
race in Texas)—triggered a sharp market bifurcation on 4 
November. While areas such as financials, health care and 
hyperscalers rallied, data center infrastructure champions 
in our strategy experienced heavy selling pressure, hurting 
relative performance. We believe investors are derisking 
positions on fears that tighter federal oversight, state-level 
moratoria, and grassroots opposition to data center grid 
interconnection will derail spending on infrastructure for 
artificial intelligence.
We have heard some market participants argue that political 
friction will pull peak infrastructure demand forward as 
developers rush projects before tighter rules take effect. 
We see the opposite dynamic: Regulatory rigor, localized 
reviews, and grid capacity constraints would not destroy 
secular demand, but rather smooth and elongate the 
duration of the cycle into the late 2020s and 2030s. 
Backlogs and committed customer investment provide 
support as the industry adapts to a more demanding 
regulatory environment. Moreover, our selected "Power 
and resources" companies possess unprecedented visibility 
to future revenue growth, strong market shares, and 
relationships with well-capitalized customers.

Weekly performance commentary
Recent days have seen a combination of earnings-
heavy news flow alongside a continued rotation out 
of AI-related stocks. Against this backdrop, our "Power 
and resources" strategy remained largely driven by 
fundamentals, delivering positive absolute returns and 
outperforming the MSCI ACWI Index in the week ending 
30 October. At the sector level, equipment, facilitators, 
and utilities contributed the strongest gains, while materials 
slightly lagged. Geographically, US and European names 
drove the outperformance.
Thoughts of the week
Deconstructing the sell-off
Independent power producers (IPPs): Vistra dropped roughly 
14%, its worst single-day retreat in nearly two years, while 
Constellation Energy shed 7%. Investor discussions centered 
around heightened regulatory uncertainty for data-center-
driven merchant power contracts and an unexpected 
reversal in Texas state politics.
Industrial and electrical equipment OEMs: Eaton fell nearly 
10%, nVent Electric fell 14%, and Forgent declined 17%. 
Equity markets immediately priced in a slower cadence of 
high-voltage transformer, gas turbine, and liquid-cooling 
hardware deliveries.
The counter-rally in clean energy and regulated utilities: 
Pure-play renewable developers and equipment suppliers 
(such as First Solar, Sunrun, and Enphase) rallied as investors 
considered renewed federal incentives or clean power 
mandates under Democratic congressional leadership. A 
decline in interest rates also supported a strong rally—
NextEra Energy rose 5%, Entergy climbed 3% and Xcel 
Energy increased 2%.
This dramatic rotation reflects an assumption that the AI 
infrastructure supercycle is facing an abrupt policy wall, 
though we disagree with the premise that this marks the 
end of the buildout.
Regional slowdowns and the regulatory overhang: 
Texas, Ohio, and more
The primary catalyst behind investor alarm was the 
momentum building behind regulatory pushback against 
data center deployment.
Texas has historically been the epicenter of rapid AI 
infrastructure expansion, driven by ERCOT’s deregulated 
market and streamlined interconnection. However, the 
election of Gina Hinojosa (marking the first Democratic 
gubernatorial victory in Texas since 1990) modifies 
the narrative. Hinojosa campaigned on establishing 
an immediate pause on new speculative data center 
construction, eliminating tax abatement, and instituting 
strict controls over water and power consumption.
Even prior to the election, current Governor Greg Abbott 
and state utility regulators had initiated reviews of 
projects to prevent residential utility rate spikes. The 
election outcome solidifies what was already becoming an 
institutional mandate: Large loads must pay their own way, 
bring their own power, and prove they do not compromise 
grid reliability—things we believe hyperscalers are willing to 
do.
The regulatory friction is not confined to the Sunbelt. In 
Ohio, a hub for hyperscale campuses, many municipalities 
placed data center development directly on the ballot. These 
included limits or temporary moratoria on projects over a 
specified level of power or water consumption.
These 
restrictions 
may 
complicate 
regional 
project 
development, but are unlikely to halt the underlying 
demand for compute. Instead, they would likely filter which 
developers and hyperscalers can cross the finish line and 
may be resolved with greater concessions (which we believe 
hyperscalers and large developers are more than willing to 
pay).
Why our core investment thesis remains intact
To evaluate the risk to our holdings, we look past high-
level headlines and evaluate three fundamental realities 
governing the electrical infrastructure supply chain:
1. Locked-in projects and record equipment order backlogs
We believe any data center project currently under 
construction or in advanced preparation is economically 
compelled to finish. The capital already committed by 
hyperscalers is significant, and if approvals become 
more challenging, existing compute should become more 
valuable.

Equipment backlogs for critical components—such as 
complex power transformers, switchgear, gas turbines, and 
specialized backup generation—stretch several years into 
the future.
Leading industrial manufacturers operate with order books 
that are committed, with onerous cancellation terms. Given 
this, we believe earnings growth for tier-one industrial 
suppliers is insulated against near-term permitting pauses, 
preserving multi-year earnings visibility even under worst-
case demand deceleration.
2. Hyperscaler balance sheets and strategic geography
Hyperscalers (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta) have 
repeatedly demonstrated both the balance sheet capability 
and willingness to self-fund behind-the-meter generation, 
co-locate with power and negotiate bilateral terms to satisfy 
local environmental and regulatory standards. While some 
high-density suburban communities have voiced pushback, 
there remains a broad roster of rural regions actively 
jockeying for data center campuses across the US. These 
municipalities welcome the high-paying construction trades, 
technical jobs, and substantial property tax revenues data 
centers generate to support local school districts and 
municipal services. We expect development to naturally 
migrate to jurisdictions offering constructive partnership. 
And though this project migration may take time, we would 
be surprised if most hyperscalers and large developers did 
not have some alternate location contingencies already 
planned—this was in part what could have driven 
some double counting of power needs across regional 
jurisdictions.
3. The reality of the project quality spectrum
A fundamental tenet of our investment underwriting 
has always been the distinction between speculative 
announcements and bankable projects. The public discourse 
frequently quotes a headline pipeline of ~400 GW in 
proposed data center power requests. We have consistently 
modeled a realistic buildout closer to ~90 GW by 2030, 
which will likely prove to be a conservative estimate. 
However, our decision to remain conservative hinges on 
our view that the announced pipeline includes duplicate 
interconnection queue requests (which ironically could help 
manage this potential regional policy disruption resulting 
from election outcomes) or speculative projects that were 
never likely to come to fruition.
Our portfolio companies typically only book orders into 
backlog once a customer has secured environmental 
permits, executed a binding utility interconnection 
agreement, and committed to a fixed delivery schedule 
with non-refundable milestone payments. More stringent 
federal or state oversight may reduce the paper backlog, but 
should not threaten commercialized orders. We continue 
to expect some additional power supply agreements will 
be announced in 4Q26 and 1Q27 despite this policy 
uncertainty.
Valuations already price in significant pessimism
The sharp decline in recent months, and on 4 November 
specifically, has created a significant valuation disconnect in 
our view. While we acknowledge recent developments have 
increased risk around the buildout, many of our holdings are 
now trading at valuations near or below where they traded 
before the AI data center narrative took hold. We think the 
market is effectively assigning near-zero residual value to 
future AI-driven power demand, which creates a margin of 
safety for long-term investors.
