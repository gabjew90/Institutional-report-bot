 
Goldman Hikes 2027 Brent Forecast For These Two 
Reasons 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 02, 2026 - 05:45 PM 
On Sunday, Goldman Sachs' head of oil research, Daan Struyven, raised his crude oil price 
forecasts, warning that the threat of renewed supply disruptions will keep a higher geopolitical 
risk premium in crude prices even as Gulf area production recovers.  
Struyven lifted his December Brent forecast to $105 a barrel from $90 and its West Texas 
Intermediate estimate to $94 from $81. For 2027, he expects Brent to average around $88 (up 
from $80) and WTI around $78 (up from $73). 
Struyven provides one major reason for the upgrade:  
Risk premium drives the rally as adaptation continues. A jump in the risk premium to around $27 
now explains most of October's crude price rally. In contrast, we estimate that Brent's fair value 
was little changed in September and October: global inventories—its main driver—held roughly 
flat as adaptation largely rebalanced global oil output and consumption. 
A larger risk premium. We now expect markets to price in a higher risk premium, reflecting 
downside risks to Mideast supply, the length of past major oil supply shocks, and many market 
participants' view that a lasting diplomatic breakthrough will be difficult. 
 

 
 
Here are Struyven's upside and downside oil price risks: 
Upside price scenario: Brent might reach $140/bbl and average around $130/bbl in 2027 if 2027 
average Gulf output remains 3.5mb/d below its 2025 average, vs. 1.1mb/d above in our base 
case. The most likely price upside triggers are intensified shipping attacks, damage to oil 
infrastructure, and a more persistent risk premium. 
Downside price scenario: Brent might decline into the $60s in 2027 if 2027 average Gulf output 
rises 2.5mb/d above 2025 levels and the disruption assumed in our global supply allowance 
doesn't occur. The most likely price downside triggers are a faster recovery in Middle East 
output 
 
 

 
The chart below shows Struyven's view that the risk premium will remain high for the coming 
months, then will moderate in the back half of next year.  
 
"Although Mideast output continues to recover as we had expected, we are raising our crude oil 
price forecasts for two related reasons: substantial downside supply risks are likely to sustain a 
large near-term risk premium, while our new global supply disruption allowance captures 
elevated high disruption risk and reduces our global oil output forecast over the medium term," 
the energy expert said. 
 
 

 
One of the more notable charts in Struyven's slide deck shows that global visible oil inventories 
held steady through September and October, suggesting supply and demand remained 
balanced despite a record number of tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz last month.  
 
He also highlighted that estimated Gulf crude exports, including shipments carried by dark 
tanker fleets, had recovered to at or above their 2025 averages (pre-war) in recent weeks. 
 
 

 
However, historical data from the EIA and IEA show that major oil supply shocks have often 
lasted for years, leaving lasting damage to production capacity. More specifically, the crisis 
remains in refining and diesel.  
 
Struyven's report was published one day before Monday morning's diplomatic headlines sent 
Brent crude futures tumbling to around $106 a barrel and WTI to roughly $97.
