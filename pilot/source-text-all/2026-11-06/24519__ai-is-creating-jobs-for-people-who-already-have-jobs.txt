 
AI Is Creating Jobs... For People Who Already Have 
Jobs 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 02, 2026 - 03:25 PM 
Good news: companies that adopt AI are hiring more people. 
Bad news: if you are 23 and looking for your first job, those people are not you. 
That is the gist of a new paper by Stanford's Bharat Chandar (yes, the same Chandar behind 
last year's "canaries in the coal mine" study, more on that below) and King's College London's 
Bouke Klein Teeselink which, as Bloomberg reportedand KCL touted last month, crunched 1.25 
billion job postings and 154 million employment records across 41 countries from January 2021 
through March 2026 (KCL's release says "between 2021 and 2016", which would make it the 
first study of AI's impact on the past) to answer the question that has been haunting every 
parent paying $80K a year in tuition: who actually gets hired when a company goes all-in on AI? 
The answer: senior employment at AI adopters rose 6.7% relative to otherwise identical 
non-adopters, total employment rose 3.3%, and junior employment fell 2.5%. As the authors put 
it, in a line that should be engraved over the door of every university career center: 
"AI is labor saving for junior workers and labor expanding for seniors in exposed occupations." 
In other words, the AI jobs boom is real, it just skips the bottom rung of the ladder. 
 

 
 
Regular readers will recall that we have been tracking this exact dynamic since August 2025, 
when the first Stanford study found that 22-to-25 year olds in the most AI-exposed jobs had 
suffered a 13% relative decline in employment. What's new now is the scale: this is no longer a 
US payroll quirk, it is a global hiring pattern visible from São Paulo to Riyadh. Below we walk 
through what the study found, what Goldman and BofA make of it, and why the "good news" 
headline may be the most misleading part of the whole story. 
The Study: Same Company, Same Country, Different Ladder 
The clever part of the paper is the design. Rather than comparing tech firms that use AI with, 
say, plumbing companies that don't, Chandar and Klein Teeselink look at 26,102 foreign 
affiliates of multinationals that started posting AI-related job ads after ChatGPT's launch, and 
match each one with non-adopting affiliates in the same country and sector, with similar 
headcount, AI exposure and junior share. They then instrument adoption with how many other 
multinationals headquartered in the parent's home metro adopted AI (peer pressure, it turns out, 
works on corporations too), which helps strip out the "booming companies both hire more and 
buy more AI" problem. 
The result, shown below, is a near-perfect scissor: the junior share of employment at adopters 
begins to slide almost immediately after November 2022 and keeps grinding lower for three 
years, ending 1.9 percentage points below controls by March 2026. Since juniors held 57.1% of 
positions at baseline, that is a 3.3% relative drop in their share. 
 

 
 
Note the sequencing (charts: Chandar & Klein Teeselink, Revelio Labs): junior employment 
drops quickly after ChatGPT's launch, falling 2.9% within a year, and then just... stays there. 
Senior employment barely moves for 18 months and then climbs steadily. Firms cut the bottom 
rung first and only later figured out they needed more people at the top, presumably to 
supervise the bots doing what the juniors used to do. 
Some napkin math from the authors themselves makes it tangible: the average treated affiliate 
had about 255 positions in October 2022, 145 of them junior. By March 2026, adoption meant 
about 8 more seniors and about 4 fewer juniors. Net hiring is positive. Net first jobs are not. 
One caveat that Bloomberg glossed over (it rounded the junior decline up to 3%): the junior 
employment drop on its own is not statistically significant; what is very significant is the 6.7% 
senior gain and the 1.9pp collapse in the junior share. Put differently, the problem isn't that AI 
adopters are firing kids, it's that they've stopped growing the junior bench while the senior bench 
expands around it. Which, for the 22-year-old with a freshly minted diploma, is a distinction 
without a difference. 
 

 
"Not Just A Rich-Country Phenomenon" 
The country-level results are where this stops being a Silicon Valley story. The junior share fell 
in 23 of the 31 countries with enough data to estimate, and the seven statistically significant 
declines span wildly different economies: the US, UK, Spain, Poland, Brazil, Mexico and Saudi 
Arabia. As the authors note: 
"The junior share falls in Brazil and Mexico by about as much as it does in the United States, the 
United Kingdom, and Spain, and it is negative, though smaller and imprecisely estimated, in 
Egypt, Colombia, and Indonesia." 
Junior losses also run somewhat deeper in richer and more digitized economies, which the 
authors attribute to high-wage firms having more to gain from replacing junior labor. Translation: 
the more expensive your entry-level workers, the more attractive it is to replace them with a 
$20/month subscription. Meanwhile, the hit is smaller where labor laws are more rigid, which 
may be the first time anyone has argued that French labor regulation has a silver lining. 
Where The Entry-Level Door Is Closing 
Interestingly, AI barely moves the occupational mix at all: in 21 of 22 occupation groups, the 
change in employment share is under half a percentage point. The one exception is computer 
and mathematical jobs, whose share of employment rises 0.8pp, making it the clearest "labor 
expanding" case in the data. 
But that's the catch. Within those same computer and math jobs, the junior share collapsed by 
3.3 percentage points.The shift isn't happening between occupations, it's happening inside 
them: the junior share fell in 16 of 22 groups, with statistically significant declines in legal 
(-4.9pp), computer & math (-3.3pp), business & financial operations (-3.2pp), sales (-2.6pp) and 
management (-1.0pp). 
 

 
 
So the AI-exposed professions are growing, they're just growing older. Which lines up perfectly 
with the census paper we flagged last month showing that graduates of the most AI-exposed 
college majors saw their odds of landing a job right after graduation drop 5 percentage points 
and their starting pay slide about 13% since ChatGPT launched, an earnings hit the authors 
compared to graduating into a large recession. Computer science and its sister majors fared the 
worst. And according to Bloomberg, Chandar and his Stanford Digital Economy Lab colleagues 
now estimate that employment among 22-to-25 year olds in highly AI-exposed jobs is 19% 
below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed roles (versus the 13% relative 
decline flagged in the original August 2025 study). 
Just in case anyone missed which entry-level jobs are next, last month OpenAI rolled out 
"ChatGPT for Financial Services," built with Morgan Stanley and Evercore and designed to do 
the comps, data crunching and pitch decks that have traditionally been the domain of Wall 
Street's first-year analysts. Nothing says "we value our junior bankers" like co-designing their 
replacement. 
Goldman: "Visible But Narrow"... For Now 
So what does Wall Street (the senior part of it, at least) make of all this? Goldman's economists, 
led by Joseph Briggs and Sarah Dong, have been tracking AI's labor impact for years, and their 
verdict remains that the impact is "visible but narrow." In their latest AI Adoption Tracker 
(available to pro subs), published October 1, the bank writes: 
 

 
"We continue to observe employment drags in specific industries where AI-use cases have 
been established such as marketing, graphic design, customer service, and some tech 
occupations. However, this is offset by construction job growth in data center-exposed 
categories, which is up 317k since 2022... The correlation between AI adoption and broad labor 
market slack measures remains limited but has strengthened in recent months, with higher 
adoption now associated with slightly weaker job growth and higher unemployment." 
So AI is creating jobs for senior engineers and for the electricians wiring up the data centers that 
will eventually replace junior engineers. Diversification at its finest. 
Goldman also took its analysis global in August ("Is AI Impacting Global Labor Markets?", 
available for pro subs). The bank found that a 10% AI exposure score is associated with only a 
0.1pp drag on annual headcount growth in France, Canada and the US, with negligible effects 
elsewhere. But read the fine print: 
"Negative effects are more visible after restricting to entry-level employees or occupations with 
high displacement risk, but global AI-related hiring headwinds still appear confined to a narrow 
set of industries and workers." 
 
In other words, Goldman and the 41-country study are telling the same story from opposite 
ends: at the aggregate level, AI's labor footprint is small; at the entry level, it is anything but. And 
notably, the US sits at the very bottom of Goldman's chart, consistent with the paper's finding 
that junior losses run deepest in the richest, most digitized economies. 
BofA is on the same page. In its mid-August Global Economic Weekly ("SurvAIval of the fattest", 
also available to pro subs), the bank's economists argued there is little evidence of broad 
 

 
AI-related job destruction so far, but conceded that AI "may already be weighing on employment 
growth in some industries and on entry-level hiring in particular." Put differently, even the AI bulls 
agree on who is paying the bill. 
The "Low-Hire, Low-Fire" Trap 
None of this is happening in a vacuum. As we noted just yesterday, initial claims printed below 
200K for the fourth week in a row, while September payrolls came in at just 29K, below all 
estimates. When nobody is getting fired but almost nobody is getting hired, the people who 
suffer most are those trying to get in the door for the first time: graduates, career switchers and 
new entrants, but mostly young people, the same people who are now rioting in virtually every 
major country in Europe.  
That is precisely the margin the 41-country paper says AI adopters are squeezing. Firms don't 
need to fire juniors when they can simply stop replacing them, and in a low-hire economy, 
attrition does the dirty work quietly. It's also why the headline data look so calm: a labor market 
can be "stable" for those inside it while the entry door quietly shrinks.

Bottom Line: No Juniors Today, No Seniors Tomorrow 
The optimistic read of the paper (and the one in KCL's press release) is that AI is "changing the 
mix of jobs within organisations rather than reducing overall employment." Adopters grow, total 
headcount rises, everyone moves up the value chain. Fine. 
But there are two problems with the happy ending, and both come straight from the paper. 
●​ First, firm-level gains are not economy-wide gains. The authors themselves caution that 
their estimate says "treated affiliates grow relative to control affiliates, not that 
employment rises in the aggregate." Adopters grow partly by cutting prices and grabbing 
market share from non-adopters, and the paper's own general-equilibrium model shows 
aggregate employment can actually fall even as adopters grow, because AI raises output 
while shrinking labor's share of the pie. A 3.3% headcount gain at Company A that 
comes out of Company B's hide is not a jobs boom. 
 

 
●​ Second, the pipeline. Today's seniors were yesterday's juniors. If firms cut the junior 
bench and then lean on experienced staff to supervise AI, who exactly becomes the 
senior workforce of 2035? The 6.7% senior bump is themechanism that hollows out the 
next generation of seniors. 
And this is with adoption still in its early innings: Goldman's tracker shows just 23.8% of US 
firms using AI in regular business functions, with 27.6% expected within six months, and 
adoption already approaching 50% in the information, professional and finance sectors, i.e., 
exactly the white-collar fields where the junior share is shrinking fastest. 
 
So yes, AI adopters are hiring. Just not the people who need jobs most, and as adoption climbs 
from a quarter of firms to half, we think the "visible but narrow" junior squeeze is far more likely 
to broaden than to fade. Goldman itself sized the eventual displacement at roughly 15 million 
US workers back in June; the 41-country paper just told us whose names are at the top of the 
list. 
Good thing the class of 2027 can always become a data center electrician.
