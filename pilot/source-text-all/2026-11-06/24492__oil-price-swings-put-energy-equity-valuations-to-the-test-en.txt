Thought of the day
Oil prices made their fourth consecutive monthly gain in October, with 
renewed attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz underlining the fragility of 
global energy supplies. Brent and WTI crude oil benchmarks ended October 
up 13.6% and 15.2%, respectively. That rally has partially reversed in early 
November, with Brent falling to around USD 105/bbl in the last two days on 
hopes for US-Iran diplomatic progress, even as the US overnight resumed 
"defensive strikes" on Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz.
The price strength in October and subsequent volatility reflect more than 
the latest geopolitical headlines. The November decline has removed part of 
oil’s geopolitical risk premium, but says little on physical market conditions. 
Middle Eastern oil flows have recovered substantially from the severe 
disruption earlier this year, but have not returned to pre-war levels. Combined 
crude flows through the Strait of Hormuz and bypass pipelines were 
estimated at more than 17.3 million barrels per day in September and 
close to 18.8mbpd in the first three weeks of October, compared with 
19.9mbpd before the conflict. Beyond numerous attacks on ships in and 
around the strait in October, volatile flows, ship-to-ship transfers, and security 
requirements continue to constrain effective tanker capacity.
The buffer provided by inventories has also diminished. Global visible oil 
inventories fell by slightly more than 500 million barrels between February 
and September, according to IEA data, with much of the decline coming 
from strategic reserves. Shipping has itself become a constraint: Middle East-
to-China very large crude carrier freight rates have risen to around USD 
30-40 per barrel, compared with USD 1-3/bbl over the previous decade. 
Recovering Chinese crude imports are removing another shock absorber from 
the market.

Against this backdrop, we forecast Brent crude oil at USD 110/bbl at year-
end, followed by USD 95/bbl in March 2027, USD 90/bbl in June, and USD 
85/bbl in September. Risks around this path remain elevated and depend on 
the course of the Middle East conflict, progress in US-Iran diplomacy, and the 
pace at which Gulf production, shipping flows, and global demand recover.
For equity investors, the key question is whether the recent oil-price volatility 
creates an opportunity to add to energy equities.
We retain Neutral ratings on both the European and US energy sectors. 
Higher crude prices improve the operating backdrop for producers, but the 
sharp reversal at the start of November illustrates why spot oil alone is an 
unreliable guide to prospective equity returns. After the gains already seen in 
energy stocks, we think selectivity matters more than increasing broad sector 
exposure.
US energy: A lot in the price, but selective opportunities remain
US energy companies have already responded to the stronger operating 
environment, with the S&P 500 Energy Index rising 59.9% year to date on a 
total-return basis, compared with 11.8% for the S&P 500. Importantly, the 
equity rally has not simply been a mechanical response to higher oil prices. US 
management teams have maintained capital discipline rather than materially 
increasing spending in response to elevated spot prices, allowing stronger 
cash generation to support debt reduction, buybacks, and dividends.
That combination remains supportive. But after such significant gains, 
valuations provide less reason to chase the sector. The S&P 500 Energy Index is 
trading at around 7.3 times expected 2027 enterprise value to EBITDA, above 
its three-year average of 6.7 times. Its 2027 consensus free-cash-flow yield 
of 8.0% remains attractive, while its 2.4% dividend yield is above that of the 
broader S&P 500. Overall, however, these metrics support our Neutral sector 
view rather than a broad overweight. Energy represents roughly 4% of the 
S&P 500, and we believe a similar portfolio allocation remains appropriate.
We therefore increasingly prefer a selective approach within the sector. We 
rate oil and gas equipment and services as Attractive, with international 
spending expected to accelerate in 2027 as investment broadens across 
international and offshore markets, supported in part by the focus on energy 
security. We also rate oil and gas storage and transportation as Attractive, 
supported by rising pipeline volumes, manageable capital expenditure, and 
strong balance sheets.
European energy valuations already reflect high oil prices
The case for European energy exposure also remains balanced. Factors in 
its favor include the sector’s relative defensiveness, given resilient energy 
demand and generally strong company balance sheets. Integrated oil and gas 
companies benefit from diversified businesses and can draw support from 
strong refining and marketing profitability, favorable natural gas conditions, 
and healthy balance sheets. This continues to make the sector useful in 
portfolios when geopolitical risks are elevated.
But a favorable operating environment does not necessarily translate into 
equally attractive prospective equity returns. Higher crude prices, robust 
refining margins, and the geopolitical risk premium are already reflected to 
varying degrees in energy-company earnings expectations and valuations. 
Lower spot oil prices could also temper near-term earnings expectations if 
the diplomatic momentum continues. We therefore retain our Neutral view

Position instead for broader commodity gains
We believe commodities can provide both a structural source of return 
and portfolio protection in scenarios where higher inflation expectations 
challenge equities and bonds. Commodities have historically offered valuable 
diversification benefits due to their relatively low correlations with traditional 
asset classes. We also see a supportive longer-term backdrop driven by 
electrification, rising power demand, AI infrastructure investment, and supply 
constraints across several markets.
In our view, investors considering adding broadly to energy equities should 
instead explore diversified exposure across precious metals, energy, industrial 
metals, and agriculture. This can capture a wider range of opportunities 
without making portfolio outcomes overly dependent on a single oil-price 
path. Given fast-shifting leadership within commodity markets, we think an 
actively managed approach can help investors participate in the commodity 
upcycle.
Caught our attention
US midterms put Congress in play. Americans vote on Tuesday to 
determine control of Congress. All 435 seats in the House of Representatives 
are up for election, along with 35 of the 100 Senate seats. Thirty-six 
gubernatorial races will also be decided. The US midterms take place at a time 
when investors are focused on high inflation, government borrowing, and 
elevated Treasury yields. The latest polls show that President Donald Trump’s 
overall net approval rating stands at -20 percentage points (pps), according to 
RealClear Polling’s (RCP) average, while the Democrats lead the Republicans 
by 8.9pps in RCP’s average of generic congressional voting polls, which ask 
voters whether they would rather vote for a generic Democrat or Republican 
in the next election.
Our view: Our base case is that Democrats will control at least one chamber 
of Congress after the election. We assign a 50% probability to Democrats 
winning both chambers, a 40% probability to Democrats winning the House 
while Republicans retain the Senate, and a 10% probability to Republicans 
retaining both chambers. A shift in congressional control would likely increase 
oversight and complicate fiscal negotiations, but narrow majorities and the 
president’s veto would constrain major policy changes. The president retains 
considerable authority over tariffs, foreign policy, and regulation, and we do 
not expect the election result to derail data-center expansion. Debt ceiling 
negotiations could generate volatility in 2027, although easing inflation 
should still help Treasury yields settle somewhat lower over the next year.
We would not treat the midterms as a binary directional event for markets. 
We believe corporate earnings, AI investment, inflation, and interest rates 
will remain more important market drivers. Investors should avoid positioning 
around a single outcome and keep portfolios anchored to durable sources of 
growth and income. Read more in the latest Election Watch 2026.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
