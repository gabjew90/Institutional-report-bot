Democrats sweep to power in both House and Senate 
The Democrats delivered a dominant performance in the 2026 Midterm 
Elections, easily flipping control of both chambers of Congress. While a 
Democratic sweep was both our and the consensus base case, the margins of 
victory were larger than just about anyone had anticipated in a clear rebuke to 
President Trump and Republicans. 
Starting with the House, Democrats have already clinched control even with 
results in 68 seats still pending, having gained a net 25 seats. This is 
substantially more than the net gain of three they needed for a majority and is 
already at the top end of the range of likely outcomes from the Cook Political 
Report, despite the count not being finished yet. Given how the outcomes have 
gone so far, the eventual gain is likely to be larger than 25 seats, which is already 
a significant number given the gerrymandered state of the current House map. 
Turning to the Senate, Democrats have won basically every toss-up plus a 
couple of states that were considered to lean Republican for a net gain of 
seven seats compared to the four they needed to regain control (see 
Figure 1). This number could grow even larger as there are still two seats left to 
call, with an eventual gain of eight or nine seats entirely possible when all is said 
and done.

The most astonishing result for us, even more than flips in red states such 
as Texas, Kansas or South Carolina, is that Mississippi is one of the two 
states that has not been called yet. This is a deeply red state that Trump won 
by about 23ppt in 2024, and we had not seen a single person suggesting it was in 
play in 2026. Even if Republicans do eventually hang on here, the fact that 
Mississippi is still in question on Wednesday morning speaks to the 
comprehensive nature of the defeat. 
We would also add that, in terms of the final count in the Senate, it may 
take a while for the Alaska result to be finalised. In this case, Alaska uses a 
ranked choice voting system with four candidates on the ballot. If no candidate 
wins 50% of the vote originally, then the candidate with the fewest first place 
ballots is eliminated and those voters’ ballots are re-assigned to their second 
choice and so on until someone wins a majority. However, the process cannot be 
finalised until all mail-in ballots are received; due to the remote nature of some 
Alaskan communities, these ballots are accepted until 18 November as long as 
they are postmarked by Election Day.

We still look for legislative gridlock 
Despite the sweeping victory, however, we are not convinced it really 
changes much about the base case outlook for policy gridlock. This would 
mean no major new legislation, especially on the fiscal side of things, so no new 
spending packages or tax cuts/hikes. 
Here, even if Democrats win the final two Senate seats to reach a net gain 
of nine, they would still be short of a filibuster-proof majority of 60 seats. 
Furthermore, if Democrats were to get around the filibuster, either by using the 
reconciliation process or convincing a few Republicans to get on board with a 
certain piece of legislation, they would still have to contend with the possibility of 
a Trump veto, and we do not think Trump would be shy about using the veto. 
The possibility of a government shutdown is an interesting issue as we can 
see things going either way. Given that Congressional legislation is required to 
fund the government and avoid a shutdown, having one party control both 
chambers of Congress could make it easier to reach agreement the first time this 
issue rears its head in 2027 (the next time funding is set to expire on 
11 December, though this would be before a new Congress takes office so 
election outcomes would not yet play a role). 
That said, there is a chance, though not a guarantee, that Trump could 
choose to veto relevant legislation coming out of a Democratic Congress 
under the thought that Democrats would shoulder the blame if they controlled

both the House and the Senate. Whether the public would buy this argument is 
another question, however. 
Still, we will see how the remaining vote count plays out and will likely provide 
additional thoughts on the policy outlook as we approach the date the new 
Congress officially takes office on 3 January. 
How will Trump and Republicans react? 
The early reaction from President Trump was interesting in that it was 
relatively conciliatory, pledging to accept the results and mulling working 
together with the Democrats. While this may be a larger possibility than it was 
before, it remains to be seen whether Trump maintains this tone. He has a history 
of being mercurial, so there is always a chance that he sticks to his prior pledge 
to veto “everything” and tries to re-affirm his assertiveness in areas where he is 
able to act without Congress, such as trade policy and foreign policy. 
For other Republicans, it will be interesting to see if some become more 
willing to cross Trump on certain issues, which very few have been willing 
to do thus far. Now, however, the President’s major policy choices likely 
contributed to the comprehensive nature of the defeat, which may have also been 
exacerbated by his choice of poor candidates in several states. Thus, it is 
possible that Trump loses a bit of his firm grip that has limited dissent in the party. 
Blue wave carries down ballot as well 
The blue wave carried on down ballot to state and local elections as well, 
notably including governorships in several key states such as Texas, Ohio, 
and Georgia changing hands. Some of these were a surprise, namely Texas, 
where Senate candidate Ken Paxton had been seen as more vulnerable than 
incumbent Governor Greg Abbott. 
These results are important on the AI front as they could result in new 
restrictions to the ongoing buildout. We remain sceptical of major AI 
regulations coming out of Washington as the veto from the very pro-AI President 
still looms large despite the clear victories by Democrats in Congress. However, 
more AI-hostile Democratic governors could easily throw up some roadblocks, for 
example to data centre construction, in a number of states. We do not think this 
will derail the AI buildout but could contribute to some delays or decisions to 
relocate certain projects. 
Looking ahead to 2028 
We would highlight that two years is a long time, especially in politics, so 
we do not think that momentum will automatically carry over to tell us 
anything about the likely outcome in 2028. We only have to look to this year 
for an example, with yesterday’s midterms offering a major shift compared to 
November 2024. 
Thus, it may depend on how Democrats respond to the midterm results to 
determine whether momentum can be sustained. For example, if they take 
the results as a mandate to try and aggressively push for the preferences of the 
left wing of the party, we think some momentum could be squandered, as we 
would see the midterms as more of a resounding repudiation of Trump than a 
ringing endorsement of the Democrats, whose party favourability according to 
RealClearPolitics is still below 40%. 
We do see the possibility that there could be some impact on the 
Republican candidate, however. Many, including betting markets, had seen 
Vice President JD Vance as very strong favourite to win the nomination, though 
given the clear rebuke offered to the administration from yesterday’s vote, we see 
a rising chance that many Republicans could want to distance themselves a bit 
more from Trump, as opposed to defaulting to his VP.
