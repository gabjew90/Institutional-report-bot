Tin hats and tunnel vision: 
Equities and higher yields
With sovereign bond yields lurching 
higher, investors have understandably 
questioned whether higher yields pose 
a threat to equity markets. History 
suggests the answer is nuanced: 
equities can continue to advance 
through periods of rising yields, 
provided earnings remain supportive. 
Equities can withstand higher yields
The bond market sell-off was a defining feature of 
the third quarter, as the US 10-year Treasury yield 
rose from 4.5% at the end of June to around 5.3% 
at the end of September. Although elevated, this is 
not unprecedented: yields approached this point as 
recently as 2023 and regularly exceeded it before 
the Global Financial Crisis of 2008–2009.
For much of the period from the late 1990s until the 
COVID pandemic hit in 2020, equities benefited from 
rising corporate profits and a secular decline in bond 
yields. That environment has since changed. Inflation, 
tighter monetary policy, and, more recently, higher 
term premia have driven bond yields structurally higher. 
Yet equities have maintained their advance, supported 
by strong earnings and investment associated with 
artificial intelligence.

Earnings remain the key driver
This backdrop may appear challenging to reconcile. 
Investors not only face higher interest rates but also 
renewed inflation pressures, heightened geopolitical 
tensions, and concentrated equity market leadership. 
Yet analysis of the past 30 years shows that, in periods 
of both rising and falling bond yields, sector and 
industry-group returns have been closely linked to 
changes in earnings expectations (see Figure 2).

Strong fundamentals bolster 
expectations
That relationship has remained visible since 2022, 
although Figure 3 highlights considerable dispersion. 
The semiconductors and semiconductor equipment 
group is a clear outlier relative to the market, reflecting 
exceptional increases in both earnings expectations and 
share prices. Even beyond this group, earnings have 
continued to be remarkably resilient.

The strength of the earnings picture is clear. 
World earnings expectation grew by 29.5% 
year-over-year in September, with estimates rising 
8% during the third quarter. All 11 sectors within 
the MSCI world Index recorded year-over-year growth 
in earnings expectations. 
So perhaps the immediate risk is not that recent equity 
returns are disconnected from fundamentals. It is that 
today’s exceptionally strong fundamentals are being 
extrapolated too far into tomorrow.
Margin for error narrows
This distinction matters because the margin for error 
has narrowed. Higher bond yields have increased the 
return available to investors outside equities. At the 
same time, the market remains unusually dependent 
on the AI investment cycle. Rapid investment in data 
centres, semiconductors, and related infrastructure 
has supported both economic activity and corporate 
earnings, but any slowdown in AI investment or 
profitability could challenge current expectations.
This is where tunnel vision can be mistaken for a laser 
focus on earnings expectations. Strong earnings can 
continue to support equities even with higher bond 
yields. But investors now face two distinct risks: 
earnings expectations may disappoint, or valuations 
may compress as higher discount rates intensify 
competition for capital. The light at the end of the 
tunnel may be the considerable long-term potential 
of AI. The journey between today’s investment boom 
and tomorrow’s returns, however, is unlikely to be 
straightforward.
That argues not for abandoning equities, but for greater 
diversification, valuation discipline, and attention to 
balance-sheet quality. Earnings remain supportive, 
but concentrated expectations and a thinner valuation 
cushion leave more room for disappointment.
The tin hat may not yet be necessary, but it should 
be kept close at hand. Defensive equity allocations, 
diversification and disciplined risk control may once 
again prove valuable.
