MARKETING COMMUNICATION
The AI Buildout Is 
Becoming a Long 
Credit Issue
Fixed income investors, especially long-duration credit investors, 
are increasingly financing one of the largest capital investment 
races in history. Yet, unlike shareholders, they receive only 
limited benefit if the winners dramatically exceed expectations 
while they remain exposed to the risk that some of those 
investments may fail to generate acceptable returns. This has 
consequences for investors and their long credit portfolios, 
particularly for liability-driven investing (LDI).
One of the most interesting developments in 
fixed income markets is the rapid rise of artificial 
intelligence (AI)–related debt issuance. Over the 
past several years, a small group of AI hyperscalers 
has dramatically increased capital expenditures 
and increasingly turned to the corporate bond 
market to help finance that investment. This trend 
is not only likely to continue, it may also accelerate. 
The result is a growing concentration in credit 
benchmarks to companies tied to the AI buildout.
Most discussions of this trend naturally focus 
on the technology itself. Will AI succeed? How 
large will the market become? Which companies 
will win? For equity investors, those are the right 
questions to ask. For fixed income investors, they 
may be the wrong questions.
Equity and bond investors play  
different games
Many investors are conditioned to think about 
markets through an equity lens. Growth is generally 
a positive attribute in equity markets. A portfolio of 
growth companies can generate exceptional returns 
even if some investments fail because the upside 
from a handful of major winners can more than offset 
numerous disappointments.
But, bond investors do not enjoy the same  
payoff profile:
•	 Unlike shareholders, bondholders generally do 
not participate meaningfully in the upside when 
a company exceeds expectations. Instead, they 
continue to receive stated coupons and, ultimately, 
repayment of principal.
•	 However, when a company disappoints, leverage 
often rises, credit ratings can deteriorate, spreads 
may widen, and default risk increases. In those 
situations, bondholders participate in the downside.
This asymmetry is important:
•	 Equity investors participate heavily in the upside 
and downside.
•	 Bond investors participate much more in the 
downside than the upside.

This is one reason why companies that are predictable, or 
“boring,” often make excellent credit investments. Stable cash 
flows, predictable business models, strong balance sheets, and 
moderate growth are often more attractive to bondholders than 
ambitious or speculative growth stories. It is not an accident that 
the largest issuers in long-duration credit tend to be centered on 
regulated utilities, consumer staples, telecommunications, health 
care, and mature financial institutions.
The AI buildout creates a different type of risk
This is especially relevant today because AI is not simply 
another technology trend. At its core, AI represents one 
of the largest corporate infrastructure investment cycles 
in decades, and it is occurring in a growth area where the 
ultimate economic returns remain highly uncertain. The 
largest hyperscalers are investing extraordinary amounts 
in data centers, computing infrastructure, cloud capacity, 
semiconductors, and supporting systems against future AI-
related revenue streams whose scale and profitability are not 
yet established. Estimated capital expenditures continue to 
rise sharply over the coming years, and the associated debt 
issuance has been equally significant. Hundreds of billions of 
dollars have already been issued, and substantial additional 
financing is expected (Figures 1 and 2).

That doesn’t mean investors should necessarily focus on 
whether AI will “fail.” In fact, the secular AI growth story may 
prove highly successful. The more relevant question for bond 
investors is whether every participant in the current race 
will earn returns on invested capital that justify the scale of 
the spending now taking place. History suggests that major 
investment booms rarely produce equal outcomes for all 
participants. Some firms emerge as clear winners while others 
earn inadequate returns on capital and become burdened by 
excessive debt.
Benchmarks facilitate this problem— 
a recurrent feature
Unlike equity benchmarks, where higher weights are typically 
associated with greater earnings expectations and progressively 
reward the winners over time, corporate bond benchmarks 
allocate more capital to companies that have issued more debt. 
Put simply, equity benchmarks tend to increase exposure to 
successful companies; bond benchmarks increase exposure to 
larger borrowers. 
This is apparent today in the long credit market, with the seven 
leading AI hyperscaler companies being among the largest 
issuers in the Bloomberg U.S. Long Credit Index (Figures 3 and 
4). Recent long credit issuance has materially changed the 
composition of the index, increasing issuer concentration. And, 
if issuance proceeds as expected, the companies’ exposure 
could more than double over the next two years.

This phenomenon is not unique to today or to AI. History reveals 
several examples during which debt-financed infrastructure 
expansion booms led to a small group of companies and 
sectors becoming major benchmark constituents:
•	 Financial institutions prior to the Global Financial Crisis
•	 Energy producers during the 2010s shale boom
•	 Telecom and internet infrastructure during the late 1990s
•	 U.S. railroads in the late 19th century
In each case, the underlying secular story involved significant 
investment in transformative or speculative technologies that 
genuinely led to solid economic gains. What investors often 
underestimated was the extent to which bond benchmark 
construction concentrated exposures around those stories. In 
many cases, a disconnect emerged between economic growth 
and fixed income investor returns.
We are not saying that AI will necessarily follow the same path. 
The lessons are simply that debt-financed growth themes can 
become disproportionately important within issuance-weighted 
credit benchmarks and fixed income return asymmetry 
does not necessarily marry well with such growth-oriented 
investments. As a result, investors should consider whether 
these investments are a good fit for their needs and portfolios.
LDI investors might bear the most risk
The concentration issue is especially relevant in long credit. 
Combined exposure in the Bloomberg U.S. Long Credit Index 
to the seven issuers examined in this analysis increased by 
more than 3 percentage points (from 4.1% to 7.5%) between 
September 2025 and September 2026, compared with an 
increase of just over 2 percentage points (from 1.5% to 3.8%)  
in the Bloomberg U.S. Intermediate Credit Index.
This means that investors with the greatest incremental 
exposure are typically those purchasing the longest-dated 
securities, such as LDI investors. Yet, the technology and 
commercial assumptions supporting much of today’s AI 
investment may be tested over a considerably shorter horizon. 
Computing hardware can become economically obsolete 
relatively quickly, while the revenue models, utilization rates, 
and returns on current data center expenditure remain 
uncertain.
We want to stress that corporate balance sheets, rather than 
individual assets, ultimately support these bonds, but there 
is a growing potential mismatch between financing duration 
and the technology-and-monetization horizon that deserves 
attention. A long credit investor may, therefore, bear spread 
and credit risk for decades while receiving limited upside even 
if the AI investment cycle produces economic outcomes that 
substantially exceed expectations.

Why SILC may be helpful
The Allspring U.S. Small Issuer Long Credit (SILC) strategy 
was designed to address broader structural issues rather 
than make a specific AI call. But the benefits of this approach 
might be especially relevant today. By focusing on the smaller 
90% of issuers within the long credit market, SILC seeks to 
reduce dependence on the largest benchmark borrowers while 
broadening exposure across the issuer universe.
This has several potential benefits, especially when paired with 
conventional benchmark-oriented managers:
•	 Broader issuer diversification
•	 Reduced exposure to issuance-driven thematic concentrations
•	 Focused on a less-crowded and perhaps less-efficient part  
of the market
•	 Greater opportunity for security selection
•	 Differentiated source of alpha and, hence, low correlation  
to other active managers
Importantly, SILC is not intended as a replacement for 
conventional long credit allocations. Rather, we believe it can 
serve as a complement within a multi-manager structure by 
providing both issuer diversification and alpha diversification. 
Conclusion
•	 The key question for fixed income investors is not whether AI succeeds but whether they are adequately 
compensated for becoming increasingly exposed to a debt-financed speculative growth theme through 
issuance-weighted benchmark construction.
•	 Equity investors may benefit enormously when a handful of winners emerge. Bond investors generally do 
not receive the same upside, yet they can be left exposed to the downside.
•	 As AI-related borrowing grows, investors should consider whether their credit portfolios are becoming more 
concentrated in a speculative growth theme than they had intended. 
•	 At the very least, this environment reinforces the importance of diligent, fundamentally driven active 
management. For fixed income investors, avoiding the eventual losers may be at least as important as 
identifying the winners.
•	 We also suggest a more deliberately diversified approach may improve LDI portfolio efficiency.

Market risk: Security markets are volatile and may decline significantly in response to adverse issuer, regulatory, political, or 
economic developments with different sectors of the market and different security types reacting differently to such developments.
Debt securities risk: Debt securities are subject to both credit and interest rate risk. Credit risk is the possibility that the issuer or 
guarantor of a debt security may be unable, or perceived to be unable or unwilling, to pay interest or repay principal when they 
become due, and credit risk increases as an issuer’s credit quality or financial strength declines. Interest rate risk is the possibility 
that interest rates will change over time such that when interest rates rise, the value of debt securities tends to fall and the longer the 
terms of the debt securities held the greater the impact of this risk.
High yield risk: If a strategy invests in high yield securities (commonly known as junk bonds), these securities are considered 
speculative and have a much greater risk of default or of not returning principal and their values tend to be more volatile than higher-
rated securities with similar maturities.

greater price volatility, and risks related to adverse political, regulatory, market, or economic developments and may be affected by
