US power and data center companies are pursuing integrated platforms and acquisitions to secure timely
electricity, expand capacity and capabilities, and become long-term energy partners as demand grows.
Introduction
 
Rising electricity demand, driven predominantly by data centers, is creating a larger market for US power
companies. Meeting that demand, however, requires generation and grid infrastructure that involve
large capital investments and can take years to deliver. The gap between growing demand and the
ability to deliver supply is influencing growth strategies across the power industry and among its
customers and investors.
A new strategy playbook for the US
power industry
Amit Mathrani
Energy Transition Specialist
Summary
Securing power on time is a strategic priority for hyperscalers, because a data center waiting
for power can lose as much as USD 10 billion in annual revenue per gigawatt (GW) of
computing capacity.

Mergers and acquisitions announced or completed since late 2025 show how power companies,
technology companies, and infrastructure investors are positioning themselves to meet data centers’
growing need for reliable, round-the-clock electricity. Acquiring operating power plants gives buyers
access to existing generation while waiting for new construction. Other transactions add development
capabilities, investment capital, and closer connections to data center customers. Together, these
strategies point toward integrated power platforms that can supply power today and develop additional
capacity as data center customers expand. 
 
Power delays put data center revenues and investment returns at risk
 
As discussed in our US power demand outlook, we project electricity consumption growth of up to 20%
by 2030. In The sprint, we explained that data center construction can take 12 to 24 months, while some
grid connections require three to seven years. Supply chain bottlenecks also limit how quickly new
power plants can be built. For example, large gas turbines can have delivery wait times of up to seven
years, while GE Vernova, a major turbine manufacturer, has reported a global gas power equipment
backlog and production-slot reservations totaling 116 gigawatts (GW).
 
These delays can reduce the economic value of investments by large technology companies, or
hyperscalers. Once a data center is built and its computing equipment is installed, every month without
electricity is a missed opportunity to earn revenue. For an artificial intelligence (AI) data center, annual
revenue could be as much as USD 10 billion per gigawatt of computing capacity.[1] NVIDIA estimates
that its computing systems represent roughly 60% to 70% (USD 35 billion) of the total capital cost of an
illustrative 1GW artificial intelligence data center. For a facility of this scale, annual equipment
depreciation could reach USD 7 billion.[2]
 
Delays leave less productive time to recover that investment, as newer chips constantly make installed
equipment less competitive. Faster access to power gives operators more time to earn revenue and
recover their investments during the equipment’s useful life. An on-site power premium of
approximately USD 10 to 75 per megawatt-hour (MWh) over grid supply adds roughly USD 88 million to
USD 657 million to annual electricity operating costs at a continuous 1GW draw.[3] That premium can be
justified by the additional earnings from starting sooner, particularly because the equipment continues
to depreciates once it is running.
 
Delivering power on time requires generation, grid access, development expertise, and capital. The
businesses currently providing these capabilities occupy different positions in the power supply value
chain (see table 1).

A fleet spanning several markets also strengthens Constellation’s ability to serve hyperscalers
developing multiple campuses, with supply tailored to each location’s electricity needs, emissions
objectives, and delivery timetable. Competitor NRG’s acquisition of approximately 13GW from LS Power
illustrates a similar expansion. These acquisitions transfer ownership of operating plants without
increasing total US generating capacity.
 
 
Alphabet gains greater control over the timing of power and data center development
 
Intersect’s development team and project pipeline give Alphabet greater control over developing power
and data centers together. Alphabet can choose data center locations with power availability in mind
and coordinate equipment orders and construction schedules across both developments. This can
reduce the risk of completing a data center before its electricity supply is ready.
 
 
Infrastructure investors connect AES’s power pipeline with capital and potential customers
 
The Blackrock Global Infrastructure Partners (GIP) and EQT-led consortium would provide capital to
develop AES’s pipeline of power projects. AES already has established demand, including 11.8GW of
signed supply agreements with major technology companies. 
 
The strategic opportunity is to combine infrastructure investors’ capital with AES’ established
development capabilities and customer commitments. This would support the development of its power
project pipeline and provide a foundation for further growth. 
 
 
NextEra’s development capabilities would gain a larger customer base in high-demand
territories 
 
NextEra’s development experience, equipment purchasing scale, and financing capacity would support
investment in Dominion’s Virginia and Carolinas territories, including Northern Virginia’s concentration
of data centers. The proposed combination would serve approximately 10 million utility customer
accounts and own 110GW of generation. Its opportunity lies in applying those capabilities to the power
plants and grid expansion needed in these markets.
 
State utility commissions overseeing Dominion’s regulated utilities review investment plans and
determine which costs can be recovered through electricity bills. The merger also requires their
approval, alongside federal approvals. Greater purchasing scale and coordinated construction could
contain project costs. With infrastructure expansion putting pressure on bills, the case for the
combination rests partly on meeting growing demand while limiting the costs passed on to households
and businesses.

Companies are moving toward platforms that connect power supply
with customers
 
The transactions discussed above point toward a common direction. Companies are combining greater
control over power supply with closer access to customers. They bring together operating plants,
development capabilities, capital, and customer relationships to meet electricity demand.
 
Companies are strengthening these connections from different starting positions (see figure 1):
 
Independent power producers are expanding their generation fleets and the supply they can offer
customers. 
Hyperscalers are acquiring power development capabilities to support their data center expansion. 
Infrastructure investors are providing capital to support power development pipelines and future
growth. 
Utilities are combining development and financing capabilities with networks serving growing
demand. 
Power developers are joining larger businesses that can provide capital and closer connections to
customers.

Combining these capabilities can help businesses coordinate where to invest, when to order equipment,
and how to schedule construction. For power suppliers, earlier knowledge of a customer’s expansion
plans can guide project development and procurement. For data center businesses, greater influence
over power development can help align new electricity supply with the opening of their data centers.
The commercial benefit is a closer match between investment in supply and the demand expected to
support it.
 
The implications for electricity supply nevertheless differ. Acquiring operating plants gives a buyer
control over existing capacity, while developing new projects can increase the electricity available to
customers. The next question is how quickly each route can meet growing demand.
 
Acquisitions secure existing capacity while expansion and construction
add supply
 
The platforms emerging from these transactions combine access to existing generation with capabilities
to develop future supply. Their immediate opportunity is to secure power for data centers that cannot
wait for new plants to be developed. Supporting continued growth also requires additional capacity,
through plant restarts, expansions, and new construction (see figure 2).
 
Selected acquisitions of gas-fired power plants since September 2025 imply costs of approximately USD
850 to USD 1,600 per kilowatt (kW), based on the announced prices for Cogentrix’s generation portfolio
and Hill Top, a gas-fired plant in Pennsylvania. By comparison, Lazard’s July 2026 report cites
construction-cost quotes of USD 2,400 to 2,600 per kW for new combined cycle gas plants expected to
enter service after 2028. Available operating plants are therefore the low-hanging fruit for buyers,
offering lower upfront costs and immediate revenue. Their value for data centers depends on whether
their electricity is available to contract and can reach the locations where it is needed.

Hyperscalers’ long-term commitments to buy electricity are also supporting the return and expansion of
nuclear generation. Constellation plans to restart the closed 835MW Crane nuclear plant in Pennsylvania
in 2027, supported by its agreement with Microsoft. Vistra, another independent power producer, has
agreements with Meta supporting 433MW of additional nuclear capacity through upgrades to operating
plants, with full delivery expected by 2034.
 
New construction adds supply beyond what can be restored or added at existing plants. NextEra’s
planned program of up to 10GW of gas generation in Texas and Pennsylvania targets initial capacity as
early as the end of 2028 and completion in 2032. Across restarts, expansions, and new construction,
these long-term electricity purchase commitments reduce uncertainty about demand before producers
commit to major spending. For hyperscalers, those commitments provide a way to secure future supply
and support their data center expansion.
 
How companies combine access to existing generation with new development will shape their longer-
term role in serving data centers.

Integrated platforms are positioning themselves to become long-term
energy partners
 
Power supply and data center development are becoming more closely coordinated. Hyperscalers are
bringing power development into their businesses, while suppliers and infrastructure investors are
assembling broader capabilities to serve them. We expect this integration to make responsibility for
delivering power increasingly important to competitive advantage.
 
Companies with available generation in high-demand locations have an immediate opportunity to
secure long-term supply commitments. Those agreements can provide reliable power for hyperscalers
and a basis for power suppliers to invest in further capacity. As supply catches up with demand in
individual markets, the premium for immediate access to electricity should ease. Companies that use
today’s supply and demand dynamic to their advantage will be better positioned to establish continuing
roles in hyperscalers’ expansion plans and sustain growth.
 
We expect the emerging supplier platforms to evolve toward an integrated energy-service model. They
would commit to meeting data centers’ long-term power needs within agreed reliability, timing, and
cost requirements, drawing on acquired plants, electricity purchases, and new development. The lasting
advantage would lie in fulfilling that commitment as demand changes, extending the relationship
beyond any individual plant or contract.
