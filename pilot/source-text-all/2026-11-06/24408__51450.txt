Summary: Key themes
	 Strong growth: Global GDP is projected to be 3% for 2026. 
The US economy continues to show economic resilience led 
by positive GDP growth (2.2% annualized in Q2 2026). Strong 
earnings led by AI-related business investment, manufacturing, 
and consumer spending are the key economic drivers.
	 Inflation persistence: Inflation remains persistent and 
“sticky,” above the Federal Reserve’s 2% target rate. High 
energy, freight, and food costs are sustaining inflation and 
weighing on consumers. 
	 US rates: Inflation has overtaken the labor market as the 
immediate monetary policy concern. The Federal Reserve 
(Fed) raised rates by 0.25% on September 16, 2026, with 
FOMC members and the markets anticipating further  
rate hikes. 
	 Labor markets: Despite a weaker than expected September 
US jobs report, the US labor market is relatively strong with 
low unemployment (4.2% in September). 
	 Artificial Intelligence (AI): The AI theme has provided fuel 
for rising equity markets, predominantly through strong 
capital expenditures (capex). AI has impacted fixed income 
markets, as hyperscalers issue record levels of debt in 2026, 
providing the Treasury market with competition.
	 Fixed income markets: Yields have risen over inflation 
fears, Fed policy, and geopolitical concerns. 
	 Portfolio diversification: For investors, diversification  
provides opportunity to participate in different asset  
classes as market strength expands beyond top-tier tech 
into international and emerging markets, US small caps,  
and alternative assets. Diversification may also provide some 
mitigation against inflation and volatility associated with 
geopolitical concerns and potential fiscal policy changes 
resulting from the US mid-term elections.

Scenarios update
To begin the fourth quarter, risk assets have continued to show 
resilience, with volatility a constant in bond markets. With the  
US mid-term elections, uncertainty may give way to more 
pronounced trends. Year-to-date, solid growth, geopolitical  
risks and inflation-driven central bank tightening have been 
contrasting themes.
Resilient growth, slow disinflation: Stable economic growth 
has been a core theme recently, while inflation has remained 
stubborn and employment has been steady if not remarkable. 
Recent inflation prints may support further Fed tightening, much 
of which markets have already priced in. For disinflation to 
commence, energy prices will need to fall.
Inflation persistence: With US GDP growth positive YTD, 
stagflation doesn’t appear to be part of near-term concerns. 
However, with the Fed in a tightening cycle, policy error 
becomes a consideration if the central bank hikes rates more 
than markets expect. This is particularly the case should growth 
weaken amid surging mortgage and variable-rate costs and 
falling sentiment.
Productivity-led growth: Ideally, AI moves from buildout to 
adoption, lifting productivity, wages and profits. Supportive 
signs may include: strong household and hyperscaler balance 
sheets, broadening market leadership, ISM manufacturing above 
52 all year and data-center construction. Falling core inflation 
lets the Fed ease without a recession.
Downside shock/hard landing: Though currently a tail risk, 
there are signs that growth is softening and that higher interest 
rates are dampening housing. This could increasingly affect 
investment and spending. What’s always missing from this 
scenario is a shock that appears or accelerates rapidly,  
becoming obvious in hindsight.
See scenario-specific investments page 5.
 

Featured monthly data
Global growth has remained 
resilient even with continued inflation 
	 Global GDP has remained resilient in 2026, projected to 
grow at 3.0%, according to the International Monetary Fund 
(IMF). This steady expansion was primarily driven by an  
artificial intelligence investment boom, strong consumer  
spending, and accommodative monetary conditions. 
	 However, the broader disinflation trend has stalled due to 
energy supply shocks and ongoing geopolitical conflicts.
	 Oil prices surpassed $100 per barrel following the start  
of the Iran war and have fluctuated above and below that 
figure since. 
	 In response, central banks have begun to raise rates  
(European Central Bank, Bank of Japan and the US Fed), 
while the Bank of England has maintained rates.
AI spending has driven business 
investment & GDP growth
	 In Q1 2026, AI-related investment contributed 1.34% to the 
US economy’s 2% growth. 
	 AI has contributed significantly to US GDP through hardware, 
networking, data center buildouts, and increased productivity. 
	 The AI boom is still climbing after year three and doesn’t 
show any signs of slowing. Prior economic booms peaked  
at year three and then started to decline.

Featured monthly data
The yield curve rises  
	 US Treasury yields have experienced notable volatility due to 
shifting Federal Reserve expectations and inflation concerns. 
Benchmark yields started the year lower, hit mid-year highs, 
and then fluctuated as markets continued to debate the path 
of interest rates.
	 Since the start of the Iran war, the 10-Year Treasury yield has 
increased by 132 basis points, reaching 5.29% as of 9/30/26.   
	 Concurrently, the 10-year US Treasury term premium—the 
extra compensation investors seek for holding long-term 
bonds instead of rolling over short-term investments—has 
also increased.
	 US real bond yields (nominal yields minus inflation expectations) 
are rising because resilient economic growth, heavy capital 
expenditures for artificial intelligence infrastructure, and massive 
government debt supply are driving a structural repricing of 
interest rates.
US inflation remains sticky  
and elevated
	 Inflation remains elevated, causing the Fed to raise rates in 
September 2026 as energy prices and geopolitical tensions 
complicate the outlook.
	 Sticky inflation is primarily driven by stubborn shelter, wage, 
and service costs, leaving US inflation well above the  
Federal Reserve’s 2% target through third quarter.

Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E): A ratio comparing the company’s current share price, as compared to its  
earnings-per-share, for the last twelve months (LTM), or estimated for the next 12 months (NTM), current  
fiscal year (FY1), or next (forward) fiscal year.
Earnings per shares (EPS) measures a company’s profitability per share of common stock. It is the company’s net  
income divided by the number of shares outstanding.
The data contained in this presentation is for informational purposes only and should not be relied upon to 
make investment decisions.
Prices of emerging markets securities can be significantly more volatile than the prices  of securities in developed 
countries and currency risk and political risks are accentuated in emerging markets. 
Alternative investments may engage in short sales and other strategies that involve additional risks, such as  
increased volatility.
Value stocks may lag growth stocks in performance, particularly in late stages of a market advance. 
ISM Manufacturing PMI® (Purchasing Managers’ Index): A monthly diffusion index published by the Institute for 
Supply Management (ISM) that measures economic activity in the U.S. manufacturing sector based on surveys of 
purchasing and supply executives.

Price Consumption Index (PCE): is the primary measure of consumer spending on goods and services in the US 
economy. It accounts for about two-thirds of domestic final spending, and thus it is the primary engine that drives 
future economic growth. PCE shows how much of the income earned by households is being spent on current 
consumption  as opposed to how much is being saved for future consumption.
S&P 500®: Is an unmanaged capitalization-weighted index of 500 stocks designed to measure performance of the broad 
domestic economy through changes in the aggregate market value of 500 stocks representing all major industries.
Consumer Price Index (CPI): A measure of inflation at the retail level.
