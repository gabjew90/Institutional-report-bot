Welcome to the fifteenth edition of the Impact Series from Barclays 
Research, in which we explore one of the defining challenges of the next 
decade: how the world will power a future shaped by AI, electrification and 
rising energy demand.
8 September 2026

of data centres, electrification is transforming transport, industry and heating, and rising living 
standards continue to increase demand for energy around the world. At the same time, recent 
geopolitical events have reminded policymakers and investors that reliable and secure energy 
supplies cannot be taken for granted.

decisions around the world. The implications for markets and economies are significant. Meeting

generation, grids, storage and infrastructure, making energy one of the most important strategic

prominent role in policymaking, while access to reliable and affordable energy could become an even 
more important source of economic competitiveness.

this evolving landscape. From energy demand and inflation to commodities, infrastructure and 
geopolitics, they explore how the global energy race could influence capital allocation, corporate 
strategy and investment opportunities in the years ahead.
We hope this report provides a useful contribution to the debate around how economies, industries 
and markets will navigate the energy addition era.

Executive summary
The energy transition is increasingly looking less like the replacement of one energy source 
with another and more like an expansion of the entire energy system. Demand for electricity, 
oil, natural gas and renewable energy is rising simultaneously, driven by electrification, 
AI, digital infrastructure, demographics and economic development. At the same time, 
geopolitical tensions have elevated energy security to a strategic priority. Together, these 
forces are reshaping investment, policy and capital allocation across the global energy system.
Three structural forces are reshaping 
energy demand
The current environment differs from previous energy 
cycles because demand is being supported by three 
powerful long-term trends:
1. Energy security
Recent geopolitical shocks have made energy security a 
priority for governments around the world, accelerating 
investment in energy production and infrastructure.
2. Electrification
Transport, industry and buildings are becoming increasingly 
electrified, driving sustained growth in power demand and 
creating significant infrastructure requirements.
3. AI and data centres
AI is emerging as a major new source of electricity demand, 
increasing the need for reliable generation, transmission 
and grid capacity.
Our global energy model points 
to sustained growth
Together, these forces point to a sustained increase in 
global energy demand over the coming decades. Using 
our proprietary global energy model, we forecast energy 
demand growth of 1.9% per year between 2025 and 2050, 
above the Energy Information Administration’s (EIA) 
current forecast. AI is expected to become an increasingly 
important driver of energy consumption. By 2040, we 
estimate data centres could require energy equivalent to 
Russia’s current energy consumption. At the same time, we 
expect electric vehicles will become much more common, 
accounting for about 60% of the global vehicle fleet by 
2050. As a result, transportation’s share of global energy 
consumption falls to ~14% by 2050 from 17.5% today. 
A global race for capital
We estimate annual investment across the global energy 
system could reach $3.6trn by 2027, more than three times 
the capital currently required for planned AI infrastructure 
development. Meeting energy demand will require one 
of the largest investment cycles in modern industrial 
history. Capital will be needed across the global energy 
value chain, but the most acute shortfalls are emerging 
in the transmission networks, storage systems and grid 
infrastructure required to make energy usable.
Energy security is increasingly shaping 
both policy and investment decisions
Against a backdrop of geopolitical fragmentation, 
ensuring access to reliable, affordable and secure energy 
has become a central policy objective. Governments are 
supporting domestic production, infrastructure investment 
and more resilient supply chains, making energy security 
an increasingly important driver of both policy and capital 
allocation decisions.
Increased risk of higher energy-driven inflation
Previous periods of energy underinvestment were 
often followed by a supply response. This cycle may be 
different. Rising demand from electrification, AI and energy 
security initiatives are occurring at the same time as 
supply constraints, permitting delays and infrastructure 
bottlenecks. If supply struggles to keep pace, energy 
markets could remain tighter for longer, increasing the risk 
of higher energy prices, renewed commodity inflation and a 
more persistent inflation backdrop than investors currently 
anticipate.

The global energy race
AI’s speed with which it spreads through the world economy, the use 
cases it generates in industry after industry, its effect on the labour 
market, the societal implications, etc.: this will be the defining macro 
theme for the next decade. The global energy race, we believe, will be 
a close second.

A decade ago, the prevailing narrative about the energy 
transition was sequential. Renewables would rise, but fossil 
fuels were supposed to decline. The energy system would 
transition from one to the other, in an orderly fashion. However, 
in 2025, the use of every major energy source – oil, natural gas, 
coal, solar, wind, nuclear – notched new highs. All of them. Oil 
consumption rose to a record. Natural gas hit a record. Coal hit 
a record. And renewable capacity additions were the largest in 
history, with solar power alone meeting more than a quarter of 
global demand growth for the first time1. This is not what the 
consensus predicted. The reality of the energy transition has 
been messier, more expensive, and hence more consequential 
for capital markets.
The world is not substituting one energy system for another. 
It is running both simultaneously, and it does not have enough 
capital deployed in either. Energy demand is rising at the same 
time as geopolitical fragmentation is increasing the premium 
on energy security. The result is a growing race for capital 
across resources, infrastructure, and power systems – a race 
that the current level of investment is not winning. Not only 
has the energy transition not stopped, but the next phase will 
require significantly more investment across a far broader set 
of assets than the market currently appreciates. That includes 
not only renewables and electrification, but also oil and gas 
exploration, LNG export capacity, power transmission and 
storage networks, refining resilience, energy services, and 
other critical infrastructure that keeps the system functioning 
while it transforms.
The capital misallocation
The energy transition was always going to be expensive. 
What was underestimated was how long the existing system 
would need to keep running at full capacity while the new one 
was being built. Between 2015 and 2022, a combination of ESG-
driven capital reallocation, shareholder pressure for returns 
over growth, and policy signals favouring clean energy led to a 
sustained reduction in upstream oil and gas investment. Global 
upstream capital expenditure is still roughly 45% below peak 
levels. The industry was told – by investors, regulators, and in 
some cases its own management teams – that demand was 
peaking and that new supply investment was stranded capital. 
The market acted accordingly. But demand did not peak.
We anticipate underlying oil demand growth at over 6 million 
barrels per day in 2025-2030, driven overwhelmingly by 
non-OECD economies. At the same time, the industry must 
replace more than 7 million barrels per day of output annually 
just to maintain existing supply, before any demand growth is 
accounted for2. It is not investing enough to do even that.
Meanwhile, clean energy investment has surged, reaching 
a record $2.3 trillion in 2025, more than double the $1 trillion 
flowing into fossil fuels. This is a remarkable achievement. 
But it has not been sufficient to displace the fossil fuel system 
it was intended to replace.
The arithmetic is unforgiving: global energy demand grew 
1.3% in 2025, and while low-emissions sources contributed 
nearly 60% of that, the remaining 40% still required more 
oil, more gas, and more coal3. The world added clean energy 
at an impressive pace. It just was not fast enough to allow 
the old system to shrink. The result is a system that is 
underinvested on both sides. Traditional energy has been 
starved of the capital needed to maintain supply. Clean energy, 
while attracting record investment, remains geographically 
concentrated – roughly 75% of global clean energy investment 
flows to a small number of markets – and has not yet built the 
grid infrastructure, storage capacity, or baseload reliability 
needed to serve as a full replacement. The capital gap exists 
everywhere one looks.

And now come the new demand 
drivers
What makes the current moment different from previous 
cycles of energy underinvestment is that demand is being 
driven by forces that are structural, rather than cyclical. 
Three in particular deserve attention.
1. Electrification is accelerating globally
Global electricity demand is forecast to grow 3.7% in 2026, 
more than twice the rate of total energy demand. The 
International Energy Agency (IEA) projects this pace will 
continue through 2027 and beyond, driven by electric vehicles, 
heat pumps, air conditioning, and industrial electrification, 
particularly in emerging economies where appliance ownership 
and cooling demand are rising rapidly. Electrification is the 
mechanism through which de-carbonisation is supposed to 
work by moving end-uses from direct fossil fuel combustion 
to the grid, then cleaning the grid. The problem is that the first 
half of that equation is proceeding faster than the second. 
Countries are electrifying their economies before the grid – 
and the generation capacity behind it – is ready.
2. AI and data centres are driving a step-change 
in electricity demand
Global data centre electricity consumption is forecast to reach 
565 terawatt-hours in 2026, up 26% from 447 TWh in 2025. 
Related power demand is expected to reach 132 gigawatts this 
year and could hit 290 gigawatts by 2030. AI-optimised servers 
now account for 31% of total data centre power consumption, 
up from roughly 20% a year ago4. The IEA projects that data 
centre electricity consumption could double to 945 TWh by

https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-06-10-gartner-says-data-center-electricity-demand-to-grow-26-percent-in-2026
2030, approximately 3% of total global electricity demand. 
This is not a rounding error. It is a new structural source of 
baseload electricity demand that did not exist at scale five 
years ago; that is growing 25-30% annually; and that requires 
the kind of reliable, continuous power supply that intermittent 
renewables alone cannot yet provide. It is showing up in the 
inflation data – AI-related electricity costs are a measurable 
contributor to the rise in core PCE – and is reshaping utility 
planning, grid investment, and power procurement across 
every major economy.
3. Geopolitical shocks have repriced energy 
security
The war in Ukraine, the conflict with Iran, and the broader 
fragmentation of the global trading system have fundamentally 
altered how governments think about energy supply. The 
assumption that energy could be sourced from the cheapest 
global supplier, transported through open sea lanes, and priced 
in transparent commodity markets has been challenged in 
ways that are not reversible. Europe’s forced pivot away from 
Russian pipeline gas – replacing it with LNG from the United 
States, Qatar, and a patchwork of alternative suppliers – was 
the most dramatic example, but it is not the only one. Asia’s 
LNG importers scrambled to diversify supply chains after the 
Iran conflict threatened critical transit routes. Countries across 
the Middle East, Africa, and Latin America are positioning 
themselves as alternative suppliers and competing to attract 
international capital.
The result is a global energy system that is less integrated, 
more regionalised, and more expensive to operate, requiring 
significantly more redundant infrastructure than the pre-2022 
model assumed.

Energy security is reshaping capital 
allocation
The geopolitical dimension deserves particular emphasis 
because it changes the investment calculus in ways that 
standard energy models do not capture. When energy security 
is taken for granted, as it largely was in the two decades before 
2022, capital allocation optimises for cost. The cheapest source 
of supply wins. That logic favoured concentrated production 
(OPEC, Russia), long global supply chains (LNG tankers, oil 
tankers through the Strait of Hormuz), and minimal investment 
in redundancy. It was efficient. It was also fragile. The system 
that is emerging is different.
Countries are building strategic reserves, contracting for long-
term LNG supply, investing in domestic production capacity, 
and – critically – diversifying not just their energy sources but 
also their energy suppliers. This is not cost optimised. It is 
resilience optimised. And resilience requires capital. More LNG 
regasification terminals. More pipeline interconnectors. More 
strategic storage. More refining capacity distributed across 
regions, rather than concentrated in the lowest-cost locations. 
Global refining is a case in point. Permanent plant closures and 
war-related damage cut global refinery output an estimated 
4.5 million barrels per day in the second quarter of 20265. Crack 
spreads – the margin between crude oil and refined products 
– have reached record levels, with the US 3-2-1 crack spread 
hitting $64 per barrel and global refining margins touching 
$59. These are not normal numbers. They reflect a structural 
shortage of refining capacity that years of underinvestment 
and geopolitical disruption have created. Even if both the 
Ukraine and Iran conflicts were to end tomorrow, it would 
take years to rebuild the refining capacity that has been lost. 
The market is telling us, in the clearest possible terms, that 
the downstream infrastructure is insufficient. The same logic 
applies across the energy value chain.
Grid investment needs to rise 50% by 2030, from roughly 
$400 billion annually to over $600 billion, just to meet 
national climate targets, according to the IEA. More than 
2,500 gigawatts of renewable energy, storage, and large-
load projects (including data centres) are currently stalled 
in grid connection queues worldwide6. The bottleneck is 
not generation. It is the wires, transformers, substations, 
and interconnectors that move electricity from where it is 
produced to where it is consumed. This infrastructure takes 
years to permit and build, has been chronically underfunded, 
and is now the binding constraint on both the energy transition 
and the AI buildout.

Energy addition, not substitution, 
is the present – and the future
The data support a clear conclusion: the world is in an era of 
energy addition, not substitution. All major energy sources are 
growing. The IEA’s own Global Energy Review 2026 confirms that 
oil, natural gas, and coal each reached record consumption levels 
in 2025. Renewables are growing faster – solar PV contributed 
the largest single share of demand growth for the first time – but 
they are growing in addition to fossil fuels, not instead of them7. 
This is likely to persist for several years. Non-OECD energy 
demand is being driven by industrialisation, urbanisation, and 
rising living standards, forces that are not discretionary and 
that require energy in all its forms. India’s electricity demand is 
growing 6-7% per year. Southeast Asia is building coal plants 
alongside solar farms. The Middle East is investing in renewable 
capacity and upstream oil production simultaneously.
The assumption that the world would transition linearly 
from fossil fuels to renewables was always an abstraction. 
The reality is a system that needs more of everything, for 
longer than the models assumed. This is not an argument 
against the energy transition. It is an argument for realism 
about its pace and its capital requirements.
The transition is happening: renewable capacity additions, EV 
adoption rates, and clean energy investment are all at record 
levels. But it is happening inside a system where total energy 
demand continues to grow, where fossil fuel infrastructure is 
ageing and underinvested, where geopolitical risk demands 
redundancy, and where entirely new sources of demand (AI, 
data centres, electrification) are adding load faster than the 
grid can absorb it.
The net result is that investment is required across resources 
and infrastructure – traditional and renewable, upstream 
and downstream, generation and transmission – to maintain 
a secure, affordable, and resilient energy system. The capital 
requirements are larger, more diverse, and more sustained 
than the market is pricing.
Opportunities across sectors 
and markets
The macro framing is simple. Energy demand is growing. 
Energy security is being repriced. The capital deployed across 
the system is insufficient. And the next phase of the energy 
transition, whether measured in barrels, megawatts, or miles 
of transmission line, will require more investment, across more 
assets, for longer than the market currently expects. The 
question is not whether this capital will be deployed. It is who 
deploys it, where, and at what return.

Barclays’ global energy model
Rising energy use globally
Global energy use is set to increase, in our base case, 
~1.9% CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate) over 2025-
50, reflective of population growth (+0.7% CAGR), rising real 
GDP (+2.5% CAGR) and real income growth (+1.8% CAGR). 
We also expect the global share of electric vehicles to rise to 
over 50% by 2050 and incremental data centres to reach over 
600GW, the bulk of which (400GW) we expect to be in the 
US. At the same time, we expect carbon emissions growth to 
be below the rate of global energy use, due to both greater 
electrification and a growing share of renewables (+15-20pts, 
to ~50%) in the global energy mix. Our base case is for a more 
moderate increase in CO2 emissions per capita (+0.6% CAGR) 
through 2050.
Emerging markets will drive higher 
emissions with higher energy use
On a regional level, our base case is for emerging markets to 
be the key driver of global energy use, with India (+3.8% CAGR) 
showing the strongest growth. Our base case has China, the 
largest energy consumer today, growing at a slightly slower 
pace (+2.4% CAGR) than some emerging economies, but still 
outpacing the US (+1.5%). We expect China’s energy usage 
to be heavily driven by energy efficiency in industrial and 
commercial sectors (where we eased the EIA’s assumption 
of significant energy efficiency gains). For CO2 emissions, 
we see India (+2.6% CAGR) registering the largest growth in 
emissions, outpacing both China (2.0% CAGR) and the US 
(showing about flat).

A historic build-out of data centres
We estimate that data centre installed capacity growth 
will average more than 30% per year in 2026-27, before 
moderating to about 20% in 2028-29 (new capacity additions 
could plateau in 2028). The US is expected to be the largest 
contributor, with installed IT capacity rising from 34GW in 2025 
to over 110GW by 2029, implying over 40% growth in the first 
two years8. This represents a very significant acceleration 
in the US build-out relative to other regions. For the rest of 
the world, we assume a more moderate growth trajectory, 
reflecting slower demand progression and infrastructure 
deployment outside of the US. We assume non-US markets 
(ex-China) will grow slower, while China is expected to pick up 
pace in the coming years.
Global installed data centre IT capacity reached some 70GW 
in 2025. We estimate that capacity additions could increase 
about 25GW in 2026 and more than 30GW in 2027, with the 
US accounting for the largest share of incremental capacity, 
contributing about 15GW and 21GW, respectively.
Beyond 2027, we expect the pace of additions to plateau, 
with the US adding a further 20GW+ per annum, reflecting 
a transition from the acceleration phase to a more sustained 
growth trajectory, as indicated above.

Growth in electrical vehicle use
We expect a steady increase in global EV fleet penetration, 
with EV mix as a percentage of the overall fleet growing at 
about 15% CAGR between 2020 and 2050, reaching nearly 
60% of the fleet in 2050.
China
Among the major markets, China will lead the EV fleet 
transition by a notable margin. Growth in the EV fleet mix 
is driven by high EV penetration of new vehicle sales (about 
64% in 2026, rising to over 90% by the mid-2040s), as well as 
government incentives to encourage the transition (such as 
“scrappage” programs that have been in place since 2024).
Europe
Growth in the EV fleet mix will likely be driven by regulatory 
policy, as the EU is maintaining its 2035 targets. EV penetration 
is expected to have a sharp uptick from roughly 30% in 2025 to 
about 75% in 2035.
US
The EV fleet mix will lag due to slower adoption of EVs, with 
2035 new sales of about 38%, roughly half the level expected 
in China/EU. This reflects the lack of regulatory support for EV 
adoption, as well as the high mix of large pickup trucks/large 
SUVs in the fleet, as these segments will likely be the slowest 
to electrify.
Rest of the World
We see uptake as somewhat contingent on the extent to which 
Chinese manufacturers continue to export EVs into these 
markets. The EV fleet transition will be more mixed across 
countries, with East Asia and Southeast Asia likely leading 
adoption.

A new era for energy expansion
Transition: A secular capital 
allocation cycle
We believe the global energy transition is entering a new phase. 
The first one was defined by climate ambitions, falling clean-
technology costs and rapid deployment, helping establish 
clean power as the dominant source of incremental generation. 
Increasingly, however, the transition is being driven by secular 
growth factors beyond climate.
Energy security, competitiveness and sovereignty are 
becoming transition drivers on a par with sustainability. The 
transition is now closely tied to AI infrastructure, defence and 
domestic industrial capacity, with countries pursuing pathways 
shaped by their resource endowments, industrial priorities 
and energy-import vulnerabilities. Rising power demand, 
transmission constraints and geopolitical fragmentation are 
further reshaping energy and technology flows. In the US, 
growing power demand and the pursuit of energy dominance 
continue to support investment despite a less favourable 
policy backdrop for parts of the clean-energy sector.
Across Asia and Europe, clean energy is increasingly being 
deployed as strategic infrastructure, broadening the 
transition’s foundations beyond climate policy and making the 
underlying capital cycle more durable.
We view this as a structurally bullish signal for both clean and 
conventional energy. Investment across the energy system 
continues to rise, with clean energy and electrification 
solutions gaining share of global energy spending. Clean 
energy investment reached parity with hydrocarbons only 
in 2019 but is expected to be twice as large in 2026. The IEA 
expects total energy investment to reach $3.4trn in 2026, with 
almost two-thirds directed towards clean energy technologies 
and infrastructure (Figure 5).
We expect capex across the energy sector, including oil and 
gas, LNG, power, grids, renewables and electrification, to reach 
$3.6trn in 2027. Our forecasts reflect the gap between the 
supply capacity supported by investment over the past decade 
and the capacity required to meet future demand. Put simply, 
above-trend energy demand growth will require above-trend 
growth in spending.
A multi-decade investment gap, 
not a temporary capex lift
Rapidly rising energy transition capex is still insufficient to meet 
global energy needs. The energy transition is a multi-decade 
megatrend. It will require capital not only for greenfield assets, 
but also for transforming the existing high-emitting capital stock. 
In short, the energy transition requires trillions of dollars of 
investment, and current spending remains insufficient.

The composition of this investment matters as much as 
the total. As noted above, the first phase of the transition 
was principally about adding clean generation and lowering 
technology costs. The next one is likely to be more system-
wide and capital-intensive: connecting clean generation, 
balancing variable output, electrifying final demand, 
transforming high-emitting industrial assets and protecting 
infrastructure against physical risks. 
We also identify rising system friction, with renewable capacity 
expanding while grid investment lags, clean-generation 
costs falling while integration costs rise, and electrification 
advancing without sufficient clean power or system flexibility. 
The constraint is shifting from whether clean technologies can 
scale to the ability of the wider energy system to absorb that 
scale reliably and economically.
In the new energy equation, 
reliability is attracting a premium
An increasingly electrified economy raises the value of clean, 
firm baseload power. While renewables will remain a leading 
source of new generation, nuclear, hydro, geothermal, long-
duration storage and other flexible low-carbon technologies 
should be viewed as complements rather than competing 
transition pathways. We are particularly constructive on 
nuclear, given its role at the intersection of decarbonisation, 
energy security and AI-related power demand, and believe the 
sector is experiencing a renaissance. Looking further ahead, 
nuclear fusion has the potential to transform the energy 
system and address the energy trilemma.
The broader lesson is that strategic importance does not 
automatically translate into attractive returns. China’s 
manufacturing scale and technological advances have 
lowered the cost of solar, batteries and other clean-energy 
technologies, accelerating deployment but compressing 
margins across commoditised parts of the value chain.
As a result, being positive on clean-energy deployment 
is not the same as being uniformly bullish on clean-tech 
manufacturers. Value may accrue more consistently to 
developers with contracted demand and route-to-grid 
advantages or to scarcer enabling assets such as transmission, 
transformers, storage and grid software. Geopolitical 
fragmentation further complicates capital allocation as 
countries balance low-cost equipment against supply-chain 
resilience and energy security.

Ultimately, closing the investment gap requires more than 
identifying the right technologies. Projects need viable revenue 
models, predictable regulation, manageable execution risk and 
access to capital. Without these conditions, even economically 
necessary infrastructure can struggle to attract investment.
Transmission is becoming as 
important as generation
The most acute shortfall is not necessarily in clean generation 
itself, but in the infrastructure required to make that generation 
usable. Cheap generation does not necessarily imply reliable 
power. Its value depends increasingly on route-to-grid, 
storage, demand flexibility, interconnection and the ability 
of the power system to manage changing patterns of supply 
and demand. The Iberian blackout illustrates the challenge. 
The Spanish government’s investigation identified insufficient 
voltage-control capacity as a principal contributor, rather than 
high renewable penetration or a simple lack of system inertia. 
Renewable generation was not inherently the problem, but its 
rapid growth had exposed weaknesses in a power system that 
had not been modernised sufficiently quickly. The investment 
is also needed for large renewable adopters such as China and 
India, evidenced by their latest capex budgets.
Bringing network investment closer to generation could define 
the next phase of the transition. Global investment into grids 
amounted to roughly $400bn globally in 2024, compared with 
about $1trn in generation assets, highlighting the potential 
scale of the investment opportunity as this ratio moves closer 
to parity (Figure 8). Our Energy Transition Barometer similarly 
finds renewable capacity growth outpacing grid investment 
in several regions, contributing to congestion, negative 
wholesale prices and greater volatility where network capacity 
and flexibility have failed to keep pace (Figure 9). We do not 
view this as a sign that the transition is failing, but rather that 
it is entering a more infrastructure-intensive phase that will 
require transmission, distribution, storage, transformers, 
cables, interconnection and digital grid management.

The financing challenge: mobilising 
capital at scale through ESG bonds
Delivering the next phase of the energy system will 
require trillions of dollars of investment across generation, 
transmission, storage, industrial infrastructure and energy-
security assets, presenting a significant financing challenge. 
Mobilising sufficient capital may prove just as important as 
identifying the technologies needed to deliver the transition.
The ESG bond market is one mechanism through which 
this capital can be deployed. Over the past decade, green, 
sustainability and sustainability-linked bonds have evolved 
into a multi-trillion-dollar market (Figure 10) and an 
important source of financing for corporates, sovereigns and 
supranational organisations in particular. To date, however, 
both issuance and use-of-proceeds have been concentrated 
in a relatively narrow set of sectors and activities. As a result, 
labelled debt markets have played an important role in 
financing renewable energy, clean transportation and green 
buildings, but have been used less extensively in many of the 
sectors and activities facing the greatest transition financing 
needs.
ESG-labelled bonds can also offer issuers benefits beyond 
financing specific projects. They can help diversify and broaden 
the investor base, provide a mechanism for demonstrating 
transition and sustainability commitments, and may offer 
issuance-related advantages through stronger order books 
and, in some cases, modest pricing benefits.
The challenge is that many of the sectors facing the greatest 
capital requirements in the next phase of the energy transition 
have made relatively limited use of ESG-labelled financing. This 
matters because the new energy equation is about more than 
renewable deployment alone. It requires investment across 
a broader set of assets, including industrial transformation, 
energy-security infrastructure and technologies that reduce 
emissions from existing systems.
This is where transition finance could become increasingly 
important. Transition bonds have the potential to channel 
capital towards decarbonisation activities in sectors that 
cannot easily issue traditional green bonds because their 
activities are not fully aligned with a low-carbon end-state 
today. However, adoption remains relatively limited. In our 
view, further market development will require greater policy 
and market clarity about what constitutes a credible transition 
investment.
Electricity networks are becoming one of the most important 
enablers of the energy transition. While labelled financing 
has historically focused on renewable generation, growing 
electrification and energy-security requirements are 
increasing the need for investment in transmission and 
distribution infrastructure. Given the role networks play in 
supporting system decarbonisation and broader energy 
resilience, we see scope for both green and transition bond 
markets to play a larger role in financing future grid expansion.

Energy in the age of fragmentation
Why energy security matters more 
than ever
For decades, global energy flows were largely driven by 
efficiency. Energy was produced where it was cheapest and 
transported around the world through increasingly integrated 
supply chains.
That model is now changing.
Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, tensions in the Middle East and 
growing competition between major powers have reminded 
governments that energy security cannot be taken for 
granted. At the same time, technological shifts, including AI, 
electrification and renewable energy are changing how energy 
is produced and where it is consumed.
The result is a new era of energy geopolitics. Access to 
energy remains critical, but governments and businesses are 
increasingly focused on resilience as well as efficiency. So the 
focus is now not simply if energy is available, it is whether it is 
secure, reliable and affordable.
Three challenges will shape the next 
decade
1. Securing Energy Supply
The world still relies heavily on oil and natural gas.
Recent disruptions have highlighted the risks of depending 
on a small number of suppliers or transport routes. As a 
result, countries are diversifying imports, expanding storage 
capacity and investing in new production, pipelines and LNG 
infrastructure.
Energy security is increasingly driving investment decisions 
alongside economics.
2. Meeting Growing Electricity Demand
The rapid growth of AI, data centres, electric vehicles and 
electrification is creating a new challenge: supplying enough 
electricity.
Meeting this demand requires far more than power generation 
alone. It also requires new grids, transmission networks, 
storage systems and backup capacity.
This means the energy transition is becoming a large-scale 
infrastructure challenge as well as a climate challenge.
3. Developing New Energy Technologies
Innovation will remain central to energy systems.
Renewable technologies continue to improve, while interest 
in nuclear power is increasing globally. Further ahead, 
breakthroughs such as commercial nuclear fusion could 
transform the economics of energy altogether.
Access to both capital and scientific expertise will increasingly 
determine which countries lead this next phase.

18
The return of energygeography
Perhaps the most important consequence of fragmentation is 
that geography matters again.
For much of the past three decades, economic activity tended 
to locate where labour was cheapest. In the decades ahead, 
energy availability may become just as important.
Energy-intensive industries, data centres and future AI 
infrastructure are increasingly being drawn towards regions 
with abundant and affordable power. In that sense, energy 
could play a similar role in the next phase of globalisation as 
cheap labour played in the last.
The countries best positioned for the next energy era are 
likely to be those that combine secure energy supplies, 
infrastructure, technology and access to capital.
Who benefits from the new energy 
map?
The shift towards electrification is changing the geography of 
energy.
Historically, economic influence in energy was concentrated 
among oil and gas producers. In the future, countries 
with abundant renewable resources, strong electricity 
infrastructure, critical minerals or advanced technology 
capabilities could become equally important.
This creates new winners and new dependencies.
The United States
Combines fossil fuel resources, renewable energy potential, 
technological leadership and deep capital markets.
China
Has established a dominant position in renewable technology 
manufacturing and critical mineral processing.
Europe
Remains capital-rich but will need major investment in grids, 
infrastructure and energy security.
The Gulf region
Is likely to remain a major energy exporter while increasingly 
investing in digital infrastructure and data centres.
Resource-rich economies
Africa, Latin America and parts of Asia may benefit from 
growing demand for critical minerals and energy supplies.
Australia
Stands out as a rare combination of abundant energy 
resources, critical minerals and political stability.

Case study 1
Public policy: 
Can the US build power fast enough? 
Meeting rising energy demand will require the rapid expansion of generation and transmission 
capacity. In our view, policy and permitting constraints could prevent the US from expanding 
energy infrastructure quickly enough and at sufficient scale to keep pace with demand.
Permitting has become increasingly 
complex
Permitting is often discussed as a single process, but in 
practice it involves multiple federal, state and local reviews 
that can add years to project timelines. For large infrastructure 
projects, we estimate developers may face more than 60 
permits and reviews across numerous agencies.
Delays are common. Across 104 energy-related projects in 
the federal permitting system, nearly one-third of completed 
milestones have been late. State and local approvals can 
further extend timelines, while litigation risk remains 
significant. Almost 30% of large energy projects have been 
cancelled or paused10.
10	 US Federal Permitting Dashboard Data Portal
Prior permitting reforms have 
underdelivered
Congress has repeatedly attempted to streamline permitting, 
but progress has been modest. The 2023 Fiscal Responsibility 
Act introduced a two-year deadline for environmental impact 
statements, yet only 39% have met that target. Meaningful 
change may prove difficult to achieve quickly.
The Trump administration has sought to accelerate permitting 
for energy and data-centre projects through executive action, 
and some permitting metrics have improved. However, it 
remains too early to conclude that review times for major 
projects have materially shortened. 
More importantly, executive action has inherent limitations: 
it cannot fully address the federal, state and local constraints 
that slow infrastructure development. Without broader 
legislative reform, we believe permitting will remain a 
significant barrier to expanding US energy infrastructure at the 
speed and scale required.

Why energy demand could keep inflation 
higher for longer
Why this cycle may be different
Despite growing EV adoption, global oil demand continues 
to rise, while supply growth is slowing as US production 
approaches a peak and investment elsewhere struggles to 
keep pace. We forecast global oil demand growth of c.1.1mb/d 
annually between 2027 and 2030, reaching 110.6mb/d by 
2030. In our view, current prices underestimate this underlying 
market tightness, leaving commodity prices vulnerable to 
renewed upside pressure.
Rising energy demand may increase 
inflation expectations more than 
the market expects
For much of the past decade, inflation has been shaped 
primarily by labour markets, supply-chain disruptions and 
central bank policy. We believe energy could become a more 
important driver of inflation than many investors currently 
expect.
The reason is straightforward. Energy demand is accelerating, 
while at the same time, supply growth is struggling to keep 
pace as permitting delays, infrastructure bottlenecks and years 
of underinvestment limit the speed at which new capacity can 
be brought online.
This creates an important question for policymakers and 
investors: could the energy supply-demand imbalance become a 
more powerful driver of inflation than other disinflationary forces?
Much of today’s market narrative assumes that AI will be 
inflationary in the near term but ultimately disinflationary 
through productivity gains. While we broadly agree with this 
framework, it may underestimate the inflation risks associated 
with the large increase in energy demand required to support 
that growth.
As such, the next inflation cycle may be driven less by 
wages and more by commodities, energy and infrastructure 
constraints.
Three ways energy can increase 
inflation risk
1. Higher Baseline Inflation
The most direct impact is through higher energy prices. 
If energy demand continues to grow faster than supply, upward 
pressure on oil, natural gas and electricity prices is likely to 
persist. Because energy affects transportation, manufacturing, 
food production and industrial activity, higher energy costs 
can flow through to a broad range of goods and services 
throughout the economy.

Even if energy prices do not surge dramatically, a structurally 
tighter energy market could result in inflation settling at a 
higher level than investors currently expect.
2. Greater inflation volatility
The second effect is increased volatility.
Tighter energy markets are generally less able to absorb 
unexpected shocks. This means geopolitical disruptions, 
weather events, infrastructure outages or supply interruptions 
can have a larger effect on prices.
As a result, inflation may become more volatile and less 
predictable. For businesses, consumers and policymakers, this 
uncertainty can be almost as important as the level of inflation 
itself.
3. More frequent inflation shocks
The final risk is asymmetry.
In theory, higher volatility could produce both positive and 
negative price surprises. In practice, today’s geopolitical 
environment suggests risks are tilted towards higher prices, 
rather than lower ones.
Recent years have demonstrated how quickly geopolitical 
events can disrupt energy markets. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine 
in 2022 and the US-Iran conflict in 2026 both generated large 
energy-price shocks that fed into inflation expectations 
worldwide.
As geopolitical fragmentation increases, such events may 
become more common. This creates a world in which inflation 
risks are not evenly balanced, but skewed to the upside.
Why it matters
Electrification, AI and energy security are creating powerful 
new sources of demand at a time when supply and 
infrastructure are struggling to keep pace. The result is a 
system that is potentially more inflationary, more volatile and 
more vulnerable to geopolitical shocks than investors have 
become accustomed to over the past decade.
If that assessment proves correct, energy may become one 
of the most important determinants of inflation in the years 
ahead. Put together, we see the above dynamics as helping to 
explain why investors are positioning for a rise in inflation risk 
premium.

Case study 2
Physical AI and energy
AI gets physical, powered by energy
The investment debate about robotics has so far largely 
focused on chips and AI models. Yet as AI moves from software 
into the physical world, energy is becoming increasingly 
important. Every humanoid robot, autonomous vehicle, 
warehouse robot and drone ultimately converts electricity 
into movement. As a result, the rise of physical AI could create 
a new source of electricity demand that extends well beyond 
data centres and into factories, warehouses and logistics 
networks. Put differently, physical AI is effectively creating 
a new category of energy consumer: machines capable of 
performing physical labour.
Robots are a new category of energy 
consumer
Physical AI is still a small market today, but our research 
suggests it could evolve into a $1trn ecosystem by 2035, 
spanning humanoids, autonomous vehicles, industrial 
automation and drones. As deployment accelerates, investors 
may need to think about robotics as not only a technology 
theme, but also an energy theme. Unlike traditional software, 
physical AI creates two-layer energy demand: cloud compute 
to train increasingly complex models and local power to run 
robots performing tasks in the physical world.
Training physical AI models is particularly energy intensive 
because they must learn not only reasoning, but also physics, 
motion, balance, navigation and manipulation, much of it 
through large-scale simulations and digital twins. Advanced 
AI model training currently consumes 100-154 MW of energy, 
with projections exceeding 4 GW by 2030. While data on 
physical AI models remain limited, we expect their energy 
requirements to be even greater, given the added complexity 
of learning and executing tasks in the physical world.
The second layer of energy demand comes from physical 
execution. Once deployed, robots continuously consume 
electricity through motors, sensors, processors and 
communication systems. Unlike large language models, robots 
must operate in the physical world, overcoming gravity, inertia 
and friction. Put simply, movement is energy intensive, making 
batteries, typically lithium-ion, a critical enabling technology.
Batteries are improving but remain a constraint. Battery 
density has increased about 50% since 2019 (Figure 17), while 
battery prices have fallen almost eightfold since 2013 (Figure 
18). These advances have been critical in making mobile 
robotics economically viable. Yet batteries remain one of the 
sector’s biggest constraints.
Current humanoids typically operate for only 4-5 hours before 
requiring recharging or battery replacement. More intensive 
tasks can reduce operating time further. Even highly advanced
