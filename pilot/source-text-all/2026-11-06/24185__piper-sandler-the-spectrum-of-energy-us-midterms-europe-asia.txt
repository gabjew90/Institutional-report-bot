Whichever the outcome, permitting reform could pass:
Both parties are focused on the Bipartisan American 
Affordability and Jobs Act (aka BAAJA), which aims to speed 
up the construction of energy infrastructure by:
1) Including a limit of no more than 2 years for federal decisions 
on environmental approvals and restraints on agencies’ abilities 
to withdraw permits once granted.
2) Amending the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) by 
reducing the scope of its applicability, i.e. who can sue and on 
what grounds, and shortening the litigation window. 
3) Accelerating the buildout of transmission lines by enhancing 
FERC’s backstop authority, which gives the federal government 
more authority to approve major lines and helps projects move 
through the permitting process much faster. 
4) On data centers, the bill is a step up from the Ratepayer 
Protection Act in that it forces more energy behind the meter and 
establishes rules to ensure data centers are responsible for 
energy costs until all incremental costs are fully recovered (even 
after contract terminations).
As noted in another recent note (link), the PSC Policy team 
believes the bill has a 50/50 chance of signed into law this year, 
which is more constructive than betting market expectations 
(which sit at ~40%), because the Democrats who negotiated the 
deal did so with the goal to pass it during the lame duck session.
However, attempting to pass this during the lame duck session carries its own set of risks: 1) The 
Trump admin continuing to meddle with offshore wind permitting could undermine Democratic support; 2) 
House Republicans could feel they have insufficient ownership of the bill and drain floor time; 3) If the 
Democrats were to outperform in the midterms, they could attempt to renegotiate for more favorable terms and 
end up punting the deal to the next Congress; and 4) Time is a key a factor working against the bill because it 
may be scarce during the lame duck period. Despite its significance, BAAJA falls behind more pressing 
agenda items such as government funding (which carries a Dec 11 deadline) and an Iran war supplemental.

IN FOCUS: GLOBAL LNG – WINTER IS COMING FOR ASIA & THE EU
Ill-prepared, and absent Qatar’s 20% of global supply, weather decides who’ll pay up
We agree that this winter’s LNG market is 
obviously a huge weather call in the northeastern 
US, NE Asia and NW Europe. Rarely are all three 
abnormally cold, but equally rarely are they all warm. 
Either way, while in America the gas market is 
already bearish, this is peak anxiety time for Asia 
and especially Europe.
Asia’s principal LNG import markets have 
alternatives to LNG. But Japan, SKorea and Taiwan 
will still pull hard on LNG suppliers from Oct through 
Feb – witness the current spot premium of JKM over 
TTF, top right. And witness the surge in imports in 
July (amidst summer heat waves), which happily 
came when Europe ‘abstained’ from imports. 
The question becomes whether the El Nino will 
curb Asia’s normal appetite for LNG and free up 
cargoes for Europe.
El Nino Impact on Asia should skew risk warmer
That’s three qualifying statements. No one needs to 
be a weather ‘expert’ to see the uncertainty in that or 
in really cool charts like the one below. 
Europe is in the more precarious position. It doesn’t have enough LNG in storage to get through a severe 
winter, nor does it have enough heating oil and that old stand-by (diesel) is already mad expensive, but at 
least it does have strategic inventory of that stuff. 
The problem is that since Qatar went down, the two importing blocs compete over who gets close to normal 
volumes; there is not supply enough for both to import normally. We’re one more year away from new supply 
(mostly US) filling the gap – but that is cold comfort to European gas markets this winter [pun intended]
•
Maybe the super El Nino brewing in the Pacific can bail everyone out delivering warm winters …

We track Europe’s storage fill, or lack of it on p. 16. 
Not only is it still in deficit, but some early cold has 
reduced fill rates further and it’s becoming ever more 
clear that the situation in the Mideast is deteriorating 
and very far from improving. Not coincidentally TTF 
quoted €100/MWh
Winter weather obviously has the greater impact 
on Europe’s winter gas demand – see the table 
below as an indication of the variability even on a full 
winter-average basis – obv the correlation has shifted 
since 2022, but weather still rules
What adds dramatically to Q1 price risk is taking 
away 2+ bcf/d of imports of Russian LNG (which 
again unsurprisingly, have run higher recently). 
The European Commission in Brussels confirmed a 
full prohibition as of January 1, 2027 of Russian LNG 
imports into the EU, inclusive of legacy long-term 
contracts. 
That will effectively create two simultaneous changes 
in the global LNG trade: 1) Europe will have to 
replace those imports, while 2) Russia must find 
adequate shipping capacity for the cargoes that were 
once a short haul trade (from the Arctic to Europe) to 
destinations further afield. Logistically that’s even 
more of a nightmare given the need for ice-class LNG 
vessels to transfer their cargoes to regular LNG boats 
going much farther afield and the uncertainty of being 
able to use European ports for the transferring of 
cargoes between the two.

As we demonstrate in the charts below, demand data 
suggest end-users (and downstream inventories) 
have been absorbing most of the shock. 
Record high prices and super fat margins forced 
most of the adjusting, which includes refiners in the 
US running much harder, and raising exports. 
In addition, more marginal refiners cranked up runs 
allowing imports from Asia, to Africa to Latam and 
Europe all to reduce imports.

With the help of Alex Potter and his team’s auto-sector sales modeling, we can now forecast with 
significant confidence some 95% of global gasoline and 16% of diesel demand (equivalent to 
30.6 million barrels a day of oil demand, or ~28% of all oil consumption in 2025)  
• Global gasoline demand associated with Light Duty Vehicle use most likely peaks in the 2030s
• Out to 2030, the growth in conventional car fuel demand across Emerging Market 
economies will, we model, be less than the losses to NEVs in China, Europe and the USA 
Per the charts on the right, NEVs are currently 
displacing ~970 kb/d of road fuel demand, and we 
estimate that will rise to 2.3 mb/d by 2030 as 
adoption grows. This will mainly be driven by 
China, the US, and Europe but the Rest of World 
will play an increasingly important role too. In 
terms of oil demand, we estimate the US, China, 
and Europe will collectively decline by 0.7 mb/d 
but interestingly grow by 1.1 mb/d in our RoW 
regions. 
Uniquely our model uses historic fleet and/or 
sales data for 78 countries, which we marry to 
forecasts from our Auto and Tech team of Alex 
Potter and Ben Johnson, which they update at 
least 2x per year…
Earlier episodes in our NEV modeling series: the 
US [link], Europe [link], China [link], Western Hem. 
ex-US [link], APAC ex-China [link], and RoW [link].
From 2005 to 2019, global LDV fuel demand grew 
from 24 mb/d to 30 mb/d at a CAGR of 1.5%. The 
pandemic put that on hold, and since then growth 
slowed significantly. 
Through our forecast period from 2026-’30, that 
growth slows to a decline rate of 0.2%.
We model that the combined gasoline and diesel 
use of the global LDV fleet (every car and/or 
truck that weighs less than 15.4k lbs) will peak 
around 31.4 mb/d in 2029.
By 2030, we estimate road fuel demand to begin a 
decline that starts off rather shallow.
Without NEVs, global road fuel demand would 
have continued to grow at a CAGR of 1.1%, 
reaching ~33.7 mb/d by the end of the decade.
As such, the delta between real consumption and 
modeled fuel use without EVs translates to some 
2.3 mb/d of demand displacement by 2030.

US Biofuels: Background ‘rationale’ & framework
The US Congress created the Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) program to reduce greenhouse gas 
emissions and expand the nation’s renewable fuels sector while reducing reliance on imported oil. This 
program was authorized under the Energy Policy Act of 2005 and expanded under the Energy Independence 
and Security Act (EISA) of 2007. Renewable Identification Numbers (RINs) are credits used for compliance 
and are the ‘currency’ of the RFS program. Arguably all this made sense back then, when we imported 12 
mb/d of oil and smog was an issue. And after MTBE failed the test in the 1990s… things make less sense now.
Anyhoo, since no politician wants to lose the ag vote, we still have The Clean Air Act requiring the EPA to set 
the Required Volume Obligations (RVO) annually. Those volumes can of course be adjusted: EISA requires 
the EPA to evaluate and adjust the standards, which are to be finalized by Nov. 30 of the year prior to  
compliance. For cellulosic, the EPA sets volume requirements based on the EIA’s projections, the EPA’s 
assessment, and other factors. For biomass-based diesel, the EPA must determine the RVO at least 14 
months prior to the year of the mandate.  
The EPA released its finalized RVO on March 30th. The final version includes a ramp up across the board. 
After a slight 1% reduction in 2026, volumes of Cellulosic Biofuel will then rise by 5% in 2027. Additionally, the 
final rule included a 48% and 2% increase for Advanced Biofuels for 2026 and 2027, respectively. Factoring 
everything in, the RVO for total Renewable Fuels now stands at just shy of 26 billion gallons per year, which is 
a 16% increase from 2025’s final ruling.
The new RVO mandate means biofuels supplies have to rise by ~1,200 million gallons (81 kb/d) from current 
levels, which is bullish for biofuel margins and RIN prices since the mandate will require a significant increase 
in utilization across both biodiesel (BD) and renewable diesel (RD) capacity. Ultimately, this will drive a 
material increase in both BD and RD margins. We estimate that RD/BD utilization will need to approach 88% in 
2026 and over 90% in 2027 to balance a market that could be undersupplied by some 2.3 billion RINs.

To compare all four balances, we use the line “call on Opec” and more specifically y/y changes in

are good enough. From the IEA we take only historical demand supply & inventory data for OECD 
countries. They’re the authority on energy statistics for OECD members (aka the DM economies).
