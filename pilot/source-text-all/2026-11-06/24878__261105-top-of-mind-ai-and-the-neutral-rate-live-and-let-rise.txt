AI and the neutral rate: Live and let rise 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Where policy rates settle in the long run, and where long-term yields trade, depends on the real neutral rate of interest, r*. 
AI is now putting upward pressure on it from two sides. The build-out of data centres, chips and power is the largest 
investment boom in decades, and the productivity gains it promises could change how fast the economy grows. This note 
asks what both do to r*, in the US and the euro area. 
 
Our answer distinguishes between two phases. During the build-out, r* rises because firms want to invest more than the 
economy wants to save, whatever the eventual payoff. Once productivity gains arrive, who receives them determines how 
much higher r* stays; narrowly distributed gains increase saving, offsetting the upward pressure on r*. During both 
phases, other capex, ageing and climate shift the same balance of saving and investment.  
 
How AI moves r*: two curves, two phases 
The neutral rate is the real interest rate that balances desired saving and desired investment at full employment. Throughout, 
r* means this real rate. We look at two horizons: the next five years, over which a temporary surge in investment can lift the 
neutral rate while it lasts, and the period beyond, when only lasting changes in growth, saving or investment matter. With 
inflation expectations anchored at 2%, the nominal neutral rate is r* plus 2%. Cheaper production from AI lowers inflation 
for a while but not r*, and we treat the term premium separately. We summarise the intuition behind our results below. 
Build-out: investment up, saving unchanged, r* up. For the next few years, firms spend heavily on chips, data centres, 
power and the grid, increasingly with borrowed money, while the extra output this capacity will produce is still years away. 
Demand for saving rises with no extra saving to meet it, so r* rises. During this time, we estimate upward pressure of about 
0.2 pp on the neutral rate in the US.  
Productivity with broadly shared gains: investment up, saving unchanged, r* up. A higher return on capital makes more 
projects pay, so investment rises again. The extra income goes mostly to households that spend it, so saving does not rise to 
match, and r* rises by a cumulative 1 pp.  
Productivity with concentrated gains: investment up by less, saving up, r* ambiguous. Dominant firms invest less per 
unit of profit, so investment rises by less. Saving rises because income flows to high-saving households and firms, and 
workers at risk of displacement put more aside. With the two curves moving in opposite directions, the net effect on r* can 
go either way. The 2010s, with record tech profits and a very low r*, show how strong the saving side can be. In such a 
scenario, we estimate upward pressure of about 0.2 pp relative to a no-AI scenario.

changes in desired investment and desired saving, expressed as shares of GDP. They are measured against a world without

Phase 1: the build-out  
The build-out works through two channels that show up in different parts of the yield curve. The demand channel, the 
spending itself, lifts growth and prices, and with them the policy rates the Fed needs over the next few years. The bond-
supply channel, the borrowing that pays for it, should raise the term premium. We give the term premium extra space 
because it is the main way long yields can rise without r* rising, so the market evidence can only be read once the two are 
kept apart. Neither channel changes r* beyond the build-out; that takes Phase 2. 
The demand channel. According to consensus equity analyst forecasts, AI investment averages around 3% of GDP a year 
over 2025–2030, roughly three times the ICT investment of past booms. Capex growth is set to slow from more than 70% 
to around 40% in 2027, but the level keeps rising. In the third quarter, investment in information processing equipment and 
software added 1.1 pp to GDP growth of 3.4%, and over the past four quarters it has accounted for close to half of US 
growth, although there are some import offsets.  
The demand arrives years before the extra supply, and it is already showing up in prices. Prices of information processing 
equipment fell by 5–10% a year in 2019–20 and were still falling in late 2025, but have since turned up sharply, to 15.7% 
in September, as data centres absorb memory and components. Producer prices of switchgear and power transformers are 
up about 90% since 2019, against 26% for the same component in core consumer prices. This inflation argues for higher 
policy rates over the next year or two, not for a higher r*. Higher growth and inflation mean that the policy rate needs to be 
restrictive and hence held above r* for a time.  
The bond-supply channel. The build-out is turning AI firms from holders of cash into issuers of long-dated debt. Capex at 
the five largest hyperscalers absorbed 70% of their operating cash flow in 2025, up from about 40% in 2018–2020, and 
their combined debt roughly doubled to USD 503bn in the year to September 2026. What matters for the term premium is 
how this compares with the supply investors already absorb, which is dominated by Treasuries. In the year to September 
2026, the hyperscalers added USD 261bn of debt, against USD 1.4 trillion of net new Treasury notes, bonds, TIPS and 
floating-rate notes held by the public. That is about a fifth, up from almost nothing a year earlier. At the long end the share 
doubles. Of the USD 288bn of bonds the hyperscalers issued, 59% runs for ten years or more. Treasury supply beyond ten 
years grew by only USD 420bn, and beyond 15 years it shrank, as bonds issued in 2011 and 2020–21 rolled below that 
horizon. Beyond ten years, the hyperscalers added 40% as much debt as the Treasury, and beyond 15 years they were the 
only source of new supply. 
The build-out lifts r* while it lasts. Extra investment demand raises r* only while it lasts, and the build-out runs over 
roughly 2026–30, so its effect falls mostly within the horizon that short rates cover and should not move rates expected 
beyond it. The ICT boom followed the same pattern (Box 1). A higher neutral rate during the build-out and a stronger cycle 
both point to higher policy rates for a few years; neither implies a higher long-run r*. 
We estimate a boost to desired investment of 1–2% of GDP a year, net of imported hardware, against roughly 1% before 
the boom. Because the push is temporary, we size it by the stock of extra AI capital that savers have to finance: with 
servers and chips wearing out after about five years, it builds to about 4–7% of GDP by 2030 and fades over the early

Distinguishing a neutral-rate effect from a cyclical one. Both mean higher policy rates today, but they diverge over the 
next few years. If the build-out lifts the neutral rate, policy rates should stay high after the boost to inflation from IT 
equipment fades next year, and model estimates of r* should drift up before easing as investment growth slows. If the 
effect is cyclical, policy rates fall back with inflation. If AI firms keep borrowing at this pace, the term premium should also 
start to rise. Real rates over the long term should not rise because of the capex boom in either case; beyond five years, r* 
depends on Phase 2. 
Markets price a temporary bump in the neutral rate. Markets show which effects of the AI build-out are already visible in 
expected policy rates, in the term premium, or in the long-run real rate. We use two sets of news days to separate them. 
• 
AI capex news days. To isolate news about the scale of the build-out, we use 21 days of news about how much will be 
spent on AI compute, mostly Nvidia earnings releases, whose revenue largely reflects hyperscaler capex, plus 
landmark moments such as Stargate and DeepSeek. Because capex now runs ahead of hyperscalers’ cash flow, the 
same news also signals how much AI firms may need to borrow, so these days speak to both the demand and bond-
supply channels.  
• 
AI bond deal days. To isolate the bond-supply channel more directly, we also look at the ten largest hyperscaler bond 
deals since September 2025. If the growing supply of AI-related debt is large enough to change the price of duration, 
these are the days on which it should be most visible in long yields and the term premium.  
 
We split the market response between the 2-year yield, 
capturing policy rates over the next two years; the term 
premium; and the 5y5y real forward rate, the usual market 
proxy for long-run r*.  
The results show a clear divide. AI capex news moves the short 
end. On the average AI capex news day, the 2-year yield 
moves 2.4 bp in the direction of the news and the 10-year 1.3 
bp. But the 5y5y real forward rate does not move on average.   
AI bond issuance reaches the long end, but only weakly. On 
the days of the ten largest hyperscaler bond deals, the 10-year 
yield rose by about 2 bp and the term premium by 1–1.5 bp, 
but the 2-year rose almost as much. That points to dealers 
hedging new issues rather than a lasting repricing of duration.  
So far, AI shows up only in short rates, which is consistent with the higher neutral rate during the build-out we suggested 
above. AI capex news lifts the 2-year yield, but barely moves the term premium or long-run real rates. Even large AI bond 
deals leave no lasting mark. Markets therefore treat the build-out mainly as a reason for policy rates to stay higher for a 
few years, not yet as evidence of a lasting rise in r*.  
Phase 2: productivity gains 
If AI raises trend productivity, the return on capital rises and so does r*. The 1995–2004 boom shows that the effect is real 
but not automatic (Box 1): how much of the extra growth reaches r* depends on what households do with the extra 
income and on who receives it. 
We start with estimates of the potential productivity gain. Estimates of AI’s boost to US labour productivity growth over the 
next decade range from about 0.1 pp a year (Acemoglu, 2024) to 1.5 pp (Goldman Sachs, 2023). We use the OECD’s US 
range: 0.4 pp a year in the low-productivity scenarios and 0.9 pp in the high ones. These are decade averages, so we allow 
almost no gain during the build-out and apply the average by the mid-2030s; with an S-shaped adoption curve, the boost 
by then could be higher.

adds 0.5–1% of GDP to desired investment, which lifts r* further.  
When gains are concentrated, the extra income goes mostly to high savers, so we cut the pass-through further. Saving also 
rises: we assume the labour share falls by 2–5 pp and that income shifted to capital owners is saved at a 20–30 pp higher 
rate (Mian, Straub and Sufi, 2021), plus precautionary saving by workers at risk of displacement. That adds 0.5–2% of GDP 
to desired saving. At the same time, market power roughly diminishes the extra investment. 
One channel pushes the other way. Cheaper capital goods mean the same real investment needs less saving, one of the 
standard explanations for the fall in r* over past decades (Rachel and Smith, 2015), and AI makes computing power cheaper. 
While chip and grid-equipment prices are rising the effect is muted, but once compute prices resume their fall it could offset 
part of the capital-deepening push. 
The productivity boost raises r* only if the gains are shared. With large and broadly shared gains (0.9 pp a year), AI adds 
about 0.8 pp to US r* by the mid-2030s. If the same gains are concentrated, extra saving offsets almost all of that: the central 
estimate is close to zero. With smaller gains (0.4 pp a year), AI adds about 0.3 pp if they are shared and nothing if they are 
concentrated. 
A correction would reverse the build-out’s lift. Firms would cut capex sharply, as with IT investment in 2001–02, and 
investment would stay below its no-AI path until existing capacity is put to use, while falling equity prices and debt 
repayment would raise saving. We model it as capex falling 1–2% of GDP below plan for two to three years. That removes 
the lift of about 0.2 pp and takes US r* back to roughly its no-AI level, about 0.9% at the central trough and about 0% in the 
euro area; only a large rise in saving, or the upper-bound sensitivity, takes it as low as 0.5%. Central banks would follow r* 
down, the Fed faster than the ECB, and as AI firms stop issuing, pressure on the term premium eases. Once the excess 
capacity is used, r* moves back towards whichever productivity path AI ends up on. 
Europe: little of the build-out’s lift, but a share of the productivity gains 
The US builds AI; the euro area mainly adopts it. In 2027, the combined capex impulse from AI, defence and clean energy 
is about 1.6% of GDP in the US and 0.3% in the EU, where clean energy and defence dominate. The last productivity boom 
shows what such a gap means for r*. Between 1995 and 2000, US r* rose by 1.4 pp while the euro area estimate stayed 
broadly flat. Today, model estimates put US r* at around 1.0% and euro area r* close to zero, a gap of about 0.9 pp that 
has widened since 2023. Three pieces of evidence suggest the AI part of the gap will last at least through the build-out. 
 
 
Europe’s savings help pay for the US build-out. In the four quarters to Q3 2025, euro area residents bought EUR 409bn of 
US securities, 2.6% of GDP and more than the entire current account surplus of 1.8%, against about 1.4% of GDP before the 
pandemic; roughly half went into equities, where AI firms dominate. That helps meet US investment demand, softening the 
rise in US rates and leaving euro area rates largely untouched. Europe joins the build-out as an investor, not a producer.

Box 1: what the 1995–2004 boom says about r* 
The last US productivity boom is the closest precedent for AI, and it plays out in the saving-investment terms of this 
note. Productivity grew fast throughout 1995–2004, but the gains were broadly shared only in the first half, and r* 
rose only then (Graph 4). 
 
A. The shock. Utilization-adjusted TFP growth rose from below zero in 1995 to about 2.5% a year by 2000 and stayed 
around 2% until 2004. 
B. Who gained. The labour share of income rose by about 6% between 1997 and early 2001, then fell by 8% by 2005: 
the gains went broadly to workers in the first half and increasingly to profits in the second. 
C. The two curves. In the broad phase, business investment rose by 2 pp of GDP, 1.2 pp of it in ICT, while private 
saving fell by about 2.5 pp as households spent their expected gains: both pushed r* up. In the narrow phase the 
curves moved the other way: investment fell back by almost 3 pp and private saving rose by about 3 pp, much of it 
profits retained by firms. 
D. The outcome. The Holston-Laubach-Williams estimate of US r* rose from 2.5% to 3.9% by 2000 and fell back to 
around 2.6% by 2003, tracking the gap between investment and saving rather than productivity. 
Productivity moves r* only through investment and saving. The first half was the broad boom of Phase 2: firms 
invested more and households saved less. The second half looked like the concentrated case: gains shifted to profits, 
saving rose, investment fell, and r* dropped even with TFP still growing about 2% a year. The end of the investment 
boom (Phase 1) and the shift to profits (Phase 2) came together, so the episode cannot separate them, but both point 
the same way. 
A Taylor rule that lets r* rise pointed to policy rates of 5–6% in the late 1990s, against about 4% with r* fixed at 2%. 
Inflation picked up after both spells in which the Fed sat below that rule: the 1998–99 cuts to 4.75%, after which core 
PCE inflation rose from 1.2% to 2%, and the easing cycle at the end of 2003. If AI lifts r*, an unchanged policy rate is 
looser than it looks.

Europe imports the US term premium, not US growth. Expected policy rates follow the ECB and euro area conditions. 
The term premium, the extra return for holding long-dated bonds, is set in one global market in which investors switch 
between Treasuries and Bunds, so more US duration raises its price everywhere. Since 2015, around 60% of monthly 
changes in the US term premium have reached the euro area, and about half of the euro area’s 108 bp rise since late 2022 
came from the US. 
Europe imports a small r* increase during the build-out, but productivity also pushes r* higher. We allow for an AI 
investment push of 0.1–0.3% of GDP in the euro area itself, plus 20–40% of the US effect through global capital markets. 
That gives a lift to the euro area neutral rate of about 0.1 pp over 2026–30. For productivity, we assume the euro area 
eventually gets the same gain as the US. But since an adopter can buy the technology rather than build it, the euro area 
needs less new capital. A broad boom then adds about 0.75 pp to euro area r*; concentrated or smaller gains add 0 to 0.3 
pp. 
The backdrop: other capex, demographics and climate act on the same curves 
AI lands on a neutral rate that other slow-moving forces are already shifting. Most push the same way as the build-out; 
only a lasting climate hit to productivity pulls r* down. 
Other capex. Global energy investment reached about USD 3.3 trillion in 2025 (IEA, 2025). Energy transition, grid and 
adaptation are the largest part of capex in Europe, where defence spending is also rising towards NATO’s core target of 
3.5% of GDP by 2035. Both raise desired investment or public borrowing, and with it r*: by the mid-2030s, +0.05 to +0.15 
pp in the US and +0.1 to +0.4 pp in the euro area, based on our capex estimates. They also compete with AI for grid 
equipment and connections. 
Demographics. The euro area’s working-age population is set to shrink by 4% by 2040 (Eurostat EUROPOP2025), and US 
Social Security and health spending rises by 1.3 pp of GDP by 2036 (CBO, 2026). Ageing pushed r* down for decades and 
some expect that to continue (Auclert et al., 2021), but we think the balance now shifts as retirees draw down savings and 
pension and health spending adds to public debt (Goodhart and Pradhan, 2020). Applying CBO’s 2–3 bp per percentage 
point of debt-to-GDP to US debt rising to 120% of GDP by 2036, with a lower end for higher saving by older households, 
gives 0 to +0.6 pp for the US and +0.05 to +0.25 pp for the euro area. AI will not close the fiscal gap: Penn Wharton’s 
estimated USD 400bn deficit saving from AI over 2026–2035 covers about a sixth of the rise in US Social Security and health 
spending. In addition, narrowly distributed gains that push down the labour share of income could actually lead to lower tax 
revenues without changes to the system.  
Climate. Under current policies, gradual warming could leave world GDP about 6% below its path by 2050, with floods, 
droughts and heatwaves adding to that. A broader set of estimates ranges from 1 to 8%. The damage builds up over time, 
so by the mid-2030s the drag on growth is about 0.2 pp a year. A lower return on capital and more precautionary saving 
pull r* down. Applying the same pass-through as for AI gives -0.1 to -0.2 pp in both regions by the mid-2030s, and more 
after that.  
What the backdrop adds to r*. On balance, other capex, demographics and climate add about 0.25 pp to r* in both regions, 
which we add to every scenario from the mid-2030s. Ageing, public debt and other capex outweigh climate by a wide 
margin, so the backdrop keeps r* up even if AI disappoints. We estimate a range of −0.15 to +0.65 pp in the US and -0.05 
to +0.55 pp in the euro area. 
Putting it together 
The pieces combine into six paths: the build-out; four long-run outcomes – broad boom (large, shared gains), winner takes 
all (large, concentrated), slow burn (small, shared) and disappointment (small, concentrated); and a boom-bust on the way 
to any of them. All start from today’s Holston-Laubach-Williams estimates, and ranges combine the low and high ends of 
every input. They are illustrations, not forecasts, but they pin down the ranking: the build-out lift is the most certain part, 
and who gets the productivity gains matters as much as how large they are.  
During the build-out, the US neutral rate rises to about 1.2% and the euro area’s to about 0.1%; both lifts fade once it ends. 
By the mid-2030s, only a broad boom lifts long-run US r* clearly, to about 2%; the other paths leave it at 1.3–1.6%. A bust 
would take it to about 0.9% at the trough. Euro area r* reaches 1% only in a broad boom and stays at 0.3–0.6% otherwise. 
Inflation and the term premium move differently along these paths. The build-out adds to inflation now and should add to 
the term premium as borrowing grows. High productivity lowers inflation only during the transition, after which distribution  
decides the term premium: broadly shared gains widen the tax base, while concentrated gains weaken it and add to transfer 
needs, pushing it up. A boom-bust pushes both up and then sharply down.

Our base case is the build-out followed by a slow-burn or 
winner-takes-all productivity phase. The US neutral rate then 
rises to about 1.2% during the build-out, and long-run r* settles 
at 1.4–1.6% by the mid-2030s, a nominal neutral rate of 3.4–
3.6%, at or slightly above the Fed’s longer-run projection of 
3.2%. Euro area r* ends at 0.4–0.6%, a nominal neutral rate of 
2.4–2.6%. Given the financing trends and the revenue gap, we 
see a meaningful chance of the boom-bust path, which would 
take US r* back to around 0.9% at the trough, though not in the 
near term. 
Surveys and market prices tell a different story for now. 
Surveys put the long-run policy rate at 3.25% for the Fed and 
2.0% for the ECB: real rates of about 1.25% and 0%. Both sit 
below our estimates (1.4–1.6% and 0.4–0.6%), and for the 
euro area the survey matches today’s no-AI level. Market forwards are much higher, 5.2% for the US 5y5y and 3.6% for the 
euro area, above even a broad boom (about 4% and 3% nominal), but they include a term premium. To square forwards 
with our base case of a build-out followed by a slow-burn or winner-takes-all Phase 2, the term premium would have to 
be about 1.7 pp in the US and 1.1 pp in the euro area. A smaller term premium means markets price something like a 
broad boom. Surveys price no lasting AI lift, while markets leave room for one.
