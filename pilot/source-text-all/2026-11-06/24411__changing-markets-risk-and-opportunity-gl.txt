Seasons have changed, and so have the signals
 
Fall has always been your author's most colourful season: brisk temperatures, changing leaves and a return 
to activity, both in life and markets, after a long summer lull. All this comes with the nostalgia that the hottest 
days are behind us and the lurkin
g reality that winter lies ahead. That feels like an apt characterization for 
markets. 
Q2 2026 earnings were sizzling (~30% earnings growth), yet some of the fastest growers were met with 
indifference, which can't help but make us reflect that, similar to changing colours, so too are market 
leadership and the equity 
outlook. ¹ 
Take AI for example . It remains transformational. For example, 
one consumer AI app quickly became one of 
the most downloaded 
mobile applications
 ¹. Yet many of the stocks in the value chain of the 
AI data centre 
buildout remain below their highs, despite significant earnings growth and positive earnings 
revisions. ¹ 
Similarly, take notice of changes in market structure. Despite above
-average G
ross Domestic Product (GDP) 
growth, with the Atlanta Fed
eral Reserve
 GDP forecasting model pencilling in Q3 
2026 GDP growth 
somewhere in the 5% range
, the best
-performing sectors quarter
-to-date are Energy, a consequence of 
geopolitical strife, and Healthcare, all while the 10
-year U.S . Treasury flirts with levels last touched in May 
2007. ¹ 
AI is still transformational, but the trade is changing
 
The prevailing view treats the 
AI value chain as one trade, and we think that is increasingly too simple.
 Recent 
political developments, ones that we think aren't being talked about enough, include bipartisan moves to slow 
data centre buildouts. What matters here is that major Republican
 and Democratic
-led states, including Texas, 
Pennsylvania, Virginia and California, are moving in the same 
direction. ¹ 
To us, this suggests that (1) grid reliability, permitting and local capacity are adding political constraints and 
(2) we are no longer merely limited by supply constraints.
 
At a Glance: 
 
•
Artificial intelligence (AI)
 remains transformational, but the trade is changing. Political resistance and 
muted reactions to strong earnings suggest value may be shifting.
 
•
Macro variables rhyme with the early innings of the late
-cycle phase. Historically, this has meant positive 
but moderate equity returns over the medium term (12
–18 months) . ¹ 
•
Elevated bearish
 sentiment argues against becoming outright defensive, even as tighter policy and 
stretched trailing returns support less risk 
concentration
. 
•
Our portfolios remain invested with a cyclical tilt but are becoming more
 balanced. Namely, we have 
reduced exposure to selected 
AI capex beneficiaries, have started engaging again in the hyperscalers 
and have emphasized diversification in quality cash
-flow compounders, albeit with a cyclical bias.

The investment implication is just that: supply. If political and permitting constraints slow construction while 
suppliers add capacity, the pricing power in memory, optical components, power equipment and related 
infrastructure that ushered in this 
AI beneficiary bull run is likely to erode.
 
In short, the long
-term buildout should remain intact, but the near
-term earnings revisions cycle becomes less 
exceptional. The 
market may already be hinting at that, with hyperscalers being rewarded by the market 
following July's sell
-off. ¹ 
Extend this logic a step further.
 If political constraints slow the pace of new data centre construction, the 
hyperscalers could disproportionately benefit because they already have data centre builds in progress and 
there would be less near
-term pressure on free cash flow. This slowdown 
also means that, when they do 
eventually get approvals, the cost to do so could be lower as supply bottlenecks ease.
 
Put differently, less infrastructure spending does not necessarily mean less 
AI value. It just changes where that 
value accrues.
 
Early late cycle is still late cycle
 
The second shift is broader than technology. Evidence increasingly points to the early stages of a late cycle. 
We are not trying to be colourful with nomenclature, just pointing out that in a late
-stage cycle, while earnings 
growth remains positive, the ra
te of change is slower.
 
Said plainly, the expected return for taking incremental risk is lower than earlier in the cycle. Practically, if Q2
 
2026 earnings grew 51% 
year -over -year on an unadjusted basis (~30% on core), it is unlikely that they will 
accelerate from these 
levels. ¹ 
The Institute for Supply Management (
ISM ) Manufacturing Index helps frame where we are in the progression 
and what equity returns may look like. Historically, readings between 55 and 60 have been followed by mid
-
to-high -single -digit S&P 500 Index returns over the next 12 months. Above 60, subsequent returns have been 
below average. ¹

August's ISM was 54.6, just below the 55 threshold and still short of the 
60 -level associated with the end of 
the cycle. For us, this isn't a crystal ball. Rather, it's our guidepost that expecting positive equity returns over 
the next 12 months remains reasonable, but that the amplitude is likely to be lower.
 Growth remains supportive, 
but the economy is moving closer to the range where we would become incrementally more cautious.
 
The direction of monetary policy also reinforces this signal. Market
-implied central bank policy rates are rising 
across all large, developed economies for the 
first time in four years. The starting points differ, but the direction 
is unanimous: a global rate
-hiking cycle has begun.

Historically, tighter monetary conditions have presaged market volatility. Recall the axiom, “the Fed tightens 
till something breaks.” We are at the beginning of this rate
-hiking campaign, and it remains early against a 
backdrop of buoyant economic growth,
 outsized earnings and transformational 
AI technology. But the big 
picture is that the torque has turned to tightening.
 
Market leadership also shares elements of late
-cycle behaviour. Rising rates, stubborn inflation and a 
commodity bid are all corroborating cross
-asset signals. Within equity markets, the leadership is Energy and 
Materials, deep cyclical sectors, along with
 defensive areas such as Healthcare, while software stocks are also 
finding a bid. 
 
No single sector rotation proves a regime change, but the pattern is nevertheless consistent with a market 
becoming more discerning about where it pays to take risk.
 Historical returns provide broader context: the 
S&P 500 Index's three
-year annualized return is now 21.6%, placing it in the 94th percentile of this data set, 
which goes back to the 1960
s. ¹ 
Yes, returns can move higher from here, but you would need a combination of continued elevated earnings 
growth, falling inflation, easing geopolitical risks and a central bank that has moved back to a neutral posture. 
That constellation of good fortune can
 occur, but the onus falls on the bulls.
 
Again, to reiterate, 
we aren't calling for a bear market
. We are just trying to be more realistic with our 
expectations going forward.

Late cycle does not mean last call.
 The chart below shows that bearish sentiment now exceeds bullish 
sentiment and sits at a 0.68 reading. What does this mean in reality? This can be interpreted as there being 
about 1.5 bears for every one bull, meaning elevated levels of caution are already
 embedded. ¹

Where we stand
 
Our response is discipline and patience, not a full retreat. We have always communicated our process of 
investing in high
-quality companies that display exploitable free
-cash -flow growth. 
We still think 
AI is transformational for business models and remain invested in some of the 
AI capital 
expenditure ( capex ) beneficiaries because of their 
strong free -cash -flow growth. However, it's no longer a 
one -way trade in which all 
AI capex beneficiaries win.
 
Research -driven differentiation on the sustainability of pricing power, competitive threats and total 
addressable market (TAM) growth remains paramount. At the margin, we have been reducing our weights in 
some of these names and/or redeploying to our lower
-beta AI exposures . 
Given the late
-cycle dynamics we've discussed, we feel that we aren't being compensated for taking sizable 
risks, so for portfolios, that means we are not stepping out on any ledges. We have therefore moved to 
companies that have historically worked well during these periods, and the hyperscalers feature prominently.
¹ 
Ultimately, it's about making sure we have diversified the sources of portfolio cash
-flow growth and reduced 
our reliance on any single theme.
 Our portfolios today include a mix of interest
-rate -sensitive Financials, high
-
quality business models in software, a healthy but lower exposure to 
AI, an increasing tilt toward the 
hyperscalers and measured Energy exposure that provides optionality when uncertainty rises. We remain 
underweight defensive sectors such as Consumer Staples because economic growth has not degraded 
enough to justify a more def
ensive stance.
 
Importantly, we remain eagle
-eyed for evidence that growth is rolling over. If it does, that would increase our 
conviction that the late cycle is progressing and warrant a more defensive mix, but we are not there yet.
 
Said plainly, positive returns are the base case, but expectations should be moderated from the high
-double -
digit experience of recent years. In that environment, diversified themes, appropriate position sizing and a 
focus on quality cash
-flow compounders 
matter more than trying to force capital into an opportunity that is 
no longer mispriced.
 
What We're Watching
 
Data centre restrictions
: Whether the policy move broadens and begins to alter orders, pricing and earnings 
revisions across the supply chain.
 
Market response
: Whether strong results again produce positive price reactions in former leadership groups.
 
Monetary conditions
: Whether expected rate increases translate into sustained tightening.
 
Economic momentum
: Whether growth remains firm enough to support cyclicals or slows enough to justify a 
more defensive stance.

Markets rarely ring a bell when an opportunity moves from underappreciated to well understood. They simply 
stop paying investors as generously for the same risk.
 We still see reasons to remain invested, but fewer 
reasons to remain concentrated. The season has changed, and our portfolios should acknowledge that.
