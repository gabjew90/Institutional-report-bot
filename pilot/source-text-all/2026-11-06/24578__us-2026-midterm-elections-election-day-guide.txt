US 2026 midterm elections: Election Day guide 
◼ 
We have covered our thoughts on the likelihood of various election 
outcomes and potential policy and economic impacts of the different 
scenarios in several prior articles. The slides from our recent webcast 
US Midterm Elections: what to expect and what the market impact 
could be highlight the main points. 
◼ 
As not much has changed over the last week heading into Election Day, 
we focus most of our final pre-election article on supplementing our 
prior coverage with some information on the logistical aspects of the 
election process. We cover a number of these topics below, including 
poll closing times, what to watch for on Election Night, when results 
might be available and key dates after Election Day. 
No last-minute shift in polling 
Not a lot has changed since our last look at the polls a week ago, so we will 
largely refer to our prior article as opposed to providing any 
comprehensive polling update. In general, the generic congressional ballot 
continues to suggest a clear advantage for Democrats in the House (see Figure 
1), with polls in many key Senate races leaning towards Democrats as well. This 
points to a near certainty that Democrats take control of the House with 
Democrats as a favourite in the Senate as well, albeit to a lesser degree (see 
Figure 2). We still handicap the odds as follows, with our base case seeing a 
Democratic sweep: 
⚫

⚫ 
Democratic House/Republican Senate: 25% 
⚫ 
Republican sweep: 10%

Legislative gridlock remains the base case 
While the Senate plays a role in confirmations of appointed positions such 
as judgeships and senior level executive posts, in many other ways Senate 
control will not be a gamechanger. On the legislative front, this comes down to 
the existence of both the filibuster and the Presidential veto, which we do not 
think President Donald Trump would be particularly shy about using, so a 
combination of President Trump and a Democratic House would likely lead to 
legislative gridlock, regardless of the outcome in the Senate, in our view. 
As well, there are numerous issues where the executive enjoys more 
leeway to act without Congress, including trade and foreign policy, so 
Congressional elections will not necessarily offer much constraint in either of 
these areas. For more detail, please see a previous Economics Focus: digging 
into the Senate. 
We would like to add two quick points, however: 
⚫ 
We have heard some speculation that a Democratic sweep scenario 
could lead to the implementation of new regulations regarding the AI 
buildout, but we are not convinced. Even if Democrats were able to 
convince enough Republicans to get on board to overcome the filibuster, 
which is not a guarantee even if some Republicans have become a bit more 
open to AI regulations, the Trump veto would loom large. Here, despite the 
wavering of some in his party, Trump has been steadfast in his support for an 
aggressive AI buildout. The President seems to view AI as an arms race with 
China that the US has to win, and therefore we think there is a good chance 
any aggressive restrictions at the federal level would face a veto. 
That said, we do think local politics could play a role here, so state and even 
county or city level elections could make a difference in terms of potential 
constraints like zoning restrictions on data centre construction. As such, we 
are not saying that elections will be irrelevant in terms of the outlook 
for AI regulation, simply that this may be more of a local politics story 
than a federal politics one. 
⚫ 
Even if Congressional elections do not have a direct impact on issues such 
as trade and foreign policy given the President’s ability to pass relevant 
policy changes via executive order, we do see a behavioural risk: if 
Republicans face a heavy defeat, then Trump could react by deciding to 
lash out where he has the power to do so, such as via renewed escalation 
in Iran or reviving the spectre of even more aggressive tariffs.

focused on, as opposed to who they voted for. Thus, we will have to wait until poll 
closing for any concrete information on the share of the vote that any candidate

What should we watch for on Election Night? 
While there are 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats up for election, 
results in many of these are a foregone conclusion, with focus on a subset 
that will determine control of each chamber. We touch on the highlights 
below. 
Senate 
Senate elections are staggered such that roughly one-third of the 100 seats 
are up for re-election every two years, meaning that the map of which 
states are up for election changes each cycle. In 2026, there will be 35 
Senate seats up for election, with Republicans defending 22 and Democrats 13 
of these. Republicans hold a 53-47 edge currently, so Democrats would need to 
gain a net of at least four seats to win the majority (as Vice President JD Vance 
acts as the tie-breaking vote in a 50-50 Senate).

House

two years. With Republicans holding a 220-215 edge, assigning all vacancies to

the lower end of this range easily enough to reach a majority.

When will results be available? 
As usual in recent elections in the US, there are huge levels of uncertainty 
as to when the results will be available, with a couple of main factors 
contributing. For one, mail-in ballots have become more common compared to 
the pre-Covid period and given that mail-in ballots can take longer to count, 
especially as some states accept mail-in ballots that arrive after Election Day, as 
long as they are postmarked by Election Day, this can contribute to delays. 
Additionally, the timing of the availability of results will likely depend on how close 
the race is. The closer the race, the longer results will likely take, as a call on the 
winner of any state will not be possible until all, or nearly all, of the votes have 
been counted. 
Senate 
Given that the Senate is expected to be a relatively close call, it could take 
some time for the result to be official. If Democrats do enjoy a dominant 
performance, then it is possible that we could have the official call by 
sometime between around midnight into early morning hours Wednesday. 
For example, there is a path solely involving states with relatively early poll 
closing times, such as flips in North Carolina, Ohio, Texas, and Maine (not a 
prediction on each individual state listed here, just an illustrative scenario). 
However, there is a scenario in which it could take days to know the final 
result, especially if Senate control were to come down to Alaska, which is 
not an entirely unrealistic possibility. In this case, not only do the polls not 
fully close until 1:00 EST on Wednesday morning, but Alaska uses a ranked 
choice voting system. This means that, if no candidate wins 50% of the vote 
originally, then the candidate with the fewest first place votes is eliminated and 
those voters’ ballots are re-assigned to their second choice, and so on until 
someone wins a majority. However, that process cannot be finalised until all mail-

House 
For the House, even if Democrats are a strong favourite, it could take days 
until the official call is made. If Democrats are on track for a solid victory, then 
the eventual result could start to be clear by late Tuesday evening, even if they 
have not officially had 218 seats called in the favour just yet. One district we have 
seen highlighted to watch in this vein is the 6th in Kentucky, where Trump won by 
about 15ppt in 2024 but the race is considered a toss-up this year. If Democrats 
win there, where polls close early so the result should not take too long, it bodes 
well for their overall outlook in the House. 
If the result is closer than expected, however, it could again take days 
before the call is official. For example, in 2022 control of the House was not 
officially called until 16 November, which was a full eight days after Election Day. 
Here, California could be a main contributor to the delay with multiple races 
potentially in play, as its election rules allow for mail-in votes postmarked by 
Election Day to be counted as long as they are delivered within the next seven 
days. 
Will there be legal challenges that risk the transfer 
of power? 
There are almost certain to be some legal challenges, particularly if the 
outcome ends up being closer than expected. In fact, the Trump 
administration has already initiated several legal challenges, such as an attempt 
to set aside some 20k mail-in ballots in California it argues may be from non-
citizens and an attempt to prevent Alaska from certifying a winner that is not 
Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan given the presence of two candidates with 
the exact same name, even if none of these challenges has met with any 
success. 
While additional legal challenges are certainly possible, and in fact likely 
the closer elections are, we do not expect that any these challenges will 
ultimately succeed. Trump and his allies have brought forth numerous election 
challenges in the past but have succeeded in none of them, including the 
aforementioned cases in California and Alaska this year. Thus, we see every 
indication that legal safeguards are up to the challenge and will hold up to 
ensure a peaceful transition of power within Congress. 
Certainly, it cannot be entirely ruled out that one or two challenges are still 
in play when the new Congress is scheduled to be sworn in on 3 January 
2027. However, we think that if this is the case, the races under 
consideration would be a small number with extremely tight margins rather 
than frivolous lawsuits aimed at overturning races with a clear winner, and 
that the Courts would try to reach a ruling as quickly as possible.
