US Midterms: Historical Equity-Volume
Patterns and the Balance of Power
Tobi Olagunju
03-Nov-2026, 10:37 ET (EST)
Ahead of the US midterms, UBS reviewed equity volumes around the 2014, 2018 and 2022 elections across
the S&P and several European indices, alongside the resulting balance of power in Congress.
The simple historical takeaway is that midterms have not materially moved the needle on equity
volumes across the US or developed European markets. The clearest and most consistent pattern is
instead a short-lived lull around Election Day, while the subsequent volume path has varied considerably
between election cycles.
Across 2014, 2018 and 2022:
• The S&P traded below its previous 20-day average on midterm election Day in all three samples: −15% in
2014, −22% in 2018 and −6% in 2022.
• The more consistent election-related lull was concentrated in the US and developed European
markets.
• Beyond T+1, there is no consistent historical pattern. Volumes stayed subdued after 2018, briefly
recovered after 2014, and rose sharply from T+2 in 2022.
The 2022 rebound deserves some caution. All eight indices the desk examined moved above their 20-day
averages at T+2, but the move should not automatically be attributed to the midterm result without separating
it from other market drivers at the time.
In other words, the 2014, 2018 and 2022 experience suggests the midterms itself has generally not
been a driver of volumes. The broader market backdrop has mattered more.

Why 2026 could be different

3
The more relevant question for 2026 is whether the election produces a policy outcome significant enough to
change that historical pattern.
A shift in congressional control could have implications for fiscal policy, shutdown and debt-ceiling risk, Senate
confirmations and the future composition of the Federal Reserve. Those channels potentially matter more for
volumes than the election event itself, particularly if the result changes expectations for the administration’s
policy agenda or introduces prolonged uncertainty around control of Congress.
Policy implications
• Split control: A Democratic House would increase the potential for gridlock, fiscal brinkmanship and
shutdown or debt-ceiling risk.
• Blue sweep: Democratic control of both chambers would increase congressional influence over Senate
confirmations and oversight.
• Fed composition: With potential changes to the Board of Governors over the coming years, Senate control
could matter for the future composition and reaction function of the FOMC.
• Policy agenda: Healthcare and Medicaid, housing, deposit insurance and payments, energy permitting, AI
oversight and semiconductor export controls could all become areas of greater congressional influence.
• Executive authority: Even under divided government, the administration would retain substantial authority
over tariffs, trade investigations, energy and banking regulation, drug pricing and foreign investment.

Execution takeaway
The historical evidence does not suggest that US midterms, by themselves, are a major volume
catalyst. The more repeatable feature is somewhat thinner activity around Election Day and T+1, particularly
in US and developed European markets.
For execution, that argues for allowing for softer liquidity immediately around the vote, but not positioning for
a mechanical post-election volume rebound. For 2026, the greater risk is that the result produces a
meaningful policy or macro catalyst, in which case the historical midterm pattern may be less informative.
