Are markets pricing AI's social licence to compute?  
AI infrastructure investment remains structurally strong. Major US hyperscaler capex is 
expected to approach $1tn in 2027, up from around $800bn in 2026e. This should feed into 
opportunities across semiconductors, servers, cloud, connectivity, data centre infrastructure, 
power and cooling. Yet, large and resource-intensive data centre campuses are putting increasing 
pressure on electricity grids, water supplies and local infrastructure, fuelling community 
opposition and political scrutiny. At least 75 US data centre projects, worth around $130bn, 
were reportedly blocked or delayed in Q1 2026 alone. With data centres in the political spotlight 
ahead of the November 2026 US midterm elections, social acceptance is becoming a key 
determinant of where, when and at what cost capacity can be built. 
We believe the financial risk to data centres’ social licence to operate remains largely 
overlooked. Our review of the Sustainalytics universe has identified 25 data-centre-related 
controversies involving 20 companies in 2025-26. Most of these are linked to water, energy, 
noise and other local impacts, yet all were classified as low impact. This contrasts with more 
severe Social & Community controversies, where the operational disruption contributed to 
prolonged share-price underperformance. A low controversy score does not mean low 
financial risk: community opposition can lengthen permitting, force project redesign, raise costs 
and, in severe cases, delay or prevent development. 
The risk may be overlooked, but it is no longer entirely invisible to markets. We screen 85 AI 
and data centre beneficiaries across eight segments: AI Compute Hardware; AI Servers & System 
OEMs; Data Centre Construction & Engineering; Hyperscale Cloud Providers; Infrastructure & 
Platforms, Optical Fiber & Connectivity; Power, Cooling & Electrical Infrastructure; and 
REITs/Colocation Providers. Within this group, we identify 35 companies that have a lower 
water and energy footprint relative to peers. YTD, this resource-efficient subset returned 
70.5%, versus 47.8% for resource-intensive peers and 24.0% for the Nasdaq 100. Since 
end-May, when scrutiny of data centre water use intensified, the resource-efficient 
companies have gained +15.8% versus -3.7% for the resource-intensive peers and +4.2% for 
the Nasdaq 100, outperforming the intensive peers in 15 of 20 weeks. While it is too early to 
establish causality, the performance dispersion suggests resource efficiency may be emerging 
as a factor in investment decisions. 
Stock selection alone will not be enough to manage this risk. Investors will also likely have 
to strengthen stewardship on local social impacts, including community consultation, 
infrastructure cost sharing, resource efficiency, site selection, and facility-level disclosure. To 
help investors in supporting this engagement, we review practices already being 
deployed across the AI and data centre value chain, from cleaner electricity, energy-efficient 
computing and closed-loop cooling to circular hardware, lower-carbon back-up systems and 
community cost sharing. These measures can help companies reduce local impacts, secure 
their social licence to operate and sustain AI infrastructure growth as resource and community 
constraints tighten.

When the AI boom meets social resistance 
AI meets the neighbours 
Data centres have moved firmly into the public spotlight as the rapid expansion of AI 
infrastructure collides with growing concerns about electricity demand, water use, and local 
impacts. Record investment commitments continue to generate strong commercial interest. 
However, project delays, community protests and tighter permitting show that the buildout is no 
longer viewed solely as a technology and investment story, but is increasingly becoming a social, 
environmental, and infrastructure challenge. 
Opposition manifests itself in different ways across markets. In the US, it has centred on 
electricity prices, water use, noise, land requirements and the limited number of permanent jobs 
created relative to project size. In Europe, similar concerns have more frequently translated into 
planning restrictions and tighter permitting, while grid-connection constraints have further 
slowed project developments. Several Asian markets remain comparatively supportive of data 
centre investment, although pressure on power systems and local resources could increasingly 
constrain development. 
Like power generation, transmission infrastructure, and other large industrial developments, 
hyperscale data centres combine broad economic benefits with highly localised costs. 
Communities are increasingly questioning who should pay for new grid infrastructure, whether 
households and businesses will face higher utility bills, and whether the local economic benefits 
justify the project’s demands on power, water and land. 
Across markets, the concerns tend to converge around five issues: 
• 
pressure on electricity supply, grid capacity and consumer prices 
• 
water demand, particularly in already stressed regions 
• 
noise and emissions from cooling equipment and backup generation 
• 
land use and effects on surrounding communities 
• 
limited permanent employment relative to the projects’ capital and resource intensity

Hey AI, can we talk about our power bill? 
The electricity requirements of AI infrastructure are increasingly turning data centre 
development into a question of local cost-sharing. New AI campuses can require hundreds of 
megawatts and, in some cases, more than 1GW of power. Meeting this demand may require new 
generation, transmission and grid connections. This leads to what is becoming an increasingly 
thorny question: who should bear the cost? 
Data centres consumed approximately 415TWh of electricity in 2024, equivalent to 1.5% of global 
demand (link). The International Energy Agency (IEA) expects consumption to more than double 
to around 945TWh by 2030, or approximately 3% of global demand, mainly driven by AI. The 
impact is likely to be considerably more concentrated in major data centre markets, where rapid 
load growth could intensify grid congestion and place upward pressure on electricity costs.

Hey AI, are you drawing from the same well? 
AI is raising the cooling requirements of data centres at the same time as rapid capacity growth 
increases data centres’ exposure to local water constraints. The higher chip and rack densities 
required by AI generate more heat, accelerating the shift from conventional air cooling towards 
direct-to-chip liquid cooling and immersion systems. These technologies can improve energy 
efficiency and reduce water intensity when combined with closed-loop or dry heat-rejection 
systems. However, lower water intensity per unit of computing does not necessarily translate into 
lower total water consumption when capacity is expanding rapidly. 
The local impact also depends on how and where water is consumed. Data centres use water 
directly for cooling and indirectly through electricity generation. A medium-sized facility can 
consume around 400 million litres annually, while hyperscale campuses may require several 
billion litres. Even where water use is modest at the national level, withdrawals can become 
socially contentious when concentrated in water-stressed watersheds or when data centres 
compete with households, agriculture and other industries for limited local supplies. 
Cooling technology matters, but it does not tell the whole story 
Not all liquid-cooling systems have the same water profile. Direct liquid cooling transfers heat 
more efficiently than conventional air cooling, but the ultimate water requirement depends on 
how that heat is rejected. Systems using evaporative cooling may remain water-intensive, 
whereas closed-loop systems combined with dry coolers can substantially reduce operational 
water consumption. Investors should therefore look beyond cooling labels and consider the 
entire cooling architecture, local climate, and watershed exposure.

Location could outweigh efficiency gains 
The geographic mismatch between data centre development and water availability is becoming 
increasingly material. S&P Global notes that more than half of India’s data centres are located in 
water-stressed areas, while it estimates that 45% of its sample of more than 9,000 data centres 
could face high water-stress exposure by 2050. The sustainability of future capacity growth will 
therefore depend not only on cooling efficiency, but also on whether new facilities are located in 
watersheds capable of supporting them.

Water constraints are already influencing project design. Opposition to Google’s proposed data

Public scrutiny is rising 
Water scarcity emerged as a prominent issue this year amid heatwaves and droughts worldwide, 
with data centres among the businesses directly affected. Since late May, media coverage linking 
data centres and water has surged: the number of related news stories globally has increased 5 
times over the past year and 2.5 times since the end of May. This rise highlights growing 
community awareness and mounting opposition to new data centre development.

Hey AI, what about the rest? 
Beyond electricity and water, the expansion of data centres also has a local impact in other areas. 
Large AI campuses require land, grid infrastructure, cooling equipment, and backup generation, 
while construction can generate traffic, noise and pressure on local services. Once operational, 
cooling systems and generators may continue to affect surrounding communities, and rapid 
equipment replacement raises additional questions around materials and electronic waste. 
These impacts matter because the local economic benefits are not always perceived as 
commensurate with the resources required. Data centres can generate tax revenues, 
construction activity and infrastructure investment, but highly automated facilities may create 
relatively few permanent jobs. Understandably, communities may consequently view the 
economic upside as being widely distributed, while the environmental and infrastructure costs 
remain concentrated locally.

Data centres emerge as hot topic in midterms 
Data centre development is moving beyond local planning disputes and into mainstream 
political debate. As projects multiply and become more resource-intensive, concerns over 
electricity prices, water availability, land use and infrastructure costs are increasingly shaping 
voter attitudes and electoral campaigns. 
Data centres have unexpectedly become a significant issue in the November 2026 US midterms. 
The backlash has moved into federal and state election campaigns. As per the poll by PBS news, 
NPR and Marist, voters oppose data centres – 58% presume that the construction of new data 
centres will have a negative effect on the country. 65% of respondents oppose data centre 
construction in their communities. Another survey shows even higher opposition, 70-75% of 
Americans oppose an AI data centre being built in their local area (link; Gallup, Heatmap).  
Survey showing increasing negative sentiment to data centre construction near residences 
Source: Heatmap 
These results expose a growing tension between national and local priorities. At the national 
level, AI infrastructure is increasingly viewed as strategically important for technological 
leadership, economic competitiveness and national security. At the local level, however, voters 
are more focused on who will pay for additional power generation and grid infrastructure, 
whether data centre demand will increase utility bills, how projects will affect water and land 
availability, and whether the resulting employment and tax benefits will justify the facilities’ 
local footprint. 
Data centres are therefore becoming part of a broader debate over how the costs and benefits of 
the AI buildout should be distributed. Political support for AI infrastructure remains strong, but 
support for individual projects may weaken when communities perceive that electricity, water or 
infrastructure costs are being transferred to households and local businesses. 
Political pressure is also beginning to influence policy. Across major data centre markets, 
authorities are tightening requirements around grid connections, infrastructure cost sharing, 
energy efficiency, water use, renewable electricity, and environmental disclosure. Approaches differ 
across jurisdictions, but the direction is increasingly towards requiring large data centres to bear a 
greater share of the costs created by their power, water and infrastructure requirements. 
The measures summarised below illustrate how this shift is taking shape, from tighter connection 
and permitting rules to dedicated tariffs, resource-use restrictions, and greater cost sharing.

Data centre footprint controversies: an unpriced 
financial risk, but for how long? 
Current controversy profile. Data centre controversies are a relatively new phenomenon and 
are generally classified as low severity. Our review of approximately 20,000 companies in the 
Sustainalytics universe during 2025–26 identified 25 data centre-related controversies involving 
20 companies. Most concerned community protests or opposition arising from water use, energy 
consumption, noise and other local impacts; the remainder involved broader issues such as 
emissions and effluents, and occupational health and safety. All were classified as Category 1 or 
2 on a five-point scale, suggesting that ESG investors relying on provider controversy screens may 
not yet be fully capturing the issue. 
Forward-looking implication. If data centre disputes escalate from protests to operational 
disruption, controversy ratings are likely to become more severe and more relevant to investor 
screening and company selection. To illustrate how these risks could evolve, we examined past 
water-related controversies with higher severity ratings.

Computing the outperformers: an ESG lens on 
data centres 
Mapping the value chain 
A data centre is made up of several components, chiefly: servers, cooling equipment, storage 
systems, networking equipment, and power storage: 
• 
Servers are computers equipped with CPUs and GPUs that process and store 
data. They account for almost 60% of electricity use in a typical data centre.  
• 
Cooling equipment: The next biggest category for electricity use is cooling, 
accounting for up to 30% of power consumption. 
• 
Storage systems are used for data storage and backup (~5% electricity use). 
• 
Networking equipment includes switches, routers and load balancers. (~5% 
electricity use). 
• 
UPS battery/power generators: Uninterruptible power supply (UPS) batteries 
and generators provide power during outages. 
 
The expansion of data centres and AI infrastructure creates a broad opportunity set across eight 
beneficiary segments: AI Compute Hardware; AI Servers and System OEMs; Data Centre 
Construction and Engineering; Hyperscale Cloud Providers; Infrastructure and Platforms; Optical 
Fibre and Connectivity; Power, Cooling and Electrical Infrastructure; Reits / Colocation Providers.

Methodology 
Screen. To capture the AI-led expansion of data centres while accounting for community 
concerns, we identify 35 Data Centre & AI stocks with a lower water and energy footprint. These 
stocks are selected from a broader universe of 85 companies with market capitalisation of at least 
USD1bn and an estimated minimum double-digit revenue exposure to the eight beneficiary 
segments: AI Compute Hardware (12 companies), AI Servers & System OEMs (14), Data Centre 
Construction & Engineering (2), Hyperscale Cloud Providers (9), Infrastructure & Platforms (16), 
Optical Fiber & Connectivity (4), Power, Cooling & Electrical Infrastructure (15), and 
REITs/Colocation Providers (13). This 85-company coverage includes the 40 companies 
highlighted in the SG Global Data Centre basket (link). 
We screen the universe of 85 data centre growth beneficiary companies on energy and water 
intensity – the two issues most closely linked to community opposition. Grid strain can raise 
electricity costs, while water withdrawals can reduce local availability and increase prices. On 
this basis, 35 companies screen favourably, reflecting lower energy and water intensity.

From a theme perspective, companies that have higher exposure to energy and water issues are 
likely to face higher scrutiny from the community, regulators and investors. We screen companies 
based on water and energy intensity per USD million of sales relative to their GICS sector peers in 
the MSCI ACWI universe. Each metric is converted into an adjustment score of 0.5-1.5 based on 
the company’s sector percentile rank, where 0.5 indicates the lowest intensity and 1.5 the 
highest. The average of the water and energy scores forms the combined adjustment factor; 
where data is missing, we assign the sector median score of 1.0. This factor is used to select 35 
companies from a universe of 85, with scores below 1.0 indicating lower combined water and 
energy intensity than the sector median. 
Performance 
We split the Data Centre & AI beneficiaries into two: the 35 companies screening better on water 
and energy intensity (“Resource efficient”) and the remaining 50 (“Resource intensive”). We 
compare their performance with the Nasdaq 100, a useful benchmark given its strong technology 
exposure. YTD up to 7 October, both screens outperformed the Nasdaq 100, with the Resource 
efficient stocks returning 70.5%, versus 47.8% for the Resource intensive stocks and 24.0% for the 
Nasdaq 100. More importantly, since end-May, when concerns around data centres’ water use 
began gaining momentum, performance has diverged materially: the Resource efficient stocks 
returned 15.8%, compared with -3.7% for the Resource intensive stocks and 4.2% for the Nasdaq 
100. This suggests that companies with less intensive use of water and energy delivered stronger 
relative performance when sustainability concerns around data centres intensified.
