Intelligence weekly #135: Can Chinese 
hyperscaler earn an attractive return on AI 
capex?
Transformational Innovation Opportunities (TRIO): Artificial Intelligence
 
Authors: Arafat Alafate, CIO Equity Strategist, UBS AG Hong Kong Branch; Delwin Kurnia Limas, CFA, CIO Equity Strategist, UBS AG Singapore 
Branch; Kevin Dennean, CFA, CIO Equity Strategist, US Technology & Telecom, UBS Financial Services Inc. (UBS FS); Achille Monnet, CIO Equity Analyst,

• China AI infrastructure can still generate attractive 
returns despite its hardware-cost disadvantage. Our base 
case suggests roughly 12-20% ROIC, with cash payback 
of around 2.5-3 years, although returns remain below 
the US largely because comparable computing hardware 
costs more in China.
• Monetization matters more than simply owning 
more compute. Owned GPU rental generates around 
12% ROIC, while owned model services can reach 
roughly 19-20%, as inference allows providers to 
monetize model quality, serving efficiency and customer 
integration rather than just renting computing capacity.
• The key upside is better utilization of every unit 
of compute. A rising inference mix, higher token 
throughput, stable revenue per unit of compute, and 
lower server costs can materially lift returns. In our 
view, China does not need to match US ROIC to create 
shareholder value, but it does need AI capacity to 
increasingly support paying inference demand rather 
than non-revenue training workloads.

Amid the recent US earning season, the market is no 
longer asking whether AI demand is real. It is asking (again) 
whether the companies funding the infrastructure will earn 
an acceptable return.
In terms of ROIC, the investment debate is more difficult in 
China. Chinese cloud providers face a higher effective cost 
for advanced computing hardware, while AI monetization 
is still at an earlier stage. We use the US as a reference 
point because it shows what the economics can look like 
when hardware is cheaper, inference adoption is deeper, 
and model pricing is stronger.

Our conclusion is constructive, but not unconditional. We 
believe China can earn a low- to high-teens return on 
invested capital from AI infrastructure. We assume this 
because the same server can be monetized in more than one 
way: It can be rented as computing capacity, used to sell 
model access, or used to support applications. The return 
improves as utilization rises and more computing shifts from 
model training, which does not generate direct revenue, to 
inference, which does.
China starts with a cost disadvantage, but it does not need 
to match US returns to create value. A 13-20% ROIC with 
a two- to three-year cash payback would still be attractive if 
demand is durable and the equipment remains economically 
useful.

Business model #1: owned GPU rental
The simplest business is to buy a server and rent its 
computing capacity to customers. We assume the provider 
earns revenue either through a long-term rental contract or 
through hourly usage. We believe this is the easiest return 
stream for investors to understand because the key variables 
are visible: price, utilization, and hardware cost.
For China, we assume server capex of CNY 10 million and 
monthly rental revenue of about CNY 310,000. After server 
and data center depreciation, electricity, maintenance, and 
labor, our base case produces a 44% operating margin, a 
12% ROIC, and a 3.2-year cash payback.
The ROIC for the same business model is normally over 30%, 
and the key difference is that Chinese firms pay materially 
more for the server. Higher server cost hurts twice: It raises 
annual depreciation and increases the amount of invested 
capital used in the ROIC denominator. Lower electricity and 
data center costs help, but do not fully offset the hardware 
gap.
We would become more positive if server costs decline in 
China, rental pricing remains stable, and advanced clusters 
stay highly utilized. We would become more cautious if 
utilization is supported mainly by internal training rather 
than paying external customers.
Business model #2: owned model services
Model-as-a-Service can generate a higher return because 
the provider is no longer selling only computing time. The 
provider is selling access to a model. We assume this allows 
the owner to capture value from model quality, software 
optimization, and customer integration, not just from the 
physical server.
The economics depend on four components: First, how 
many tokens each GPU can produce. Second, how much 
computing is allocated to inference rather than training. 
Third, the realized price per token. Fourth, the cost of the 
underlying server.
In our base case for China, we assume 4,200 tokens per 
second per GPU, a 50% inference mix, and blended pricing 
of CNY 9 per million tokens. We assume the remaining 
computing capacity is used for training and other non-
revenue workloads. On these assumptions, MaaS reaches a 
76% gross margin, a 53% operating margin, about 19% 
ROIC, and a 2.6-year payback.
We do not assume token pricing stays high simply because a 
model is more intelligent. A larger model may charge more 
per token, but it may also produce fewer tokens per GPU 
and cost more to serve. We therefore focus on revenue 
per unit of computing capacity. A lower-priced but efficient 
model can earn a better return than an expensive model that 
uses too much compute.
Business model #3: rent the servers, the asset-light 
alternative
A cloud or model provider does not need to own every 
server. It can rent capacity from a neocloud or another third 
party, then resell the capacity or use it to provide model 
access. We assume a customer price of CNY 250,000 per 
month and a compute rental cost of CNY 200,000 per 
month. This produces an operating margin of about 20%.
The margin is lower because the infrastructure owner 
keeps part of the economics. However, the provider avoids 
the upfront server investment and begins generating cash 
immediately. We believe this model is attractive when 
demand visibility is uncertain or technology is changing 
quickly. Owning capacity offers more upside when demand 
is durable; renting capacity protects the balance sheet when 
it is not.
We would expect a rational provider to own the core 
capacity needed for strategic customers and steady demand, 
while renting incremental capacity for peak demand or 
uncertain workloads. The right comparison is therefore not 
simply higher versus lower margin, it is return potential 
versus flexibility and obsolescence risk.

China can narrow the operating-profit gap through better 
model efficiency, but it cannot fully close the ROIC 
gap unless hardware cost also falls. Better software 
improves the numerator. Cheaper hardware improves 
both the numerator, through lower depreciation, and the 
denominator, through lower invested capital.
Conclusion
We believe China's AI capex can earn an attractive return, 
but the route is narrower than for companies in the US. 
Our base case supports roughly 12% ROIC for owned GPU 
rental and about 19-20% for owned model services, with 
cash payback of around two-and-a-half to three years. These 
returns are lower than in the US because Chinese firms 
pay more for computing hardware and currently allocate 
less capacity to revenue-generating inference. The upside is 
that China's AI sector has more room to improve. Lower 
hardware cost, better model-serving efficiency, and a higher 
inference mix can all raise returns.
We would stay constructive as long as three conditions hold: 
Advanced capacity remains highly utilized, paid inference 
becomes a larger share of usage, and revenue per unit of 
compute remains stable or improves. If these conditions are 
met, we believe China's AI sector does not need to match US 
ROIC for AI capex to create meaningful shareholder value.
