China's Uranium Stockpiles A Key Factor For
The Secular Call On Nuclear
China’s uranium behavior looks unusual because its procurement has often
exceeded the needs of its operating nuclear fleet. The central question is why
China would accumulate a uranium stockpile far larger than the commercial
inventories typically held by U.S. or European utilities. China is not optimizing for
today’s fuel burn, but for energy security, reactor growth, geopolitical resilience,
and a much larger nuclear system in the 2030s and beyond.
Scale Matters: China’s current uranium stockpile is not known with precision, but a reasonable
public-market estimate is approximately 120,000-160,000 tonnes uranium content, or 310-415m lb
U3O8 equivalent. That is roughly 8-12 years of current reactor uranium requirements, depending
on the assumed annual burn rate. On a forward-looking basis, however, the same inventory is far
less extreme. If China reaches roughly 180-200 GW of nuclear capacity by the mid-2030s, today’s
inventory could represent only 3.5–5 years of future requirements.
Metric Choice Matters: Uranium inventories can be expressed in tonnes uranium content, pounds
U3O8 equivalent, or years of reactor supply. The most useful strategic metric is years of forward
reactor requirements. A stockpile that looks excessive against a 60 GW nuclear fleet may look
merely prudent against a 150-200 GW fleet. That is why China’s inventory should be evaluated
against future demand, not current consumption.
Comparison Clarifies: China’s inventory appears much larger than inventories in the U.S. or Europe.
U.S. commercial uranium inventories are more transparent through EIA data and are roughly 126m
lb U3O8 for reactor owners/operators and about 170m lb U3O8e for total commercial inventories.
The EU/Euratom utility inventory is roughly 42,500 tU, or about 110m lb U₃O₈e. Those inventories
imply around three years of reactor coverage in the U.S. and Europe, versus China’s estimated 7-13
years of current coverage.
Strategic Distinction: The comparison is not perfectly apples-to-apples. U.S. and European
inventories are mostly commercial utility holdings across the fuel cycle. China’s likely includes
utility inventories, state-directed strategic reserves, trader inventories, fuel-cycle pipeline material,
overseas-controlled material, and possibly material held by state entities. Not all of it is reactor-
ready fuel. Some may be yellowcake, UF6, enriched uranium, or fabricated assemblies. Still, the
broad conclusion is clear: China has built a larger strategic cushion than Western commercial
norms would suggest.
Policy Logic: China has several reasons to do this. Its nuclear buildout is the world's largest. Each
new reactor requires not only annual reload fuel but also a large first-core loading. China is import-
dependent for uranium, and its energy system remains exposed to fossil-fuel import risks, maritime
chokepoints, sanctions, and geopolitical instability. Uranium is unusually attractive to stockpile
because it is compact, stable, energy dense, and cheap to store relative to its strategic value.
A Key Swing Factor For Secular Frameworks: If China already has more uranium coverage
than Western utilities, in our view, it reflects a participant in a multi-decade or century-long
strategic resource game, rather than a difference in utility risk management. This suggests investor
frameworks on nuclear as a secular theme need to factor in the follow-on question: from a top-
down game theory perspective, how much uranium should China accumulate before it stops?
