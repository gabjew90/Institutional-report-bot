A t a s t e of Wa rs h’s m e d i c i n e
As widely anticipated, the FOMC concluded with the decision to keep the federal funds rate unchanged 
at 3.75-4%. The most interesting part of the meeting was perhaps the way Kevin Warsh described 
it. Commenting on the effectiveness of the policy, the new Fed leader emphasized that “monetary 
‘medicine’ (...) is beginning to take effect”, adding, however, that it is “still too early to draw any definitive 
conclusions.” The vote was 10-2, with Governor Cook and Cleveland Fed President Hammack dissenting 
in favor of a hike, so part of the room would clearly have prescribed more. The statement itself read 
like a chart update: activity upgraded to “expanding at a strong pace” from “a solid pace”, inflation still 
“elevated”, and the line about a “timelier return” to 2% quietly dropped. The treatment is no longer 
promised to cure on a schedule, and “the accumulation of more information” will decide the next step. 
That leaves a December hike very much on the table, in our view.
This is consistent with our editorial last month: no r*, no forward guidance, only vital signs. The difference 
is that the vital signs were not reassuring in October. US CPI accelerated to 3.7% year-over-year (core 
2.6%), with price pressures broadening beyond gasoline, as attacks on tankers and Houthi strikes on 
Saudi targets took crude to new highs recently. Meanwhile, payrolls of just +29’000 in September 
suggest the medicine may be acting on the labor market faster than on prices, which is precisely the 
side effect a patient dreads. The 10-year hovering around 5% shows that markets are not waiting for the 
doctor’s diagnosis and are pricing their own.
Equities, for their part, were treated for a different ailment. The AI trade, the market’s most prized 
organ, showed signs of strain: reports of a $20bn shortfall in OpenAI’s annualized revenue, Oracle’s 
desperate tricks (trucking natgas to server farms as a workaround to power bottlenecks), Chinese 
chipmaker CXMT closing in on Micron and SK Hynix, and above all Meta posting its first cash outflow 
in over a decade and Alphabet burning $5.9bn in a quarter (its first negative in 22 years as a public 
company). The result was a split decision (S&P 500 -0.9%, Nasdaq-100 -6.7%, Stoxx 600 +1.9%, SMI 
+7.1%, Nikkei 225 -5.2%, MSCI EM -8.1%), with the pain concentrated in AI and semiconductors rather 
than spread across the index.
Proof that when the doctor refuses to give a prognosis, investors are left to read their own charts, and 
this month the AI line on the chart began to look expensive to fund.

C R E D I T
Credit spreads widened marginally in 
Oct (e.g., iTraxx XOVER 5yr 326bp 
i.e., +25bp, CDX HY 5yr 356bp i.e., 
+15bp), driven by surging global 
gov’ bond yields, escalating military 
tensions in the MidEast, murkier 
Fed 
communication 
and 
rising 
political/fiscal risk concerns in Europe 
(esp. around French bond yields). 
Financing conditions are increasingly 
bifurcated, 
favoring 
financially 
stronger issuers, while rising market 
volatility and refinancing pressures 
are leaving financially weaker issuers 
more vulnerable. Under a severely 
pessimistic scenario, the TTM global 
corp default rate for the HY category 
could end the year at 4.5%, according 
to Moody’s, above its long-term 
average of 4.2%. By Sep ‘27, however, 
the rate may climb up to 6.2%.
A bit of ‘monetary medicine’
As anticipated, the Oct 27-28 FOMC 
voted 10-2 to hold the benchmark 
Fed funds rate in a range of 3.75% to 
4% (Governor Cook and Cleveland 
Fed President Hammack dissented, 
preferring to raise rates). While 
uncertainty remains elevated owing, 
in part, to geopolitical developments, 
domestic 
spending 
has 
been 
resilient, and economic activity is 
now described as “expanding at 
a strong pace”, up from “a solid 
pace”. Inflation “remains elevated”, 
as before, but the Fed dropped the 
line that its policy action would 
support a timelier return to the 
2% goal, stressing instead that the 
“accumulation of more information” 
would guide its next steps, therefore 
signalling optionality for a hike in 
Dec. Chair Warsh dodged questions 
on where the Fed’s policy is headed 
going forward, confirming his stance 
against issuing forward guidance. 
“The overwhelming judgement is that 
what we’ve done with this monetary 
medicine is starting to work some,” 
he said, though he noted there were 
no “firm” conclusions yet on the path 
of monetary policy going forward.
The ECB on Oct 29 remained on hold 
(interest rates on the deposit facility, 
the main refi operations and the 
marginal lending facility left at 2.50%, 
2.65% and 2.90% resp.), as expected. 
In its statement, the central bank said 
energy prices had “continued to rise, 
posing upside risks to the inflation 
outlook and downside risks to the 
growth outlook”. Underlying inflation 
has “remained broadly stable so far 
and wages do not show a material 
response at this stage”, it said, but 
warned that the size and duration 
of the energy shock were “likely to 
drive up inflation and lower growth”. 
The meeting was overshadowed by 
President Lagarde’s announcement 
that she will leave her post next Feb, 
months before her term ends.

C O M M O D I T I E S
WTI and Brent futures rallied in Oct 
(+17.8% and +19.3% to $104.1/bbl and 
$113.4/bbl, resp.). Prices edged lower 
early on, after (i) Trump signaled his 
appetite to ban US diesel exports had 
cooled given the risk of unintended 
price spikes, (ii) France proposed 
that European countries and IEA 
members release 100mb of diesel 
and crude, and (iii) Saudi Aramco 
cut prices for its benchmark grade 
to Asia to a six-year low as Persian 
Gulf producers race for market share 
with Hormuz flows increasing. They 
then gradually recovered, after (i) 
Iran stepped up attacks on tankers in 
the SoH, (ii) reports surfaced that the 
White House asked the Pentagon for 
strike options against Iran executable 
before the midterms, (iii) offshore US 
oil production dropped as Hurricane 
Isaias pushed toward a refinery-dotted 
coastline, and (iv) Houthi rebels 
attacked Saudi airports over several 
days. Futures jumped mid-month, as 
(i) a major refinery in Ryazan (Russia) 
caught fire, with Ukraine pledging 
to resume hitting Russian crude-
processing facilities, (ii) Israeli reports 
suggested Iran may be preparing a 
pre-emptive strike following unusual 
military movements detected by 
Western intelligence, and (iii) crude 
inventories surprisingly fell by 1.9mb 
to 410.6mb in the week ended Oct 
23 (vs. cons. +15k), before slipping 
over the last days, as (iv) investors 
weighed a meeting between the 
Iranian president and Pakistan’s army 
chief, a key US-Iran mediator, against 
continued Houthi attacks on Saudi 
infrastructure. According to the IEA, 
despite lower demand, oil market 
balances are forecast to remain 
in deficit through year-end, with 
constrained middle distillate supplies 
likely to persist well into ‘27. World 
oil demand will fall by 2.5mb/d YoY in 
4Q26 and 2.6mb/d on average in ‘26. 
Oil supply, meanwhile, is projected 
to decline by an average 5.8mb/d to 
100.7mb/d this year, before reversing 
and rising by 7.7mb/d in ‘27.
Gold back to $4,000/oz
Gold (spot -6.8%) hit its lowest level 
since mid-Aug, as oil climbed and 
inflation 
concerns 
grew, 
leading 
to renewed bets on a hawkish Fed 
ahead (traders pricing roughly a 80% 
chance of a 25bp hike at the Dec 
FOMC meeting vs. less than 40% a 
month ago). Prices across the base 
metals complex were mixed. Iron 
ore underperformed (Dalian active 
futures -4.2%) on oversupply from 
robust shipments by major suppliers, 
combined with weak demand from 
loss-making 
Chinese 
steel 
mills. 
Copper struggled (LME 3M futures 
-1.3%) despite mine supply disruptions 
in 
Chile, 
where 
Antofagasta’s 
Centinela had a week-long stoppage 
and Codelco’s El Teniente remains 
shut after a deadly landslide.
F O R E IGN E XCHAN G E
The dollar edged higher (DXY +0.4%) to hit its 
highest level in over three months as a selloff in 
European bonds and renewed Iranian attacks in the 
Middle East fueled investor appetite for havens. 
Despite some concerns around the US AI complex, 
the greenback even jumped in the middle of the 
month against all of its G10 peers after higher-
than-expected US inflation (headline/core CPI 
+3.7%/+2.6% YoY in Sept vs. +3.4%/+2.4% in Aug) 
pushed traders to ramp up bets on Federal Reserve 
rate hikes (roughly a 80% chance of a 25bp hike at 
the Dec FOMC meeting vs. less than 40% a month 
ago). The euro weakened (EURUSD -1.5%) as French 
government bonds slumped after a weekend of 
violent unrest and rising tensions between the US 
and Ukraine over its war with Russia.
Most EM currencies weakened as surging oil 
prices and elevated US Treasury yields weighed on 
risk appetite. The Brazilian real underperformed 
(USDBRL +5.1%) following Lula’s surprise re-
election, amid concerns over increased spending 
and fiscal challenges. Mexico’s peso struggled, too 
(USDMXN +4.0%), as a dovish Banxico rhetoric (it 
won’t mechanically mirror Fed hikes) sharpened the 
focus on a narrowing rate differential, with carry-
trade unwinds, geopolitical uncertainty and stalled 
USMCA negotiations adding to the pressure.
T H E M O N T H A H E A D
Incoming economic data releases include U.S. ISM 
indices (Nov 2) and U.S. nonfarm payrolls (Nov 6). 
Neither the Fed nor the ECB will convene this month, 
but minutes from the Oct FOMC meeting (Nov 18) will 
be read carefully, as several officials dissented in favor of 
a rate hike amid lingering price pressures.
