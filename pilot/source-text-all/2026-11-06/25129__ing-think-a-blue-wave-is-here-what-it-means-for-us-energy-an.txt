  SUSTAINABILITY   UNITED STATES
How the blue wave could reshape US
energy and data centres
Energy affordability shaped the midterms. A divided government will
now face pressure to shield consumers from rising data centre power
costs while speeding up new energy supply. Onshoring and trade
protections will continue, despite higher near-term costs, as energy
security remains a priority
After the midterm elections, the government will need to minimise data centre-related costs for
consumers, bring new power supply online faster and protect supply chains. Pictured: Data centre in Ohio
Exit polls from the US midterm elections highlighted a key concern shaping voters’ decisions:
energy affordability. Nearly 90% of voters said the cost of living was a crucial or major factor in
their vote for Congress, with many pointing to gasoline and utility costs. Meanwhile, as the AI
data centre boom strains power supply and infrastructure, 56% said the issue was a crucial or
major factor in their vote.
With Democrats now set to control both the House and Senate, what does this mean for US
energy and data centre policy?
Newly elected lawmakers will face pressure to address these concerns, requiring the
Democratic Congress and Republican White House to find common ground on shielding
consumers from data centre-related costs and accelerating new power supply, including
through permitting reform. Onshoring and supply chain protections are also likely to continue.
While they can raise near-term costs, they remain important to US energy security.

Developments in all three areas could have significant implications for businesses.
That said, Congress needs to strike a balance when drafting legislation aimed at achieving
these goals. More aggressive measures could face a veto from President Trump.
Strengthened data centre regulation ahead, but the devil is in
the detail
The data centre industry is facing heightened local opposition. Recent polls suggest that
around one in seven Americans is concerned about or opposes data centre development in
their neighbourhoods.
In response to this issue, there is likely to be tighter data centre regulation. State-level policy
will be more important to watch, as action is politically and practically easier at this level. The
most likely approach is targeted regulation, much of which is already emerging. This includes
data centre-related taxes, reduced tax incentives, environmental standards, and requirements
for developers to bear relevant cost increases. Outright moratoriums remain unlikely to
become mainstream. Where they are introduced, they are more likely to be temporary, giving
states time to develop a data centre policy framework.
At the federal level, the key unanswered question is how much authority should remain with
the states and whether federal legislation should mandate state regulations or simply provide
guidance. Republicans generally favour letting states lead, while Democrats tend to support a
stronger federal role.
We think sweeping data centre legislation is unlikely because of President Trump’s veto power.
Federal action is more likely to focus on affordability by protecting electricity customers from
higher costs and formalising data centres' responsibility to help fund grid and energy
infrastructure upgrades. Such measures could be added to broader legislation, such as must-
pass spending bills needed to avoid a government shutdown, or a bipartisan package like
permitting reform.
More broadly on AI, we expect the Democratic Party to increase scrutiny of technology
companies, as discussed here, while utilising the next two years to prepare an AI governance
framework for 2028 and beyond.
However, tighter AI and data centre regulation is unlikely to fundamentally alter the industry’s
growth trajectory. AI’s importance to US economic growth and competition with China will
remain powerful drivers of expansion.

Business implications
State-level policy would more directly affect project siting, which is important for data
centre companies to watch. Where state rules are tighter, project development
could slow. However, a more restrictive regulatory environment could also increase the
value of data centres already approved or under construction, easing some of the
industry’s capital constraints. Higher valuations could also encourage developers to be
more selective and expand at a more measured pace, ultimately improving the quality
of the project pipeline. 
Developers should also treat community engagement as a strategic investment.
Recent surveys show that while only 20-30% of Americans support local data centre
construction, support can rise to 50-70% when there are accompanying community
investments, such as in public infrastructure improvements or reductions in residents'
costs. More developers are now signing binding community benefit agreements as part
of project development.
Finally, sustainability will become more important for project development, not only to
meet potential environmental standards, but also to build community support. Read

Enabling faster power generation through permitting reform
Both parties support timely expansion of electricity supply to meet rising demand from AI and
manufacturing reshoring. They also generally agree that energy abundance can help reduce
cost pressures. While Democrats and Republicans differ on the preferred energy mix, there is

However, the greatest potential for policy action may lie not in specific technologies, but in
permitting reform.
Today, many energy projects can spend over five years navigating the federal permitting
process. Around $240bn to $280bn of infrastructure investment across the eight sectors below
enters the permitting pipeline each year. Lengthy delays can therefore leave substantial
capital tied up and project benefits unrealised.

With both parties recognising the need for reform, the Senate introduced the Bipartisan
American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) in September 2026. Key permitting proposals
include:
Speeding up approvals for energy projects
Providing greater certainty and consistency across administrations
Making it easier to permit power transmission lines
Despite debate over its environmental impact, there is broad agreement that the BAAJA would
meaningfully improve the permitting process. The key question is whether it can become law
under the current Congress. The Senate is expected to vote on 9 November, after which the bill
would still need approval from the House and President Trump. Democrats may want to delay
the bill and seek changes once they regain control of Congress next year, but doing so could
increase the risk of a presidential veto. This creates an incentive to reach a deal sooner rather
than later.

Even if the BAAJA does not pass under the current Congress, permitting reform is likely to
remain a bipartisan theme. The growing need to expand power supply and infrastructure more
quickly will keep pressure on future Congresses to streamline permitting.
Business implications
For energy companies – both renewables and fossil fuels – the BAAJA could
significantly shorten permitting timelines. If enacted, it would give project developers
greater certainty, reducing the risk of litigation and permit cancellations. This could
further expand project siting options for developers.
Nevertheless, in the very near term, projects may need to retain flexibility around their
permitting strategy while the new policy framework is developed and implemented.
Expect more protectionism and reshoring efforts against China
dominance
From clean energy technologies to the critical minerals that underpin much of the US
economy, China controls a large share of global production capacity. Consequently, reducing
reliance on China will remain a US policy priority. Such efforts would increase rather than
reduce project costs in the near term. But the long-term benefits of stronger domestic
manufacturing and more secure supply chains could outweigh those costs.

geopolitical tensions. We have already seen this with rare earths, where Chinese export
restrictions contributed to disruptions for US auto manufacturers. Second, China’s supply chain
dominance can put US manufacturers at a competitive disadvantage.
Building more secure energy and natural resource supply chains has long attracted bipartisan
support in the US. The Trump administration has supported domestic rare earth production
through funding, purchase agreements, and equity investments. In solar, China-focused trade
and tax credit restrictions are also supporting the expansion of domestic manufacturing.
We expect this focus to continue. The executive branch could pursue further
protective measures and is unlikely to face major opposition from Congress. Congress could
also authorise additional funding to strengthen rare earth supply chains.
Furthermore, a Democratic-controlled Congress could push to extend clean manufacturing tax
credits as part of broader negotiations with President Trump. Given the administration’s focus
on countering China and expanding US manufacturing, an extension could emerge as a
compromise in a broader legislative package under an optimistic scenario. All these efforts
would also extend into future administrations.
Business implications
For US companies:
Clean energy companies and other industries that rely on rare earths, including
semiconductors, AI, defence, and electronics, should have enough policy certainty to
build long-term supplier relationships outside China. In the near term, however,
diversification could raise supply chain costs as alternative sources may be more
expensive. Solar could face particular cost pressures, as some tariffs on supply chain
equipment also apply to non-Chinese suppliers.
For overseas companies:
US efforts to build supply chains outside China are creating opportunities for
manufacturers elsewhere. The US is supporting rare earth production in Australia and
Brazil, while solar and battery suppliers in countries such as South Korea and Japan
could benefit from stronger demand. This could also encourage more manufacturing
investment in the US.
Meanwhile, European clean energy developers could face greater competition for non-
Chinese supplies. While the EU has not imposed US-style tariffs, it is tightening
restrictions on Chinese equipment through funding, procurement, auction, and subsidy
rules. This could intensify global competition for non-Chinese suppliers and push costs
higher.
