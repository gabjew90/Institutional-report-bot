Credit Strategy Weekly 
More treats than tricks 
Credit markets recover but… Our rates colleagues believe there are No tricks or treats, but, in our 
view, the credit markets are ready for more treats than tricks. And this week we already saw a 
healthy recovery after the sharp widening of previous weeks. We believe this was the result of high 
levels of uncertainty and a market willing to shoot first and ask questions later. This week has been 
the opposite, with more stability in the sovereign bond world and the oil markets, even though 
prices remain very high. But at least nothing worsened materially, and this was enough for the credit 
markets to sigh with relief. We believe the markets will recover some of the ground lost but we no 
longer expect them to recover fully and hit our previous year-end forecasts. Rather, we see a little 
respite into year-end, before credit comes under pressure again. 
… the issues have not been resolved. We believe that the better tone in the credit markets 
this week doesn’t remove the issues that pushed spreads wider in the past couple of months. 
That is, oil prices remain volatile (and rising), the war in the Middle East remains uncertain, and 
sovereign bonds remain volatile. The good news is that, for now, economic growth is still 
holding (eurozone growth surprised on the upside, though French numbers disappointed) and 
earnings continue to look very healthy. The key for us is stability, and if sovereign bonds move 
in very gentle ranges, even if yields go up, then credit can gain further ground for longer. 
A decent week for the primary markets offers hope for the rest of the year. The primary 
markets had a strong start to the week, with €13.65bn priced in the first two sessions. Activity 
then tailed off and, ultimately, we saw a total of €19bn priced (excluding Friday’s deals), in line 
with last week’s €17.3bn. This put the total for the month at €51.7bn and the year-to-date at 
€765.7bn. We expect November to post very strong activity again (like last year) and see 
volumes rising above last year’s record numbers.

Macro view 
Elections, economic data and the 3Q earnings season. This week 
saw a recovery from the poor performance of previous weeks across 
most markets. Sovereign bonds were more stable (even though 
yields remain very high) while oil prices crept higher at a slower pace 
than previously. And we had the FOMC and ECB meetings, with 
central banks keeping rates unchanged. Our economists still expect 
the Fed to hike once more by March 2027, and the ECB to hike one 
or two more times. Next week brings the US midterm elections, with 
the Democrats expected to do well. We’ll also have several 
economic data releases, including the NFP figures on Friday, as well 
as the second busiest week of the 3Q earnings season on both sides 
of the Atlantic. All of these events should prove relatively positive for 
credit, or at least we don’t believe they will have a major negative 
impact. However, another sharp deterioration in the Middle East, or 
an increase in sovereign bond volatility would very likely push credit 
spreads wider across the board. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Cash 
Better all round. As soon as we published our more bearish 
spread forecasts, the credit markets began to recover. But we 
already expected a better tone into year-end than was seen in 
previous weeks, believing that credit fundamentals remain solid. 
In our view, the sharp widening we saw prior to our change in 
forecasts was the result of a very quick rise in macro uncertainty 
and not a reflection of the overall health of the credit markets. So 
this week the euro IG index gained back 6bp and all other indices 
rallied too. Excess returns improved, total returns were a little 
higher and we should see a better performance into year end. 
However, as our latest spread forecasts indicate, the issues that 
pushed credit wider remain. That is, oil prices are still high and the 
next steps in the war in the Middle East are anyone’s guess. 
Sovereign bonds remain under pressure and many risks remain. 
Credit should recover gently, but we still expect wider spreads in 
1H27.

Strategy Scorecard 
Regular readers will be familiar with our SG Credit Scorecard, a tool we have used for more than 
a decade to help us forecast the direction of credit spreads.  
For readers who are not familiar with the Scorecard, we construct it in three stages. First, we 
group indicators into various categories. Macroeconomic fundamentals, for example, 
comprises GDP and inflation numbers, but also indicators such as monetary policy and fiscal 
policy. Second, we compare our forecasts for these indicators over the next six to 12 months with 
the consensus view. This gives us an impact, which can range from a maximum positive of +3 to 
a maximum negative of -3. These are shown in the table as +++ and ---; a neutral reading is =. 
Finally, we assign a weight to the indicator. This ranges from 1 (relatively unimportant) to 3 
(relatively important), shown in the table as a number of boxes. The weights depend on our view 
of where we are in the credit cycle. The scorecard for each indicator is the impact multiplied by 
the weight, so it can range from +9 to -9. The average level of macroeconomic fundamentals is 
therefore the average of the two sub-indicators, GDP and inflation and policy; the average level 
of the whole scorecard is the average of the five indicators (macroeconomic fundamentals, 
corporate fundamentals, investor demand, supply, and valuations).

The week ahead 
Elections, sovereigns volatility and the rest. Next week will see the US go to the polls for 
Tuesday’s mid-term elections. The markets will be watching closely and if the Democrats take 
control of Congress there could be a shift in US policy on all fronts. Closer to home, we believe 
the credit markets will continue to look towards the sovereign bond markets. If volatility doesn’t 
flare up, then we suspect credit will continue its slow recovery from the dismal performance of 
the past couple of months. And finally, we’ll be keeping an eye on developments in the Middle 
East, any resulting impact on oil prices and the final busy week of the earnings season. 
A full economic calendar to come. Next week we’ll have a very busy economic calendar, with 
PMI data in the three major economic blocs, unemployment figures, trade numbers in the US and 
some sentiment indicators. In our view, the employment figures and the inflation numbers will 
be among the most important numbers. And at the moment the market expects CPI in the 
eurozone to inch a little higher with the y/y measure at 3.9% vs 3.8% in September and the core 
figure which is expected to remain unchanged at 2.5%.

Yields and curves 
Yields move in small ranges 
This week credit spreads improved but sovereign bonds moved up and down and, in the end, the 
combination of the two saw credit yields improve gently. The iBoxx Corporates index yield 
dropped to 4.39%, some 5bp lower than a week ago, and 6bp lower than at the end of September. 
In the meantime, the HY index also saw the yield drop, albeit at a higher pace thanks to the 
stronger spread rally. The yield settled around 7.09%, around 20bp better than a week ago, after 
hitting 7.31% on Monday, the highest level of the year so far. 
Yields heading down slowly now 
 
Source: Markit, SG Cross Asset Research/Credit 
The USD indices had a more mixed performance, in the first half of the week, but the gentle rally 
in the US Treasury market in the second half of the week helped yields fall across the board. The 
IG index saw the yield fall by 4bp to 6.19% (some 180bp higher than the euro IG index yield) while 
the HY index saw the yield drop by around 15bp to 8.28%.

This week the Industrials umbrella remained the most active sector thanks to a couple of multi-
tranche deals. We also had some Consumer deals as well as one Real Estate bond out. So far this 
year, Industrials, TMT and Utilities remain the most active sectors and this is unlikely to change 
by the end of the year. 
The breakdown by maturity saw bonds from every tenor but there was a tilt towards the longer 
maturities with the 7-10y and 10y+ tenor being particularly active. As we can see in the left-hand 
chart below, all tenors are having an almost identical share of issuance, with the exception of the 
short end which is lagging the rest.
