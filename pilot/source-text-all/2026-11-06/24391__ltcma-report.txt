uncertainty and volatility around geopolitics, oil markets, deficits, valuations and

couldn’t be a better time to share J.P. Morgan Asset Management’s 2027 Long-Term 
Capital Market Assumptions. 
For the publication’s 31st year, we have gathered a team of 90-plus industry-leading 
portfolio managers, economists and research analysts worldwide to produce 
these return assumptions for 200-plus assets in more than 20 base currencies. 
Many investors, including our own, rely on these assumptions to set strategic asset 
allocations and establish risk and return expectations for the coming 10 to 15 years. 
The goal is the same across all of our insights: to give clients the tools to navigate a 
complex world with facts and research.
This year, our experts expect resilient markets with a wider range of outcomes.
They forecast stronger growth, greater AI-driven productivity and higher corporate

two decades. In this evolving environment, advice, research and risk management 
earn their keep. 
For more than three decades, the LTCMAs have reflected our dedication to rigorous,

On behalf of J.P. Morgan Asset Management, we hope our 2027 analysis helps guide your

The best developed world growth outlook in 15 years 
A global investment surge, centered on AI and extending to 2040, boosts total factor productivity by 50%, 
to 120bps, pushing our U.S. real GDP growth forecast up to 2.1% and developed world nominal growth up 
to 4.2%. The mix of GDP-to-inflation is the most favorable since the pandemic.
U.S. equity upgrades, a global bond renewal and a first look at bitcoin 
Profit margins of over 15% push our U.S. large cap equity forecast up 80bps, to 7.5%, leading to our 
developed market equity forecasts overtaking our emerging market equity forecasts. Higher real yields 
drive global aggregate bond forecasts from a low of 2.1% in 2021 to 5.3% today – the highest in two 
decades. And for the first time, we put a range on bitcoin returns, though we do not yet include bitcoin in 
our main covariance matrices.
Alternatives for return enhancement, diversification and inflation resilience 
We upgrade diversified hedge funds by 140bps on sustained alpha trends and higher rates – with 
alpha to play for, manager selection is vital. Real asset returns are resilient in the face of persistent 
inflation volatility, with global transportation returning 8.1%, global core real estate 7.9% and global 
infrastructure 7.1%.
A USD 15 trillion global capex supernova fuels higher growth and strong asset returns over the next 10 to 15 years. 
Productivity gains mitigate rising inflation risks and an aging workforce. Despite the tail risks, a resilient economy 
drives resilient markets.
Three forces shape the future and drive returns 
Economic nationalism boosts domestic investment but risks a crosscurrent of inflation volatility. 
Technology adoption is the fair wind filling the global economy’s sails. Rising domestic investment 
drives capital rewiring, resulting in shifts in public and private capital flows. 
Greater opportunities to diversify, greater rewards for doing so 
A balanced global stock-bond portfolio rises 60bps, to 7.0%, but higher stock-bond correlation demands 
further diversification. Alternatives can boost risk-adjusted returns: Allocating 30% to diversified 
alternatives can push returns up to 7.5% and boost Sharpe ratios by 21% compared with a simple 
balanced portfolio. Diversifying relative to themes as well as across assets can further strengthen 
portfolio resilience.

In brief 
•	 The 31st annual edition of our Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions 
explores how three key themes – economic nationalism, technology 
adoption and capital rewiring – will define the economy and drive asset 
returns over the next 10 to 15 years. We expect governments to invest in 
national resiliency, companies to race to deploy new innovations and these 
changing patterns of spending to shift how capital gets deployed.
•	 A USD 15 trillion global capex supernova, centered on technology adoption, 
fuels higher growth and strong asset returns over the next 10 to 15 years. 
Productivity gains mitigate rising inflation risks and an aging workforce. 
We raise our global growth outlook by 20 basis points (bps), to 4.2%, 
while also forecasting elevated inflation volatility.
•	 Higher growth boosts profits and yields in pushing up the projected 
returns for a global balanced stock-bond portfolio in USD1 by 60bps, to 7.0%. 
A balanced portfolio with a 30% alternatives allocation sees forecasts rise 
50bps, to 7.5%, and enjoys a 21% higher Sharpe ratio compared with a public 
markets-only portfolio.
•	 We see more resilient profit margins in the U.S. than in emerging markets, 
which are dominated by traditionally cyclical sectors, where margins for 
artificial intelligence hardware are at extreme levels. As a result, developed 
market equity forecasts rise above emerging market forecasts.
•	 Global bond return forecasts are the highest in two decades as real yields 
have fully normalized and put the abnormally low returns of the post-
financial crisis era firmly behind them.
•	 The trade-weighted dollar remains structurally overvalued, but more so 
against Asian than European cross-currency pairs. 
•	 Higher inflation volatility, ongoing risk of currency debasement and growing 
opportunities to capture active alpha reinforce the role of alternatives as 
essential portfolio diversifiers and drivers of return.

In each of our annual editions after the pandemic, 
we highlighted increased investment, solid growth 
and more volatile inflation, making the case for strong 
returns despite geopolitical tension. In 2026, the pattern 
extended: A global balanced stock-bond portfolio in USD 
delivered a 9.1%2 total return – 220 basis points (bps) 
above the 25-year average. 
Looking ahead, we acknowledge that volatility is 
inevitable over our 10- to 15-year forecast horizon as a 
global capex boom fuels higher growth and strong asset 
returns. Productivity gains mitigate rising inflation risks 
and an aging workforce. Despite the tail risks, a resilient 
economy drives resilient markets. This environment calls 
for a broad investment opportunity set – and raises the 
rewards for diversification.
This view attracts scrutiny, and rightly so. Market 
participants point to demographics, deficits, energy 
transition and geopolitical fragmentation as causes for 
concern. These issues are important, but they are starting 
conditions that define the world today. The choices made 
to adapt to them will define the world, and the returns, 
of tomorrow. 
Three powerful forces shape the economy and drive 
returns. Economic nationalism boosts domestic 
investment but risks a crosscurrent of inflation volatility. 
Technology adoption is the fair wind filling the global 
economy’s sails. Rising domestic investment drives 
capital rewiring, resulting in shifts in public and 
private capital flows.
Economic nationalism3 has become entrenched as 
governments focus inward and spend on projects that 
bolster domestic resilience, at times trading economic 
efficiency for political expediency. Some elements of 
economic nationalism – industrial strategy, for instance – 
can be useful in reframing global issues such as climate 
change.4 While this can add to overall demand over time, 
it may also spur inflation and market volatility.

Technology adoption5 today is synonymous with artificial 
intelligence (AI), but breakthroughs in other areas, 
from medicine to robotics to space, will likely advance 
in the next decade, further supporting trend growth via 
investment and productivity. As investment intensifies, 
there is scope for capital rewiring.6 Former nation savers 
will become nation spenders. 
Capital allocation ultimately steers the economy toward 
its destination. This has important implications for 
the nature and geographic spread of growth over our 
forecast horizon.
These themes may be disruptive, but together they boost 
our G77 nominal growth forecast by 30bps, to 4.2% – the 
best in 15 years, with the best mix of real growth and 
inflation since the pandemic (Exhibit 1). Still, economies 
driven by investment and productivity risk becoming 
more unequal as gains accrue faster to capital than 
to labor. That inequality is central to today’s K-shaped 
economy,8 which likely persists over our investment 
horizon. A K-shaped economy, with unequal returns 
to capital vs. labor, is a metastable state – surprisingly 
durable, but sensitive to stress or shock. Its persistence 
relies on channeling sufficient economic reward to the 
lower leg of the K to maintain stability while optimizing the 
growth and profit accruing to the upper leg of the K.
Higher growth and fiscal deficits imply higher real rates 
and larger term risk premia. These factors, combined 
with starting yields that have fully normalized from the 
low rates of the 2010s, give us the highest global bond 
forecasts in two decades.

Cyclical challenges won’t derail long-
term returns 
In a centuries-old tale, a weary traveler asks a farmer 
how to get to Dublin, only to be told, “Well, I wouldn’t start 
from here.” The story should resonate with investors 
today. The cyclical starting point for their journey includes 
demographic challenges, high deficits, ongoing energy 
transition and geopolitical fragmentation.
But while these conditions define our cyclical starting 
point and create a hurdle, they do not derail long-term 
growth or asset returns. Indeed, the normalization of 
yields since 2022 creates a compelling entry point not 
just for bonds but also for other assets, including many 
alternatives, that have repriced relative to bonds.

Post-pandemic, governments are more willing to use 
fiscal levers to ignite better growth – and if corporations 
can be brought along for the ride, all the better. 
Fiscal spending by G7 nations in the 2020s has risen by 
7% compared with the 2010s, and corporate capex is now 
growing at 6.1%, 1.4% and 1.6% year-over-year in the U.S., 
Europe and Japan, respectively. 
Investment is a necessary precondition for lifting 
productivity. The McKinsey Global Institute finds that 
a change in investment11 explains 80% of productivity 
gains from 1997 to 2022. With investment rising as fiscal 
faucets are loosened and firms are scrambling to assert 
technology leadership, we see growth supported and 
increasingly dictated by capital deepening12 and total 
factor productivity (TFP).13 Even then, our productivity 
forecasts14 are more measured than OECD estimates.15
Higher overall growth can partially offset unequal 
distribution of economic gains across capital and labor, 
but it cannot entirely mitigate the division that fuels 
economic nationalist tendencies. These spending forces 
will influence both demand and potential supply. How this 
nets out will determine inflation. On average over our 
forecast horizon, we expect supply to keep pace and 
therefore inflation to remain in check, and are forecasting 
a global rate of 2.6%. However, we anticipate periods of 
supply-demand mismatch and therefore episodes of 
choppy inflation along the way, particularly in higher 
deficit nations such as the U.S. and the UK. 
Economic nationalism: The good, the bad 
and the dangerous 
This year, we dive deeper into our first theme, economic 
nationalism, which we have discussed in prior editions. 
It is a response to growing concerns over security and 
resilience. Economic nationalism is also a consequence 
of inequality16 and political polarization, creating fertile 
ground that more-extreme political opportunists 
can exploit.

Economic nationalism can be a headwind to growth, 
yet some aspects are certainly good housekeeping. 
Developing an industrial strategy can create strategic 
advantages, as we explore in our article on how climate 
change affects trend growth.17 Improving supply chains, 
energy security and defense can boost investment 
and near-term growth. Nevertheless, such spending 
may demand trade-offs between economic efficiency 
and resiliency.18 
Even where intent is benign, spending driven by an 
agenda shaped by economic nationalism carries the 
risk that the investment fails to translate into productivity 
gains or that it crowds out other useful investments. 
At the more extreme end of policy choices, restrictions on 
trade and labor migration can directly suppress growth. 
On balance, we see economic nationalism as a modest 
growth risk but a greater risk to inflation. 
In this year’s Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions, 
cash return forecasts are mostly stable. Meanwhile, 
our projection of modestly steeper curves at equilibrium, 
together with higher starting yields, pushes up bond 
return forecasts sharply, leading to global sovereign 
bonds offering the best return forecasts in two decades

starting to glimpse the economy-wide potential of AI. 
While only 6% of U.S. work hours are AI-assisted today,

AI adoption is following a contour familiar from prior 
innovation cycles.20 Initially, innovators capture 
supernormal profits and invest to build scale. Then other 
sectors jump in; only later are rewards shared more 
broadly as investments become embedded into business 
processes. The transition from the early to later phases 
is often bumpy. But over the full cycle, we see AI providing 
an upside risk to trend growth sufficient to more than 
outweigh the negative impact on labor force growth that 
comes from population aging and hostility to migration. 
AI capex has boosted profits in regions with large 
technology sectors, notably the U.S., Korea and Taiwan. 
Over our full investment horizon, tech sector profit 
growth will likely moderate from current levels. But as 
productivity gains materialize, earnings in other sectors 
should rise. Korean and Taiwanese indices are dominated 
by cyclical technology sectors (chipmakers). Even if 
this tech cycle proves more durable than past ones, 
eventual normalization of cyclical tech profits weighs 
on aggregate margins, as other sectors are too small to 
provide an offset. Since Korea and Taiwan account for 
51% of the emerging market (EM) index, our EM equity 10-
15 year forecast falls below developed markets for the first 
time in our publication’s history (Exhibit 3). 
Developed market equity returns are higher this year, 
supported by margins and the investment cycle, but high 
cyclical margins in Korea and Taiwan bring down emerging 
market returns

By contrast, the U.S. is more balanced across defensive 
and cyclical technology sectors, and is well diversified 
beyond tech. So even as tech profits normalize, we expect 
profits in other sectors to counteract the decline, 
resulting in greater aggregate margin resilience. U.S. 
firms also have a strong record of turning capex into 
returns: U.S. RoIC over the last decade averages around 
17%, vs. low-teens in Europe and Japan. The result is 
a sizable uplift to our U.S. equity forecast, and the U.S. 
vs. rest of world (RoW) return differential swings in 
favor of the U.S., from -70bps last year to +10bps in this 
year’s edition. 
The technology cycle also widens the opportunity set 
for private equity (PE) funds, partially offsetting the 
drag from dry powder21 and the still-challenging exit 
backdrop. Adoption of AI and automation is reshaping 
cost structures and competitive moats, in turn creating 
clearer dispersion between winners and losers. 
Together with growing competition for capital and a 
pipeline of dynamic innovation, these factors support 
the alpha share of total returns in large cap private equity 
– even as higher financing costs trim large cap private 
equity median manager returns by 60bps, to 9.6%.
Aside from PE, investment in the ecosystem enabling AI 
(compute, power, cooling, fiber and data centers) also 
supports returns across infrastructure, as well as in the

Asset allocation: Fair winds, choppy waters 
Our upbeat growth outlook may seem at odds with 
today’s flood of global headlines. Still, we’re constructive 
on returns despite the later-cycle turbulence. The key is 
building a resilient portfolio. 
We examine the rationale for upgrading productivity 
forecasts and margins in the macro and public markets 
chapters. Still, we recognize the inherent uncertainty 
involved in adopting a disruptive innovation and in 
translating a burgeoning investment cycle into real and 
durable economic gains. We also acknowledge that 
valuations offer little cushion and that capex cycles often 
take a volatile final turn. 
So alongside upgrading our return forecasts, we raise 
our volatility projections, notably in equities. Sharpe ratios 
are up for U.S. large cap equity, flat for EAFE and down 
for EM equity, where extreme cyclical margin expansion 
has effectively borrowed a lot from future returns 
(Exhibits 7A and 7B). 
As a result, the relative Sharpe ratios between public 
and private assets tilt in favor of real assets. This reflects 
our economic narrative of increased competition for 
capital and higher inflation volatility: Alternative assets 
with inflation resilience, capacity for alpha capture and 
low correlation to public markets are an essential part of 
portfolios in the world we envision. 
Hedge funds are winners from this new world. 
The higher yields we expect provide a boost to hedge 
funds via the carry collected on their short positions. 
As governments look for new ways to fund growth and 
pools of capital open up, opportunities for uncorrelated 
alpha, which hedge funds are positioned to capture, 
increase sharply. These supportive factors, together with 
improvements to our forecasting methodology, push 
our diversified hedge fund median manager returns up 
140bps, to 6.7%, this year – with dispersion across funds 
offering further rewards from manager selection. 
Without question, there are risks. Strong asset returns in 
2026 may imply calm waters, but our themes of economic 
nationalism, tech adoption and capital rewiring create 
potent undercurrents. Our outlook implicitly projects a 
continuation of the K-shaped economy, which doubtless 
creates social (and moral) issues that policymakers must 
grapple with.

equities are little changed overall while Sharpe ratios for real assets remain strong.

The greatest risk to our outlook is a miscalculation 
of the balance between the upper and lower legs of 
the K. Underappreciating the needs of the labor pool 
could destabilize the system. Equally, overzealous 
attempts to redistribute returns from capital to labor 
could disincentivize investment. While we expect the 
metastable equilibrium of the K-shaped economy to 
persist, factors that push the balance of rewards between 
capital and labor too far in either direction could cause 
bouts of volatility. 
Other risks to our constructive outlook include 
restrictions on the flow of capital and widespread capital 
misallocation. Economic nationalism today affects labor 
and trade, but it has left capital flows largely untouched. 
Should this change, investment-led growth would 
surely suffer. Meanwhile, if today’s investment boom 
fails to boost productivity, the net effect would be lower 
trend growth and excess capacity that raises the risk 
of disinflation. 
There are topside risks as well. Past innovation cycles 
have led to meaningful improvements in living standards. 
If that recurs, it might restrain some of the most 
damaging instincts of populism. And as AI adoption 
spreads, its impact can compound when it combines with 
other, possibly more mundane, inventions in business 
supply chains, creating further upside potential for 
corporate profits. 
Portfolios: More return, more risk, a lot 
more opportunity 
Over our 10- to 15-year investment horizon, we believe 
that investors can capitalize on the fair winds of solid 
growth and favorable asset returns while navigating the 
undercurrents of economic nationalism, tech adoption 
and capital rewiring. 
In portfolios, this calls for balance and robustness. 
It also demands willingness to use a broad investment 
opportunity set. A strong margin outlook reinforces the 
case for U.S. equities in portfolios despite concerns 
over valuation and sector concentration. Still, regional 
diversification is essential. 
Diversification with EM equities is compromised by 
stretched cyclical margins, which weigh heavily on return 
forecasts. Increasingly, however, developed market 
equities offer diversification options, particularly in 
Europe and Japan, where rising capex and investment 
are improving the growth outlook. 
High quality bonds reflect much of the inflation volatility 
we anticipate in current yields. Should growth undershoot 
our projections, we see upside to our bond return 
forecasts. This in turn implies diversification potential to 
stocks, even allowing for positive stock-bond correlation 
driven by a volatile inflation environment. 
A balanced stock-bond portfolio is an attractive starting 
point. But to navigate the themes in play, as well as 
uncertainty around the investment cycle and policy, 
alternatives can play a vital role in portfolios. 
Alternatives can serve as return enhancers – adding 30% 
of a blend of private equity, venture capital and direct 
lending can boost balanced portfolio returns by 80bps 
and the Sharpe ratio by 11%. Alternatives can also serve 
as diversifiers – adding 30% of a blend of diversified 
hedge funds, gold, infrastructure and transportation 
boosts the balanced portfolio Sharpe ratio by 22% while 
maintaining portfolio returns (Exhibits 8A and 8B). 
Other alternative assets, such as real estate, can serve 
both to diversify and to enhance returns, given that our 
global core real estate forecast sits at 7.9% while global 
value-added real estate is at 9.3%.
Investing in a volatile world shaped by the three powerful 
themes we have discussed can be daunting (Exhibit 9). 
Focusing too much on the prevailing news cycle is 
distracting, but thoughtful asset allocation can keep the 
impulse in check. And when events heat up, a robust and 
diversified portfolio allows us to ask calmly: Has anything 
changed other than the price?

Government spending: The quest for 
economic resilience
We have acknowledged for some years that the 
global economy has entered a new regime in which 
governments need and desire to influence economic 
activity. Events over the past year have only deepened 
that conviction. Uncertainty about the reliability of 
traditional allies and the prospect of supply chains being 
weaponized have broadened the list of strategic activities 
that many governments now deem necessary to bring 
back in-house – from defense and energy to critical 
minerals and, increasingly, technological capabilities. 
Economic nationalism also shows up in political 
pressures to reduce immigration. 
The challenge comes in paying for these self-sufficiency 
initiatives. Decades of outsourcing created room for 
expanding welfare and making rising commitments to 
aging electorates. Reversing that outsourcing means 
funding both self-sufficiency and social spending 
priorities as political pressures to reduce immigration 
further slow the growth of the working population.
Reducing government deficits and putting public debt 
on a sustainable path at a time of rising spending looks 
exceptionally challenging in many Western economies, 
not least the U.S. Indeed, U.S. policymakers appear to 
have accepted that debt will rise faster than output and 
are asking bond investors for larger amounts each year. 
This should support rising yields over the forecast horizon.1
AI investment and deployment become 
the primary growth accelerants
Equity investors have become accustomed to 
excitement about AI and other evolving technologies, 
but until recently their measurable growth effects were 
concentrated in the capital spent to create them – on 
data centers, chips and power generation infrastructure. 
That phase is giving way to a new capital rewiring as the 
emphasis shifts from the creation of AI to its integration 
into broader corporate life. 
Since 2023, we have incorporated incremental upgrades 
to our total factor productivity (TFP)2 and capital growth 
assumptions because of AI’s potential impacts. This year, 
we have rebuilt our AI methodology to reflect growing 
evidence of the technology’s impact and faster-than-
expected adoption. Firms are investing in tools and 
reorganizing processes, workers are experimenting,

and the early evidence is consistent with an outlook for 
durable and diffuse productivity gains. 
In our framework, the uplift comes through two channels: 
capital deepening as the build-out adds to the capital 
stock and TFP as AI adoption raises output per unit 
of labor and capital. (See the chapter, “A brighter 
productivity outlook for the AI era” in this volume.) 
This potential uplift in productivity has been a long time 
coming. In many ways, productivity is an economy’s most 
reliable source of improved living standards. Productivity 
can raise incomes over time and, by strengthening the 
tax base, help governments support aging populations, 
potentially easing the political tensions that arise when 
average living standards stagnate.
Capital rewiring: The mechanism 
widening divergence 
Government spending on economic nationalism and 
corporate spending on technology adoption and 
adaptation will lead to a significant capital rewiring. 
Some of this will happen organically. As productivity gains 
are felt across the corporate sector broadly, investors will 
reallocate assets to take advantage of potential profits. 
This will shift the natural flow of capital by both sector 
and geography. 
Some of the rewiring will be forced or encouraged. 
Governments may want to play a role in redirecting 
capital more actively through a combination of carrots 
(tax incentives) and sticks (home-bias mandates). 
“Make at home” and “invest at home” government 
policies are proliferating. 
On the whole, we are optimistic about the potential for

Inflation: Stable, more volatility 
The forces of economic nationalism and technology 
adoption make the inflation outlook highly uncertain. 
Whether the long-term outcome ultimately ends up 
inflationary or deflationary will depend on the extent to 
which these spending initiatives increase supply vs. 
demand, and over what time horizon. 
In the near term, political disruption and trade frictions 
may push inflation higher. Governments’ desire to build, 
onshore and upgrade infrastructure may also add to 
inflation pressures – or at least trigger sharp bouts of 
inflation like those of recent years. But supply capacity 
could eventually catch up with demand and even 
overtake it, creating a disinflationary force. 
We caution against the idea that productivity gains are 
purely disinflationary. While they expand an economy’s 
capacity to produce, they also increase income. If those 
income gains are fully spent by firms and households 
through investment and consumption, the resulting 
demand impulse can offset the supply boost, leaving 
inflation little changed. That said, the net inflation 
outcome will depend on how the gains are distributed 
across income cohorts, the relative timing of output vs. 
income growth and whether the technology increases or 
diminishes competition across the economy.

Our inflation forecasts are thus stable: Global inflation 
remains at 2.6%, with developed markets at 2.3% and 
emerging markets at 3.0%. We modestly lower our U.S.

and for Japan, as domestic spending has lifted these 
regions permanently from the plague of too-low inflation. 
We revise China higher, as the disinflationary impulse 
we expected from weaker exports to the U.S. has been 
less pronounced and China has successfully diversified 
its export markets. Even so, our forecast remains below 
the central bank’s target, largely reflecting persistent 
demographic headwinds (Exhibit 4).

Overall, while we expect inflation over our forecast

more substantial bouts of volatility, which have important 
implications for interest rates, relative asset class returns

In brief 
•	 We believe artificial intelligence (AI) will drive a meaningful, durable uplift to 
productivity over our 10- to 15-year forecast horizon, adding to our growth 
and return expectations. 
•	 We have incrementally incorporated AI’s potential into our Long-Term 
Capital Market Assumptions since 2023, but the dramatic acceleration in 
capabilities and investment now requires more sophisticated analysis. 
This year, we have rebuilt our methodology to capture both the efficiency 
gains from scaling AI and the capital deepening required to deploy it.
•	 AI adoption remains early and uneven. Only 6% of U.S. work hours are AI-
assisted today, even as two-thirds of tasks may be theoretically exposed. 
We expect adoption to reach 40% of today’s tasks by 2040 as AI shifts to 
more agentic, and even robotic, workflows and spreads from knowledge-
intensive industries to routine work across sectors.
•	 Our base case estimates that AI will boost U.S. total factor productivity (TFP) 
growth by roughly 40 basis points (bps) above the pre-AI norm, to 1.2% 
annually, and lift U.S. capital stock growth by about 30bps, to 2.3% per year. 
Together, these gains raise our U.S. labor productivity forecast by 50bps 
over the same period, to 2.2%. In our forecast, improved productivity is the 
main force behind U.S. GDP growth returning to a 2%-plus pace over the 
coming decade.
•	 The pace of AI diffusion and the resulting productivity boost are likely to 
be gradual, with the most significant gains emerging in the 2030s and 
beyond. This is an important premise of our macroeconomic and asset 
class assumptions. Steady uptake should limit rapid, economy-wide job 
displacement while giving supply chains time to adapt. 
•	 The U.S. looks well positioned to lead AI adoption. It registers the largest 
AI-driven TFP increase in our forecast universe, reflecting the economy’s 
robust technology ecosystem and supportive institutions. Europe, Asia 
and emerging markets stand to see material benefits while facing varying 
constraints based on industry mix, regulation, infrastructure and cultural 
and workplace norms.
•	 We don’t think markets fully appreciate the economy-wide potential of AI, 
but selectivity and thoughtful diversification are key to investing in this 
space. We believe portfolios can best capture AI’s opportunity by balancing 
exposure across the AI value chain and funding cycle, spanning public and 
private markets.

AI’s promise is becoming real. As the technology races 
forward, trillions of dollars are flowing into semiconductors, 
data centers and power suppliers – the new AI ecosystem. 
But will AI make the economy more productive and, if so, 
by how much, how quickly and for whom?
No force has a greater impact on our Long-Term Capital 
Market Assumptions (LTCMAs) than productivity growth, 
the pace at which an economy turns its labor and capital 
into more output. Productivity underpins economic 
growth, living standards, corporate earnings, inflation 
and interest rates. As the economist Paul Krugman once 
said, “Productivity isn’t everything, but in the long run, it is 
almost everything.”­
We believe AI will raise productivity meaningfully and 
durably over our 10- to 15-year horizon, reshaping the 
nature of work and patterns of investment. While we have 
incrementally incorporated AI’s potential into our LTCMAs 
since 2023, the dramatic acceleration in capabilities and 
investment now requires more sophisticated analysis. 
To capture that shift, we have rebuilt our AI methodology 
and embedded it within our macroeconomic building 
blocks. Anchored around a Cobb-Douglas production 
function, it attributes an economy’s output to its labor 
and capital, and how efficiently those are used (see 
Methodology Handbook).
1	 TFP is economic growth not attributable to labor or capital growth in isolation. Capital deepening is the increase in capital available per worker.

3	 Alexander Bick and Adam Blandin, “GenAI Adoption Tracker,” Harvard University’s Project on Workforce, May 2026.
In that framework, AI lifts labor productivity, measured 
as output per hour worked, through two channels. 
First, it makes existing work more efficient, enabling the 
same people and machines to produce more (total factor 
productivity, or TFP). Second, it equips workers with 
improved tools, systems and infrastructure as investment 
scales (capital deepening).1 Here, we present our new 
methodology and the insight it delivers.
AI capability has outrun adoption

Today, frontier models can reason through complex 
multistep problems, write code and call upon external 
tools and information sources. The newest models can 
autonomously execute tasks that would take skilled

suggests only 6% of U.S. work hours are currently AI-

To date, we’ve seen only faint signals of AI’s impact on

Building our LTCMA base case
What might lie ahead? Forecasting productivity is difficult 
even in ordinary times, and profound technological 
change makes the challenge even greater. Our updated 
methodology should offer a solid framework from which 
to reason and refine as new evidence develops. 
For total factor productivity, we focus on the specific 
tasks AI can perform, and how efficiently. We then map 
those gains across the economy, an approach grounded 
in Hulten’s theorem as applied by Daron Acemoglu and 
others.6 We estimate capital deepening by projecting how 
AI-related investment affects the economy’s productive 
capital stock, net of depreciation and “crowding out” 
(displacing other productive investments).
By 2040, we believe, AI adoption will have shifted to 
more embedded agentic,7 and even robotic, workflows, 
spreading beyond knowledge-intensive early users to 
routine work across sectors. 
But diffusion will be gradual. We expect a slow start, 
with adoption accelerating in the early 2030s and then 
tapering as remaining use cases become harder or less 
economically attractive. Scaling AI will require integrating 
with legacy systems and redesigning operating models. 
Friction may be most acute where oversight and liability 
management require human involvement, particularly 
where AI missteps could prove costly.

be used and is ultimately used. We expect this will rise

Capital deepening: How much AI

Beyond TFP, our outlook hinges on how much the surge

Since 2023, AI demand and enthusiasm have driven

In brief 
•	 Climate degradation is accelerating, but the typical analysis – measuring 
costs and inferring a drag on GDP growth – misses the investible point: 
Although GDP tracks the flow of factors driving output, climate damage 
impairs the stock of wealth.
•	 Although storms, heat, floods, etc., degrade assets and threaten the 
reliability of production, asset loss doesn’t mechanically subtract from GDP. 
Yet rebuilding activity after losses adds to measured economic output.
•	 The core risk from climate events over our 10- to 15-year forecast horizon 
is not a steady, smooth drag on trend growth but an uneven dispersion 
of costs and risks across sectors. Climate events, like supply shocks, 
can increase inflation volatility and term risk premia. 
•	 Differences between GDP accounting and corporate cost accounting mean 
the cost of climate damage doesn’t show up cleanly at the national level, 
reducing states’ incentive to invest in mitigation.
•	 At the national level, climate investment can generate strategic advantages 
when framed as a matter of energy security, industrial strategy and 
resilience. At the firm level, focusing on the cost of inaction would be better 
suited to incentivizing investment.
•	 Technology may determine whether mitigation is a cost or a source of 
competitive advantage. As renewable and grid technologies become less 
expensive – even as the demand for power for artificial intelligence raises 
the stakes on grid investment – transition capex is shifting from a defensive 
expense toward more of a productivity-enhancing investment. The resulting 
gains, however, will likely accrue unevenly across regions and sectors.
•	 We expect national industrial strategies to increasingly and more explicitly 
seek to align climate investment with government and firm interests, 
consistent with our conviction that economic nationalism and capital 
rewiring are key long-term investment themes. Such an industrial strategy 
could give firms strategic advantages and lessen input cost volatility.
•	 Understanding which entities bear climate damage’s cost, which accounts 
book the damage remediation activity and which control the rebuilding and 
transition supply chains is a more meaningful analysis than reducing the 
impact to a single GDP haircut or a “climate risk premium.”

A growing problem, a shrinking response
The measurable effects of climate change are 
accelerating worldwide. The Intergovernmental Panel 
on Climate Change (IPCC) reported that global surface 
temperatures in 2011–20 were 1.1° C (around 2° F) higher 
than during 1850–1900, and found a 200 millimeter 
(about 8 inch) sea-level rise between 1900 and 2018.1 
Copernicus Programme data show that large parts of 
Europe now experience weeks of consecutive heat stress 
( 32° C to 38° C [around 90° F to 100° F]), which was rare 
50 years ago.2 And wildfires in many regions threaten 
lives and livelihoods with increasing regularity.
Yet despite mounting climate-related costs, the apparent 
impact on GDP remains limited. GDP is a flow measure 
that tracks current activity, whereas climate events 
erode the stock of wealth and threaten the reliability 
of production. Over a 10- to 15-year horizon, GDP may 
capture only a modest effect of climate-driven disruption, 
even as it disperses across the economy and its impact 
on prices grows. Inconsistent measurement can lead 
to misaligned incentives to tackle climate change and 
may partially explain the shortfall between promised and 
actual spending on climate remediation.
Here we explore how to better frame the economic impact 
of climate change, aligning the macroeconomic impact 
with the costs experienced at the industry and sector 
levels. We also consider how governments view their 
incentive to invest in climate remediation and sustainable 
technologies, and the implications for investors.
In our view, three factors are commonly underappreciated 
in estimating the economic effects of climate change:
•	 The accounting problem: The acute costs of climate 
damage are measured differently at the firm, 
household, sectoral and national levels. 
•	 	The impact problem: Acute damage can reverberate 
across the economy like a supply shock, with complex 
impacts. 
•	 	The policy problem: The incentive for governments to 
invest today for a diffuse future benefit is weaker than a 
policy aligned to national strategic advantage.
1	 “AR6 Synthesis Report: Climate Change 2023,” IPCC. A sea-level rise of 20 centimeters pushes shorelines back by meters and makes once-rare coastal 
floods recurrent, magnifying the economic exposure of coastal assets.
2	 Copernicus is the European Union’s Earth observation program, which provides open climate and environmental data through a network of satellites (the 
Sentinel missions) and in situ measurements.
3	 GDP accounts do not have a line item that subtracts the value of assets destroyed by disasters.
4	 Incidence, or burden, can be shifted and shared across sectors after an initial hit – for example, to utilities hit by storms or real estate by floods. Second-
order effects, as we discuss, include supply chains, prices, employment, insurance, credit markets, public finance and lost inputs in some geographies.
Unpacking the accounting problem
Debates about the economic impact of climate change 
often treat national economies like households: If your 
house is damaged, you are poorer. While this is undeniably 
true, GDP is not a wealth account but a measure of current 
production. An asset lost to climate damage isn’t booked 
as negative GDP,3 and the effects of output interruptions 
and damaged productivity accrue only slowly. Meanwhile, 
spending to repair or replace lost assets adds to output, 
even if it merely restores prior capacity.
This accounting inconsistency undermines political 
incentives (Exhibit 1). Policymakers anchor their efforts 
to headline GDP, often over a short horizon, and may 
systematically underestimate the erosion of capital stock 
and productive capacity. This in turn risks encouraging 
policymaker complacency if the measurable effect of 
climate degradation on GDP is small once an acute 
event passes.
Viewing climate economics as a subtraction issue counts 
only one side of the ledger. Damage that reduces output 
through asset destruction, supply chain disruption and 
lost working hours impairs the capital stock. While these 
are real and critical channels at the corporate level, 
they do not map cleanly onto GDP, as there is no clear 
depreciation term in many GDP accounting frameworks.
For a firm or sector, lost labor hours and damaged assets 
(or supply chains) are recognized straight away in cost 
accounting. This creates immediate pressure to replace 
lost assets, adapt processes and build resilience to 
maintain a competitive position. In many sectors, swift 
adaptation is companies’ dominant response – particularly 
under the invisible hand of shareholder pressure. Yet long-
horizon GDP damage estimates often assume that private 
sector adaptation is limited, slow or exogenous.
Climate-induced losses do not necessarily stay within the 
affected sectors, making second-order effects important. 
This “incidence” or burden problem – who ultimately 
bears the economic cost of a climate shock4 – means that 
climate shocks can manifest as supply shocks. Where 
demand is inelastic (e.g., food and energy), prices adjust 
and higher costs pass through to consumers as inflation. 
Elasticity, in effect, decides whether a shock is measured 
as inflation or as forgone output.

The aggregate effect of remedial and adaptation 
investment at the firm level, in addition to the risk of higher 
inflation, can leave nominal GDP appearing resilient even 
as real incomes and welfare suffer. This tilts the balance 
of risk toward higher term risk premia, steeper curves and 
higher yields – the opposite conclusion of studies that 
translate incurred climate costs directly into a drag on GDP.
Competitive and shareholder pressure incentivizes firms to 
remediate damage quickly after a disaster. Policymakers 
could wield a powerful tool by reconfiguring climate policy 
to encourage investment in prevention and resilience 
instead – for example, with nudges such as accelerated 
depreciation and subsidies. Such investment under an 
industrial strategy would, in aggregate, advance climate 
goals, protect welfare and support GDP.
Unpacking the impact problem
Over the next decade, climate events will likely appear as 
volatility and localized disruption, not clean drags on trend 
growth. Investors should focus not only on the assumed 
aggregate costs of climate change but also on where 
frictions are emerging and which firms can pass through 
costs or recover capital.
Climate impact varies by sector but follows a common 
pattern. Agriculture experiences climate variability 
as yield volatility and elevated crop failure risk. 
Transport faces disruptions (e.g., low river flow limiting 
shipping, heat buckling rail tracks). Construction and 
manufacturing confront heat-driven productivity and 
safety constraints, forcing a reallocation of labor that may 
preserve output but raise unit labor costs. This matters 
beyond those sectors’ own margins, as labor-intensive 
industries are central to the adaptation and transition 
build-out. Climate stress raises the cost of the supply-side 
response at the same time that it raises the demand for 
it, making the delivery of physical capacity more inelastic 
than capex plans may assume.
Incidence – the rate, frequency or probability of an event 
that can multiply its impacts – complicates the picture. For 
example, in Europe, Rhine river shipping disruptions don’t 
hit the transport sector in isolation. Goods can be rerouted 
to road or rail. Rising freight prices support sector income, 
while losses are incurred downstream, in industrial supply 
chains, higher shipping rates and lost output.

climate stress. Despite evidence that climate stress 
meaningfully reduces agricultural productivity (Exhibit 2A), 
the small size of the sector in many developed nations 
mutes the overall impact on GDP (Exhibit 2B). The supply 
response is slow: Adjusting cropping patterns, irrigation 
and land use takes seasons or years, so yield shocks pass 
into prices before capacity adjusts.

Unpacking the policy problem
Climate investment has lagged commitments: The USD 
100 billion annual pledge for developing economies, 
made at the UN’s 15th Conference of the Parties (COP15, 
2009) was met in 2022, two years late,5 and public 
adaptation finance commitments cover less than a tenth 
of estimated needs.6 
Investors may view global climate policy as purely 
an international relations subject when it is in fact a 
key transmission mechanism directing capital flows. 
While climate investment may be underwhelming today, 
we believe leaders are beginning to consider it a matter 
of strategic advantage with profound economic and 
financial implications.
Two armed conflicts in four years, in Ukraine and the 
Gulf, have roiled energy markets and underscored the 
strategic advantage of energy independence. Europe 
has felt this acutely: With almost half of the Continent’s 
energy grid due for replacement in the next 15 years7 and 
the cost of renewable technology falling rapidly, building a 
sustainable grid has become a clear strategic issue.
Outside the European Union (EU), leaders are reframing 
climate investment similarly – not only for energy 
security but also from the perspective of technology 
leadership (decarbonization and renewable energy 
rely on technical innovation and critical minerals) and 
industrial competitiveness. 
The dominant levers differ by jurisdiction: regulation 
in Europe,8 tax incentives in the U.S.,9 revenue floors in 
Australia10 and state-directed planning in China.11 
This policy variation in climate economics and investment 
is linked inexorably with geopolitics and national 
resilience considerations. Such pressures may in turn 
create subsidy races, protectionism and bottlenecks

The investing implications of climate 
economics
Climate investment requires understanding the 
incentives and costs felt by all actors. Misalignment 
of incentives or mismeasurement of costs can lead to 
complacency and can delay investment. We believe 
climate should be seen less as a global coordination 
problem with an international policy solution and more 
as a matter of strategic advantage with an industrial 
policy solution. 
At the state level, policymakers, who tend to optimize for 
growth, are drawn to investments that secure a strategic 
advantage. Meanwhile, firms, which are feeling the effects 
of climate damage acutely through asset losses and 
production downtime, are motivated to invest to mitigate 
those costs. We expect national industrial strategies will 
increasingly and more explicitly seek to align climate 
investments across these interests.

Distinguishing sectoral winners from losers rests on 
three questions: 
First, who can pass through costs? Firms serving 
inelastic demand can recover much of the climate hit 
through price; those facing elastic demand absorb it in 
their margins.
Second, who captures remediation spending? Suppliers 
of adaptation goods and services, such as engineering, 
grid technology and resilient infrastructure, tend to gain 
as remediation and prevention budgets expand. 
Third, who bears the residual? Those left carrying the 
loss are typically downstream processors and end 
consumers. Exhibit 3 maps sectors against these 
three tests.
There are three key implications for long-term investors:
•	 Don’t assume climate damage subtracts from trend 
growth over a 10- to 15-year horizon. Expect it to 
manifest as periodic supply shocks leading to elevated 
inflation volatility and term risk premia.
•	 As renewable technology cheapens, energy-intensive 
industries will benefit in those regions able to reduce 
their dependence on fossil fuels. Europe leads in 
this regard.
•	 Regulated utilities, some consumer staples and 
reinsurers with pricing power, as well as companies that 
process key minerals to a usable purity (often domiciled 
in China), stand to be relative winners.
Climate costs are booked in different places by different 
actors – as impairments and capex for firms, as 
revaluation on national balance sheets, as the price of 
goods and services for households – and barely at all in 
the GDP flow that policymakers watch. That dispersion, 
not a smooth drag on trend growth, is what investors 
should underwrite. The question is not how large the 
climate discount should be but who bears the cost, who 
captures the remediation and who controls its delivery.

Government bonds: Repricing a more 
capital-hungry world
For government bonds, we build our return assumptions 
from the real cash rate and inflation through the cash-to-
10-year curve to the 10- to 30-year segment. Returns can 
be broken down into cash, carry, roll-down and a 
normalization component. In general, we see our key 
themes pushing up both yields and returns (Exhibit 3).
Economic nationalism is a rates-up theme working 
through two channels. First, investment in national 
imperatives – such as energy security, defense and grid 
and infrastructure upgrades – increases debt loads and 
tends to push real rates higher. Second, populist policies, 
from higher minimum wages to restricted immigration, 
lift the inflation risk premium by constraining labor 
force growth and raising costs, prices and uncertainty. 
The result is a steeper curve, with more issuance and a 
higher inflation risk premium embedded at the long end. 
Capital rewiring lifts yields through competition for 
capital. The normalization of yields outside the U.S., 
driven higher by domestic spending, also erodes the real 
yield advantage U.S. Treasuries offer vs. other developed 
market sovereigns, prompting in turn the potential 
reallocation of global bond holdings. 
Separately, defined benefit pension funds and insurers, 
the traditional price-insensitive buyers of duration, 
are shrinking as a share of the market, and the public and 
private sectors now compete for the same savings at the 
same time, offering higher yields to attract those savings. 
We raise our forecast for long-end U.S. yields relative to 
other developed markets, partly because U.S. investment 
is being repatriated into domestic markets and partly 
because of higher inflation risk premia. Still, starting 
yields and inflation forecasts – as well as forecasts for USD 
depreciation, explored next – mean a narrow spread of 
real returns across DM bond markets and the potential for 
capital to diversify further across sovereign markets.
Technology adoption raises potential growth and the 
neutral real rate, lifting cash rates as a result. Over our full 
forecast horizon, the effects of technology adoption on 
inflation likely net off. Still, there are distinct inflationary 
risks early on as capex and power demand squeeze 
supply chains. Later in our horizon, the productivity 
boost we expect presents a disinflationary risk. Even if 
the effects net off on the level of inflation, technology 
adoption contributes to the volatility of inflation over our 
forecast horizon. 
Inflation-linked bonds should carry a lower inflation risk 
premium over the long run as productivity improves and 
topside inflation risks diminish, partially offsetting the 
nationalism effect. Inflation-linked bonds are a natural 
winner from the broader inflation backdrop, although 
we believe our prior forecasts captured much of the 
higher inflation environment; as a result, we do not make 
further changes at this time but note the attractiveness of 
inflation-linked bonds in a balanced portfolio.

returns through persistent share growth, but data show 
a newfound buyback impulse in the region. Japanese 
authorities continue to pressure companies trading 
below book value to unlock balance-sheet value and 
shrink their low return businesses. And we have long 
emphasized the importance of Korea’s Value-Up 
program4 in our modeling.
4	 A government-led initiative to encourage listed companies to adopt shareholder-friendly policies and improve shareholder returns and corporate value, 
typically through better governance, capital efficiency and transparency measures.
There are risks to our optimistic global equity 
assumptions. Stock-based compensation in the AI talent 
race is increasing share supply, and the pipeline of 
technology listings adds incremental float to the market. 
We also expect free-float percentages to rise as lockups 
expire, eventually increasing the share count. The rising 
index weight we anticipate of low dividend technology 
firms may also reduce the U.S. market’s overall dividend 
yield over time. 
Hyperscalers’ upfront capex has boosted margins for 
their suppliers. The hyperscalers, however, capitalize that 
spending and expense it as depreciation over several 
years, which will weigh on their earnings. The drag will 
be larger if AI revenues take longer to arrive, or if the 
equipment becomes obsolete faster than is assumed. 
We expect the benefits of this investment to outweigh 
the depreciation cost, but depreciation remains a risk 
to earnings.
Given the risks, as well as pockets of elevated multiples 
across equity markets, we balance out our upgrades 
to equity return forecasts with a higher assumption of 
volatility. In sum, this maintains Sharpe ratios close to 
last year’s levels and reflects a more optimistic but more 
uncertain path ahead for global equities.
Risks to our outlook
Aside from the specific risks to equity returns we’ve 
discussed, the risks to the outlook cluster at the overlap 
of our core themes.
At the interface of capital rewiring and technology 
adoption, the risk of capital misallocation is central. If the 
AI capex wave produces depreciation without productivity, 
our return estimates will likely be too high. We do not 
think capital is being significantly misallocated today. 
Also, higher rates impose greater capital discipline, 
raising the hurdle for new spending and sharpening the 
focus on returns. However, as the capex cycle extends, 
this is a key downside risk to watch. 
Fiscal overspending is at the intersection of economic 
nationalism and capital rewiring. Should government 
debt markets become unruly, the rebuilding of term 
premia might continue past the point that equity 
valuations can absorb. Still, we don’t believe our 
current equity valuation forecasts are overly optimistic, 
and recent history suggests equity valuations can be 
resilient to higher interest rates.

At the moment, we do not make a case for bitcoin (BTC) as a diversifier in portfolios due to its unstable market 
correlations. We see bitcoin as a speculative asset and a hedge against monetary easing. 
Investors are nevertheless increasingly exploring crypto assets, supported by more defined regulation and growing 
infrastructure (e.g. exchange-traded funds, futures, publicly traded crypto companies, options, bank-grade custody 
and financing markets). With over 60 million coins and tokens, bitcoin is the anchor of the crypto ecosystem, 
offering transactional security, longevity, improving regulatory clarity and liquidity. 
Today, crypto is an asset class worth over USD 2.5 trillion, roughly the size of the Swiss stock market or the entire private 
credit market. Tokenized assets, such as money market funds, are valued around USD 40 billion, with scope for rapid 
growth. J.P. Morgan Private Bank’s 2026 Global Family Office Report found that around a tenth of clients surveyed said 
their portfolios held crypto assets, and 17% of respondents planned to prioritize crypto and other digital assets.
Originally designed to facilitate pseudonymous peer-to-peer payments, BTC is now seen as a scarce store of wealth 
independent of central banks. As yet, it lacks a strategic role in portfolios other than as a bet on fiat currency expansion 
or future asset trading on blockchains. 
BTC mostly trades like a high beta risk asset that tracks global money supply growth, but it is sometimes treated as 
“digital gold.” Unstable correlations with other assets present a modeling hurdle. A further challenge is forecasting an

Over our 10- to 15-year investment horizon, three powerful 
forces – economic nationalism, technology adoption 
and capital rewiring – drive both risk and return in public 
and private markets. Collectively, these forces lead 
our G71 nominal growth projections to the highest level 
since 2012, but their impact varies widely across private 
markets. Investors need to understand how this new 
era – marked by higher rates, greater inflation volatility, 
faster productivity-driven growth and wider return 
dispersion – will impact return drivers for real assets and 
financial alternatives, as well as their respective roles 
in portfolios. 
Here we address the implications of each theme in turn. 
As globalization gives way to economic nationalism, 
governments and companies trade efficiency for 
resilience, rerouting supply chains and prioritizing 
energy security and defense. Technology – notably the 
diffusion of artificial intelligence (AI) – is already an 
essential means of addressing those strategic needs, 
offering productivity-enhanced growth at scale. But the 
scarcity of real economy assets (power availability and 
grid interconnection, among others) both enables 
and constrains AI’s build-out. The resulting tension 
drives capital rewiring as resources flow toward asset 
owners and platform providers that can secure access 
and deliver results despite shifting regulatory and 
stakeholder requirements.
As portfolio construction evolves, once-clear boundaries 
between asset class and exposure become less distinct. 
This means that outcomes – whether focused on 
return enhancement or diversification – become more 
important. We simultaneously expect performance 
dispersion to widen as these shifts place greater value on 
cash flows, pricing power and execution. To build resilient 
private market portfolios, investors will need to prioritize: 
•	 Exposures that offer inflation-resilient and less 
correlated return streams 
•	 Scarcity – as both a growth driver and a portfolio 
diversifier 
•	 Sources of excess return beyond concentrated 
public markets

Part one: Economic nationalism – trade

This year, we revisit our analysis of economic nationalism, 
which continues to rise as concerns grow over security 
and resilience, particularly in the U.S. In this new era, 
industrial strategy, national security priorities and 
election-driven policy swings reroute both capital and 
supply chains.
Economic nationalism poses a clear risk to inflation, 
which is likely to remain volatile over the forecast horizon.
We expect rates will be higher for longer, return drivers will 
shift, and income will increasingly accrue to capital and 
businesses that operate within – and profit from – supply-

The impact of this dynamic will be felt across all private 
markets, with real assets receiving a boost from more

potential for returns, however, lies with those operators 
that can effectively grow the cash flows to generate higher

will likely enhance returns for venture capital, private

benefit from the same tailwinds. 
In both core real estate and infrastructure, AI is boosting 
demand for data centers and for logistics/industrial sites 
that offer ample and reliable electric power. Over the 
near term, constraints on the supply of properly entitled 
land, available power and fiber capacity will enable the 
owners of scarce, well-located assets to raise rents faster. 
This dynamic will be amplified by constraints related to 
capital spending, reinforcing the importance of manager 
operational efficiency and income as drivers of returns.

Conclusion: Fair winds, with volatility in the forecast
Our three themes – economic nationalism, technology 
adoption and capital rewiring – are reshaping the 
investment landscape and our long-term outlook for 
returns across private markets (Exhibit 10). Managers 
that can navigate this environment, especially those that 
can steer capably through shifts in policy, will be able to 
deliver compelling alpha and contribute positively to a 
range of multi-asset portfolio goals. 
But where should one allocate the incremental 
investment dollar today? Leveraging an outcome-
oriented approach focused on either return 
enhancement or diversification will be key, with income 
growth and operational efficiency key attributes to 
consider as manager selection gains importance. 
The risks, however, are clear: Normalized interest rates 
will limit upward pressure on multiples for most assets. 
That means operational expertise – the ability to deliver 
earnings growth and enhance the quality of revenues 
– will become a critical skill, particularly as AI adoption 
drives transformation. Legacy real estate, for example, 
often has hidden and complex capex requirements. 
As a result, liabilities, especially those tied to power 
quality obsolescence, may be understated. We would 
also encourage investors to avoid weaker private credit 
vintages in which more permissive documentation and 
crowded yield-chasing look set to collide with higher-for-
longer financing costs. 
Over the coming decade, asset class and strategy 
choices still matter, but investors should view them 
through the lenses of diversification and return 
enhancement. Further, manager selection will be equally, 
if not more, important amid rising return dispersion 
as new forces, such as a manager’s ability to improve 
underlying assets’ earnings power, gain significance. 
Investment decisions will be increasingly tied to 
outcomes, and while plenty of alpha remains to be had, 
the key will be recognizing that the drivers have changed: 
Income growth and operational efficiency now top the list.

How intentional thematic exposure can build portfolio resilience
Technology adoption
Types of shocks: We believe the adoption of artificial 
intelligence (AI) will ultimately boost productivity, 
economic growth and corporate profit margins. But we 
also believe the AI build-out and adoption across the 
economy will come with its fair share of market volatility. 
Investors should prepare for market shocks related to 
turbulence around the AI theme, as seen in January 2025 
and July 2026. As past large innovation/capex booms 
have shown, they bring with them an elevated risk of 
eventual busts and capital misallocation, even if the 
long-run impact of the investment on the economy is 
net positive. 
Volatility: Optimism about the impact of AI on revenues 
and margins has pushed up our global equity forecasts. 
However, uncertainty over the timing and impact of these 
gains leads us to also raise our global equity volatility 
assumptions. This results in largely unchanged Sharpe 
ratios. U.S. equity volatility in particular increases, 
given higher valuations and increasing risks of capital 
misallocation. History shows that in any large and 
rapid capex cycle (and the AI capex boom may well 
be unrivaled), episodes of market volatility are to 
be expected.
Correlation: Exposure of various asset classes to the AI 
theme is rising as the AI build-out encompasses more 
industries and regions – and draws on various funding 
sources across public and private markets (Exhibit 5). 
Currently, the highest concentration to the theme is 
found in equities, especially the U.S. and Asian equities. 
Going forward, the representation within investment 
grade credit is also set to rise meaningfully. Private equity 
and private credit show noticeable AI concentration as 
well; as a result, their correlations to public equity and 
credit are on the rise.
Portfolio construction implications: Over the coming 
decade, the AI ecosystem will evolve as investors 
reassess the scale of capex, the pace of AI adoption and 
what markets have and haven’t priced in. Understanding 
exposure to the AI theme holistically across a portfolio will 
be essential. As we assess the current landscape, the U.S. 
equity market is the global market most directly exposed 
to the tech adoption theme. But even more concentrated 
exposure exists elsewhere – notably in Asia, where 
exposure to the most cyclical elements of the AI value 
chain is especially acute.

In credit markets, the transmission channel to the

Tech exposure is broadening as issuance accelerates,

This suggests these assets can serve as useful portfolio

AI exposure varies across alternative assets.9 Venture

streams and strengthen portfolio diversification.
Capital rewiring
Types of shocks: The capital rewiring theme refers to 
shifts in public and private capital flows arising from an 
increasing focus on domestic investment. It is also driven 
in part by policymakers’ efforts to unlock dormant pools 
of savings. While it is a slow-moving force, it can abruptly

reshape markets. For example, fiscal activism can cause 
rapid, policy-induced unwinding of foreign investments 
and a reshoring of capital flows. 
Volatility: Capital rewiring shocks generally lead to 
greater turbulence across markets. However, standard 
asset volatility metrics may not fully capture the impact 
of these shocks. Since currencies are a crucial offset to 
sharp changes in capital flows, the impact on portfolio FX 
exposure may be more important than the direct effect on 
asset volatility.
Correlation: After 15 years of negative correlation, 
the correlation between the U.S. dollar and U.S. stocks 
may be changing (or at least proving to be more unstable, 
as it was in 2025). In addition, the potential for disruptive 
position unwinding can cause assets to become 
temporarily correlated to one another.
Portfolio construction implications: Global investors may 
especially benefit from having well-diversified portfolios, 
given the potential for sudden unwinds of crowded 
or momentum positions. Additionally, investors may 
need to rethink their currency exposure. For U.S.-based 
investors, unhedged international equity exposure can 
offer diversification to U.S.-driven moments of volatility via 
foreign currencies. 
For non-U.S. dollar-based investors choosing to invest in 
U.S. stocks, the optimal currency hedging decision may 
be changing from fully unhedged exposure to partially 
hedged exposure.10 In short, capital rewiring has the 
potential to create advantages for non-U.S. markets, 
but preparing for this move can be costly. Adding 
exposure to hedge funds and real assets can 
be beneficial, given they benefit from the capital 
in motion.

What the numbers don’t tell us …
The theme-aware portfolio construction framework relies 
on our LTCMA forecasts for risk and return. These are 
forward-looking projections of asset class behavior over 
the next 10 to 15 years. The near-term performances of 
portfolio assets could deviate from the long-term forecast 
meaningfully, however. There could be pockets of short-
term investment opportunities in asset classes that are 
less favored by our LTCMAs. 
For example, EM Asia equities have surged as chip 
demand has increased. In our long-term framework, such 
cyclical booms tend to normalize eventually, but in the near 
term, chip demand remains elevated. That makes further 
near-term gains possible even in extended markets. 
Investors should evaluate their asset allocation decisions 
periodically with a clear time horizon in mind.
In building portfolios resilient to the themes of economic 
nationalism, tech adoption and capital rewiring, 
asset allocation is critical, as we have discussed. But the 
asset allocation playbook of the past won’t work as well 
going forward. Given the speed and magnitude of the 
disruption we expect, markets will likely deliver bigger 
winners and bigger losers, with greater dispersion among 
them. (The 2026 sell-off in software and semiconductor 
stocks may be a harbinger of volatility to come.) Active 
managers will have a lot more to play for. Some will 
succeed while others will stumble.

Already, manager dispersion is on the rise as stock-
level outcomes become more differentiated (Exhibit 7). 
Unlike in prior cycles, dispersion is now occurring outside 
a typical crisis backdrop. In the past, elevated dispersion 
was usually tied to recessions, liquidity strains or abrupt 
policy shocks. In 2026, dispersion remains high despite 
an economic expansion and a period of strong earnings 
growth. This suggests to us that we are already starting 
to see the effects of the crosscurrents of economic 
nationalism, tech adoption and capital rewiring.
We see two potential implications. First, manager 
selection becomes more consequential as the 
opportunity set broadens and idiosyncratic winners 
and losers separate. Second, the case for breadth 
in implementation strengthens, as conviction in 
concentrated exposures may be less reliable when 
market leadership is fragmenting across sectors, styles 
and business models. 
We believe a resilient portfolio should therefore 
emphasize process diversification by combining 
genuinely differentiated managers and multiple return 
drivers rather than concentrating exposure in a narrow 
set of themes or the last cycle’s leaders.

Conclusion
Portfolio resilience is multifaceted. Thematic 
diversification is one way to strengthen that resilience 
and provide a useful complement to asset class 
diversification and manager selection. 
We believe the tailwinds of our three themes will 
ultimately prove more powerful than the headwinds for 
a variety of asset classes. However, as volatility rises 
and correlations become less stable, investors should 
continually evaluate how economic nationalism, tech 
adoption and capital rewiring might interact to shape 
portfolio outcomes. Of course, no forecast can be 
perfectly certain. But portfolio construction should be 
intentional, based on a deliberate decision to lean into, 
or away from, thematic exposures.
