Dear readers,
As the year’s final quarter got underway, markets were on 
edge again. October brought another bout of artificial 
intelligence (AI)-related volatility and a reminder that the 
air has become a bit thinner at these heights.
Reports that a Chinese company had developed a more 
efficient memory cell1 put pressure on margin expecta-
tions. Investors are also scrutinizing the cost of building 
data centers, delays to construction, and how these  
projects will be financed. The enthusiasm for all things AI 
is meeting a more skeptical market, especially with more 
than USD 3.5 trillion invested in an industry where many 
companies are still counting on future revenues. Hotter- 
than-expected inflation and oil prices above USD 100 per 
barrel amid attacks on tankers in the Middle East also 
weighed on markets. For the first time in a while, bonds 
outperformed equities. 
We had previously noted that the path ahead would likely 
become choppier. The divide between companies with 
strong profits and cash to spare and those reliant on out-
side financing is becoming more apparent. Valuations 
have come down, with the S&P 500 Index’s forward earn-
ings multiple2 falling from around 25 × to below 20 ×, but 
we see limited room for further multiple compression, which 
puts the onus on earnings to drive returns from here.

In Europe, French government bond spreads over German 
Bunds have neared euro-crisis highs. In our view, France’s 
large budget deficit and heavy debt burden are becom-
ing harder to address as public frustration over the cost 
of living adds to political pressure, especially ahead of 
elections. Even so, we don’t expect the problems in France 
to trigger a wider financial crisis. But the debate over 
spending and fiscal discipline is likely to continue, leaving 
French bonds exposed to further volatility in the months 
ahead.
Across the Atlantic, Democrats are expected to take con-
trol of Congress in the US midterms, setting the stage for 
a changing policy debate. Our October edition explored 
what the election results could mean for markets. The risk 
of re-escalation in the Middle East is still a possibility 
regardless of the outcome.
Casting our gaze forward, we expect economic growth, 
inflation, and 10-year US Treasury yields to have peaked 
and anticipate 2027 to offer flatter terrain: slower growth 
and possibly lower inflation. As fiscal support around the 
world is poised to fade, especially in the US, we believe 
demand could lose some steam. You can find the full de- 
tails from page 7, where we map out the potential routes 
for markets and the economy over the next year, the junc-
tions that deserve close attention, and the developments 
that could make us change our view. 
We’re already gearing up for the year ahead, keeping a 
close eye on the opportunities and obstacles along the 
way. Our aim is to support our clients at every step, even 
when the weather turns.

Investment strategy
Having held up well for most of the year, financial mar-
kets hit a rough patch in October. A sell-off in government 
bonds pushed yields higher, and the turbulence spilled 
over into equities. Later in the month, a tech-driven stock 
correction prompted a flight to safety that lifted govern-
ment bond prices.
Energy markets were thrown off balance too, as Iran-
aligned Houthi forces claimed responsibility for attacks 
on Saudi Arabian energy and civilian infrastructure, re­- 
viving concerns that the world’s oil supply could be at risk. 
With the situation in the Middle East already fragile, the 
new disruption sent Brent crude well above USD 100 per 
barrel, and the jump was soon felt in refined products 
such as diesel and gasoline. Keen to bring prices down 
before the midterm elections on November 3,3 US Presi-
dent Donald Trump threatened to curb diesel exports, then 
secured Europe’s agreement to coordinate the release  
of part of its diesel reserves. The push was likely a sign of 
how politically sensitive high fuel costs have become.  
Growing strains
Citing US national interests, Trump also suspended parts 
of a Russia sanctions package days before it was due to 
take effect. With the US midterm elections over soon, we 
believe he may turn more of his attention abroad, which 
carries the risk of headlines on tariff threats or escalation 
in the Middle East. 
The Multi Asset Investment Committee tilted its allocation 
slightly more defensively. We closed our overweight in 
high-yield (HY)4 bonds and moved to neutral, as we believe 
growth is poised to slow and we see less compelling 
risk-reward in lower-quality credit. We upgraded govern-
ment bonds to neutral from underweight. At what we 
consider attractive yields, we believe investors are well 
compensated for adding duration, and we count gov­
ernment bonds among the most effective hedges against 
recession fears. Our overweight positions in emerging- 
market stocks and gold are unchanged. Find the details 
on page 5.

Bonds 
Our overall underweight stance on bonds remains 
unchanged, though we reduced our overweight posi-
tion in HY bonds to neutral, as we expect economic 
growth to slow and believe lower-quality credit offers 
a less appealing risk-reward profile. We used the  
proceeds to close our underweight in government 
bonds, bringing that allocation back to neutral. The 
global bond sell-off has pushed risk-free yields8 to 
multi-decade highs, so investors may now be able  
to earn attractive returns without taking on corporate 
credit risk. The move also lets us add duration9 
ahead of what we expect to be a softer growth and 
inflation backdrop in 2027. HY bonds tend to strug-
gle when growth slows. If economic activity weakens 
or high borrowing costs push defaults higher, credit 
spreads could widen and erode the income advantage 
of high coupons. In our view, government bonds are 
better positioned in that environment and have histor-
ically been among the most effective hedges against 
recession fears.

Equities
We reiterate our overweight stance on equities. Mar-
kets kept reaching new all-time highs, but investors 
faced several headwinds: elevated oil prices amid ten- 
sions with Iran, a sharp technology sector sell-off  
on concerns over AI-related spending and rising Chi-
nese competition in semiconductors, and stron-
ger-than-expected inflation data that led the US Fed-
eral Reserve (Fed) to raise rates. The fundamental 
backdrop remains supportive as labor markets appear 
resilient, corporate earnings are holding up, and 
AI-related investment is still going strong. We also 
maintain our overweight in emerging-market stocks. 
4 
Gold
Our overweight allocation to gold is unchanged. Gold 
prices remained volatile in October, hovering around 
USD 4,100 – 4,200 per ounce. The yellow metal faced 
pressure from higher bond yields and real interest 
rates, which increase the opportunity cost of holding 
non-yielding assets. We view this pressure as tem- 
porary and believe the current tightening cycle is likely 
to be limited in scope and duration. Structural 
demand also appears supportive amid purchases by 
official institutions and investors, which provide  
an important source of support for gold prices. Com-
bined with our more muted growth outlook and ele-
vated geopolitical uncertainty, we continue to view a 
modest allocation to gold as a potential portfolio 
diversifier.

Commodities
Commodities have once again played their part as an 
inflation hedge this year, particularly when supply 
shocks have driven prices higher. They can also act 
as a cushion against uncertainty from geopolitical 
tensions, which adds to their diversification value. Still, 
given our expectation of moderating economic 
growth heading into 2027, a neutral stance on this 
cyclical asset class appears justified to us.

Bending, not breaking 
2026 was the year of resilience. What do we expect for 2027? 
Investors spent much of 2026 worrying about a series of 
shocks that, on paper, appeared capable of derailing the 
global expansion. Geopolitical tensions in the Middle East 
escalated, the temporary closure of the Strait of Hormuz 
pushed Brent crude prices above USD 100 per barrel, and 
renewed trade disputes fueled fears of a return to the  
tariff battles of 2025. Yet the global economy absorbed 
these blows far better than many had expected. Rather 
than faltering, growth remained strong. Manufacturing 
activity strengthened across much of the world, with the 
global manufacturing cycle regaining momentum. 
The US stood out, as the ongoing AI investment boom un- 
leashed a wave of spending on data centers, semicon-
ductors, and digital infrastructure. What initially appeared 
to be a narrow technology story increasingly spilled over 
into broader business investment and industrial activity. 
Nor was this resilience limited to the US. Across Asia, 
export-oriented economies benefited from a recovery in 
global demand and the continued strength in the tech­
nology cycle. South Korea, often regarded as a bellwether 
for global growth because of its exposure to trade and 
electronics, reported surging exports throughout much of 
the year, sending a reassuring signal about underlying 
demand in the world economy (see chart 1). In Europe, 
Germany’s export-sen­sitive economy enjoyed a manu- 
facturing recovery as well.

Market highlights
Even amid dramatic headlines, the economic backdrop 
became more stable. Measures of economic policy 
uncertainty generally trended lower over the course of  
the year (see chart 2). While tariff threats featured promi-
nently in the headlines, many proposed measures were 
ultimately softened, delayed, or never implemented, 
reducing the economic damage feared by financial mar-
kets.
Our outlook for growth, inflation, and monetary policy  
in 2027
The global economy may have weathered 2026, but the 
outlook for 2027 is likely to be more challenging. Higher 
oil prices have rekindled inflationary pressures, prompting 
major developed-market central banks to tighten mone-
tary policy. The European Central Bank (ECB), for exam-
ple, has raised rates by a cumulative 50 basis points 
(bps), bringing the deposit facility rate to 2.50 percent, 
and the Fed delivered a 25-bps hike in September, lift­- 
ing the federal funds rate to a range of 3.75 – 4.00 percent. 
In Asia, the Bank of Japan has pushed its policy rate to  
a 31-year high of 1.25 percent. Monetary conditions have 
tightened as policy rates have increased and bond  
yields have risen. In the US, the 10-year Treasury yield has 
climbed by more than 100 bps in 2026, breaching the  
5 percent threshold. The move has reverberated across 
global fixed-income markets, pushing up European  
sovereign yields and driving French borrowing costs higher 
amid mounting fiscal concerns. This repricing has not 
been confined to highly indebted countries. Yields have 
also risen markedly in lower-debt markets such as Ger-
many and Switzerland, which showed the global reach of 
the sell-off. We believe higher policy rates and bond  
yields are likely to act as an increasing headwind to growth 
in 2027.
The impact is already becoming visible in interest-rate- 
sensitive areas of the economy, with housing typically 
serving as the earliest indicator of a turning business cycle. 
In the US, the 30-year fixed mortgage rate, which is 
closely tied to bond yields, pushed past 7.6 percent in late 
October. Mortgage applications fell in its wake (see  
chart 3), highlighting the impact of higher borrowing costs 
on housing demand and residential investment. Costlier 
consumer credit is likely to weigh on household spending, 
while steeper funding costs encourage businesses to  
rein in capital expenditure. Rising interest rates can also 
put pressure on equity valuations, reducing wealth  
effects and thereby tempering consumption. Finally, higher 
debt-servicing costs leave governments with less fiscal 
room to prop up economic activity.

Although we expect economic growth to moderate, a 
recession is not our base case. In our view, the economy 
looks sturdy enough to withstand somewhat tighter 
financial conditions. Households are in relatively healthy 
shape, having pared back their leverage since the Global 
Financial Crisis. As a result, higher interest rates are more 
likely to slow economic activity at the margin than to trig-
ger the balance-sheet stress seen in previous downturns. 
We also believe the economy is robust enough to digest 
current oil price levels. Nominal oil prices may appear high, 
but real oil prices, once adjusted for inflation and income 
growth, sit well below the levels seen during past energy 
crises (see chart 4). This distinction is relevant because 
oil prices of USD 100 per barrel sting far less when house-
hold incomes, corporate revenues, and the overall price 
level stand much higher than they did a few decades ago. 
Lastly, and perhaps most importantly, the global economy 
is still buoyed by a powerful capital-expenditure cycle. We 
expect investment spending to remain supported by the 
continued AI boom, which is driving large-scale spending 
on data centers, power generation, and digital infrastruc-
ture. At the same time, many countries are accelerating in- 
vestments aimed at reducing strategic dependencies  
on the US and other foreign suppliers, while rising defense 
spending commitments are providing an additional tailwind. 
Together, these forces should help keep economic activ-
ity afloat even as tighter financial conditions weigh on 
more rate-sensitive sectors of the economy.
What about the inflation outlook? To answer that, it helps 
to step back and consider what drove prices in 2026. 
Much of this year’s upward pressure traced back to three 
sources: the lingering impact of higher tariffs, the Middle 
East energy shock, and the surge in AI-related investment. 
Inflation remains uncomfortably high, but we expect some 
of these forces to lose their intensity in 2027. The energy 
shock, for one, may gradually unwind as crude flows 
through the Strait of Hormuz normalize, additional capac-
ity comes online in Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emir-
ates, and producers outside the Gulf maintain solid output 
growth. As a result, energy prices are unlikely to exert the 
same inflationary jolt next year as they did in 2026. 
Not every driver is fading, though. The buildout of AI infra-
structure continues to feed demand for labor, electricity, 
data centers, semiconductors, and a range of industrial 
inputs, creating pockets of upward price pressure across 
the global economy. Even so, we believe the disinflationary 
effects from easing energy pressures and the fading tariff 
shock should outweigh these sector-specific inflationary 
forces, allowing overall inflation to trend lower in 2027.

We also expect tighter financial conditions and slower eco-
nomic growth to help contain demand-side inflation  
pressures as 2027 unfolds. Higher interest rates, elevated 
bond yields, and a cooling economy should gradually 
dampen demand, while wage growth remains surprisingly 
subdued across most major economies. This is encour-
aging, as wages are often the channel through which tem-
porary price shocks become embedded in broader  
inflation dynamics. Taken together, underlying inflation 
pressures are likely to moderate rather than intensify over 
the coming year.
When it comes to the monetary policy outlook, we 
believe the current tightening cycle has limited longevity. 
Headline inflation has been pushed higher by supply- 
side developments, but underlying inflation trends remain 
considerably more benign. In the US, cyclical inflation,  
as measured by the San Francisco Federal Reserve’s Cycli-
cal Core Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE)  
Inflation gauge, has not reaccelerated (see chart 5). If any-
thing, the opposite has occurred: the measure has kept 
inching lower, suggesting that demand-driven price pres-
sures remain well contained despite the recent energy 
shock. Longer-term inflation expectations have also been 
stable. Five-year, five-year forward inflation swap rates10 
have stayed remarkably well anchored, an indication that 
investors continue to view the recent inflation pickup as  
a passing supply shock rather than the beginning of a new 
inflation regime.
The labor market also warrants close attention. Histori-
cally, central-bank tightening cycles have often ended not 
because inflation suddenly disappeared, but because 
economic activity and labor demand began to soften. The 
US labor market has remained resilient so far, yet there 
are signs of gradual cooling beneath the surface. We be- 
lieve the next meaningful deterioration in employment 
conditions could give policymakers the catalyst they need 
to bring the current tightening cycle to a close. 
Key risks to our 2027 outlook 
While our base case remains one of continued, albeit 
slower, economic growth and somewhat sticky but  
manageable inflation in 2027, the range of potential out-
comes is wide. In our view, several risks could tilt the 
economy toward either a more inflationary or a more 
deflationary path.
We believe geopolitics remains the foremost upside risk 
to inflation and downside risk to growth. A renewed sup-
ply shock, whether it originates in the Middle East, East-
ern Europe, or some other flashpoint, could once again 

11
disrupt trade flows, energy markets, and global supply 
chains. The global economy appears less vulnerable to 
energy shocks than in the past, but another surge in  
commodity prices would complicate the inflation outlook 
and constrain central banks’ ability to support growth.
Fiscal sustainability is a second concern. Government 
debt levels have risen significantly across much of the 
developed world, and higher interest rates have increased 
debt-servicing costs. Markets have so far been willing to 
finance large deficits, but investor confidence cannot be 
taken for granted. Any reassessment of fiscal sustain­
ability, particularly in major economies such as the US, 
France, or the UK, could potentially trigger higher bond 
yields, tighter financial conditions, and stoke market vola-
tility.
On the downside, central banks may be overestimating 
how much restrictive policy the economy can bear. Mone-
tary policy tends to work with long and variable lags, so 
the full weight of past tightening may yet be felt. If rates 
stay restrictive for too long, weakness in rate-sensitive 
sectors such as housing could spill over into consumption, 
investment, and employment, increasing the risk of 
recession.
Finally, the possibility of an AI-related downturn is one 
that investors may wish to keep in mind. As discussed 
above, the current wave of investment in AI infrastructure 
has become a major driver of global capital expenditure. 
The long-term potential of AI remains considerable, but 
periods of technological fervor have historically been 
accompanied by episodes of overinvestment. A slow-
down in AI spending, weaker-than-expected commer- 
cial adoption, or a breakthrough by competing regions 
could temper expectations and weigh on growth, par- 
ticularly in sectors that have benefited most from the boom.
These risks point in very different directions. Geopolitical 
and fiscal shocks would likely prove inflationary, whereas 
a recession born of overtightening or a reversal of the AI 
investment cycle would lean deflationary.

Running ahead
On October 28, the Fed held its target range at 3.75 –  
4.00 percent, with two members favoring a 25 bps 
increase. December tightening remains a market expec-
tation, not a Fed commitment. The gap is in 2027: the 
Fed’s median projection shows rates flat, while markets 
anticipate close to two further hikes (see chart 1).
We believe the market is more likely to move toward the 
Fed than the other way around. Energy has driven much 
of this year’s rise in inflation, and wage growth has stayed 
moderate. If sticky core services inflation eases, the extra 
rate hikes priced in for 2027 would likely be the first to go. 
The composition of the rise in yields supports this view. 
Almost all of this year’s rise in 10-year Treasury yields re- 
flects higher real yields rather than higher inflation com-
pensation, and a higher expected path for policy rates 
rather than a higher term premium. Firmer growth plays  
a part, with second-quarter gross domestic product re- 
vised up and activity holding up into the autumn. But  
the Fed’s own projections point to inflation as the driver. 
This is an oil shock filtered through the expected Fed 
response, and it shows up in real yields because the market 
trusts the Fed. Such a move can reverse. In our view, the 
main risk would be doubt about the Fed’s resolve, which 
could weigh most on longer maturities.
At around 5 percent, we believe 10-year yields may offer 
an attractive asymmetry, with potential gains appearing 
larger than potential losses. For illustration purposes 
only, over 12 months, a yield rise of about 75 bps could be 
absorbed before a US dollar investor incurred a loss, 
while a one-percentage-point fall could imply a return of 
more than 12 percent (see chart 2).* 
In credit, spreads have widened, most visibly in high yield, 
where stress has spread from CCC-rated bonds to  
BB and B.11 With yields around 8 percent, no recession in 
our base case, and companies still able to raise new  
funding, we see no case for turning negative. But spreads 
still look tight relative to volatility, so we have moved high 
yield to neutral, favoring BB. This reduces active positions 
where risk has grown and adds duration where we see  
a more compelling risk/reward. 
Investment-grade (IG) spreads also remain thin and offer 
little compensation for the supply ahead. After a pause 
since August, AI-related issuance in the US IG market 
looks set to resume with Microsoft’s planned bond sale, 
while hyperscalers’ spending plans point to further large, 
long-dated deals. With little spread cushion, returns are 
likely to come from carry12 and rates, not tighter spreads, 
so we remain cautious.

A change of mood
Few themes have had a greater impact on equity mar-
kets than AI, and few have been as volatile, in our view. 
In recent weeks, the AI trade suffered one of its steep-
est setbacks yet, as chipmakers recorded their longest 
losing streak since 2008. Yet the weakness was con- 
centrated within technology. Defensive sectors such as 
consumer staples, energy, and health care gained 
ground, styles such as quality and low volatility outper-
formed, and the average stock held up better than the 
major indices.
The sell-off came down to four main concerns. First, high-
er-than-expected US inflation sent bond yields to their 
highest levels in over two decades, weighing on equity 
valuations (see chart 1). Second, investors grew more 
concerned about how the AI boom would be financed. 
Building the infrastructure requires enormous capital, 
much of it funded with debt, and as borrowing costs rose, 
bonds issued by the most indebted AI computing pro­
viders slumped. The worry quickly spread to equities as 
industry leaders warned that access to financing could 
become a bigger constraint on AI growth than chips or 
electricity. Third, progress announced by CXMT, a Chi-

Finally, crowded positions in a small group of AI winners

In our view, the AI cycle has not yet peaked. Demand is 
still strong, and the latest company results confirm that 
computing power is in short supply. Investors are weigh-

and becoming more selective, rewarding companies that

We believe several factors may support equities from here. 
The earnings picture has been encouraging across 
regions (see chart 2), and bond yields appear to be stabi-
lizing from their peak, taking some pressure off valuations. 
Sentiment and positioning have become less stretched, 
leaving markets on a somewhat healthier footing. Con-
cerns about debt could recede if large cloud providers 
fund more of their AI spending from their own cash flows.
Risks haven’t disappeared. Inflation could stay sticky, en-

and volatility is poised to stay elevated. But overall, we 
see a healthy adjustment in an ongoing investment cycle. 
It also highlights that concentration carries risks, as port-

14 Commodities
From tight to tamer
2026 was a year of tight, volatile oil markets. Supply dis-
ruptions kept prices well supported, even as demand 
suffered its second-largest annual decline in 60 years. 
Despite the recent strength, our base case for 2027 is 
one of gradual normalization, with firmer prices in the 
first half and softer prices in the second.
Inventories absorbed most of the supply shock. Rather 
than being offset by an immediate increase in production 
elsewhere, hundreds of millions of barrels were drawn 
from government and commercial stockpiles to offset lost 
Middle Eastern supply, preventing prices from rising  
further. Yet high prices ultimately did what high prices 
tend to do: they curtailed demand. The International 
Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that global oil demand fell 
by around 2.5 to 2.6 million barrels per day in 2026, most 
visibly in Europe and Asia.13  
The outlook for 2027 looks different (see chart 1). As Mid-
dle Eastern exports normalize and trade flows recover, 
consensus expectations are for the market to swing from 
deficit into surplus. Non-Middle Eastern producers have 
little incentive to restrain output after benefiting from ele-
vated prices throughout much of 2026, so supply growth 
could stay robust, at least early in the year. A looser mar-
ket does not automatically translate into a big drop in 
prices. The 2026 inventory drawdowns have created sub-
stantial restocking needs, meaning part of the expected 
surplus may never reach the spot market, as governments, 
refiners, and traders seek to rebuild depleted reserves. 
The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR),14 which is 
near its lowest level since 1982 (see chart 2), is a case in 
point. No meaningful refilling has taken place yet. Com-
mercial oil inventories, meanwhile, have already shown 
signs of rebuilding, while product inventories are lagging. 
This supports our view that oil prices could stay rela-
tively well supported in the first half of 2027. As restock-
ing runs its course and surplus production becomes 
harder to absorb, softer market fundamentals could be- 
come increasingly visible in the second half.
Where could we be wrong? One risk is that the Middle 
East conflict escalates into a broader regional war, dam-
aging energy infrastructure, disrupting key shipping 
routes, and removing significant volumes from the market. 
We do not view this as the most likely scenario, as we 
believe few parties want an oil shock that could tip the 
global economy into recession and destroy oil demand. 
Conversely, the cohesion of the Organization of the Petro-
leum Exporting Countries and its allies (OPEC+) may be 
tested. Some producers may prefer to maximize exports 
while prices remain high over managing supply jointly. If 
producers end up competing for market share, oil prices 
could fall more than expected.

Currencies
The euro pays a French risk 
premium
The euro has had a difficult autumn. Its exchange rate 
against the US dollar (EUR / USD) has fallen from just 
above 1.20 in January to around 1.12, and the drop since 
mid-September has been faster than higher US rate 
expectations alone would explain. We believe French 
fiscal uncertainty has added to the pressure.
The gap between French and German 10-year govern-
ment bond yields has widened sharply since July, to levels 
last seen during the euro debt crisis, and the euro has 
weakened alongside it (see chart 1). The speed of the move 
has stood out more than its size, a sign that market li- 
quidity has thinned. Our analysis suggests that changes 
in growth and inflation expectations explain only a small 
part of the widening. Most of it appears to reflect the bud- 
get and politics. Cutting a deficit of around 5 percent of 
GDP is difficult for a fragmented parliament ahead of the 
2027 presidential election.
Still, we see this as a French problem, not a euro crisis.  
In 2011, sovereign bonds, banks, equities and money 
markets came under pressure at the same time. Today, 
the strain is largely confined to French government 
bonds. Italian and Spanish bonds show little stress, and 
the ECB’s measure of systemic stress is near historic 
lows. As long as the widening reflects French fiscal risk 
rather than disorderly markets, the bar for ECB interven-
tion is likely to stay high. We expect EUR/USD to trade in 
a range of roughly 1.10 to 1.15 until a budget is passed.  
A credible budget could quickly remove part of the French 
risk premium, while a downgrade of France’s credit rating 
or a failed budget vote could push the euro toward the 
lower end of that range.
A rates bounce, not a new dollar uptrend
The dollar has benefited from the Fed’s hawkish shift, and 
the US Dollar Index has moved back toward the upper 
end of its range since mid-2025. We see this as a rates-
driven bounce rather than the start of a new uptrend. 
Markets now price in rate hikes across the G10 curren-
cies,15 and US hikes are expected earlier, so the dollar’s 
rate advantage is expected to peak in the first half of 2027 
and narrow after that (see chart 2). Large fiscal deficits 
and a weak external position remain medium-term head-
winds. We believe geopolitics is the main risk, as it could 
revive safe-haven demand.
As for the Swiss franc, it has given back part of its gains 
against the dollar but has firmed against the euro as 
French stress led investors to seek safety. With the Swiss 
National Bank (SNB) policy rate at zero and in no rush to 
raise it, the yield gap is likely to cap further gains, and the 
SNB would probably counter renewed strength.
