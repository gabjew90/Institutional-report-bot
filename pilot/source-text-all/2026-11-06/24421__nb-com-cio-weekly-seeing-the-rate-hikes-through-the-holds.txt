Last week’s rate holds at several major central banks shouldn’t be read as pauses yet.
Hikes are coming.
The U.S. Federal Reserve, European Central Bank (ECB) and Bank of Japan (BoJ) all did
what was expected last week and held interest rates steady, choosing to wait until their
December meetings before potentially hiking again.
Markets had been pricing the holds, but persistent inflation and continued strong
economic growth made that decision harder to justify, and the Fed itself saw two dissents
in favor of a quarter-point rise. Last week’s elevated U.S. Personal Consumption
Expenditures print followed recent hot Consumer Price and Producer Price Index data, an
inflationary trend that extends across the other economies and the U.K.
Indeed, the Bank of England (BoE), which meets Thursday, is expected to hike for the first
time since August 2023.

As we outlined recently in our Fixed Income Outlook 4Q 2026, we see these four central
banks heading into a broadly synchronized but relatively short tightening cycle to contain
inflation, with local macro and political dynamics shaping the timing and extent of each
move.
The Fed’s Dovish Hold
With the midterms this week, the political backdrop favored a hold even as inflation rises,
increasing the importance of Chair Warsh’s press conference as the market sought
guidance on the pace and timing of policy adjustments. Going into the meeting, the
market had priced a quarterly path of hikes through June 2027.
In our view, the tone of the conference was starkly different from September’s. Warsh’s
comments overall were moderately dovish, underscored by his consistent pointing to the
committee’s data-dependent lean and its willingness to accumulate more data before
further policy adjustments. He also highlighted the tightening of financial conditions since
the September hike, which he said he wants to see play out between now and December.
In response, the market modestly reduced pricing of an aggressive hiking cycle, pushing
rates higher at the long end of the curve and lower at the short end.
Our base case remains that the Fed will hike by 25 basis points in December before a
potentially lengthy pause, with the neutral rate settling around 4.00 – 4.25%. However,
there is a tail risk of more hikes if energy prices remain elevated or there is a feed-through
to core inflation.
ECB and BoJ Eye December Hikes
Energy and core pass-through matter well beyond the U.S., and the Fed’s patience is a
luxury that, in our view, the ECB and BoJ can afford less. Both are contending with rising
inflation, and both held rates last week, strengthening market expectations of December
hikes.
In Japan, underlying inflation has risen steadily this year and has now broadly reached
the BoJ’s long-held 2% target, supporting the case for further hikes. We expect 25 basis
points in December, with another potentially in the second quarter of next year.
The ECB’s calculation is more complex. It must contend not only with high and rising
energy costs—European gas prices jumped above €100 a megawatt hour last week for
the first time in four years, on the threat of a re-escalation in the Iran conflict—but also
with volatility in the French government bond market and a succession race for the
presidency of the bank.
ECB President Christine Lagarde announced ahead of the meeting that she will step
down in February next year, setting up a leadership contest that may inject greater
uncertainty into the policy path.
2/7

In its statement, the ECB cited stable underlying inflation and wage growth, together with
tightening financial conditions, as reasons for holding. It also said for the first time that
higher energy prices for longer would weigh on growth, which gave the statement a
broadly dovish tone.
We still think a December hike is likely if gas prices stay above the €77 level in the ECB’s
“adverse scenario,” and we anticipate a pause next year as the focus shifts from inflation
to growth.
How We See Positioning
Taken together, we expect U.S. interest rates to be range-bound around current levels,
with a bias to shift lower on any sign of risk-off. The market continues to price more hikes
than the Fed has projected, which is why we hold a long-duration bias in the U.S. That
bias also fits the tail risk we see in credit, since a policy-induced slowdown would tend to
pull yields down.
In Europe, we have moved slightly longer, concentrating in the five- to 10-year part of the
curve. In particular, we have returned our positioning in France to neutral, viewing the
sell-off as largely overdone, and see convergence opportunities in Italy and Greece at five
to seven years and in Central and Eastern Europe.
Hikes are coming, but with much of the tightening already priced and financial conditions
doing part of the central banks’ work, a key question is how growth and credit absorb it.
We are positioned accordingly, holding duration where we believe yields can fall and
staying selective where spreads have widened.
