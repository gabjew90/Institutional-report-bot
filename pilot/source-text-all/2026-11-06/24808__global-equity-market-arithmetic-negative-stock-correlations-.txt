Global Equity Market Arithmetic 
Negative stock correlations are both an opportunity and a challenge 
Running quant stock strategies and having lived through the 2007 quant crash creates a degree 
of paranoia when you see highly unusual moves in cross-sectional stock returns. Last Thursday 
was one such paranoid day, triggered in part by a substantial daily drawdown in our Machine-
Learning-based US mean-reversion strategy (SGEPMRU Index), which up until that point had 
been having a strong year. Seeing your long leg lose 4.6% while your short leg rises 3.1% is both 
highly unusual and painful. We looked back at previous periods when the long and short legs 
were so negatively correlated, and the answer was straightforward: in 20 years of data…never! 
We also noticed a major daily rotation out of high-volatility stocks and, unlike last week, this 
was accompanied by a strong upward move in low-volatility stocks. A US low-vs-high-volatility 
stock strategy rose 7.3% on Thursday, despite the average stock being 50bp higher. A very rare 
event in data going back to 1994, as shown in the top-right chart. 
Of course, the AI thematic dominated Thursday, but so did Energy, and it is these two dominant 
themes that are helping to drive significant levels of negative stock correlation. Of the 124,750 
possible pair-wise correlations in the S&P 500, 40% are negative, many significantly so. 
Such dispersion, while a challenge, is also highly useful for long/short models. Multi-factor 
models also have exposure to value and quality, and these have held up comparatively well. 
Our US mean-reversion strategy (SGEPMRU) had a challenging 
week, as the long leg went down as the short leg went up!
