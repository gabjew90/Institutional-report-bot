AI in 2027: 20 questions for the global
economy
The AI boom is reshaping growth, productivity, trade, jobs and markets.
Investment still has room to run in 2027, but financing is getting riskier
and expectations are sky-high. We ask how long it can last, who stands
to gain and what happens if the promised returns fail to arrive
In a nutshell
The AI boom still has room to run. Investment is likely to continue rising in 2027,
supported by strong demand and constrained supply. But its contribution to US
growth is probably close to its peak as the sheer scale of investment makes
further acceleration harder.
The boom is entering a new phase. The story is expanding beyond chips and
data centres into power generation, grids and cooling. At the same time,
financing is shifting gradually from hyperscalers' enormous cash flows towards
debt, private credit and more complex structures, increasing the risks if expected
returns fail to materialise.
The economic effects are becoming broader and more complicated. AI is

The winners will not necessarily be those building the most AI infrastructure.
Europe and Asia are benefiting from critical positions in the supply chain, while
ultimately the largest gains may go to companies and economies that use AI

Our base case is a plateau, not a bust. Today's leading technology companies
are highly profitable and much less leveraged than their dotcom predecessors.
But expectations are exceptionally high. The crucial question for 2027 is whether
AI revenues and productivity gains begin to justify the extraordinary investment.
If they do not, overcapacity, tighter financing and falling valuations could turn a
plateau into a much harder landing.
How big, how long?
Railroads, electrification, dotcom fibre: how does today's AI
boom compare with the great investment manias of the past?
Bigger than the dotcom boom, more important for growth and, so far, more safely financed.
Investment in US data centre structures, computers and software reached 5.6% of GDP in the
second quarter of 2026, up from 3.8% in 2022, or 5.2% once imported hardware is netted out.
That already puts the current boom above the dotcom peak in 2000, when business
investment in computers, communications equipment and software reached about 4.5% of
GDP, although still below Britain’s railway mania of the 1840s, when railway investment alone
reached roughly 6-7% of GDP.
So while this is not the biggest investment boom in history, it is already bigger than the last
great technology boom. And it is having a much bigger economic impact: IT investment
accounted for 16-18% of US GDP growth in 1998-2000; AI-related investment now accounts
for 37%.
The biggest difference though is how this boom is being financed. Railways and telecom
networks were built on debt and ended in defaults, largely through unrated railroad bonds in
the 19th century and high-yield telecom debt after 2000. This time, investment has so far

been funded mainly from the cash flows of some of the world’s most profitable companies,
with debt and equity only now being added at the margin.
The assets are different, too. Rails and fibre lasted for decades. Data centre shells, grids and
power plants will also have long lives, but GPUs may have an economic life of just three to six
years. That much shorter shelf life is central to the debate over whether today’s investment
will generate sufficient returns.
Still, the payoff could come faster. Factories took decades to redesign their floors around the
electric motor. AI coding tools are paying off within months, and adoption is already disrupting
software, accounting, online retail and advertising.
The common thread is that transformative technology does not necessarily mean good
returns for those who finance the transformation. The Nasdaq lost almost 80% between 2000
and 2002, yet the fibre laid during the boom went on to underpin the streaming economy.
Investors build the road; others get to drive on it.
How much more will be invested in AI in 2027, and is the boom
still accelerating?
Still accelerating in dollars, but probably peaking in its contribution to growth. 
Momentum remains strong. Taiwan's leading semiconductor companies report order books
that are full on a rolling 18-month basis, with any new capacity snapped up as soon as it
comes online. Industry forecasts point to tight supply-demand conditions until 2028. Barring a
wave of order cancellations or defaults, that points to another year of heavy AI investment in
2027, with hyperscaler capex expected to exceed US$1 trillion, increasingly geared towards AI
infrastructure.
So is the boom peaking? In dollar terms, no. The constraint today is not appetite but supply:
chips, power, permits and skilled electricians. When the bottleneck is the factory gate rather
than the order book, spending is more likely to be stretched out than cut back.
But economists should be careful what they wish for. GDP growth responds to the change in
investment, not its level. Investment that rises 50% a year adds a lot to growth; investment
that rises 10% from a much bigger base adds far less; and investment that merely holds
steady at a record high adds nothing at all. As the base gets bigger, the arithmetic gets
tougher. That is why we expect AI investment to keep rising in 2027 while its contribution to US
growth should peak.
What would signal a genuine top? Shortening lead times for chips and equipment, cancelled or
postponed data centre projects, capex guidance that stops being revised up, and a widening
gap between investment and AI revenues.

For now, the bigger risk is not that the money runs out in 2027, but that it keeps flowing so fast
that the questions about returns grow much louder in 2028.
Chips and data centres are only the first layer. How big is the
second wave of investment in grids, power generation and
cooling?
Bigger, slower and longer-lived than the first wave – and potentially more important for the
macro outlook.
As a rule of thumb, US$1bn buys roughly 25MW of fully equipped AI compute capacity. Not all
of the c.US$1 trillion of hyperscaler capex expected in 2027 will go to AI compute; some will
fund conventional cloud infrastructure. But even if only half does, that implies more than
12GW of additional power demand – roughly the output of a dozen large nuclear reactors,
needed around the clock.
That is where the second wave comes in: transmission lines, substations and transformers, gas
turbines and renewables, battery storage and industrial cooling. In the US, grid connection
queues and transformer lead times have already become binding constraints, and some
developers are building their own generation on site rather than waiting for the grid.
This second wave differs from the first in three ways. First, the assets last for decades rather
than years. A GPU may be obsolete by 2030; a substation built today will still be humming in
2060. That makes grid investment less vulnerable to a sudden AI downturn and more like the
railway build-out of the 19th century. Second, it is slower. Permitting, planning and
construction take years, which spreads the investment impulse over time and could stretch
out the cycle. Third, it is more widely shared: grid and power equipment draws on suppliers in
Europe and Asia as well as US construction and engineering firms, so the benefits travel
further.
There is a catch. Somebody has to pay for the grid, and in regulated markets that somebody is
often the household electricity customer. Rising power bills are already turning data centres
into a political issue in parts of the US, and they feed straight into the inflation debate.
Chips are the sprint. Power is the marathon – and the marathon may end up mattering more.
Is this an American party, or does Europe get an invitation?
Who supplies the picks and shovels?
So far it is very much an American party – but Europe supplies the catering, and may turn out
to be the smarter guest.
The investment gap is stark. US imports of computer equipment and semiconductors, a rough

proxy for AI investment, have risen by about 258% since 2019, compared with 38% in the UK.
Europe's own build-out is held back by high energy prices, scarce grid capacity – think of the
data centre moratoria in Dublin and Amsterdam – and fragmented capital markets. The EU's
AI gigafactories initiative is an attempt to catch up.
But Europe sells crucial picks and shovels. ASML expects €44-45bn of sales in 2026 and says
customers keep raising their equipment-demand forecasts because of the scale and duration
of AI investment. Infineon expects its AI-related revenues to rise from around €1.5bn in FY2026
to €2.5bn in FY2027. Add electrical and grid equipment, cables, power generation and cooling,
and Europe's industrial base is well plugged into the American build-out.
Asia has been one of the biggest beneficiaries of the AI boom. The gains extend well beyond
Korea and China, which have benefited from surging demand for memory chips and advanced
technology hardware. Across ASEAN, economies have also attracted significant FDI into
semiconductors, electronics manufacturing, and supporting supply chains as firms expand
capacity and diversify production networks.
More importantly, the broad economic gains from AI come from using it, not building it – and
here Europe is surprisingly good. Uptake is strong, and European SMEs use AI almost as
intensively as large corporates, because the upfront investment is low. Nor does AI look like a
winner-takes-all technology: switching costs are low and network effects limited. If model
providers end up competing on price, the spoils go to the users – and Europe does not need to
build nearly as many data centres to compete. Late to the construction site, Europe could still
be early to move in.
There is a silver lining, too. Europe is far less exposed if the boom turns to bust. Its AI capex is
smaller and the wealth channel much weaker: equities make up 13% of UK households'
financial assets, versus 44% in the US. Missing the party also means skipping the hangover.
Which sectors stand to win most, and which risk being left
behind?
The winners sell the infrastructure or use AI to do more; the losers are those whose business
model is the very task AI now performs.
The clearest winners are the suppliers of the build-out: semiconductors and chip equipment,
utilities and grid operators, electrical equipment, construction, cooling and data centre real
estate. They are already seeing it in their order books.
Software is mixed. AI is a powerful selling point, but it also threatens the per-seat pricing
model: if one employee with an AI agent does the work of five, the customer needs fewer
licences.

Most at risk are legacy IT services and business process outsourcing, call centres, parts of
professional services, and media and advertising models that depend on search traffic. AI-
based services may also disrupt online retail, including online travel agencies, as AI assistants
increasingly do the browsing and the booking.
But don't write off every exposed sector. The Jevons paradox – cheaper supply raising demand
– could favour some of them. Legal services is a good example: if AI makes contract review or
legal advice much cheaper, demand may rise faster than the cost per task falls. Growth may
also come from unexpected places. In healthcare, AI-driven drug discovery and diagnostics
could open up new treatments, creating demand rather than merely cutting costs.
Ultimately, productivity gains depend more on the tasks employees perform than on the
sector they sit in. Workers handling large amounts of data, text or graphics are likely to see the
largest gains. The chart below, from our 2023 work, shows how the potential benefits of

In short, the winners will be those that build AI or use it to work smarter. The losers will be
those whose core activities can increasingly be automated.
Growth, prices and trade
How much of US growth in 2027 will come from AI capex, and
what would growth look like without it?
Today, more than a third. In 2027, probably less – which is exactly why the rest of the
economy matters.
At first glance, AI looks like the engine of the entire US economy. Data centre construction,
equipment investment and software spending have grown at a pace that makes their headline
contribution to GDP growth look spectacular. But much of that money leaves the country
straight away: from Taiwanese chips to servers assembled abroad, the hardware is largely
imported. Netting out those imports, we estimate that AI-related investment accounts for 37%
of US real GDP growth. Substantial, but a long way from the 'AI is the only game in town'
narrative.
The more uncomfortable finding lies outside tech. Rather than lifting the overall growth rate,
the AI boom seems to have cannibalised growth elsewhere. Strip out AI, and the rest of US
private non-residential investment had fallen year-on-year for six consecutive quarters by
mid-2026. Before 2021, tech and non-tech investment moved broadly in step; since then, they

have moved in opposite directions. One explanation: shareholders reward companies that
embrace AI, so capital budgets are being redirected towards tech at the expense of everything
else. So the boom isn't so much about creating additional investment as redirecting
investment from elsewhere in the economy.
What would US growth look like without AI? Not simply 37% lower. Part of the capital,
engineers and electricity absorbed by AI would have found other uses. A world without the AI
boom would most likely show weaker growth, but not by the full amount of AI's contribution –
and with a more balanced investment mix.
For 2027, our plateau base case implies a smaller AI contribution to growth. That would give
the non-tech economy room to breathe – but only if it is ready to take over.
For now, AI is a capex story, not a consumer story. And capex stories have a habit of ending
with a hangover.
The productivity question: when, if ever, does AI show up in the
statistics?
In the US and China, it already does – partly. The big gains are still to come, and may arrive
abruptly.
It depends on where you look. In the US, labour productivity has picked up markedly since the
start of the AI boom. Part of that comes from high-skilled services, exactly where you would
expect AI to show up first. But part of it reflects the enormous data centre investments, which
are capital-heavy and need very little labour. Total factor productivity – the best available
measure of gains from technological progress – has improved decently in the US, but without
an exceptional acceleration. While AI-related sectors are providing a significant boost to
several Asian economies, this largely reflects rapid investment and higher capital utilisation
rather than a sustained improvement in underlying productivity. However, in China, we’ve seen
labour productivity growth pick up in the last two years, breaking what had been an extended
downtrend as Chinese companies race to incorporate AI in workflows. In the eurozone, which
lacks comparable AI investment, labour productivity growth remains lacklustre, with very few
exceptions, like for example, in the Netherlands.
The technology holds real promise for broad-based productivity gains. But reaping them
requires wider adoption and investment in infrastructure and skills, both within companies and
across the economy. That suggests a J-curve: heavy upfront intangible investment – time
spent learning new skills and reorganising workflows – could initially mute measured
productivity before the investment pays off, possibly quite abruptly. Electrification is the classic
precedent: the electric motor arrived in the 1880s, but factory productivity only took off in the
1920s, once factories had been rebuilt around it.

And even then, a shift of the workforce towards sectors where productivity gains are harder to
achieve – think healthcare and care work – would dampen the macroeconomic effect.
Measurement doesn't help either: when AI makes a free digital service better, GDP statistics
barely notice.
Still, after decades of slowing productivity growth, AI is likely to be a game changer. The
question is not so much whether it will show up in the statistics, but when. In 1987, Robert
Solow quipped that you could see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity
statistics. It took about a decade to prove him wrong.
Is AI inflationary or deflationary? Electricity, memory chips and
construction costs point one way; long-run efficiency gains
point the other.
Inflationary today, disinflationary tomorrow – but don't hold your breath for the outright
deflation Silicon Valley promises.
It depends on your time horizon. For now, the effect runs in the inflationary direction. AI's
enormous energy demand is adding to the current energy shock, memory chip prices have
surged, and the scramble for electricians, transformers and construction capacity is pushing
up costs. Meanwhile, the investment boom is pouring fuel on the fire of a US economy already
running with above-target inflation.
In the longer run, once the technology lifts productivity, it should lower unit labour costs and
exert disinflationary pressure across the economy. Like most technological breakthroughs, AI
will probably end up being disinflationary. But the effect will be concentrated in services and
much smaller for goods with physical inputs. Algorithms can't make steel, copper or concrete
cheaper.
There is also a less comfortable twist. AI allows companies to price dynamically, adjusting
prices to what each customer is willing to pay and thereby capturing more of the consumer
surplus. That could make some prices stickier rather than lower. Together with the physical
constraints, this makes the outright deflation Sam Altman envisages rather unlikely.
For AI itself, the picture is different. As we argued in 2024, we expect a downward price trend
for AI services, as with other software categories, and at the sector level we continue to hold
that view. Competition between model providers should keep the pressure on.
For central banks, this means the AI disinflation dividend is a story for the second half of the
decade, not for 2027. Until then, the boom adds to demand faster than it adds to supply – the
textbook recipe for inflation, not deflation.

How is AI reshaping global trade and investment flows, from
Taiwanese chips to Dutch lithography?
AI is redrawing global trade around a single supply chain – and rewarding the countries that
own its choke points.
AI is increasingly sorting the global economy into winners and laggards. The IMF has raised its
outlook for economies benefiting from the AI investment boom, particularly those embedded
in the global technology supply chain, while countries outside it see much less of a boost.
Taiwan is riding booming semiconductor demand, South Korea is benefiting from demand for
high-bandwidth memory, and the effects travel upstream to the suppliers of advanced
chipmaking equipment, including the Netherlands.
This illustrates how AI is reshaping both trade and investment flows. Spending on data centres
and computing capacity in the US drives demand for chips from Asia, which in turn requires
investment in semiconductor capacity and equipment from Europe, Japan and the US. The
result is an international AI investment chain, with growth increasingly flowing to the countries
that hold its critical links. It also explains a good part of the surge in the US import bill: every
data centre built in Texas shows up in Taiwan's export statistics.
Trade policy is the second force reshaping the chain. Export controls on advanced chips to
China, tariffs and subsidy programmes such as the US Chips Act (or direct government stakes
in companies) are pushing companies to build capacity closer to home or in friendly countries.
That makes the chain more resilient on paper, but also more expensive – another reason why
the deflationary promise of AI may take longer to materialise.
The flip side is concentration risk. If the chain snaps – through a demand slump, an escalation
of export controls or a Taiwan crisis – the countries benefiting most today would feel it first
and hardest.
What does AI mean for labour markets, and for how central
banks read them?
So far, AI is reshaping who gets hired rather than how many are employed – which makes it a
real-time headache for central banks.
Evidence on AI's labour market impact remains fragmented, and the economy-wide effect is
still limited. If history is any guide, fears of mass unemployment are overdone: with every
major technology of past centuries, growth was strong enough to create jobs elsewhere, even
as demand for some types of labour fell. But if adoption is so fast that labour demand and
supply can't adjust in time, unemployment could rise temporarily.
The first cracks are showing at the entry level. In the US, job growth has remained positive but

weaker in sectors with high AI exposure; evidence from the eurozone is mixed. The Dallas Fed
finds that employment among young workers in AI-exposed occupations has weakened,
mainly reflecting lower hiring rather than more layoffs. According to the Stanford Digital
Economy Lab, employment among 22-25-year-olds in highly exposed occupations was

help alleviate any negative effects of AI on employment. For developing Asia, particularly India
and the Philippines where outsourcing sectors are highly exposed to AI, the evidence points to
adaptation rather than decline. AI is changing the nature of outsourced work, not reducing
demand for it.
AI is not firing people; it is quietly not hiring them. Still, none of this points to mass
displacement. Yet. And with ageing populations and persistent labour shortages, the real
question may be whether shortages or AI turn out to be the bigger problem.
For central banks, AI is less of a short-run issue than you might expect. Higher productivity
growth combined with more muted job growth could be the healthy sign of an economy
absorbing a new technology, and come with more moderate inflation. The tricky part is
reading it in real time. A soft payrolls print could signal either a weakening economy or a more
productive one – and the right policy response would be very different. Get it wrong, and a
central bank either cuts rates into a productivity boom or sits on its hands as the economy
weakens.
Who pays?
Is the boom still funded from cash flows, or are hyperscalers
increasingly turning to debt?
Still mostly from cash flows – but the cushion is thinning and the borrowing has begun.
The largest hyperscalers have so far paid for the AI build-out out of their own pockets, which is
the biggest difference from the dotcom and railway booms. But capex is absorbing a rising
share of operating cash flow, squeezing free cash flow and leaving less room for share
buybacks. For companies that used to return cash to shareholders at a record pace, that is a
notable shift in priorities.
Borrowing is stepping up. Several hyperscalers, such as Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, and Oracle
have issued jumbo bond deals since 2025, with Oracle the most leveraged of the group. Equity
markets are being tapped too: several big tech firms announced plans to raise equity in June.

None of this is alarming on its own – these are investment-grade companies with strong
balance sheets – but it marks a change of phase. Cash-funded booms are self-limiting: they
stop when the cash runs out. Debt-funded booms can run further, and fall harder.
The key is to distinguish between the players. The cash-rich hyperscalers can absorb a
disappointment in AI returns; their core businesses in advertising, cloud and software keep
generating cash regardless. The neoclouds and AI labs are a different story. They depend
entirely on outside money – venture capital, private credit, vendor financing and strategic
investors – and their business models only work if revenue keeps growing spectacularly. They
are the most likely point of stress if financing conditions tighten.
In between sits a growing grey zone of leases, joint ventures and special purpose vehicles that
keep debt off the hyperscalers' own books.
The direction of travel is clear. The boom started as a self-funded venture by the richest
companies in the world. It is gradually turning into a credit story.
Leases, SPVs, private credit: how opaque has AI financing
become?
More opaque than the hyperscalers' balance sheets suggest – but so far, the risk rests on some
of the strongest borrowers in the world.
A growing part of AI financing is moving off balance sheet. Special purpose vehicles and joint
ventures, such as Meta's Hyperion data centre financed with Blue Owl, long-term leases and
GPU-backed loans allow companies to fund the build-out without the debt appearing directly
on their own books. Securitisation of data centre assets through asset-backed securities and
commercial mortgage-backed securities is growing as well, bringing a wider pool of investors
into the game.
Leases are a particular blind spot. They are recognised as on-balance-sheet commitments
only once contracts are final; until then, the extent of incremental lease liabilities is uncertain.
There is also the question of who carries the risk if the tenant walks away after the initial term.
Although initial lease terms of 15 years are not uncommon, these can still be shorter than the
economic life of the underlying infrastructure, leaving some residual risk around renewal or re-
leasing.
That said, risk has to be assessed contract by contract. The largest technology firms, with very
solid balance sheets, are well placed to take on additional liabilities, and much of the off-
balance-sheet structure ultimately rests on their creditworthiness. The problem is less hidden
insolvency than hidden concentration: it is hard to see how much of the system leans on the
same handful of tenants.

Opacity also complicates the macro picture. When financing is spread across SPVs, private
credit funds and securitisations, it becomes harder for investors, regulators and central banks
to see where risks are concentrated – and harder to judge how a shock would travel.
Looking ahead, capital may eventually become a major constraint on the AI build-out,
alongside permitting and access to power. The more the industry relies on creative financing,
the more it depends on investors' continued willingness to lend. Financial engineering can
prolong a boom, but it can't make the returns appear.
How exposed are banks, and is private credit the shadow
banking of this cycle?
Direct bank exposure looks modest; private credit is the obvious weak spot – but contagion
would run through markets rather than bank failures.
Banks' direct exposure to AI looks limited. The hyperscalers fund themselves mainly through
cash flow and bond markets rather than bank loans. Indirect exposure is harder to gauge:
banks lend to private credit funds, data centre developers and construction firms.
Private credit is the obvious weak spot. Is it the shadow banking of this cycle? Partly. It has less
of the maturity mismatch that made pre-2008 shadow banking so fragile: much of the capital
is locked up in closed-end funds, so investors can't run. But it shares other features: opacity,
infrequent valuation marks and leverage on leverage, with funds borrowing from banks to lend
to borrowers that are themselves highly leveraged. And it is increasingly exposed to AI through
loans to neoclouds, GPU-backed financing and data centre developers.
The pressure point is the semi-liquid vehicles sold to retail and wealth clients. They promise
periodic liquidity on assets that are anything but liquid. In a downturn, redemption requests
could force managers to impose gates, damaging confidence across the asset class. So far,
these vehicles are not core to the AI build-out, but they are where a shock could turn into a
story.
If stress does materialise, we would expect contagion to run through tighter financial
conditions and wider credit spreads across the economy rather than through bank failures.
That is less dramatic than 2008, but not harmless: a repricing of credit risk would raise funding
costs far beyond tech, hitting the very non-tech investment that is already struggling.
Banks may well escape an AI bust largely unscathed. The economy they lend to may not.

fund their own customers?
Worth watching, but this is not Lucent and Nortel all over again – mainly because the balance

sheets are much stronger.
The dotcom precedent is uncomfortable. Lucent, Nortel and Cisco lent money to telecom
start-ups so they could buy their equipment. When the customers went bust, the suppliers
took the hit, and much of the reported demand turned out to have been financed by the
sellers themselves.
Today, there are echoes. Chipmakers are taking stakes in AI labs and neoclouds that then buy
their chips. The same handful of firms raise money, buy chips, lease compute and book
revenue off one another. The concern is less solvency than signal: circular deals inflate
reported demand and blur true end-demand. Revenue that only exists because the supplier
paid for it isn't demand.
But the picture differs in important ways. Many of these contracts appear to be normal
commercial arrangements, and AI labs also generate fast-growing revenues from customers
outside these related deals. AI-related revenues are still only part of the much larger
businesses of the hyperscalers. And the macro backdrop is very different: the overall US
corporate debt-to-GDP ratio is not worrisome, in stark contrast to the leverage that built up
before the dotcom bust and the global financial crisis. Circularity in a cash-rich system is a
transparency problem; circularity in a leveraged system is a solvency problem.
That does not mean investors can relax. Circular deals create concentration risk: if one key AI
lab stumbles, the shock would travel quickly through its suppliers, landlords and investors, who
are often the same companies. And they make it harder to judge how much genuine end-
demand there is for AI – precisely the question on which the whole boom rests.
The test is simple: follow the money until it reaches a customer who isn't also an investor. The
longer that trail gets, the more nervous we should become.
Higher Fed rates helped end the dotcom bubble. Could
monetary policy or bond yields pop this one too?
Probably not as the trigger – but monetary policy could make any bust more painful, because
the safety net is thinner than in 2001.
In 1999-2000, the Fed hiked rates to 6.5% before the dotcom bubble burst, then cut by almost
five percentage points in 2001. This time, an energy shock is keeping inflation above target.
Actual rate hikes and more rate hikes to come could bring both the Federal Reserve and
European Central Bank closer to risky territory.
High policy rates do not necessarily stop AI investment first. If expected returns on AI remain
very high, capital may keep flowing in while financing becomes uneconomic for lower-return
investment elsewhere. Tight money could deepen the split between tech and non-tech

investment rather than end the boom.
At the moment, we expect hyperscaler funding needs to increase further as investment in AI
infrastructure, data centres and power capacity accelerates. We forecast total issuance of
around US$280bn in 2027, including approximately US$190bn in USD and US$65bn in EUR

The long end of the yield curve matters more than the policy rate. As the AI build-out becomes
increasingly debt-funded, higher bond yields and term premia raise the cost of financing long-
term investments, from data centres to power plants. A sustained rise in long-term yields
would therefore hit hardest where the build-out depends most on external funding.
The bigger issue is the safety net. If the bubble bursts, central banks can't come to the rescue
as aggressively as in 2001 while inflation is above target. Fiscal space is also far more limited
than a quarter of a century ago.
But don't rule out a different kind of rescue. AI is not just an economic tool; it is also about
power – geopolitical and technological. If things went south for the sector, some form of
government support would be conceivable, from public procurement to guarantees or
targeted support for strategically important companies. The dotcom bust was left to the
market. An AI bust might not be.
Monetary policy is unlikely to pop this bubble. But it could decide how hard the landing is – and
Washington may decide who gets a parachute.
Bubble or not?
What do valuations and revenue gaps tell us?
Bubble-like pockets, but not a bubble across the board: the leaders are hugely profitable, while
the real uncertainty sits with the AI labs and the revenue gap.
Valuations first. At its 2000 peak, Cisco traded at well over 100 times earnings. Today’s AI
leaders are expensive, but profitable, and analysts expect earnings growth to bring their
multiples down considerably. Microsoft’s P/E is expected to fall from 29.2x based on fiscal 2026
earnings to 21.9x in 2028, Amazon’s from 28x based on 2025 earnings to 21x in 2027, and
Alphabet’s from 20.9x to 17.9x over the same period. With earnings estimates being higher
than a couple of months ago, that does not look particularly stretched. The larger uncertainty
sits with the AI labs, whose valuations follow the venture capital playbook: spectacular revenue

growth, uncertain long-term economics.
Then the revenue gap. Capex is running ahead of AI revenues, as it can take several years for
investment to translate fully into revenues. Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet have invested
around US$600bn over 2023-25. As a rough rule of thumb, every dollar of AI infrastructure
may need to generate 40-50 cents of annual revenue (assuming hyperscaler-like margins),
implying US$240-300bn a year – although not all of this capex was AI-related. That looks large,
but less so next to labour costs. At US$100,000 per employee, US$300bn is the cost of three
million workers. AI doesn't need to replace them: a 20% productivity gain across 15 million
employees would deliver the same value. Consumers alone won't close the gap; the money
has to come from enterprises.
Two caveats. Concentration: the weight of the largest tech stocks in the S&P 500 exceeds the
dotcom peak, so any disappointment would hit the whole index hard. And circularity in
financing and revenues makes true end-demand harder to measure.
Finally, a bubble in prices doesn't mean the technology is a dud. Railways had a bubble. So did
the internet. Both still changed the world.
What could end the boom prematurely: disappointing demand,
power bottlenecks, a cheaper-model breakthrough, or Taiwan?
Most likely not a single trigger, but a slow squeeze: overcapacity, eroding margins and tighter
financing if the revenue gap fails to close.
Start with the usual suspects. Demand could disappoint if enterprise adoption stalls and pilots
fail to turn into profits. Power bottlenecks are more likely to cap the pace of the boom than to
end it, and could even stretch it out. A Taiwan crisis remains a low-probability, extreme-impact
event.
Cheaper models are the most intriguing risk. The DeepSeek shock of January 2025 wiped
almost US$600bn off Nvidia's market value in a single day. Cheaper compute can raise
demand – the Jevons paradox – but higher volumes can come with lower margins. That makes
the profitability of AI labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic the big question mark. If it
disappoints, the hardware will still be needed, but the build-out would likely slow. Nvidia's
exceptionally high margins could also normalise if competition increases or the scarcity value
of leading-edge compute declines.
Missing from most lists is financing: rising bond yields, a credit event at a leveraged neocloud
or private credit fund, or an energy shock that keeps central banks hawkish.
Ultimately, we see the biggest risk in overcapacity. Continued innovation in chips, more
efficient models and optimised algorithms could eventually create an imbalance between

supply and demand, pushing down utilisation and returns. For now, though, the industry is still
struggling to scale compute and secure enough power, while new business models and
applications keep emerging.
There is one more way to read today's capacity commitments: as strategic entry deterrence.
Announcing gigantic investment plans discourages competitors from entering the market –
and some of those plans may never be fully executed if returns disappoint. That would be a
quieter ending than a crash. Not with a bang, but with a series of downward revisions to capex
guidance.
If tech stocks fall 30%, how much does the wealth effect hit
US consumption?
Less than you might think, but more than during the dotcom bust – and the real damage
would come through investment and jobs.
US equity wealth is highly concentrated. The top 20% of households by income (earning more
than $155k a year) hold 84.1% of equities, mutual funds and defined-contribution pension
assets. That limits the wealth effect. Fed research suggests the marginal propensity to
consume out of wealth fell from around 3.5 cents per dollar (1964-2011) to 2.6 cents in 2020.
A 30% stock market correction would wipe out roughly $24.5tn of household wealth. At 2.6
cents per dollar, that means a drop in consumer spending of around $640bn, or 2.9% of
nominal consumer spending (an annualised $22.3tn in August). With wealth even more
concentrated than in 2020, the multiplier is probably weaker; at 2.2 cents, the hit would be
around 2.4%. That is a level effect, typically playing out over one to two years – but a big
number nonetheless.
History offers some comfort: during the dotcom bust, US consumer spending barely flinched.
But it could be worse this time. First, wealthy households are currently the only ones holding
up spending; the bottom 60%, who spend roughly as many dollars as the top 20%, are
squeezed by tariffs and higher fuel prices. Second, the Fed has less room to cut than in 2001.
Third, the credit cushion is thinner: rising delinquencies on car loans and credit cards show
stress building at lower incomes.
The good news: this is not 2008. US household debt stands at around 70% of GDP, the same as
in 2001 and well below the 100% reached in 2007.
In the end, the wealth effect is the smaller channel. After the dotcom bust, business
investment fell by 11% in two years. IT equipment was the least of it, falling just 6%, transport
equipment fell 21% and manufacturing buildings plunged by a whopping 55%. Tech may light
the fuse, but the explosion happens elsewhere.

What would a tech sell-off mean for the dollar and FX
markets?
Not necessarily the classic safe-haven dollar rally – this time, a tech sell-off could well hurt the
dollar.
The usual playbook says risk-off means dollar up. But an AI-led correction would be a US-
centric shock. Foreign investors hold large, partly unhedged positions in US equities, built up
over a decade of American outperformance. A sell-off in US tech could trigger repatriation and
higher hedge ratios, both dollar-negative – as in the 'sell America' episode of April 2025. The
IMF estimates the dollar would fall by around 6% in an AI-led equity correction.
The rates channel points in the same direction. Negative wealth effects, weaker consumption
and slower US growth would eventually push the Fed towards rate cuts, narrowing interest
rate differentials against the dollar. The timing would depend on inflation: with prices still
running above target, the Fed may cut later than markets hope, which could limit the dollar's
fall in the early stages.
Among the winners, the yen and the Swiss franc would play their traditional safe-haven role.
The euro could benefit relatively, given Europe's lower exposure to both AI capex and equity
wealth (4Q). A tech crash made in America would, for once, be a reason to hold European
assets rather than flee them.
The losers would be the currencies of Asian tech exporters, notably the Taiwan dollar and the
Korean won, whose economies are leveraged bets on AI demand. They would come under
heavy pressure, although intervention by local authorities and the legacy of huge trade
surpluses would cushion the blow somewhat.
The bottom line: in an AI bust, the dollar may lose its reputation as the place to hide. Not
because the world has found a better safe haven, but because this time the fire would start in
the safe house.
Our base case for 2027: boom, plateau or bust, and which
signposts should readers watch?
Our base case is a plateau at altitude: the boom continues in 2027, but its contribution to
growth peaks. A bust remains the main risk scenario.
Early indicators point to tight semiconductor supply into 2028, suggesting the investment
cycle still has legs. But as capex growth slows, AI's contribution to US growth should fade.
The difference between the two scenarios matters enormously for the US. A plateau would
give the non-tech economy room to breathe: capital, energy and workers no longer absorbed

by AI could flow back, allowing non-tech investment – and potentially employment – to
rebound. A bust would be a very different animal: an abrupt choking off of tech investment,
similar to 2001, combined with a negative wealth effect on consumers through falling stock
markets. And this time, central banks would have less room for rate cuts as inflation remains
their main concern.
Which signposts should readers watch? For the boom itself, the key indicators are hyperscaler
capex guidance, Taiwanese order books and lead times, and AI revenues relative to capex.
Markets will focus on realised AI revenues at the large technology firms, margins on
incremental revenue and how the next phase of the build-out will be funded; figures that
disappoint relative to company budgets and analysts' forecasts could trigger a sell-off.
On financing, credit spreads on AI-related debt, private credit flows and redemptions, and bond
yields and term premia will show whether investors remain willing to fund the build-out. In the
real economy, the questions are whether non-tech investment finally recovers, how hiring in
AI-exposed sectors develops, and what productivity, grid connection and power data are
telling us.
The question for 2027 isn't whether AI will change the world. It's whether anyone gets paid for
it in time. If earnings fail to match sky-high expectations, the plateau could turn out to be a
ledge.
