Bottom line up top
What lies beneath U.S. economic resilience? While hard data continues to 
point to strength in the underlying U.S. economy, the drivers of growth have become 
more concentrated, much as they have in the U.S. equity market. The advance 
estimate of third-quarter GDP growth came in at a healthy +3.4% annualized, 
driven by strong consumer spending and surging business investment. This was 
higher than consensus forecasts and an improvement over the prior quarter’s 
+2.2%. Beneath the headline figures, however, economic momentum remains 
disproportionately dependent on just two groups: AI-focused corporations investing 
heavily in the technology’s infrastructure, and affluent households benefiting from 
rising financial asset prices. This increasingly narrow foundation raises questions 
about the sustainability of growth.
Meanwhile, inflation remains stubbornly elevated. September’s headline PCE Price 
Index accelerated to 3.6% year-over-year, while core PCE — the Federal Reserve’s 
preferred inflation gauge — reached 3.1%. More concerning, real disposable 
personal income declined -0.3% month-over-month, even as consumers continued 
to spend. With household savings remaining historically subdued, the divergence 
between spending and inflation-adjusted income suggests we may see mounting 
pressure on lower- and middle-income consumers.
Against this backdrop, last week’s Fed meeting laid bare policymakers’ difficult 
balancing act. The Fed held rates steady at 3.75% to 4.00%, but unlike the 
unanimous July decision to hike, the latest vote was divided, with two dissenters 
favoring additional tightening instead of a pause. Chair Kevin Warsh acknowledged 
the economy’s resilience while conceding more work needs to be done on the 
inflation front. That leaves open the prospect of a December rate hike (Figure 1).
Corporate earnings season shines a new light on AI investments. Alphabet 
(Google’s parent company) reported extraordinary third-quarter growth in its 
cloud business, but disappointing search and advertising results, with negative 
free cash flow underscoring the high costs of the AI buildout. Capital expenditures 
(capex) for megacap tech companies are expected to keep increasing, and investors

Portfolio considerations
A rough quarter, but a real opportunity. The municipal bond market as 
measured by the Bloomberg Municipal Bond Index returned -6.35% in the third 
quarter, the worst quarterly showing for munis since 1981. But the losses were 
driven largely by rate dislocation rather than credit deterioration. Municipal yields 
are near historic highs, at 4.21% for the overall market as of 30 October. That’s in 
the 95th percentile of yield levels over the past 20 years, meaning current yields 
are in the highest 5% of all observations over the past two decades. 
Yet credit fundamentals remain sound, with state and local revenues supported by 
healthy growth in income, sales and property taxes. Demand is high with investor 
inflows on track for their second-highest annual total on record, at over $77 billion 
year to date. The recent selloff in munis was fueled by a surge in 10-year U.S. 
Treasury yields — which drove negative returns across almost fixed income sectors

Elevated starting yields matter for future returns. Income has historically 
accounted for more than 85% of municipal total return, underscoring the value of 
a high starting yield (Figure 2). Over the past 20 years, when the index yield has 
exceeded 4%, the subsequent annualized three-year return has averaged +6.19% 
(source: Bloomberg). Using that history as a basis and given current yields, if 
yields were to fall by 1% over the next three years, the index would theoretically 
gain +11.63%, versus an expected -2.34% loss if rates instead rise by 1%. Put 
another way, there could be approximately 5x as much upside as downside with a 
+/-1% move in yields, although future results are, of course, never guaranteed.
The current backdrop presents a compelling window for putting capital to work 
in the muni market. We favor selectively extending duration, particularly between 
15 and 25 years given the steepness of the municipal yield curve. We also prefer 
high-quality general obligation and essential-service bonds. Lastly, we are closely 
watching the U.S. midterm elections, as 17 states have enacted or are proposing 
property tax relief measures, and several other states are considering or have 
implemented new income taxes. These potential tax changes highlight why careful, 
credit-specific research remains essential in municipal security selection.
