Weak Consumer Spending Exposes  
Competitive Differences
Four takeaways from earnings season
November 3, 2026
The results and commentary of more than two dozen Consumer 
Discretionary and Consumer Staples companies that have 
reported earning in recent weeks offer a window into how 
financially strained consumers and ongoing geopolitical 
uncertainty are affecting growth and competition across 
industries. Here are four takeaways.
1. Fuel prices are changing shopping habits
Walmart has said that gasoline prices of $4–5 a gallon represent 
a critical threshold at which consumers begin limiting their 
spending to just the necessities. The resulting affordability 
pressures have been visible in recent months at US restaurant and 
convenience-store chains, as well as retailers in Mexico.
Despite running several promotions this year, including a value 
menu with items under $3, McDonald’s US sales slowed as 
consumers sought cheaper alternatives, although management 
partly attributed the weakness to limited awareness of the 
discount menu and inconsistent adoption by its franchisees.
Convenience stores have become an increasingly popular 
destination for cheap meals on the go, occasionally stealing 
customers from fast-food chains such as McDonald’s. Even 
so, Couche-Tard, owner of Circle K stores, warned that higher 
fuel prices and inflation were beginning to pressure sales of 
discretionary items including candy and salty snacks. And in 
Mexico, Walmex’s store traffic slid roughly 1% in the latest  
quarter, with shoppers buying fewer items and seeking more 
economical options.
2. The business effects aren’t felt uniformly
Several consumer-goods companies including Church & Dwight, 
L’Oréal, Marico, and Unilever delivered strong growth in sales 
volumes and market-share gains, while others such as Procter 
& Gamble (P&G), Hindustan Unilever, and parts of Colgate 
struggled despite their established brands, scale advantages, and 
substantial investments in pricing, innovation, and distribution. 
In a weak consumer-spending environment, companies can’t just 
rely on the overall growth in demand of their product categories.

Operational performance increasingly depends on product 
relevance, the makeup of a company’s portfolio, and the quality of 
its innovation and execution.
In Church & Dwight’s case, for example, its lower-priced laundry 
detergents, including Arm & Hammer and Xtra, outperformed 
the overall detergent market. While competitors such as Henkel 
and P&G have reinvested tariff refunds into greater promotional 
activity to appeal to increasingly price-sensitive consumers, 
Church & Dwight reduced promotions and still gained market 
share. The company benefits from a combination of brand 
differentiation through its longstanding use of baking soda 
as an ingredient, a cost advantage stemming from its access 
to abundant trona mineral reserves in Wyoming that supply a 
substantial portion of its baking soda production needs, and a 
value-oriented positioning in some categories that has resonated 
with budget-conscious shoppers.
Colgate, meanwhile, acknowledged that some of its products 
had become expensive compared to competitor brands, which 
led to market-share losses in the US. The company said it is 
now working to address those pricing gaps, without elaborating 
on how. “We can’t be uncompetitive in terms of pricing and 
promotion,” CEO Noel Wallace said on a July earnings call  
while adding, “We want to be sure that we’re not leading that 
further down.”
3. Some companies are making  
smart trade-offs
For large Consumer Staples companies, high market shares and 
steady cash flows can make it tempting to prioritize profit margins 
over research and development that may not yield benefits for 
some time. Lately, as AI and other initiatives lead to productivity 
gains, companies such as Colgate and its rival Haleon have faced 
questions about whether they’re reinvesting enough, or effectively 
enough, in product innovation and brand building. Colgate’s 
marketing costs are already at a record high as a percentage of 
sales, yet reported profits are down.
Contrast that with L’Oréal. Even though the business is already 
growing faster than the overall beauty market, it has continued 
to invest heavily in new products, advertising, and e-commerce 
capabilities. Those investments appear to be paying off, with the 
company reporting strong sales across its product lines and most 
regions in its latest quarter. Online sales have risen 17% over the 
trailing nine months, supporting results in North America and 
Europe, while the company is also expanding its reach in Indonesia 
through social- and live-shopping initiatives on TikTok.
4. Weight-loss drugs play a mixed role
Companies in the food business are contending not only with 
changes in consumer behavior brought on by persistently high 
inflation but also with the growing use of GLP-1 medicines that 
suppress appetite. These drugs appear to have been a headwind 
for some businesses, including Domino’s Pizza.
Taco Bell’s parent Yum Brands estimates that 10% of the fast-
food chain’s customers now use a GLP-1 drug. Unlike Domino’s, 
however, Yum Brands has observed little effect on purchasing 
behavior, noting that customers continue to buy similar amounts 
of food even if they consume less of it.
At stores, Couche-Tard suggested that GLP-1s may be reinforcing 
a shift away from national food brands, which have lost favor with 
consumers after years of price hikes. In personal care, meanwhile, 
companies such as Haleon and L’Oréal are positioning certain 
products as solutions to the nutritional and skin-related side 
effects of weight loss.
Taken together, earnings across the Consumer Discretionary and 
Consumer Staples sectors suggest that competitive advantages 
are not disappearing, but their durability is being tested in new 
ways as higher fuel prices squeeze household budgets and 
technology and medicine reshape how consumers discover, 
evaluate, and purchase goods. Scale and brand strength remain 
important, yet companies that continue to innovate, invest in their 
brands, and adapt to changing consumer preferences appear 
better positioned to sustain growth and win market share.
