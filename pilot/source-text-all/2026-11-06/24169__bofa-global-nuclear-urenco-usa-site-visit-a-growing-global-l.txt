Industry Overview 
   
 
 
Key read-throughs for CCJ and smaller enrichers 
Earlier this week, we hosted an investor site visit to the US operations of privately held 
Urenco. Urenco is an international supplier of uranium enrichment services and fuel cycle 
products for the civil nuclear industry, serving utility customers worldwide from 
operation in Germany, the Netherlands, the UK, and the US. Its US Operations, known as 
Urenco USA (UUSA) are located in New Mexico. Estimates from nuclear fuel consultancy, 
UxC, LLC show Urenco accounting for 29% of global enrichment production in 2025, 
with company-wide annual production capacity of 17.2 million (m) separative work units 
(SWU). We see Urenco’s rapidly growing enrichment order book is a positive signal for 
Buy-rated Cameco (CCJ), and Neutral-rated enrichment supplier, Centrus (LEU). Urenco’s 
expansion experience points to substantial capex for smaller developers, while its robust 
contracting experience supports more US enrichment supply. 
Urenco’s growing order book extends to 2050 
Urenco’s nuclear fuel enrichment order book increased to €27.3 billion (bn) at 30-Jun-26 
from €21.3bn at year-end 2025; and up from €18.7 bn at year-end 2024, and €14.7bn at 
year-end 2023. Its contract book now extends to 2050. Meanwhile, the conversion and 
natural uranium suppliers, like CCJ, have experienced more subdued contract book 
growth. We think this reflects a preference by utility buyers, particularly in the U.S. due 
to the Russian import ban, to focus first on securing upstream fuel supply (enrichment) 
before turning focus to the downstream (conversion and natural uranium). As enricher 
contract books fill, we think that convertor and miner books will grow more quickly. 
The order book justifies current expansion plans 
Necessitated by the robust order book, that is “very full” through 2034, Urenco 
announced on 2-Jun-26 a 2.1m SWU annual capacity expansion at its New Mexico 
facility. This nearly 50% expansion is to start construction in 2029 and complete in 
2036. It is in addition to a 700k SWU expansion announced on 6-Jul-23, that is to reach 
completion next year. The ‘nth of a kind’ 2.1m SWU should cost around $2 billon (bn), or 
roughly $1bn per m SWU. UUSA noted that the replacement value of the entire New 
Mexico facility, or its ‘1st of a kind’, (in current nominal dollars) cost would require capital 
of $10-$11bn, or $2-$2.2bn per m SWU (as designed). 
Existing & new nuclear drive the next leg of growth 
Underlying LEU demand from the existing fleet remains healthy, while utilities pursuing 
longer refueling cycles and uprates create incremental potential for LEU+ as higher 
burnups may be needed to maximize reactor output. At the same time, light-water 
reactors add to conventional LEU demand, while advanced reactors broaden the 
opportunity into LEU+ and HALEU. Urenco is positioned across this spectrum, giving it 
exposure to greater enrichment needs from the existing fleet and incremental demand 
from new nuclear builds, rather than relying on any single source of nuclear growth.

Power & Utilities View 
Underlying LEU demand remains intact, with LEU+ providing incremental upside 
We believe this underlying demand for LEU remains well supported, while LEU+ provides 
another potential growth avenue as utilities pursue longer refueling cycles, higher 
burnups, and power uprates across the existing fleet. 
• 
Existing LEU demand remains healthy: Urenco serves 50+ customers across 20+ 
countries, and its record €21.3bn order book extends into the 2040s, providing 
significant visibility into conventional enrichment demand. 
• 
LEU+ could facilitate longer refueling cycles: Higher enrichment can support the 
higher fuel burnups needed to make longer cycles feasible at additional reactors.  
• 
Uprates can similarly increase fuel requirements: The NRC notes that utilities 
can support uprates through slightly more enriched uranium, a higher percentage of 
new fuel and/or plant modifications.  
We are already seeing tangible evidence of both trends across the utility fleet: 
• 
Constellation Energy’s (CEG) Calvert Cliffs Units 1 & 2 operate on 24-month 
refueling cycles, while CEG is working through NRC licensing changes to transition 
Byron Units 1 & 2 and Braidwood Units 1 & 2 to 24-month cycles. Along with this 
CEG has announced an agreement with Google for 890 MWs of uprates across 11 
reactors and an agreement with Amazon for 190 MWs of uprates at its Calvert Cliffs 
site 
• 
Southern Company’s (SO) NRC pathway for Vogtle Units 1 & 2 contemplates 
enrichment above 5% U-235, higher burnups and ultimately potential 24-month 
cycles, demonstrating how LEU+ could expand the operating flexibility and output of 
existing reactors. 
• 
NextEra Energy (NEE) has been pursuing a 24-month cycle at St. Lucie Unit 2 
• 
Public Service Enterprise Group (PEG) has pursued a similar 24-month-cycle 
transition at Hope Creek 
• 
Vistra Corp. (VST) plans to add 433 MW of incremental nuclear capacity through 
uprates at Beaver Valley, Davis-Besse and Perry, VST could have further upside at 
Comanche Peak with ~200 MW of potential additional uprates  
The opportunity is also beginning to commercialize directly for Urenco, which has signed 
LEU+ contracts with several existing light-water-reactor operators, suggesting LEU+ is

Alternative Energy View 
New nuclear adds another layer of fuel demand, while advanced reactors provide a path 
into HALEU. 
We see the growth in new nuclear as an incremental source of enrichment demand for 
Urenco, with the opportunity extending across conventional large reactors, light-water 
SMRs and advanced reactors. Large reactors and light-water SMRs generally add to 
traditional LEU requirements, while more advanced reactor designs can require LEU+ or 
HALEU, creating a pathway for Urenco to move into higher-enrichment products. U 
Importantly, we are starting to see this opportunity translate into actual customer 
contracts: 
• 
Ontario Power Generation (OPG): Urenco USA has a long-term agreement to provide 
LEU enrichment services for up to four GE Hitachi BWRX-300 SMRs at Darlington, 
representing Urenco's first commercial customer in Canada and the first long-term 
fuel supply agreement for an SMR.  
• 
Jimmy Energy: Urenco has contracted to supply LEU+ for Jimmy's first 20 MWt 
high-temperature gas-cooled microreactor. Importantly, Jimmy plans to initially use 
LEU+ before potentially transitioning to HALEU once supply becomes available, 
providing a tangible example of LEU+ acting as a bridge to HALEU for advanced 
reactors. 
• 
Standard Nuclear (STDN): Urenco has Standard Nuclear as an advanced-reactor 
customer under an LEU+ contract. Initial LEU+ production has already occurred, 
with first deliveries targeted for 2027. 
• 
Radiant: Urenco has signed an agreement to provide HALEU enrichment services for 
Radiant's Kaleidos microreactor 
• 
Antares Nuclear: Antares Nuclear and Urenco signed the first long-term, multi-year 
commercial HALEU supply contract in May 2026  
For Urenco, we therefore see new nuclear as incremental to the existing utility 
opportunity rather than dependent on a single SMR technology reaching 
commercialization. New conventional reactors and light-water SMRs add reactors that 
need fuel for decades, while advanced designs broaden the addressable market into 
LEU+ and HALEU.

Urenco’s history and background 
Urenco was founded in 1970 under the Treaty of Almelo, an agreement between the UK, 
German and Dutch governments establishing safeguards and government oversight of 
the company’s uranium enrichment technology and operations. A Joint Committee 
representing the three governments oversees non-proliferation, technology and 
enriched-uranium exports, security and certain ownership and technology-transfer 
matters, while having no role in day-to-day operations. Similar safeguards were 
subsequently extended to Urenco’s US and French activities through the Treaties of 
Washington and Cardiff. The treaty structure is particularly relevant from an investor 
perspective as it places Urenco’s strategically sensitive enrichment technology, 
ownership and technology transfers within a government-controlled framework, creating 
substantial barriers around control and transfer of the company’s core technology. 
 
Urenco’s ownership structure 
Urenco is privately held in three equal one-third interests. The UK government owns 
one-third through Enrichment Investments Limited, while the Dutch government owns 
one-third through Ultra-Centrifuge Nederland N.V.. The remaining one-third is held 
through Uranit UK Limited, which is ultimately owned equally by the German utilities 
E.ON SE and RWE AG. On a look-through basis, this gives E.ON and RWE an effective 
~16.7% interest in Urenco each.

Urenco has four facilities globally 
Urenco operates uranium enrichment facilities across four countries: the UK, Germany, 
the Netherlands and the United States, providing the company with a geographically 
diversified production footprint across Europe and North America. In addition to 
enrichment, several sites house strategically important technology, isotope, stewardship 
and advanced-fuel activities. 
• 
Germany – Gronau: Urenco’s German uranium enrichment facility is located in 
Gronau. The site is also home to the company’s Technology and Design function, 
which is responsible for enhancing Urenco’s centrifuge technology. 
• 
Netherlands – Almelo: The Dutch enrichment facility is located in Almelo. The site 
also houses Urenco’s isotopes business, which supplies isotope products and 
services for medical, research and industrial applications. 
• 
United Kingdom – Capenhurst / London: Urenco’s UK enrichment operations are 
located at Capenhurst, which also houses the company’s nuclear stewardship 
capabilities and planned advanced fuels facility. Urenco’s corporate head office is 
located in Paddington, London. 
• 
United States – Eunice / Arlington: Urenco’s US enrichment facility is located in 
Eunice, New Mexico, and is the only commercial uranium enrichment facility 
currently operating in the US. The company also maintains a sales office in 
Arlington, Virginia.

Where we are in the cycle: price & volume 
Spot vs. term Market 
Uranium (yellow cake, conversion, and/or SWU) is bought and sold in one of two 
markets, either the term or the spot. While the spot market is defined by any activity 
that calls for delivery within the next 12 months, spot price indicators are largely driven 
by activity that calls for “prompt” delivery over the next three months. Meanwhile, the 
term market covers product that will be delivered 12 months out (or longer).  
The exhibit below maps the relationship between the spot and term prices for U3O8 
(Yellowcake). Term prices generally tend to trade at a premium to spot. However, in late 
2023, this trend flipped as spot began trading at a premium to term. This marked the 
start of another bullish period for uranium, similar to the period from 2004-07. While the 
2004-07 period suggests that there may be a correction once supply and demand 
balance, our view is that this will only occur when the uranium deficit levels out, with risk 
to this being extended. The spot and the long-term prices are now trading essentially in 
line, a sign of balance in the spot market. As we highlighted earlier, several catalyst 
could push the spot price higher, potentially back to a premium to LT prices.

Historically, brokers, traders and other financial institutions have been the dominant 
group on the selling side of the spot market (see Exhibit 21). On the buying side, utilities 
were long the dominant group, but were dethroned in 2005 with the invention of the 
uranium carry trade, when brokers, traders, and other financial institutions used their 
balance sheets to help utilities offload inventory from their balance sheets. In 2005, 
brokers, traders, and other financial institutions accounted for 60% of annual spot 
purchase volumes (see Exhibit 22). In 2025, brokers, traders, and other financial 
institutions accounted for ~74% of annual spot purchase volumes. 
In the term market, buyers are almost exclusively utilities while sellers are predominantly 
producers. Over the last three years, producers have consistently accounted for a smaller 
share of annual term selling volumes, decreasing from 92% in 2023 to 76% in 2025. 
Rising utility participation in the term market is a driver of higher U3O8 prices, as churn 
(when brokers, traders, and other financial institutions trade amongst themselves) is 
replaced by end-user consumptive demand.

Cameco Corporation (YCCO / CCJ) 
Our C$190 ($133) PO is based on 1.0x our NAV and 35.0x 2026E & 34.5x 2027E 
EV/EBITDA (all 3 equally weighted). We use a CADUSD FX rate of 1.42. The 35.0x 2026E 
EV/EBITDA is above its long-term average of c.15x, but below the long-term peak of 
c.40.0x that occurred when nuclear energy growth was last very strong. The 34.5x for 
2027E is a slight discount to 35.0x used for 2026E given time-related risk. The 1.0x 
P/NAV is above the longer-term average of c.0.9x but below peak of 1.35x. We think 1.0x 
is justified given Cameco's world-class tier one assets in favorable jurisdictions (Canada) 
& potential exploration upside partially offset by voluntary idling of one of those tier-one 
assets (but is in the process of restarting). 
 
Downside risks: 1) slower-than-expected global energy demand growth, 2) continued 
push-out of a Japanese nuclear fleet restart, 3) any worsening in sentiment toward 
nuclear or more favorable sentiment toward alternative power fuel sources, and 4) any 
production problems at Cameco's only operating mine, Cigar Lake. 
Upside risks: 1) additional potential mine disruptions that may further improve supply-
demand dynamics, 2) better pace of reactor development in key future demand countries 
(China, Japan, and India), 3) more stringent carbon emissions restrictions in key 
countries, encouraging nuclear power as an environmentally friendly base line energy 
source, 4) a material rise in natural gas prices making nuclear power generation 
competitive in the US.

Standard Nuclear (STDN) 
Our $15 PO is based on a DCF, which we believe is the most appropriate methodology 
given the company's early-stage commercialization profile and limited set of direct 
public comparables. To reflect the varying levels of commercial certainty across the 
business, we separate future cash flows into three categories: contracted revenue, 
qualified customer revenue, and future market opportunity. 
Contracted revenue is discounted using a market-based cost of equity, reflecting the 
highest level of commercial visibility within our framework. Qualified is discounted using 
a fuel-manufacturing peer framework, reflecting greater uncertainty than contracted 
revenue but lower risk than broader market adoption. Future market opportunity is 
discounted using an advanced-reactor peer framework to reflect the speculative nature 
of long-term advanced nuclear deployment. We apply a 2.0% terminal growth rate, which 
we view as appropriate for a mature advanced nuclear fuel business. 
Combining the three cash flow buckets results in an enterprise value of $1.27bn. 
Incorporating our 2030 balance sheet assumptions, which we use as the common 
valuation year across our nuclear coverage universe, produces an equity value of 
$2.19bn. Based on the shares outstanding, this supports our $15 PO. 
 
Risks to Downside: Delayed reactor deployment, lower capacity expansion, lower 
customer conversion, regulatory issues, increased competition, fuel supply chain delays, 
lower pricing
