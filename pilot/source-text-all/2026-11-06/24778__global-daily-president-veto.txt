Market comment 
Associated Press is calling the Congressional race in favour of the Democrats. The party wins both 
the House of Republicans and control of the Senate, changing the balance of power when the 
new Congress convenes at the start of 2027. However, President Trump still has the powers of 
veto and executive order, creating a divided government in the US. 
The blue wave that swept through Congress will affect the policies that are proposed from next 
year, but Trump will probably veto any bill that he does not like. “I have the ultimate power – it’s 
called veto,” he said in an interview before voting closed, adding that “if we didn’t win, I veto 
everything. I’ll be vetoing, vetoing”. And, despite the Democrats’ big win, they will not have the 
required two-thirds majority in both chambers to overturn a veto. So, their policy proposals may 
not get far.  
Trump added that his veto powers will eventually force the Democrats to make deals with him. 
But it may not be so easy to make concessions. Both parties share little common ground, so our 
US strategist believes that a legislative gridlock is the more likely result.  
That’s particularly problematic for fiscal policy. Gridlock should limit additional spending or tax 
cuts, but the divided government also reduces the odds of a serious effort to shore up public 
finances. As a result, the term premium will probably remain a force keeping US long-term rates 
pinned at higher levels.  
A first government shutdown approaches on December 11, and the debt limit may lead to more 
political brinkmanship in 2027. Aside from the uncertainty that this creates, the US economy 
should not expect any meaningful fiscal impulse. So, these results could weigh on US equities.  
Meanwhile, President Trump may also test how far his other “tremendous power” reaches. Given 
an unsupportive Congress, the president will probably try to implement his policy agenda through 
executive orders. That particularly allows him to continue course on foreign policy, trade policy, 
and immigration.  
That includes the decision on the way forward in the Middle East. The decision to attack Iran 
again, or not to, lies with the Commander in Chief. President Trump is showing restraint for now. 
He told the Financial Times that “We’re locked and loaded if we need to [strike Iran again], but 
there may be no reason for it.”  
Oil futures fell sharply earlier in the week, after Trump indicated he wants to give diplomacy 
another chance, and front-dated futures have stayed lower since then. However, the recent rise in 
2027 pricing suggests that markets may be shifting expectations to a scenario of tight supply and 
elevated transport costs into next year. To reiterate, we remain sceptical that there is enough 
common ground for a deal between the White House and Tehran, and believe that escalation in 
the coming weeks is likelier than not. 
And, finally, by winning the Senate, Democrats can now also effectively block Trump’s 
appointments. That includes potential replacements for Fed Governors Cook, if she is forced out, 
and Powell, if he decides to resign from the central bank’s board.

Day ahead 
The preliminary HICP inflation estimate for October will probably show further impact of the 
energy shock on Eurozone inflation. Thus far, there has been no evidence that the energy shock is 
spreading, and that’s expected to remain the case. The consensus forecast sees headline HICP 
accelerating to 4.0% y/y, and inflation estimates for the member states –where available– point to 
a similar inflation print. However, core inflation is seen steady at 2.5% y/y. The ECB’s wage tracker 
should also confirm the absence of second-round effects through higher wage demands.  
Final PMIs for the Eurozone, UK, and the United States are due, but revisions are generally small. 
Meanwhile, ISM publishes their competing US services index.  
The ECB’s Lane and Moulin will be speaking.
