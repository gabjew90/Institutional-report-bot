Can commodities weather higher interest rates? 
Higher inflation adjusted interest rates are typically viewed as headwinds for commodities, yet commodities 
(measured by the Bloomberg Commodity Index) continue to rise to multi-year highs, suggesting that the 
relationship may not always be straightforward. History shows that similar interest-rate environments can 
produce very different commodity returns. Although commodities are sensitive to rates, economic growth and 
shifts in supply and demand can outweigh those effects. 
That distinction is particularly relevant this year, with commodity prices rising 35% year-to-date, as of October 20, 
even as the 10-year inflation adjusted Treasury yield rose to multi-year highs of 2.9%. At a glance, this performance 
seems at odds with history, which suggests that over the long run higher or rising inflation adjusted yields should 
coincide with weaker commodity prices (see Chart 1).

Even so, while the level and direction of real yields over the long run correlated negatively with commodity returns, 
year-to-year performance did not always follow a similar path and there have been periods of decoupling (see 
Chart 1). That dispersion reinforces an important takeaway — similar interest-rate environments have not always 
produced similar commodity returns. Instead, economic activity, expanding industrial activity, and tight supply 
dynamics have at times outweighed the pressure of higher rates.  
Today, we see similar themes as economic activity has remained firm. U.S. real gross domestic product (GDP) grew 
at a 2.2% year-over-year in the second quarter, and we expect this strength to carry into 2027 with our U.S. real 
GDP forecast of 2.2%. While manufacturing activity continues to expand rising to its strongest reading since 2022,

Meanwhile, the AI buildout is creating strong secular commodity demand. Spending on data centers, power 
generation, electrical grids, and infrastructure is increasing demand for energy, copper, and other metals. Yet, mined 
supply remains sluggish, with the International Energy Agency estimates that copper demand could exceed supply 
by nearly 30% by 2035.1 
Implications for investors 
Overall, higher rates raise the hurdle for commodities, but firm demand and supply constraints help explain why 
prices have remained resilient. Looking into 2027, we continue to favor commodities and their potential benefit as 
a hedge against inflation and geopolitical risks. However, we would temper expectations relative to the steep 
outperformance seen this year. While economic growth and secular demand themes could support prices, we do 
not discount the headwinds from relatively elevated inflation adjusted interest rates. However, our 2027 year-end 
targets do not suggest a strong rise in inflation adjusted 10-year yields from current levels (October 22), and even 
some moderation from recent highs. Therefore, we continue to favor commodities and would use pullbacks as 
opportunities to add exposure.

Can stocks compete with 5% Treasury yields? 
Higher bond yields have revived a familiar question: Are bonds becoming more attractive than stocks? The 10-year 
U.S. Treasury yield is currently near 5%, up more than one percentage point from its February 27, 2026, low. Those 
higher yields mean investors can potentially earn more income from high-quality bonds than before, prompting 
some to reconsider the relative appeal of equities. One gauge for comparing the two is the ERP, which measures the 
additional return investors expect from stocks over U.S. Treasuries. It is commonly calculated by subtracting the 
10-year Treasury yield from the S&P 500 Index’s earnings yield. The earnings yield is the inverse of the price-to-
earnings (P/E) ratio. For example, a P/E ratio of 20 implies a 5% earnings yield. 
Chart 3. The equity risk premium is near its 25-year low 
 
Sources: Bloomberg and Wells Fargo Investment Institute. Data from January 31, 1990, through October 20, 2026. The equity risk premium is calculated as the 
difference in the earnings yield of the S&P 500 Index and the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield. An index is unmanaged and not available for direct investment. Past 
performance is no guarantee of future results. 
The ERP has fallen significantly and now sits near the low end of its 25-year range, largely because higher Treasury 
yields have narrowed the premium offered by equities. Our year-end 2027 target of 5.25%–5.75% for the 10-year 
Treasury yield creates the possibility that the ERP could fall below zero for the first time since 2002. A negative 
ERP may appear ominous because it means the earnings yield on stocks is lower than the yield on U.S. Treasuries. 
In other words, investors receive no upfront yield premium for accepting equities’ greater volatility and potential 
losses.  
Still, a negative ERP is not a warning signal, in our view. The ERP moved above and below zero multiple times 
during the 1990s, when the S&P 500 Index generated an annualized total return of approximately 18.2%. Unlike a 
bond’s fixed coupon, equity returns can benefit from earnings growth, productivity gains, dividends, and share 
repurchases. Additionally, the ERP is not a reliable market-timing signal or proof that bonds will outperform stocks. 
In our view, equities remain attractive even in a higher-rate environment as corporate profits continue to grow at 
an impressive rate.

France's bond selloff sends a warning to global investors 
France’s bond selloff is a warning for global investors. Rising debt and political division have pushed French yields 
higher and bond prices lower. The lesson extends beyond France: While default still is not on the horizon, yields 
may not adequately compensate investors for growing fiscal and political risks across DMs. 
France, the European Union’s (EU) second-largest economy, faces a difficult combination of high government 
spending and rising debt. The budget deficit was 5.1% of GDP in 2025, meaning the government spent more than it 
collected. That was well above the EU average, with government debt projected to rise further. French bond yields 
have risen further than other DMs (see Chart 4) amid financial concerns. Investors are demanding higher yields to 
compensate for the added risks. Bond prices generally fall when yields rise, weighing on French bonds. 
Chart 4. French bond yields have risen significantly in 2026 
 
Sources: Bloomberg and Wells Fargo Investment Institute, Data as of October 20, 2026. The French - German 10-year spread represents the yield of 10-year French 
government bonds less the yield of 10-year German government bonds. Yields represent past performance and fluctuate with market conditions. Current yields 
may be higher or lower than those quoted above.  
France is the most visible example, but other developed economies face similar pressure. Political disagreements 
can make it harder to control deficits amid competing spending priorities. Governments are balancing higher 
defense and energy spending with stretched public finances. Italy plans to increase spending while Spain’s 
November election adds political uncertainty to the budget outlook. Japanese bond yields are also rising, consistent 
with our outlook for DM yield volatility. Debts are still being paid, but investors are quickly reevaluating their risks, 
even in traditionally high-quality bonds.  
We remain unfavorable on DM Ex-U.S. Fixed Income. Even if issuers are still able to pay their debts, investors 
should make sure the potential returns justify the risks. Yields in many DMs remain below the U.S. even as risks 
increase, leaving investors with less income to offset potential price declines. U.S. Treasuries also face fiscal risks 
and political uncertainty. However, we believe the strength of the U.S. economy should support its ability to meet 
debt obligations, while Treasuries’ central role in global markets should sustain investor demand.

Navigating Private Equity’s uneven recovery 
Through the third quarter, private equity continued its gradual yet uneven recovery. Total deal value moderated 
during the quarter, but the number of deals increased.  
Buyout deals, which focus on established companies, picked up after a softer second quarter amid concerns about 
supply-chain disruptions, inflation, and tighter credit conditions (see Chart 5). We believe this improvement 
highlights the market’s broader resilience. 
Venture capital, which invests primarily in startup companies, remains on pace for a record year. While third 
quarter deal value slowed from earlier in the year, it remained above levels seen between 2022 and 2025. Through 
third quarter, investment activity exceeded $500 billion, about 45% above the previous full-year record set in 2021 
(see Chart 5). 
Chart 5. Private equity continued an uneven recovery 
 
Sources: Pitchbook and Wells Fargo Investment Institute. Data as of September 30, 2026. Q1 = first quarter. Q2 = second quarter. Q3 = third quarter. Q4 = fourth 
quarter. Past performance is no guarantee of future results. 
However, beneath the strong headline numbers, the market remains highly concentrated. For example, AI-related 
startups captured more than 80% of U.S. venture capital deal value through third quarter, while the largest 5% of 
deals accounted for roughly 75% of third quarter deal value.2 
We believe the slow exit environment has contributed to this concentration. There are nearly 1,000 unicorns (i.e. 
startup companies valued at over $1 billion) with a combined $5.7 trillion valuation, while only 18 venture capital-
backed companies went public during third quarter, leaving a large backlog of companies seeking potential exits.2 
Looking ahead, we believe the economic environment and the direction of interest rates remain important. Higher 
rates could increase financing costs and continue to limit exit activity. Against this backdrop, we favor secondaries, 
which help generate liquidity, as well as higher-quality areas such as growth equity and small- and mid-buyouts for 
qualified investors.

Risk considerations 
Each asset class has its own risk and return characteristics.  The level of risk associated with a particular investment or asset class generally correlates with the level 
of return the investment or asset class might achieve. Stock markets, especially foreign markets, are volatile.  Stock values may fluctuate in response to general 
economic and market conditions, the prospects of individual companies, and industry sectors.  Foreign investing has additional risks including those associated with 
currency fluctuation, political and economic instability, and different accounting standards. These risks are heightened in emerging markets. Small- and mid-cap 
stocks are generally more volatile, subject to greater risks and are less liquid than large company stocks. Bonds are subject to market, interest rate, price, 
credit/default, liquidity, inflation and other risks. Prices tend to be inversely affected by changes in interest rates. High yield (junk) bonds have lower credit ratings 
and are subject to greater risk of default and greater principal risk. Although Treasuries are considered free from credit risk they are subject to other types of risks. 
These risks include interest rate risk, which may cause the underlying value of the bond to fluctuate. The commodities markets are considered speculative, carry 
substantial risks, and have experienced periods of extreme volatility.  Investing in a volatile and uncertain commodities market may cause a portfolio to rapidly 
increase or decrease in value which may result in greater share price volatility. Real estate has special risks including the possible illiquidity of underlying properties, 
credit risk, interest rate fluctuations and the impact of varied economic conditions. 
Secondary investments are interests in existing private equity funds that are acquired in privately negotiated transactions after the end of the private equity fund's 
fundraising period. Typically these funds have portfolios of existing investments as well as capital available for new investments. Alternative investments, such as 
hedge funds, private equity, private debt and private real estate funds are not appropriate for all investors and are only open to “accredited” or “qualified” investors 
within the meaning of U.S. securities laws. 
Forecasts, estimates, and projections are not guaranteed and are based on certain assumptions and views of market and economic conditions which are subject to 
change. 
Alternative investments, such as hedge funds, private equity/private debt and private real estate funds, are speculative and involve a high degree of risk that is 
appropriate only for those investors who have the financial sophistication and expertise to evaluate the merits and risks of an investment in a fund and for which the 
fund does not represent a complete investment program. They entail significant risks that can include losses due to leveraging or other speculative investment 
practices, lack of liquidity, volatility of returns, restrictions on transferring interests in a fund, potential lack of diversification, absence and/or delay of information 
regarding valuations and pricing, complex tax structures and delays in tax reporting, less regulation and higher fees than mutual funds.  Hedge fund, private equity, 
private debt and private real estate fund investing involves other material risks including capital loss and the loss of the entire amount invested. A fund's offering 
documents should be carefully reviewed prior to investing. 
Hedge fund strategies, such as Equity Hedge, Event Driven, Macro and Relative Value, may expose investors to the risks associated with the use of short selling, 
leverage, derivatives and arbitrage methodologies. Short sales involve leverage and theoretically unlimited loss potential since the market price of securities sold 
short may continuously increase. The use of leverage in a portfolio varies by strategy. Leverage can significantly increase return potential but create greater risk of 
loss.  Derivatives generally have implied leverage which can magnify volatility and may entail other risks such as market, interest rate, credit, counterparty and 
management risks.  Arbitrage strategies expose a fund to the risk that the anticipated arbitrage opportunities will not develop as anticipated, resulting in potentially 
reduced returns or losses to the fund. 
Definitions 
Bloomberg Commodity Index is comprised of 22 exchange-traded futures on physical commodities and represents 20 commodities weighted to account for 
economic significance and market liquidity.  
Purchasing Managers Index (PMI) is an indicator of the economic health of the manufacturing sector. The PMI index is based on five major indicators: new orders, 
inventory levels, production, supplier deliveries and the employment environment.  
S&P 500 Index is a market capitalization-weighted index composed of 500 widely held common stocks that is generally considered representative of the US stock 
market.
