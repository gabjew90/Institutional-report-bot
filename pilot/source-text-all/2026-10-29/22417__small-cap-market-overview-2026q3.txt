3Q26 Small Cap Market Overview
Smaller stocks in general fell out of favor during the third quarter after 
investors turned their attention to a small cohort of large artificial intelligence 
(AI)-related names.

Though the economic backdrop remained constructive, inflation pressures and rising bond yields served as a 
headwind to smaller companies. The Russell 2000 Index declined 7.2% during the quarter, with performance 
for Russell 2500 Index and Russell Midcap Index slightly better at -6.4% and -3.0%, respectively. In contrast, 
the S&P 500 Index gained 2.3%. However, the smaller stocks continue to outpace their large cap counterparts 
for the year-to-date period, with a 13.7% and a 14.9% return for the Russell 2000 and Russell 2500 Indexes, 
respectively, compared to a 12.7% advance for the S&P 500. Value outperformed growth during the quarter 
across capitalizations.1
A Strong Foundation for Growth
After limited growth in fourth quarter 2025, US real gross domestic product (GDP) growth has accelerated in 
the first half of 2026, with the economy expanding at a rate of 2.5% and 2.2% in the first and second quarters, 
respectively, driven by business investment and consumer spending.2 Momentum is expected to pick up still 
further, as the Atlanta Fed GDPNow estimate is calling for 3.6% growth in the third quarter.3 Global economies 
in general have adjusted to energy supply shocks caused by the war in Iran by sourcing crude oil from other 
regions, drawing on strategic and commercial inventories, and some demand destruction and substitution.
While Gulf oil exports have picked up thanks to rerouting efforts and increasing dark transits, energy prices 
remain elevated, which, combined with resilient growth, has pushed inflation above target in much of the world. 
In the US, for example, consumer prices increased 3.4% for the latest 12-month period, driven by a 16.3% 
increase in the energy component.4

After months of anti-inflation rhetoric from Chair Warsh and others, the Federal Open Market Committee in 
mid-September voted unanimously to increase the federal funds target rate by 25 basis points to a range of 
3.75–4.00%. The dot plot of policymaker forecasts, meanwhile, suggests one more hike is likely before year end. 
Treasury yields, which had marched steadily upward since the outbreak of the Iran war at the end of February, 
legged still higher on the hawkish news.5 The 10-year Treasury yield finished the quarter at around 5.3% while 
the two-year traded at 4.9%; these levels have not been seen consistently since before the global financial crisis.6
It remains to be seen whether we have entered a 
prolonged hiking cycle or if this hike represented a 
one-off that will be followed by more “data-dependent” 
decisions moving forward. While we view additional 
rate hikes as possible, the deliberate lack of formal 
Fed guidance under the Warsh regime makes policy 
trajectory difficult to assess. Further, today’s Fed lacks 
the policy flexibility that many of its predecessors had 
given today’s fiscal situation. The ability to increase 
interest rates meaningfully in the face of inflationary 
pressures is likely constrained by the government’s need to continually roll over its very large primary deficit at 
prevailing higher interest rates. 
Smaller companies tend to be more reliant on short-term, floating-rate debt than large cap names, so the shift 
toward tighter policy could have a material impact on the companies in this space. That said, rising rates coin­
cidental with a robust economic backdrop have not historically been an impediment for small company returns; 
instead, strong economic activity has tended to support corporate fundamentals, to the benefit of these stocks. 
With the earnings resurgence continuing—published forecasts reflect 86% earnings growth for the Russell 2000 
in 2026 and 44% in 2027 compared to 35% and 15%, respectively, for the S&P 500—we would not be surprised to 
see this dynamic play out once again, especially if there is a successful conclusion to the Iran war in the coming 
months and energy prices and inflation ease.7
Recycling Capital as Opportunities Emerge
The very strong first half enabled us to rotate out of certain segments—such as technology and industrials—and 
redeploy assets into sectors that we believe are more attractive—including healthcare and energy—as well as 
more idiosyncratic opportunities. As always, we remain focused on ensuring that valuations are overall cheaper 
than the market and well positioned for upside.
