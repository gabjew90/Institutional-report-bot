As Fast Money Gets Stopped Out And Beijing Steps

BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 12:40 PM 
In January, gold was trading at $5,595. Last Thursday it was trading like a margin call. 
Ever since the yellow metal was "betrayed" last week, sliding below $4,000 for the first time in 
more than three months on a day when stocks puked, yields tumbled and the dollar slipped, the 
market has been stuck in what UBS's precious metals desk describes as a "low-conviction 
range": too much buying at the bottom for the bears, not nearly enough momentum for the bulls. 
And with spot trading around $4,015 this morning, that range is about as tight as it gets. 
That is the picture painted in the latest UBS "Bullion Report" (available to pro subs), whose title 
says it all: "Structural Support, Tactical Caution." Here is how the Swiss bank's sales desk 
summed up the mood in New York: 
New York precious-metals activity remains very quiet, particularly in the afternoon, with an 
exceptionally narrow trading range highlighting the current slowdown. The broad client stance is 
structurally bullish but tactically cautious, while recent stop-outs across several fast-money 
trades have reinforced the market's lack of conviction. Volatility-focused participants have 
moved to the sidelines because current price action offers limited opportunities for meaningful 
exposure. As year-end approaches, participants are increasingly focused on protecting existing 
P&L rather than adding incremental risk. 
In other words, everyone still likes gold, nobody wants to own more of it before Christmas. 
Meanwhile, under the surface, the physical market is telling a very different story, and so, as of 
Friday, are the DeMark technicals. 
 

 
Stopped Out, Sitting Out 
To understand why the fast money is licking its wounds, recall what happened last Thursday. As 
we detailed in "Glass Half Full? Morgan Stanley Says So..." over the weekend, the Nasdaq 
dropped 2.9%, 10Y yields fell 17bps and real yields 14bps, a textbook setup for a gold rally. 
Instead, gold fell 2.8%, and as MS's Nick Savone pointed out, the last time gold did this badly 
on an equivalent drop in 10Y yields was December 2008: 
 

 
 
UBS's own flow data confirms that Thursday was the day the pain landed. Oct 22 accounted for 
roughly 26% of the week's electronic XAUUSD cash turnover, as gold "was under pressure the 
 

 
majority of this day, trading below $4050 for the first time since August." Every client segment 
saw its volumes peak that day, and asset managers did 30% of their entire week's trading in that 
one session. That's what a liquidation looks like: 
 
The specs are bailing too. According to Goldman's commodities desk, managed money cut net 
precious metals exposure by $3.6 billion in the week through Oct 20 (and that was before 
Thursday's flush), with only grains (-$5.7 billion) seeing a bigger cut in the bank's tally. 
Not that the sell-side is throwing in the towel. On the contrary, UBS stresses that the 
"longer-term structural support remains intact through geopolitical risk, de-dollarization, 
non-dollar settlement demand, and continued central-bank buying." The problem is the near 
term: 
Gold remains caught in a low-conviction range, supported by meaningful buying interest near 
the lower end but lacking sufficient momentum for a sustained rally. The tightening 
monetary-policy backdrop continues to constrain the upside, leaving the market treading water 
and providing little incentive for discretionary participants to take significant positions. 
 

 
That "tightening backdrop" probably deserves a footnote: Goldman's economists expect the 
FOMC to skip this Wednesday, then deliver another hike in December. A non-yielding asset 
fighting a hiking Fed is a tough trade, though as we will see below, someone out there has 
decided to take the other side anyway. 
Follow The Kilobars 
The most interesting part of the UBS note comes from far away from the quiet New York 
screens, in the vaults (and refineries) of Asia: 
Against this subdued trading backdrop, physical demand from Asia remains strong, led by 
kilobar demand destined for mainland China, alongside some large-bar activity. Buying began 
around the start of October and has remained consistent, arriving earlier than in recent years 
despite the period's traditionally stronger seasonal demand. Refinery conditions are also 
tightening, with production orders and available refinery slots becoming more difficult to secure. 
Chinese demand is contributing to front-end tightness in gold, while some market participants 
are showing interest in transacting against renminbi rather than exclusively against US dollars. 
Translation: Chinese buyers want their gold in kilobars faster than the refiners can cast them, 
and some of them would rather pay in yuan. Regular readers will not be surprised: two weeks 
ago we reported that China has spent a record $158.8 billion importing more than 1,000 tonnes 
of gold in just the first eight months of the year, versus $96.5 billion for all of 2025. And yet, the 
PBOC continues to report only a fraction of what it actually buys, as we noted on Friday: 
Goldman agrees. In its latest "Precious Comment", also available to pro subs), which we 
discussed last Monday, the bank's commodity team estimated that central banks bought 79 
tonnes of gold in August, or 85 tonnes per month on a three-month seasonally adjusted basis, 
five times the pre-2022 average of 17 tonnes: 
 

 
 
And China was the largest identifiable buyer, with Goldman's nowcast at 25 tonnes for August, 
well above what the PBOC officially owned up to: 
 

 
 
According to Goldman, "EM central banks, particularly China, remain underweight gold relative 
to plausible long-term allocations," which is a polite way of saying the buying has a long way to 
go. Meanwhile, the debate is shifting "from whether to own gold to where to store it," something

Of course, this is the kind of demand that doesn't show up in an intraday chart, but it does

lower end." The fast money sets the range, and Beijing sets the floor.

it did, at least by the minimum standard:

but it met the minimum condition to do so at 3,996.10 (23-Oct). Fresh upside beyond 4,082.80

and Perl sees a path back toward $4,400, about 10% above the low (which, for context, would 
still leave gold more than 20% below January's record). Fail, and the supports at $3,975, $3,923 
and $3,871 come into play. Here is the full map: 
 
The irony, of course, is that DeMark turned bullish right as the desk's clients turned cautious, so 
either the technicians are early, or the clients are about to be late. We know which way we 
would lean with China paying up for refinery slots. 
Silver: All Headlines, No Conviction 
If gold is treading water, silver is flailing in it. UBS calls the gray metal "more fragile and 
headline-sensitive," noting that recent news triggered "an aggressive short-term reaction despite 
little material change in the underlying fundamentals," while its industrial side "may partly 
distinguish its behavior from gold." 
Last Friday was a case in point: XAGUSD volumes peaked at 33% of the weekly total as silver 
"printed fresh lows near $53 prior to paring some losses into the close," and asset managers did 
more than 60% of their week's silver trading in that one session. Banks and hedge funds each 
did more than 30%: 
 

 
 
The DeMark read is less flattering here, with Perl neutral and the risk skewed lower until silver 
can reclaim $56.27: 
Silver is neutral. The daily chart is completing Wave 2 or B, but there needs to be fresh gains 
beyond 56.27 to alleviate the immediate downside threat and pressure on 53.02, 52.31 and 
49.62. Resistance is 56.27, 58.61, 61.50, 63.59 and 65.68. 
None of this will surprise those who have been following Goldman's Robert Quinn, who last 
week said silver is "Still Stuck" after $2.2 billion of spec selling over three weeks. With silver now 
around $54, about 6% below the $57.48 it traded at when Quinn wrote that note, the specs have 
kept on selling (and judging by the latest CFTC data, they probably aren't done). 
As for platinum, Perl is also neutral: the daily chart is completing Wave 5 or 2 and the weekly 
Wave 4, "so the downside from hereon is likely limited," but it needs gains beyond 1,637.78 to 
relieve pressure on 1,528.63 and 1,477.76.

Washington, Beijing And The $4,082.80 Question 
So what breaks the range? UBS lists the usual suspects: US elections, European political 
developments, and "the broader shift toward more nationalist and right-leaning politics, which 
could contribute to geopolitical uncertainty or reinforce the currency-debasement narrative." But 
the desk is careful not to oversell the European angle: 
 

 
However, a direct connection between European political developments and gold remains too 
speculative. For precious metals, developments in Washington and Beijing are more 
immediately relevant to the macroeconomic backdrop. 
We agree, and we would add that the two are now pulling in opposite directions. Washington, 
via a Fed that is still hiking into an AI-led growth scare, is capping the upside; Beijing, via a 
central bank buying far more than it admits and a private sector cornering the kilobar market, is 
propping up the downside. Hence the range. 
Our bet is that the floor proves more durable than the ceiling. As QTR put it in "Gold Could 
Crash...Before It Doubles," in a real deleveraging investors "sell what they can sell," and gold is 
very sellable, so another flush toward Perl's $3,870s is certainly possible if the AI unwind gets 
uglier. But central banks buying 85 tonnes a month and Chinese buyers fighting over refinery 
slots don't sound like a market about to break down for good. The fast money got stopped out at 
the bottom of the range; we suspect the next stop-out will happen above $4,082.80.
