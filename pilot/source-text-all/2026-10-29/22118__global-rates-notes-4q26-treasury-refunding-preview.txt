The rise in yields since the August refunding announcement and the shift in buybacks 
announced and implemented during the refunding quarter have heightened focus on the 
supply mix, especially at the long end. There is the risk that Treasury decides to reduce 
long-end auction sizes following the tweak in guidance at the August refunding round 
about evaluating potential future “changes” rather than “increases” of auction sizes. 
Further, Treasury’s surprise increase in buyback sizes was a clear departure from prior 
behavior, making it difficult to strictly rule out a more dramatic step of eliminating the 
20y point. However, our base case is that Treasury will maintain or modestly increase 
buyback envelope sizes but keep 10-to-30y auction sizes steady, with increase 2-to-7y 
auction sizes starting in May 2027 for several reasons. 
First, the fiscal trajectory and financing requirements over the coming years reinforce 
the value of maintaining the flexibility afforded by an array of liquid benchmark points, 
acknowledging that funding needs continue to grow and that the optimal financing mix 
varies across macro environments. Second, we think the space to reduce 20y auction 
sizes is likely limited before issuance becomes too small and adversely impacts liquidity. 
Third, more specific to the upcoming decision, the most recent refunding guidance still 
included the outlook for unchanged issuance sizes for “at least the next several 
quarters;” given that the fiscal picture has been relatively stable, a decision to alter 
auctions could run counter to Treasury’s predictability objective. And finally, there is 
little to suggest market indigestion or outsized pressure on long-end yields that could be 
readily attributed to the amount of long-end supply.

We think Treasury may make further changes to its guidance, explicitly nodding towards 
the potential for auction size adjustments to come. Consistent with the revision in 
August and preexisting framing, we expect language would be two-sided (i.e. “changes” 
rather than “increases”) with emphasis on structural trends in demand. One possible 
shift would be to adjust the sentence “Treasury anticipates maintaining nominal coupon 
and FRN auction sizes for at least the next several quarters” to “Treasury anticipates it 
may be appropriate to consider changes to nominal coupon and FRN auction sizes over 
the next several quarters.” 
The fourth question of this quarter’s dealer questionnaire reflected Treasury’s continued 
focus on the demand backdrop as it considers future auction size changes. We recently 
noted that throughout the early autumn selloff no particular investor base played any 
unusual, outsized role, but rather UST absorption has been a collective effort. But we 
think there are several key trends that should inform the broader path (Exhibit 3 & 
Exhibit 4). 
We expect the diminished footprint from less price sensitive non-Fed investors to 
continue. In particular, foreign official buyers are unlikely to become a substantial source 
of net demand absent a period of material dollar weakness. And long-end demand 
dynamics have shifted durably as the relative footprint of defined benefit pensions 
dwindles on both sides of the Atlantic, leaving the maturity mix of demand from 
longer-term savers closer to the Aggregate Bond Index (rather than skewing heavily 
towards the long-end).
