October MPM preview: BoJ expected to indicate that inflation 
is “at level consistent with” 2% target 
(original Japanese report issued on October 26, 2026) 
 
Key points 
◼ 
We project BoJ will leave policy rate on hold at 1.25% at October meeting 
after raising rates in June and September 
◼ 
Bank seen indicating that underlying inflation is “at a level consistent with” 2% 
target, 13 years after it was announced 
◼ 
Policy conduct likely to continue to emphasize upside risks to prices for now; 
we anticipate additional rate hikes in December 2026 and April 2027 
 
The Bank of Japan will hold a Monetary Policy Meeting on October 29-30, with the 
official statement to be released on the second day along with the latest Outlook for 
Economic Activity and Prices (“Outlook Report”). We continue to forecast that the 
BoJ will keep the policy rate steady at 1.25%. With only a little over a month since 
the September hike and no new upside risks to inflation emerging, we expect the 
Bank to adopt a wait-and-see stance. We anticipate an 8-1 majority vote, with 
Hajime Takata, the most hawkish member of the Policy Board, dissenting in favor 
of back-to-back rate hikes. 
There are a number of key points to watch at the meeting. First, in the Outlook 
Report, we expect the BoJ will conclude that the underlying rate of consumer price 
inflation (“underlying inflation”) is at a level generally consistent with the 2% price 
stability target. This would signal the Bank’s acknowledgment that the inflation 
target set jointly with the government in the January 2013 Joint Statement, some 
13 years ago, has finally been achieved (Graph 1). However, simply touching the 
target once is not enough. The BoJ will now shift from a focus on the downside 
risks to inflation to a stance of monitoring risks in both directions. At his October 16 
press conference following the G20 Finance Ministers and Central Bank Governors 
Meeting, BoJ Governor Kazuo Ueda said, “We cannot tolerate any further rise [in 
underlying inflation],” using stronger language than before to signal his 
determination to keep the upside risks to prices in check. As consecutive or larger 
rate hikes could become possible depending on circumstances, the Bank’s 
assessment of inflation warrants closer attention than in the past. We will also be 
watching to see whether the Takaichi administration, which has so far confined 
itself to stating that Japan is “not in deflation,” considers declaring an end to 
deflation.

The content of the Outlook Report should also be important to monitor in relation to 
the Bank’s “change in policy phase.” We expect the BoJ to raise its real GDP 
growth forecasts (Table 1) for FY26 and FY27 from the July projections of +0.6% 
and +0.8%, respectively, to reflect recent developments. We think the risks to 
economic activity will remain “generally balanced.” The inflation forecasts for FY26 
and FY27 may be revised slightly higher in response to rising costs and the recent 
rebound in crude oil prices. Based on its analysis, the Bank has concluded that 
companies are passing on higher costs more quickly than before, and we expect it 
to reaffirm that the risks to its inflation outlook are “skewed to the upside.” It is also 
likely to reiterate that “there is a risk that [underlying inflation] will deviate upward to 
a level above the price stability target of 2%.”1

With regard to the risk balance, risks to economic activity are generally balanced.
Regarding the outlook for the CPI, risks are skewed to the upside. As for underlying
CPI inflation, there is a risk that it will deviate upward to a level above the price
stability target of 2 percent, given factors such as firms' behavior shifting more toward
raising wages and prices and medium- to long-term inflation expectations continuing to
rise. It is necessary to pay due attention to keep such a risk from materializing and
thereby exerting an adverse impact on the economy afterward.

As for the future conduct of monetary policy, given that underlying 
CPI inflation has been approaching 2 percent and financial 
conditions have been accommodative, the Bank will continue to 
raise the policy interest rate and adjust the degree of monetary 
accommodation, in response to developments in economic activity 
and prices as well as financial conditions. In this regard, it will 
consider the timing and pace of adjustment, while examining the 
likelihood of realizing the baseline scenario of the outlook for 
economic activity and prices and the risks to the outlook, including 
the impact of the situation in the Middle East, the expansion in 
AIrelated demand, and developments in foreign exchange rates. In 
particular, the perspective of stabilizing underlying CPI inflation at a 
level around 2 percent becomes important in order to keep the risk 
of underlying CPI inflation deviating upward to a level above the 
price stability target of 2 percent from materializing and thereby 
exerting an adverse impact on the economy afterward. With the 
price stability target of 2 percent, the Bank will conduct monetary 
policy as appropriate from the perspective of sustainable and 
stable achievement of the target.

The Bank’s monetary policy guidance is also likely to undergo some revisions. 
Table 2 shows the wording adopted at the September meeting, when the policy 
rate was increased. We think the phrase “given that underlying CPI inflation has 
been approaching 2%” may be replaced with something along the lines of “given 
that underlying CPI inflation is at a level generally consistent with the 2% inflation 
target.” We do not anticipate any other notable changes and expect the Bank to 
reiterate its view that “financial conditions have been accommodative.” The 
September Tankan showed little change in the DIs for corporate financial positions 
and bank lending attitudes (Graphs 2 and 3) while the corporate loan demand DI in 
the October edition of the Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey on Bank Lending 
Practices at Large Japanese Banks rose from the previous survey, indicating 
stronger demand for funds. At least in bank lending, there is little evidence that rate 
hikes have had a significant impact. At his September 18 press conference, 
Governor Ueda explained that he assesses financial conditions from three broad 
perspectives: 1) interest rates, 2) bank lending, and 3) asset markets. He said that 
“interest rates, particularly in the long- and super-long-term sectors, have become 
considerably more restrictive. The other two channels remain somewhat 
accommodative, but we intend to keep a close eye on future developments” 
(translation by MUMSS). 
At the post-meeting press conference, the governor is likely to explain the contents 
of the Outlook Report as outlined above while refraining from specifying the timing 
of the next rate hike, saying that the decision will depend on economic activity, 
prices, financial conditions, and the Bank’s assessment of risks. Last week the 
markets moved into “risk-off” mode, with equities falling, the yen strengthening, and 
bonds rallying. If financial markets are still risk-averse at the time of the press 
briefing, he may say that market sentiment will also be taken into account when 
deciding the appropriate pace of future rate hikes.

We continue to expect the BoJ to raise the policy rate to 1.50% at the December 
2026 meeting and to 1.75% at the April 2027 meeting, thus completing its 
adjustments to “the degree of monetary accommodation.” Maintaining a three-
month interval between tightening moves would imply the hike at the December 
17-18 meeting would be followed by another at the March 17-18 meeting next year. 
But given the increased sensitivity of deposit and mortgage rates to rate hikes, we 
think the Bank would probably prefer to avoid raising rates immediately before the 
fiscal year-end. As such, the April Outlook Report meeting, at which the BoJ will

The upside risk scenario for the terminal rate relative to our forecast is one in which 
demand and supply shocks combine to lift underlying inflation, prompting the BoJ 
to continue raising rates even after real rates turn positive. In the downside risk 
scenario for the terminal rate, where the Bank stops tightening at 1.25% or 1.50% 
or pauses its rate hikes, prolonged instability in global financial markets increases 
the downside risks to economic activity. In this regard, we intend to keep a close 
eye on global developments in equities and exchange rates.
