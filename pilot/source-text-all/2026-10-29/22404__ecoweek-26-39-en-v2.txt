EDITORIAL
WHY CHINA RESISTS REBALANCING ITS GROWTH MODEL?
China has never exported as much as it does now, and its trade surplus has never been higher. At the same time, its 
economic growth is slowing, held back by weak domestic demand. The imbalances in China’s growth model, and the ex-
ternal imbalances they give rise to, are therefore continuing to worsen. With Europe taking a tougher line and the United 
States, with which China shares nothing more than ‘strategic stability’, the standoff looks set to continue. However, the 
European Union and China would have a great deal to lose from a lasting deterioration in their relationship. It is in the 
interests of both parties to seek to restore the balance. To this end, it is important to understand Beijing’s objectives and 
the constraints facing China. Why does the country resist adjusting its growth model? Whilst exports are a vital asset for 
sustaining economic activity and asserting China’s technological and industrial power, this strategy also comes at a cost.
IMBALANCES ARE WORSENING
A K-shaped economy. China’s economic growth slowed to 4.6% year-
on-year (y/y) over the first three quarters of 2026, from 5.0% in 2025. 
More worryingly, the gap is widening between weak domestic demand 
and the very strong performance of exports, driven by artificial intelli-
gence equipment and green technologies. Furthermore, the dynamism 
of export-oriented industries has not, so far, had any positive knock-on 
effects on other sectors or on households.
Domestic demand is struggling. The property crisis, now in its sixth 
year, and a sluggish labour market are weighing on household confi-
dence and consumption. Since the start of 2026, households have been 
prioritising debt reduction (bank loans to households have fallen by 
1% y/y), house sales have continued to fall, and retail sales have been 
stagnating (Chart 1). On the investment front, the decline is becoming 
more pronounced and widespread: it is affecting not only the property 
sector but also infrastructure and many manufacturing sectors (with 
the exception of the textile industry, transport and communications, 
and electronic and electrical equipment).
Exports remain very buoyant. Exports continue to be the main driver of 
growth (their net contribution is expected to account for around a third 
of real GDP growth in 2026, as it did in 2024–2025). Against a backdrop 
of rebound in global trade, Chinese companies maintained their market 
share at 14.6% of total exports (measured in US dollars) in the first 
half of 2026, as they had in 2025. Growth in goods exports accelerated 
(21.9% y/y in USD over the first nine months, compared with 5.2% in 
2025), driven by both volumes and prices. These have rebounded after 
three years of decline, driven by rising costs of raw materials and the 
prices of goods required for AI, but they have increased at a slower rate 
than the global price average. Exports from high-tech sectors (batte-
ries, electric vehicles, robots and AI equipment) are posting the highest 
growth rates (+55.8% y/y for all AI-related goods over the first nine 
months of 2026).
Record external surpluses. Imports, which had been stagnating since 
2022, have been recovering since the start of the year (up 27.2% y/y 
over the first nine months), also driven by both volumes and prices. 
The countries benefiting from this rebound in the Chinese market are 
commodity exporters and Asian producers integrated into AI value 
chains (South Korea in particular). Otherwise, China has significantly 
reduced its reliance on imports over the past few years. Overall, the 
trade surplus has grown at a slower rate in 2026 than in previous years. 
Nevertheless, it has still reached a record high: USD1,249 billion over 
the twelve months to September 2026, which is three times its 2019 
level (see Chart 2).

Meanwhile, the current account surplus is expected to approach a re-
cord level of USD 800 billion in 2026, representing nearly 4% of China’s 
GDP (a ratio already seen in the late 2000s). As a proportion of global 
GDP, this is unprecedented (at 0.6%).
Imbalances set to persist. The imbalances inherent in the growth mo-
del are likely to persist, fuelled notably by the strong price and non-
price competitiveness of Chinese goods. This competitiveness remains 
underpinned by:
i/ innovation efforts and the move up the value chain in high-tech sec-
tors and the low-carbon transition;
ii/ low production costs (labour, low-cost credit, land and energy, tax 
benefits1);
iii/ the yuan’s exchange rate. Its slow appreciation since mid-2025 has 
not offset the cumulative depreciation from 2022 to 2025 (nominal and 
real depreciation due to low inflation), and its real effective exchange 
rate (REER) remains 10% below its 2021 average. At the end of 2025, 
the IMF estimated that the REER was undervalued by between 12% 
and 20%.
Beijing’s economic strategy is exacerbating imbalances. The 15th Five-
Year Plan2, launched this year, prioritises innovation, high-tech (ex-
port-oriented) sectors and technological self-reliance above all else, 
whilst reforms to the social security system and private consumption 
remain on the back burner (see Chart 3). Furthermore, fiscal policy, 
which had provided moderate support for household spending for a 
time in 2025, was tightened in 2026 (until the recent announcements 
of new measures for the final quarter).
WHY BEIJING IS DIGGING ITS HEELS IN
Beijing is not giving ground on any key issues. Faced with trading 
partners calling on China to rebalance its economic model, reduce in-
dustrial subsidies and strengthen the yuan, Beijing responds that the 
trade surplus reflects the competitiveness of its goods, points to the 
United States’ insufficient savings, and uses critical materials as a bar-
gaining chip in negotiations.
The need for growth. Exports are a key driver of growth at a time when 
the Chinese economy is undergoing a period of transition. The country 
has moved away from its pre-2022 model, which was based on in-
vestment in property and traditional industries and financed by debt. 
Growth is now driven by ‘new productive forces’, and exports must pro-
vide producers with the market that domestic demand does not. Fur-
thermore, innovation and the roll-out of AI must stimulate productivity 
gains and offset the impact of the shrinking labour force on economic 
growth. It is essential to limit the slowdown of growth in order to sup-
port job creation and absorb the economy’s excess debt.
The strategic argument. Exports are a means of asserting China’s in-
dustrial and technological supremacy, weakening its competitors, and 
strengthening ‘national security’. They place China at the heart of va-
lue chains in the most strategic industries, such as AI and the low-car-
bon transition. Furthermore, the pursuit of self-sufficiency and import 
substitution — which exacerbate external imbalances — also help to 
build national sovereignty by reducing dependencies.
A political and fiscal choice. Expanding the social safety net is seen as 
a solution for providing long-term support for household consumption3 
by reducing their savings rate (estimated at 35.7% of disposable income 
in 2025, which is more than double the average rate for the euro area).

However, this is not a priority for Beijing, certainly due to ‘ideologi-

A major reform of the healthcare or pension systems would place a 
strain on public finances in both the short and long term, particularly 
as the population ages. Whilst the central government remains in a 
sound financial position, local governments are already under strain 
following several years of falling land sales proceeds, high deficits and 
rising debt. The authorities do not have a large fiscal room for ma-
noeuvre and wish to preserve it in anticipation of future shocks.
LIMITATIONS AND POSSIBLE ADJUSTMENTS
In China, a society gripped by anxiety… Economic imbalances are ta-
king their toll on households. The emphasis placed on manufacturing 
rather than services is having a negative impact on employment and 
wages (the share of value added allocated to employees’ pay is esti-
mated at 50% in the services sector and 41% in manufacturing). The 
downturn in the property market is eroding households’ wealth, and 
their savings are mainly held in low-yield bank accounts. Finally, social 
spending is rising slowly (by 5.2% a year since 2020, compared with 
9.7% over the previous five years).
… and enterprises under pressure. The low prices of Chinese goods, 
both for export and on the domestic market — resulting from excess 
production capacity and ‘involution’ — are eroding corporate profit 
margins. Total profits for industrial enterprises have recovered this 
year but they are highly concentrated, particularly in AI-related sec-
tors, while the proportion of industrial companies reporting losses has 
continued to rise (30%, compared with 26% in 2022). These vulnerabili-
ties are having increasing contagion effects on the rest of the economy, 
notably through worsening payment delays and rising pressure on the 
profitability of financial institutions.
Abroad, trading partners are becoming increasingly intolerant. This 
is particularly the case of the United States and the European Union4,

2027 when the current truce (which has mainly led to a reduction in 
tariffs following the peak in H1 2025) comes to an end. Against a back-

Europe, for its part, is reinforcing its strategic approach and expanding 
its toolkit of measures, moving towards more targeted restrictions and 
stricter market access conditions, especially in case of serious market 
distortions). Rather than pursuing a strategy of decoupling, the EU is 
seeking to reduce risks and provide greater protection for critical sec-
tors (including the automotive and chemical industries) without clo-

This strategy will also lead to tensions. With imbalances becoming 
increasingly unsustainable, what course of action would make it pos-
sible to avoid a sudden adjustment precipitated by episodes of crises?
A ‘concerted’ adjustment is difficult to envisage. A negotiated adjust-
ment, along the lines of the 1985 Plaza agreements, seems unlikely in 
the current international and military context. Furthermore, China and 
its trading partners largely disagree on the diagnosis and the causes of 
the imbalances. Moreover, a simple revaluation of the yuan would, on 
its own, have only a limited effect on China’s competitiveness.
A gradual adjustment may be possible… in the long term. However, the 
reduction of imbalances will need to come both from partner countries 
(through increased savings) and from China. In the short term, China 
can ease trade tensions by investing more abroad, which is all the 
easier given that it has huge current account surpluses. This would 
enable it to maintain its access to foreign markets and support the 
production capacity of its trading partners. More structural changes 
(a more flexible exchange rate regime, less fiscal support for indus-
tries and more support for households) will only be feasible once Bei-
jing considers that the costs of its export-led model will outweigh the 
strategic gains. A key challenge for trading partners will be to work out 
how to shift this cost-benefit balance.

ADVANCED ECONOMIES
In brief. The week was dominated by a continued global selloff in tech 
stocks, leading to safe haven flows into US Treasuries, Japanese Go-
vernment bonds and German bunds. As a result, their yields fell des-
pite energy prices surging to or past their Spring highs. Economic data 
generally showed continued resilience. French bonds were under rising 
pressure, but Moody's unexpected decision to maintain its France ra-
ting unchanged could, at the margin, help stabilise the spread. The Fed, 
ECB and BOJ should be on hold next week while signaling further rate 
action is likely.
EUROPEAN UNION
A win for securitization. The Council and the European Parliament have 
reached a provisional political agreement on measures to revive and 
simplify the EU’s securitization market. The updated framework will 
reduce banks’ capital requirements for the safest and most well-struc-
tured transactions, notably for a newly introduced “resilient” category 
of securitization. As a key component of the “One Europe, One Market” 
initiative and the EU’s Savings and Investment Union agenda, this aims 
to channel increased lending to households and businesses. The rele-
vant legal texts must now be finalised.
EUROZONE
Expansion continues. The flash PMIs weakened in October, mainly on 
the back of a decline in France. The EZ composite index dipped to 51.9 
(-1.2 m/m), dragged down by services (51.7, -1.3 m/m), although ma-
nufacturing also dipped (52.4, -0.5 m/m). Both remain in expansion 
territory, and manufacturing new export orders rose to their highest 
level since early 2022. Price signals were also rather reassuring, with 
the services output price index easing to 54.9 (-1.7 m/m). Households 
are more cautious: consumer confidence fell for a second consecutive 
month to a five-month low (-17.9). Households’ inflation expectations 
edged up at short horizons (+0.1 pp to 3.6% at one year and 3.1% at 
three years), but long-term expectations remained anchored, with the 
five-year median stable at 2.5%.
France
Rising market stress despite decent economic news; a reprieve from 
Moody’s. The spread on French 10-year bond relative to Germany’s 
peaked to 174 bps on Friday, the highest since 2011 and only 16 bps 
short of its all-time high, as markets price in anticipation of worst-case 
scenarios regarding the country’s ability to repair its public finances. 
The 10Y yield briefly exceeded 5.1% before falling back to 5% (above 
the US equivalent). Nonetheless, services output was stable in August, 
following a 0.6% m/m increase in July, a much higher pace than in Q2. 
The rebound is driven by the catering sector, the digital sector, business 
services, and real estate. The INSEE business climate improved from 
96 to 97 (long-term average = 100) in October, but the survey was 
conducted before the riots. The main improvement was in manufactu-
ring, which rose from 101 to 105, driven by increased new orders in the 
aeronautics sector, and to a lesser extent by the services sector (from 
98 to 99). The PMI survey (conducted partially after the riots) was 
less positive: the composite index fell to 48.4 (-2.7 m/m), with a sharp 
decline in services (48.4; -2.8 m/m) and a slightly smaller decline in 
manufacturing (49.3; -1.3 m/m). Moody's unexpectedly announced af-
ter the market close on Friday that it was maintaining France’s ra-
ting at Aa3 on account of France’s diversified economic structure and 
sound public institutions, but maintained its negative outlook. This is 
on par with the UK and one notch above S&P and Fitch. Moody’s made 
clear keeping this rating is conditional on a budget being adopted that 
reduces the fiscal deficit, along with an easing of social and market 
stress going forward. Saturday’s demonstrations across the country 
saw strong attendance but only limited violent incidents.
Germany
Prices continue to rise, but confidence surveys remain on an upward 
trend. Producer prices rose 6.7% y/y in September (+2.1pp m/m; the 
highest level in 3.5 years) due to higher prices for energy and inter-
mediate goods, primarily metals. The ZEW Economic Sentiment Index 
improved beyond consensus forecasts in October and returned to its 
highest level since May 2023 (-35.9; +11.2 m/m), albeit the Economic 
Expectations Index fell to its lowest level since July (28.1; -6.6 m/m). 
The recovery in economic activity continues, although a slowdown is 
evident: the flash Composite PMI slowed (52.8; -1 m/m; the lowest in 
2 months) due to weaker growth in the services sector (51.7; -1.1 m/m; 
the lowest in 2 months) and in manufacturing output (55.1; -0.9 m/m; 
the lowest in 3 months). Nevertheless, in both sectors, hiring is rising 
slightly, supported by growth in export sales and the spillover effects of 
investment plans. Despite persistently high input prices, selling prices 
are slowing (reaching their lowest level since February in the manu-
facturing sector).
UNITED KINGDOM
Inflation, wage growth and activity accelerate. CPI inflation overshot 
consensus on energy prices, printing at 0.4% m/m and 3.5% y/y in Sep-
tember (consensus: 3.4%, prior: 3.1%), its highest in 11 months. Core 
CPI came in at 2.8% y/y (up 0.2 pp), in line with consensus. Pressures 
remained persistent according to the PPI at 8.3% y/y (prior: 6.7%). La-
bour market and activity data also surprised positively. Payroll growth 
reached 102k in August (3m/3m), bringing the unemployment rate to 
4.7% (down 0.2 pp), its lowest since July 2025. October PMI surveys 
beat expectations in both manufacturing (52.8, consensus: 51.8, prior: 
51.9) and services (53.1, consensus: 51.8, prior: 52.1). The only negative 
news came from headline retail sales, falling 0.4% m/m in September, 
with the core gauge (excluding petrol) deteriorating by -0.7% m/m.
UNITED STATES
Construction struggling, GENIUS Act implemented. Housing starts 
dropped -5.2% m/m in September (-7.4% y/y). This third consecutive 
monthly decline dragged the Q3 average to its lowest since Q2 2020. 
Building permits also came in negative, at -0.2% m/m (-3.0% y/y), while 
the NAHB homebuilder sentiment index plunged to its weakest since 
2012. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate eased to 7.32% (still 
134bp above pre-war level), as the ongoing AI selloff pushed down 
US treasury yields. The GENIUS Act final implementation rule from the 
Office of the Comptroller of the Currency’s (OCC) leaves the door open 
for stablecoin customers to receive yield or rewards. Issuers remain 
prohibited from paying holders any form of interest or yield solely 
in connection with the holding, use, or retention of payment stable-
coins. This represents a shift from the OCC’s February proposal and 
has renewed concerns within the banking industry about deposit flight. 
The rule will take effect on January 18, 2027.
JAPAN
Activity and inflation keep rising. Composite PMI was unchanged in 
September at 52.3, solidly in expansion territory, as an improvement 
in services activity offset slower output growth in manufacturing. The 
Manufacturing PMI disappointed at 53.3 (-0.8 m/m; an 8-month low).

EMERGING ECONOMIES
ASIA
China
New monetary policy tool and export controls. The central bank’s Go-
vernor Pan Gongsheng unveiled a plan to switch (“when appropriate”) 
its key policy rate from the 7-day reverse repo rate (at 1.4% since May 
2025) to the overnight reverse repo rate, in order to reflect develop-
ments in the interbank market. New export controls: The government 
plans to add 40 key technologies to the list of technologies prohibited 
or restricted for export, including in the AI (for ex. LLMs, computing 
clusters), biotechnology and photovoltaic sectors.
Indonesia
BI kept policy rate unchanged at 5.75%, raised its deposit facility rate 
by 25bp to 5% and announced a phased cut in reserve requirements 
from 9% to 7% by December. This combination of limited FX support 
and measures to support credit growth sends mixed signals amidst 
pressures on the rupiah following MSCI’s downgrade warning and un-
dermines the central bank’s credibility. The rupiah has lost 1% against 
the USD since mid-October, with little contagion effect on other Asian 
currencies.
South Korea
The Bank of Korea (BoK) kept its policy rate unchanged at 3.0%, but 
the tightening cycle may not be over. CPI inflation was 2.9% y/y in Sep-
tember, above the central bank’s target of 2%, while high oil prices, 
rapid growth in Seoul housing prices and wage pressures (though 
concentrated in the electronics sector) raise the risk of lasting infla-
tion pressure. As economic activity remains robust, supported by strong 
exports and recovering investment, the BoK has scope to raise rates 
further.
EMEA
Hungary
Monetary status quo and 2027 budget in consolidation mode. The 
Central Bank kept its policy rate unchanged at 5.5%, as expected. A cau-
tious stance will likely prevail in the short term. Inflation has been 
picking up in recent months, albeit mildly, and will continue to move 
upward as the government recently removed the margin cap on fuel. 
Separately, the Budget for 2027 and the medium-term fiscal plan show 
Hungary’s goal to join the Eurozone; the government expects to reduce 
the fiscal deficit to 4.9% of GDP in 2027 (from 7% expected in 2026) and 
towards 2.8% of GDP by 2030.
Türkiye
Cautious monetary loosening. The Central Bank lowered its policy rate 
by 100 bp to 36% at its October Monetary Policy Committee meeting, 
in line with market expectations. The overnight lending and borrowing 
rates were also reduced by 100 bp, to 39% and 34.5%, respectively. 
The improvement in underlying inflation has yet to translate into a 
sustained period of low inflation, justifying the CBRT’s preference for a 
measured pace of monetary easing.
LATIN AMERICA
Brazil
President Lula da Silva was re-elected on Sunday for a fourth term 
(with 51.8% of the vote), defeating far right-wing candidate Flavio Bol-
sonaro (48.2%). Lula will enter this new term facing significant econo-
mic and political challenges. Public debt has risen to more than 82% of 
GDP, while high borrowing costs and rigid mandatory spending leave 
limited room to reconcile fiscal consolidation with social spending and 
investment. Restoring confidence in the fiscal outlook will be a key 
challenge in the short term.
COMMODITIES
Pressure rising. Amid growing rumours of imminent re-escalation of 
military operations in the Gulf, energy prices rose sharply this week, 
with brent touching $117/b – near its April peak, and European gas 
prices (TTF) rising to 97 €/MWh on Monday, their highest level since 
Dec 2022. The Asian gas benchmark (JKM) is following the same trend 
(4.6% w/w to 31.4 $/mbtu, equivalent to around 96 €/MWh). Diesel 
crack spreads are slightly decreasing in Europe (-2.8% w/w on Monday) 
and in the US (-4% w/w on Monday). The first Russian diesel tanker 
intended to the US market left Russia this Friday and should supply the 
US market in 2 weeks. US diesel average retail prices remain oriented 
to the upside (0.7% w/w at 6.32 $/gallon on Monday). US commercial 
crude inventories fell by 3.1 mb in the week ending 16 October, signi-
ficantly exceeding market expectations for a 340,000-barrel decline.
