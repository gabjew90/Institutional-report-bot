CIO View: Crude oil
Giovanni Staunovo, Strategist, UBS Switzerland AG
• We expect Brent crude prices to stay elevated, near USD 110/bbl at 
the end of the year, as Middle Eastern supply remains volatile and 
geopolitical uncertainty persists.
• Global inventories have fallen sharply, with pledged strategic oil 
reserves releases largely completed. An undersupplied market could 
push commercial stocks lower and should sustain oil prices. Record 
freight costs, constrained tanker capacity, and recovering Chinese 
imports are other factors likely to keep oil prices high.
• Uncertainty around our forecasts remains elevated, reflecting both 
the unpredictable nature of the Middle East conflict, and the pace of 
recovery in Gulf production and global oil demand.
We expect oil prices to remain elevated over the coming months as Middle 
Eastern flows, while improving, have not yet fully normalized. This has led 
us to revise our forecasts upward. We now expect Brent to trade at USD 
110/bbl by year-end, up from USD 100/bbl previously. We raise our March 
2027 forecast to USD 95/bbl from USD 90/bbl, our June 2027 forecast to 
USD 90/bbl from USD 85/bbl, and our September 2027 forecast to USD 85/
bbl from USD 80/bbl. We widen the WTI discount to Brent to USD 10/bbl for 
December, while continuing to assume an unchanged discount of USD 4/
bbl for 2027. Owing to the downward-sloping futures curve, our December 
Brent forecast remains above the corresponding market prices, while our 
forecasts for next year are in line or slightly below prevailing market prices. 
Hence, we retain a moderately constructive near-term outlook for crude oil. 
That said, uncertainty around our forecasts remains elevated, reflecting the 
unpredictable nature of the Middle East conflict, and the pace of recovery 
in Gulf production and global oil demand.
What is driving the changes to our forecasts? Middle Eastern crude 
loadings have recovered substantially from the severe disruption earlier 
this year. Combined crude flows through the Strait of Hormuz and bypass 
pipelines are estimated at more than 17.3 million barrels per day (mbpd) 
in September and close to 18.8mbpd in the first three weeks of October, 
compared with an average of 19.9mbpd in January-February, before the 
conflict, according to Petro-Logistics.
However, this recovery has coincided with a sharp deterioration in maritime 
security. At least 30 attacks on ships in and around the Strait were reported 
in October, the highest monthly number on record. Higher oil flows have 
not translated into a normalization of the market: Vessels remain exposed 
to attacks, insurance and freight costs have risen sharply, and increased 
ship-to-ship transfers required to cross the Strait of Hormuz are absorbing

At the same time, global visible oil inventories have fallen since the start 
of the conflict in the Middle East. In its latest oil market report, the IEA 
estimates that global visible oil inventories stood at 7.8 billion barrels at the 
end of September, down by slightly more than 500mb since February. Most 
of the decline has come from releases from OECD strategic oil inventories, 
which fell by 225mb over that period. These releases have limited the price 
response so far, as OECD commercial oil inventories have moved broadly 
sideways this year, down by about 38 million barrels between February and 
September. Historically, OECD commercial oil inventories have been a key 
driver of price formation. With most strategic oil inventory releases now 
behind us, a still-undersupplied oil market should lead to a sharper decline 
in OECD commercial oil inventories over the coming months if conditions 
on the ground do not improve, keeping prices elevated. In addition, recent 
media reports suggest that the risk of renewed tension between the US 
and Iran is rising again, fueling upside risks to oil prices in the near term, in 
our view. With global visible oil inventories at multi-year lows and likely to 
take time to rebuild once the conflict is resolved, oil prices may also remain 
higher for longer as inventories first need to be replenished.
In addition, high shipping costs provide a second source of support for 
Brent prices and are widening the spread between Brent and WTI. Tanker 
freight rates have reached record levels, with Middle East to China very 
large crude carrier rates (VLCCs carry about 2 million barrels of oil when 
fully loaded) at around USD 30-40/bbl, compared with USD 1-3/bbl over 
the previous 10 years. This increase reflects longer voyages, ship-to-ship 
transfers, poor vessel positioning, and reduced availability of usable tanker 
capacity. These conditions are also supporting Brent prices by improving 
the competitiveness of shorter-haul North Sea barrels for European buyers.
Due to these high freight rates, oil market balances are not only influenced 
by the number of barrels produced, but also by the reliability and cost 
of delivering those barrels to consumers. Freight rates currently play an 
important role in shaping oil prices: The market needs not only enough 
barrels, but also sufficient economically available shipping capacity to 
move those barrels to where they are needed. Freight rates determine the 
delivered cost of crude and when shipping capacity is constrained, it can 
materially alter regional trade flows, refinery economics, and oil prices. 
Unsurprisingly, the OPEC+ alliance highlighted this issue in a press release, 
saying that its member states would engage with governments, industry 
stakeholders, and international organizations to improve global shipping 
conditions.
A final factor to watch is Chinese crude imports. They have recovered 
materially from the exceptional lows reached earlier this year, removing 
an important shock absorber for the global oil market. Imports fell 
from 12.58mbpd in February to a low of 7.15mbpd in June, as China 
relied more heavily on accumulated inventories, before recovering in 
subsequent months; the latest data point was 9.06mbpd in September. 
Based on preliminary seaborne import data, October imports could return 
to 10mbpd. This earlier compression in imports was an important factor in 
limiting the price response earlier this year. However, that inventory cushion
