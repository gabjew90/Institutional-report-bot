What does the current data tell us about 
the U.S. economy?
For the most part, the economy is humming along. 
Since our last conversation, the Bureau of Economic 
Analysis increased its calculation of second-quarter 
real GDP expansion from 1.5% to 2.2%. The BEA 
estimates second-quarter nominal GDP increased 
8.5%, a monster number and worth noting even if 
economists generally don’t focus on nominal 
expansion. The resilience continues; the Federal 
Reserve Bank of Atlanta’s real GDPNow indicator 
for third-quarter growth has been 3.6% recently. 
Meanwhile, inflation continues above the Fed’s 
target with energy and gas increasing at double- 
digit rates in August.   
Other indicators counterbalance the growth outlook. 
The economy added 29,000 jobs in September, 
when analysts had expected 84,000, and the 
unemployment rate ticked up slightly to 4.2%. The 
Bureau of Labor Statistics also revised job gains 
lower by a combined 60,000 for July and August. 
The market rallied the day of the BLS release on 
optimism there was less pressure on the Fed to 
raise interest rates.
Wages rose 3% on an annual basis, according to the 
BLS, lower than the rate of inflation. Yet Bank of 
America data on credit card activity indicates 
consumers increased spending more than 6% for 
the four weeks ending September 26 versus a year 
ago, which suggests consumers are dipping into 
savings. Spending on gas increased nearly 23% over 
the period tracked by BofA versus a year earlier, 
while airline spending increased 12.8%. Grocery 
spending declined slightly, which could reflect less 
eating and drinking due to the adoption of GLP-1 
medications. 
Once again, I circle back to the great AI build. The 
most powerful companies in the country are 
engaged in an AI race, building to win and not 
worrying about costs. Interestingly, if the energy 
dynamics were stable, I think asset pricing would be 
higher, and yet, if not for the AI build, I think the 
economy would likely be much slower, with asset 
prices lower. Bottom line: the AI build is leading to 
this major tradeoff and unique economic balance, 
with the average worker getting strained.

Let’s switch to our special topic: 
bond market turbulence. We started 
discussing the rise in U.S. yields in 
September. Could you broaden the 
discussion to other countries?
Since 2021, yields on 10-year government bonds are 
up about 300 to 450 basis points in the U.S., U.K., 
France, Germany, and Japan. The biggest surprise 
is in Japanese rates, with the 10-year up nearly 300 
bps since 2021, including 100 bps year to date, after 
decades of being below 1%. Inflation has been a 
global headwind for bonds, driving yields higher 
and pressuring bond values across the globe.
China is the exception with the 10-year yield down 
about 130 bps over the past five years, as their 
economy faces disinflationary forces.

What are commodity prices telling us 
about inflation pressures?
The good news is diesel and crude oil curves are in 
backwardation out to early 2028, signaling that 
commodity investors expect prices to decline over 
the next year or two. The bad news is that across 
energy and food commodity curves investors still 
expect prices in early 2028 to be higher than they 
had forecast a few months ago for that same period, 
even if energy prices trend down from today’s level. 
Essentially, the entire futures curves have shifted 
upward. I believe those expectations reflect 
everything we have discussed, including the 
disruption of seaborne traffic through the Strait of 
Hormuz, the Russia-Ukraine war, and the AI build.

Why does the price of diesel have such a 
meaningful impact on inflation?
I think diesel prices are an underestimated but 
fundamental driver of inflation in this cycle. While 
consumers care about the price at the pump, which 
they are confronted with, almost everything else 
gets transported using diesel, in addition to farming 
and heating needs. The average premium of a barrel 
of diesel to crude oil over the past five years is 
about $31, as diesel provides more energy per 
gallon; at the end of August, that premium hit $88, 
nearly triple. The main driver of that premium has 
been the disruption of the strait. While crude oil 
shipping through the strait is close to pre-conflict 
capacity, refined products such as diesel are closer 
to 20%. In addition, Ukraine has attacked Russian oil 
refineries, and Russia’s government has severely 
limited diesel exports. Meanwhile, U.S. exports have 
increased to meet global demand, with U.S. 
refineries running near capacity. 
At this point, even if the strait were to fully reopen, 
global diesel inventories would take time to replenish, 
assuming it could despite Russia’s constraints, and 
the winter season is coming, which should increase 
heating demand.

Speaking of the Fed, Kevin Warsh has 
used his short tenure thus far as chair to 
emphasize price stability. What does the 
path of interest rates look like beyond 
this year?
The outlook is highly uncertain, partly because 
Warsh is curtailing information over time. Also, once 
the conflict with Iran began, everything changed. 
The market has discounted the energy market 
disruption, with hopes for a resolution rising and 
then falling; meanwhile the geopolitical landscape 
has evolved and the hyperscalers are steamrolling 
the AI build. Honestly, I don’t have a great answer, 
beyond the likelihood of two to three more rate 
hikes before the Fed is done.
How has the trend in inflation affected 
inflation expectations?
As we were coming out of the COVID pandemic, 
inflation expectations were in the 4% to 5% range, 
then trended down to 2.6% in early 2026, prior to 
the U.S. attack on Iran. Inflation expectations 
peaked within a few months of the attack to 6.6% 
and have since trended back to the mid-4% range. 
One could focus on the fact that expectations have 
come down from the peak, but keep in mind they 
remain well above the Fed’s 2% target for inflation, 
and those expectations remain above target looking 
out into 2027. The Fed has already raised rates once. 
Depending on the trend in jobs and wages, we 
could see one or two more hikes this year.

Year-to-date returns of U.S. Treasuries and 
investment-grade corporate bonds have turned 
negative, as they are largely fixed-rate instruments 
that tend to decline in value when yields rise. 
However, high-yield bonds and bank loans, which 
are largely influenced by spread volatility, have fared 
better, with bank loans returning more than 3% year 
to date. Rates on bank loans generally are 
adjustable, rising and falling with market trends, and 
thus bank loans can outperform in a rising rate 
environment. Indeed, bank loans are outperforming 
the Agg by more than 600 basis points year to date. 
We have long argued for a diversified and balanced 
fixed income portfolio to navigate market cycles, 
and this is a classic example.  Currently, I suggest 
staying calm as this volatility persists. Keep a bank 
loan allocation and short-term allocation if you have 
them. When the market eventually settles, there 
may be an attractive opportunity to extend 
duration. The MOVE Index settling could signal a 
time to consider duration. 
Final thought: yields on IG corporate bonds are in 
the 6% range and for leveraged finance in the 8% 
range. Those are attractive levels.

Definitions:
A 10-year Treasury note is a debt obligation issued by the United States government with a 10-year maturity period.
The Atlanta Fed GDPNow provides a running estimate of real GDP growth for the current quarter using available 
economic data.
Bank loans (or floating-rate loans) are financial instruments that pay a variable or floating interest rate. A floating 
rate fund invests in bonds and debt instruments whose interest payments fluctuate with an underlying interest-rate 
level.
The Bloomberg Commodity Index (BCOM) is made up of 24 exchange-traded futures on physical commodities, 
representing 22 commodities which are weighted to account for economic significance and market liquidity.
The Bloomberg US Aggregate Bond Index (Agg) is composed of investment-grade U.S. government bonds, 
investment-grade corporate bonds, mortgage pass-through securities, and asset-backed securities, and is commonly 
used to track the performance of U.S. investment-grade bonds.
The Bloomberg US Corporate 1-3 Year Bond Index measures the investment grade, fixed-rate, taxable corporate 
bond market with 1-3 year maturities.
The Bloomberg US Corporate Bond Index measures the investment grade, fixed-rate, taxable corporate bond 
market. It includes USD denominated securities publicly issued by US and non-US industrial, utility and financial 
issuers.
The Bloomberg US Corporate High-Yield Index measures the USD-denominated, high-yield, fixed-rate corporate 
bond market.
The Bloomberg US Credit Index measures the investment grade, US dollar-denominated, fixed-rate, taxable 
corporate and government related bond markets. It is composed of the US Corporate Index and a non-corporate 
component that includes foreign agencies, sovereigns, supranationals and local authorities.
The Bloomberg US Treasury Index includes public obligations of the U.S. Treasury.
A bond is a fixed-income instrument and investment product where individuals lend money to a government or 
company at a certain interest rate for an amount of time. The entity repays individuals with interest in addition to the 
original face value of the bond.
The Consumer Confidence Index (CCI) is a survey, administered by The Conference Board, that measures how 
optimistic or pessimistic consumers are regarding their expected financial situation. The CCI is based on the premise 
that if consumers are optimistic, they will spend more and stimulate the economy but if they are pessimistic then 
their spending patterns could lead to a recession.
Coupon refers to the interest payment that a bond issuer promises to pay to a bondholder.
Fixed income refers to assets and securities that pay a set level of income to investors, typically in the form of fixed 
interest or dividends.
Investment grade refers to the quality of a company's credit. To be considered an investment grade issue, the 
company must be rated at 'BBB' or higher by Standard and Poor's or Moody's.
The Morningstar LSTA US Leveraged Loan Index is a market-value weighted index designed to measure the 
performance of the US leveraged loan market.
The Russell 1000 Growth Index measures the performance of the large-cap growth segment of the U.S. equity 
universe. It includes those Russell 1000 companies with higher price-to-value ratios and higher forecasted growth 
values.
The Russell 2000 Value Index measures the performance of the small-cap value segment of the U.S. equity universe. 
It includes those Russell 2000 companies with lower price-to-book ratios and lower expected growth values.
S&P 500 Index is a stock market index tracking the performance of 500 large companies listed on stock exchanges in 
the United States.
