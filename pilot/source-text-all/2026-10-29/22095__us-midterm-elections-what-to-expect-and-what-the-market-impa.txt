2026 midterms arrive under a tough backdrop for Republicans
3
• The incumbent President’s party typically struggles in midterm elections, with only two 
instances since WWII when it has gained seats
• 2026 is unlikely to offer anything different, as the backdrop looks like a difficult one for 
Republicans given widespread concerns over affordability and discontent around 
President Donald Trump’s handling of several key issues
• This is reflected in Trump’s overall approval rating, which has now dipped below 40% to 
put him more than 20ppt underwater when compared to his disapproval rating
• In addition to his overall approval rating, Trump’s approval on issues that used to be his 
bread and butter has deteriorated significantly as well, as he is further underwater with 
regards to the economy (-32.4ppt) and inflation (-43.8ppt), and is now underwater on 
immigration (-10.6ppt) as well
• This combines to make Democrats a strong favourite in the House and a favourite in the 
Senate as well, albeit to a more modest degree. We would handicap the odds as:
o Democratic sweep: 65%
o Democratic House/Republican Senate: 25%
o Republican sweep: 10%
o Republican House/Democratic Senate is a highly unlikely combination

House polling suggests Democratic control a near certainty
4
• This backdrop is most evident in the House, where Democrats look a near certainty to re-
take control
• Republicans currently hold a 220-215 edge (if the two vacancies are assigned to the party 
that previously held the seat), meaning that Democrats would need a net gain of at least 
three seats to take control
• Polling for the generic congressional ballot gives Democrats a clear edge, as they 
currently lead by a margin of more than 8ppt
• When compared to the 2024 cumulative House vote that Republicans won by 2.6ppt, this 
represents a swing of close to 11ppt towards Democrats over the past two years
• Admittedly, polling is not perfect, but the magnitude of the swing means that anything 
other than a Democratic victory would be a major surprise, with odds of around 90% that 
Democrats retake control
• One silver lining for Republicans is that redistricting efforts (eg gerrymandering) have 
limited the number of seats that are truly in play, which may now only be in the 20s
• That makes a wave-type election with gains of 40+ seats highly unlikely, though the net 
gain of three seats needed to re-take control is not too high a bar

Senate is a closer call with a modest lean towards Democrats
5
• The Senate can be a different animal with roughly one-third of seats up for re-election each two-year cycle, compared to all 435 House seats, meaning the 
shifting map can play a role
• In 2026, Republicans have to defend substantially more seats than Democrats, and given the difficult backdrop a number of seats in decently red states seem 
in play
• With Republicans holding a 53-47 edge currently, Democrats need a net gain of at least four seats to regain control, as a 50-50 tie would be broken by Vice 
President JD Vance
• In looking at the list of key races to watch below, there is a clear path toward that net gain of four seats, though this is not a guarantee, and we would place 
Democrats odds at around 65% in this chamber

Midterm elections likely to lead to legislative gridlock
6
• As long as Democrats win the House, the likely outcome will involve legislative gridlock, regardless of the Senate result. This comes down to the fact that 
even if the Democrats capture the Senate, they would still face obstacles in the form of the filibuster and the Presidential veto, which we do not think Trump 
would hesitate to use
• This means that Senate control is not a game changer in many ways, though could still have some impact in several ways. We note a few issues below:
o Judges and other appointments: while the President chooses nominees for judgeships and various other appointments, these must be confirmed by the Senate. Thus, if Democrats 
were to gain control of the Senate, they would be able to block Trump’s choices for key vacancies that could open up later in his second term. This would likely be the most significant 
impact of the Senate outcome
o Oversight and investigations: Democrats will most certainly open several investigations into Trump and/or members of his administration and winning the Senate would allow them to 
double down. The investigations themselves may not lead to any concrete outcomes, though at the very least they could be a thorn in the administration’s side that take up time it would 
otherwise prefer to devote to other priorities. We see a good chance that some of these investigations could lead to another impeachment, though we would see any impeachment as 
noise, if we are being frank, as there is little chance that Democrats would be able to reach the two-thirds majority in the Senate that would be needed for removal from office
o Legislation: as noted above, we expect that legislative gridlock will result regardless of the outcome in the Senate given the existence of the filibuster and Presidential veto. Admittedly, a 
veto can be overridden, but the two-thirds majority in both chambers necessary to do so would represent a high bar to achieve with Democrats likely to hold only narrow majorities even in 
the situation in which they sweep to control of both chambers of Congress
o Government shutdown: this is an issue that could go either way. In theory having one party control both chambers could make it easier to reach agreement on funding the government, 
but there is always a chance Trump could choose to veto legislation coming out of a Democratic congress if he thought the blame would fall on Democrats as the party controlling 
Congress. Overall, there is a decent chance that we could face another government shutdown at some point next year
o Tariffs and trade: we do not expect Congressional election outcomes to change much on this front, as Trump is relying on authority that Congress has previously delegated to the 
president in past legislation. The new Congress could in theory overturn past legislation, or implement some limitations on the President’s trade powers, but the veto again looms large
o Foreign policy: again, the President tends to have greater authority over foreign policy, so we would not expect any major shifts based on the election outcome

Midterm elections: rates, curve, term premium
Source: Bloomberg, Crédit Agricole CIB
8
Midterm elections are approaching and could have important implications for 
the fiscal outlook and Treasury market. Our base case is that Democrats 
regain control of the House. Under this scenario, legislative gridlock is likely 
regardless of which party controls the Senate.
A divided government would reduce the likelihood of major spending 
programmes and tax cuts, easing upward pressure on Treasury term premium 
and long-term interest rates. All else equal, this would be supportive of lower 
long-end yields and a flatter yield curve. Front-end rates, however, are 
primarily driven by monetary policy expectations. With energy prices remaining 
elevated and the Fed likely to deliver additional rate hikes, front-end yields 
such as the 2Y Treasury yield, are likely to remain high.
The federal budget deficit surged to USD1.99trn in fiscal year 2026 (ended 30 
September), driven by USD7.4trn in spending (up 6%) against USD5.4trn in 
revenue (up 3%). This represents a significant increase from the prior two 
years’ deficits of USD1.775trn (FY2025) and USD1.817trn (FY2024). At 
roughly 6% of GDP, the deficit is exceptionally large for a period without 
recession or major economic crisis.
The Congressional Budget Office projects the debt-to-GDP ratio will climb from 
100% currently to 120% within a decade. While economic growth could 
marginally improve the outlook, it cannot reverse the debt accumulation 
trajectory – achieving 6% real GDP growth with 5% interest rates would be 
required merely to stabilise debt levels.

House polling suggests Democratic control a near certainty
14
• The experience since 1976 suggests that the two possible 
outcomes of the midterms could have very different FX impact: 
• In cases of a Democratic Congress coupled with a GOP 
president, the USD weakened in the months after the vote. 
• The results reflect the impact of the 1985 Plaza Accord but also 
the USD’s weakness at the start of both George H W Bush’s 
presidency and George W Bush’s second term. 
• The two episodes also featured a slowing economy, Fed rate 
cuts and a Middle Eastern conflict.    
• During periods with a GOP President and Senate coupled with a 
Democrat House, the USD rallied across the board. 
• The last such episode was during Trump’s first presidential term, 
after the 2018 midterms. It coincided with a recovering US 
economy and a hawkish Fed. 
• Earlier episodes from the 1980s were less USD-positive, 
however, partly due to weak economy and Fed easing.
