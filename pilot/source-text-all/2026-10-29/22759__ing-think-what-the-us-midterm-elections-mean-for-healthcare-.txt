What the US midterm elections mean
for healthcare and pharma
Rising fuel prices and the war with Iran have significantly increased the
chances of a Democrat-controlled Congress. This could mean
greater pricing pressure on pharma and more healthcare affordability
measures. However, surprises do happen. We therefore assess
three possible scenarios and what they mean for healthcare and
pharma
Republicans and Democrats want more domestic manufacturing and lower drug prices, but there are
differences in how they get there
The US midterm elections take place on 3 November. All 435 seats in the House of
Representatives and 35 of the 100 Senate seats are up for election. With Trump’s approval
rating hitting a record low according to a Fox News poll, it is no wonder that the pollsters
currently suggest the Democrats lead by around 8 percentage points nationally. A Democratic
takeover of Congress therefore seems most likely, but a Democratic clean sweep is not certain.
We look at what the possible outcomes could mean for healthcare and pharma.

The key differences
For healthcare, US voters face a clear choice: Democrats want to expand Medicaid and
insurance coverage, as well as permanently extend Affordable Care Act (ACA) subsidies.
Republicans favour the opposite approach, with greater responsibility shifted to individual
states.
For pharma, on the other hand, the old political divide of "Democrats regulate, Republicans
leave industry alone" is largely gone. Both parties now see the pharma and biotech sector as
key to national security, and therefore want more domestic manufacturing. They also both
want lower drug prices. The difference is how they get there. Democrats lean toward
government negotiation under Medicare and incentivising domestic manufacturing through
other means than tariffs; Republicans lean toward most-favoured-nation (MFN) pricing and
trade pressure. In short, the policy risks differ slightly depending on the outcome, but the
objectives are largely bipartisan.

Scenario 1: Republican revival
A Republican victory will embolden President Trump to entrench his healthcare and industrial
policy legacy during his final two years in office. His administration would face fewer
congressional obstacles to implementing Medicaid restrictions, maintaining a more limited
federal role in insurance coverage and resisting restoration of enhanced ACA subsidies.
For consumers as well as healthcare providers and insurers, the main risk would be further
coverage erosion. Hospitals in Medicaid-heavy markets could see more uncompensated care.

For pharma, Republican control of Congress would give Trump greater scope to embed MFN
pricing and use tariff exemptions and company agreements to further secure US
manufacturing commitments. Pharmaceutical companies would gain greater clarity about the
direction of policy, but not relief from pricing or supply-chain pressure. In short, this would be
the scenario with the greatest continuity in pharma policy.
Scenario 2: Trump constrained
A Democratic House could obstruct legislation, control committees and launch investigations.
But bills restoring ACA subsidies or mitigating Medicaid cuts would face a Republican Senate
and, ultimately, Trump’s veto. Healthcare policy would therefore become more confrontational
but not necessarily very different in practice. Coverage losses and stricter Medicaid eligibility
rules would remain immediate issues, while Democrats would use hearings and must-pass
spending legislation to put pressure on the administration.
Pharma would face detailed scrutiny of MFN deals, tariffs, and the implementation of Medicare
negotiations. Yet neither party would control all the levers required for sweeping reform. This
could create room for narrower bipartisan measures, particularly around Pharmacy Benefit
Managers (PBM) and hospitals. Broader reform would probably stall, meaning that not much
would change for the US consumer.
Scenario 3: Democratic clean sweep
Control of both chambers would give Democrats their strongest platform to restore ACA
subsidies, soften Medicaid cuts and make healthcare affordability a central condition in budget
and defence negotiations. This would lower premiums and out-of-pocket costs for US
consumers, while helping more people retain or gain health insurance. Coverage expansion is
also likely to improve hospital finances and prescription volumes.
For pharma, Democrats would likely slow MFN momentum by demanding transparency
around the MFN deals as well as challenging their design and cost. It is also likely that they
would want to prioritise expanding or accelerating the Medicare drug-price negotiations
agreed under the IRA, alongside greater scrutiny of launch prices and PBMs.
Moreover, stronger congressional protection of National Institutes of Health (NIH) funding,
scientific institutions and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) review capacity would support
biotech and early-stage innovation. If Democrats truly make drug-price negotiations a priority,
then this scenario could lead to a mixed outcome: greater price pressure, but a stronger public
research base and a better-funded FDA.

Why a Democratic clean sweep would be good news for
European governments
For Europe, pressure on the pharma industry to localise in the US will remain under all three
scenarios. Democrats may criticise the means, but they also view pharmaceutical supply
chains as a national-security issue. Republican and Democratic policy objectives therefore
broadly align, while Trump retains substantial executive authority over trade.
The biggest difference is that a Democratic clean sweep would demand greater transparency
around MFN deals and increase price pressure through Medicare negotiations. That scrutiny
could raise the chances of European governments slow-walking their own price negotiations in
the expectation that MFN terms may be delayed or revised. By contrast, a Republican clean
sweep would reinforce policy continuity and further strengthen the pull for manufacturing
investment to move to the US.
Europe’s structural competitiveness issue therefore remains under all scenarios, but is most
pronounced if the Republicans win both the House and the Senate. Unless Europe improves
commercialisation, capital market depth and harmonises regulation, it risks losing both
production to the US and intellectual property/partnerships to China.
Beijing is now more likely to block licensing deals than
Washington
China will also keep facing bipartisan pressure. Both parties now view pharma and biotech as
key to national security interests and support greater domestic manufacturing and
reduced foreign dependencies. However, China’s dominance in active pharmaceutical
ingredients is unlikely to wane any time soon.

Furthermore, commercial demand for Chinese innovation will not disappear simply because
supply-chain policy becomes more restrictive. We still forecast Chinese outlicensing to increase
to more than $250bn this year, nearly double the deal value of 2025 despite geopolitical
tensions. What's more, licensing restrictions are now more likely to come from Beijing than
Washington. The sector has convinced US policymakers that these deals are needed to access
innovation, while China is considering restrictions on acquisitions of biopharma platforms
involving key technologies.
These deals will continue under any scenario, but especially if Republicans retain control of
Congress. If there is a Democratic clean sweep, however, Trump will tend to focus more on
foreign and trade policy, meaning China could end up in the crosshairs. This, in turn, could
mean greater scrutiny of licensing and slower growth – not a decline – in dealmaking in 2027.
