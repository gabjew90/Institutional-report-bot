The AI boom is fuelling growth, particu-
larly in the US and Asia. 
However, a significant slowdown is on 
the horizon for 2027. 
AI has an inflationary effect in the short 
run, while unemployment remains low 
for the time being.
Martin Weder and Marina Zech, Economic Research
GLOBAL
AI is dominating developments on financial markets
Rapid advances in the field of artificial intelligence (AI) 
are a major focus of attention for financial markets, the 
media, businesses and the general public. Whilst the big 
tech firms are driving the equity market to new highs, 
the economic impact of AI has so far been analysed far 
less extensively. We aim to bridge this gap and provide 
an assessment of future developments.
Exponential growth in the semiconductor sector
The ongoing AI boom is most clearly evident in the semi-
conductor sector. Global turnover now stands at just un-
der USD 160 billion per month (Chart 1). That is more 
than double the figure from a year ago. However, this 
astronomical growth is attributable not only to higher 
production but also to sharply rising prices. A closer look 
at the details reveals that growth is primarily driven by 
the integrated circuit sector and the memory segment. In 
contrast, annual growth for discrete semiconductors and 
sensors remains in the single-digit range. The industry is 
also dominated by a small number of countries and com-
panies. Asian emerging economies such as South Korea 
and Taiwan account for more than half of global turn-
over. Just over a third is attributable to the US, whilst the 
rest of the world is relatively insignificant.

Soon to be bigger than the pharmaceutical sector
Experts estimate that semiconductor revenue will rise to 
over USD 1.5 billion this year. Compared with the previ-
ous year, this represents growth of just under 90%. Next 
year, the market is expected to grow by a further 27% to 
USD 1.9 billion. For 2028 and 2029, a further slowdown 
in growth and an increase to USD 2.1 billion in sales is ex-
pected (Chart 2). This would see the semiconductor in-
dustry overtake the pharmaceutical sector and rapidly 
close the gap on the automotive industry.
Growing importance in global trade
The strong growth in the semiconductor industry is also 
reflected in foreign trade. According to the World Trade 
Organisation (WTO), trade in goods directly linked to the 
AI boom – such as semiconductors, servers, telecommuni-
cations equipment and related intermediate goods – in-
creased by over 20% last year. This accounted for almost 
half of the global growth in trade in goods and was the 
main reason why trade was able to grow at an above-av-

Limited impact at a global level
From a global perspective, however, the movement of 
goods cannot provide any growth impetus, as exports 
and imports cancel each other out. Consequently, do-
mestic investment in capital goods – such as the construc-
tion of data centres and expenditure on computer equip-
ment and software – remains a potential driver of 
growth. Below, we examine the economic significance of 
AI in the major regions in greater detail. A look at GDP 
growth provides an indication of which countries are 
benefiting particularly strongly from the AI boom and 
are therefore experiencing above-average growth (Chart 
3). Taiwan, for instance, is currently recording impressive 
growth rates that are well above its long-term potential. 
In Europe and Switzerland, by contrast, there is little sign 
of the AI boom.
 
US
AI is not the main driver of growth
The AI boom has triggered an impressive wave of invest-
ment in the US. US technology companies are engaged 
in an arms race for global leadership, both amongst 
themselves and with foreign competitors from China. Im-
pressive double-digit growth rates are currently being 
recorded, particularly in the areas of data centres, com-
puter equipment and software. Investment in data-pro-
cessing hardware has recently even surpassed that in 
housing construction. The strong momentum in the AI 
sector, however, masks the weakness in other areas of 
investment. At 5%, aggregate investment in computer 
equipment and software as a proportion of gross domes-
tic product (GDP) has reached a new high (Chart 4).

AI accounts for no more than a third of growth
However, AI-related investment cannot be strictly sepa-
rated from other investment in the statistics, which tends 
to lead to an overestimation of the growth effect. Fur-
thermore, a large proportion of the computer equip-
ment and semiconductors used in the US is manufac-
tured in Asia and has a negative impact on GDP growth 
via the import channel. According to estimates, on bal-
ance only 10% to 20% of expenditure on computer 
equipment contributes positively to value added. At the

same time, direct consumer spending on AI subscriptions 
is currently still limited. The same applies to international 
trade in computer services, which includes, amongst oth-
er things, foreign AI subscriptions and token purchases. 
Overall, depending on the methodology and the period 
under review, AI investment accounts for between a 
quarter and a third of economic growth in the US. With 
current GDP growth of around 2%, this corresponds to a 
contribution of 0.5 to 0.7 percentage points. However, as 
was the case during the internet and dot-com boom, 
growth in the current investment cycle is mainly driven 
by private consumption. Without the AI boom, however, 
GDP growth would undoubtedly be significantly lower, 
particularly as booming equity markets and the associat-
ed increases in wealth are also supporting consumption.
AI boom intensifies inflationary pressure 
Optimists hope that the AI boom will bring significant 
productivity gains and, as a result, lower costs for busi-
nesses as well as falling prices for consumers. So far, 
however, the high levels of investment in AI are clearly 
having an inflationary effect, as they are leading to high-
er prices for semiconductors and electricity, as well as ris-
ing construction costs. The US Federal Reserve currently 
estimates the impact on inflation at around 0.4 percent-
age points, which is primarily attributable to the higher 
cost of computer equipment. By contrast, the impact on 
electricity prices and construction costs is significantly 
smaller. However, it can still be considerable  at a local 
level where there is a high density of data centres. 
Ongoing job losses in the IT and financial sectors
The impact on the labour market has so far been mixed. 
The unemployment rate remains at a comparatively low 
level of around 4%. There is a fragile balance, with few 
new jobs and low redundancy rates. In many sectors, the 
number of entry-level jobs has fallen due to the spread 
of AI, which is why young people in particular – and uni-
versity graduates especially – are finding it increasingly 
difficult to find work. Sectors particularly affected by AI, 
such as IT, financial services and management consultan-
cy, have gradually cut jobs in recent months. However, in 
other sectors such as health, education and social care, 
culture and leisure, and the construction industry, thou-
sands of new jobs are being created every month, more 
than offsetting these losses.
EUROPE
AI boom largely bypasses Europe
The AI boom is largely passing Europe by, both techno-
logically and economically. The leading companies in the 
design and manufacture of high-quality chips, data stor-
age devices, and infrastructure and cloud solutions are, 
almost without exception, based in the US and Asia. The 
European semiconductor industry has been a successful 
supplier for years in sectors such as communications, 
transport, defence and energy. However, it has fallen be-
hind in cutting-edge technology. One notable exception 
is the Dutch company ASML, which produces the lithog-
raphy machines used to manufacture chips and, with a 
market capitalisation of around EUR 650 billion, is by far 
the most valuable company in Europe. To have a signifi-
cant economic impact, Europe would not necessarily 
need to have leading manufacturers, provided that com-
panies and households prove to be particularly tech-sav-
vy and adaptable. However, according to various surveys, 
this is not the case. Compared with most major emerging 
economies, AI has so far found less use in everyday busi-
ness and private life, and significant sections of the pop-
ulation are sceptical or even hostile towards the new 
technology (Chart 5).

Hardly any growth impetus at European level
AI has therefore not yet made a significant economic im-
pact. Whilst the information and communications sector 
is recording above-average growth of around 3% per 
year, the economy as a whole is expanding by only 
around 1%. However, this means the sector contributes 
only around 0.2 percentage points to GDP growth. In-
vestment is not currently growing at an exceptionally 
rapid rate. Current growth stands at just under 3% for 
both capital expenditure and intellectual property, al-
though only a portion of this is attributable to AI. The 
pace of data centre construction is also significantly 
slower than elsewhere. Europe accounts for only around 
5% of global computing capacity, whilst the figure for 
the US ranges from 75% to 80%. Complicated and pro-
tracted planning permission procedures, as well as high-
er energy prices, are preventing faster expansion. Ac-
cording to ECB estimates, AI could boost productivity 
growth by 0.2 to 0.4 percentage points per year in the 
medium to long term. Switzerland offers a ray of hope: 
In terms of the number of AI researchers and developers 
relative to the total population, it is already among the 
world leaders. Furthermore, software and databases 
have been the main driver of growth in capital expendi-
ture in recent years, overtaking outlays for research and 
development. 
Prices for mobile phones, tablets and laptops are rising
In terms of inflation, the same drivers are at work as in 
the US. However, as AI investment is significantly less dy-
namic than in the US, the effect on driving up prices is al-
so less pronounced. The shortage of memory chips has 
led to price rises – in some cases significant ones – for 
mobile phones, tablets and laptops in recent months. 
However, the impact on inflation is only in the region of 
around 0.2 percentage points. Compared with energy 
prices, which have risen sharply since the start of the war 
in Iran and account for almost half of the 3.8% inflation 
rate, this effect is, however, of little relevance to mone-
tary policy. 
More unemployed graduates in Switzerland
The AI boom has so far had little impact on the European 
labour market due to lower levels of investment and 
adoption rates. The unemployment rate in the Eurozone 
has been hovering at a level of just over 6% for the past 
three years. Whilst employment growth has slowed sig-
nificantly, this is also attributable to the subdued eco-
nomic climate and high political uncertainty. Further-
more, demographic developments mean that the 
working-age population is already in decline, which 
tends to reduce structural unemployment over time. 
With its high proportion of knowledge-intensive service 
sectors, Switzerland is more exposed to the impact of AI 
than other countries. Consequently, the number of un-
employed graduates has more than doubled over the 
past four years. However, at around 3%, the unemploy-
ment rate is close to the long-term average, and leading 
indicators suggest that the labour market situation will 
improve again in the coming months.
ASIA
Highly concentrated production in Asia
Emerging economies benefit in very different ways from 
artificial intelligence across the value chain. As men-
tioned at the beginning, semiconductor production is 
heavily concentrated in Asia. The manufacturing process 
is extremely complex and is currently mastered by only a 
handful of companies in China, South Korea and Taiwan. 
China has only recently established itself as a major man-
ufacturer and, despite considerable progress, still lags 
behind the global standard. South Korea and Taiwan, on 
the other hand, are technological leaders in memory and 
logic chips and processors respectively. The assembly, 
testing and packaging of semiconductors (ATP) is also 
providing further impetus for growth in the region. This 
takes place predominantly in China, Taiwan and Malay-
sia. However, other South-East Asian countries such as 
Vietnam, Thailand and the Philippines are also gradually 
gaining market share, albeit only to a limited extent so 
far. Furthermore, the construction of data centres in Asia 
is being strongly promoted. Ultimately, however, among 
the emerging economies, only China possesses the capa-
bility to develop AI models and train them using a suffi-
cient volume of data and create applications for end us-
ers. The country operates across the entire value chain 
and is the only nation to pose any significant competi-
tion to the US in the race for supremacy in the field of ar-
tificial intelligence.

Persistent capacity bottlenecks
Currently, demand for semiconductors far exceeds sup-
ply. Expanding capacity takes several years, particularly 
as production requires a high degree of specialisation. 
Furthermore, the physical lifespan of semiconductors is 
generally four to six years. Taking the replacement cycle 
into account, the two-year boom in the semiconductor 
sector is therefore set to continue for some time yet 
(Chart 6). Manufacturers in South Korea, Taiwan and 
China can therefore look forward to above-average de-
mand in the coming years, and companies in the ATP sec-
tor are unlikely to run out of work any time soon. How-
ever, growth will slow down.

One-sided driver of growth
The extent to which the upturn in these countries will 
gain momentum depends on several factors. The con-
trast with the employment-driven industrial boom of the 
‘Asian Tigers’ in the 1980s is, however, striking. The semi-
conductor industry accounts for only a fraction of the la-
bour market, as its production is more capital-intensive 
than that of traditional manufacturing. Large-scale job 
creation is therefore unlikely. Developments in the la-
bour market will therefore not lead to a consumer 
boom, nor is the considerable increase in wealth result-
ing from the technology-driven rally on the equity mar-
ket. This is because, in South Korea for example, the sav-
ings rate is high and, as in other more developed 
economies, wealth is concentrated amongst the older 
generation. However, this generation bears a compara-
tively high mortgage burden and the population is age-
ing rapidly. The picture is more favourable in Taiwan. 
The industrial sector is somewhat less diversified, mean-
ing that upstream and downstream sectors benefit more 
from the high demand for semiconductors. However, 
even on the island, economic sectors are growing at dif-
ferent rates, meaning that economic development is K-
shaped, similar to that in South Korea (Chart 7).

Limited inflationary impact
The differing growth dynamics have so far ensured that 
the high growth in production has had little impact on 
inflation. In the medium term, however, the breadth of 
growth will be decisive. The economic upturn is generat-
ing additional tax revenue, which could be distributed 
through tax relief, cost reductions or cash payments to 
households, causing the broader population to partici-
pate in the industrial upturn. On balance, such a “scat-
tergun” approach will certainly drive up prices as long as 
the productivity-boosting use of AI has not yet had its 
deflationary effect. However, these developments are 
still in their very early stages.﻿
Construction boom thanks to data centres
The construction of data centres has fuelled economic 
momentum in recent years, particularly in Malaysia and, 
to a lesser extent, more recently in Thailand as well. 
However, the gross value added generated by construc-
tion activity in the AI sector amounts to only a fraction of

the actual building costs account for only around a quar-

data centres requires only minimal staffing. Ultimately, 
therefore, there is no sustained increase in employment 
beyond the point of completion. Construction activity 
therefore merely triggers a temporary boost to growth. 
But the export of ICT services can provide sustained sup-
port for the economy, provided that the infrastructure is 
not used predominantly for domestic purposes. As a re-
gional hub, Malaysia will certainly benefit increasingly 
from this in the coming years.

AI use reinforces comparative advantage
Whilst the production of AI is regionally concentrated, its 
use is more geographically diverse. However, not all 
countries benefit to the same extent. Among emerging 
economies, India, for example, is one of the potentially 
biggest beneficiaries of generative AI (e.g. in the crea-
tion of texts and code). The outsourcing sector, in partic-
ular, has evolved in recent years: moving away from sim-
ple, repetitive tasks towards more complex work. In this 
context, the use of AI is likely to prove beneficial for the 
local labour market in the face of global competition. 
Applications of physical AI (e.g. in robotics), in turn, ben-
efit export-oriented economies that have a high level of 
technological adoption in the manufacturing sector and 
possess the necessary capital stock to implement the 
technology. These include, on the one hand, China, 
South Korea and Taiwan. On the other hand, Eastern Eu-
ropean countries such as Hungary, the Czech Republic 
and Poland also carry a certain potential. Other emerg-
ing economies will also be able to derive productive ben-
efits from the new technology. However, they are un-
likely to gain a comparative advantage in global 
competition as a result.
