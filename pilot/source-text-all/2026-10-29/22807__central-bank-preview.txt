A global pause rather than an end to tightening
Written by
Trading Strategy, Pictet Trading & Sales
This content is intended for distribution to professional clients, accredited investors, expert investors and institutional investors only, and it is reserved for the exclusive use of the recipient.  Please refer to important disclosure and disclaimer information here.
Markets expect the Federal Reserve, ECB and BoJ to leave rates unchanged at their October policy meetings this week, but that is not being read as the start of an easing cycle. Instead, markets continue to price further tightening, as elevated energy
prices and persistent underlying inflation keep the prospect of additional restraint alive even as hiring, household confidence and parts of the growth outlook begin to soften.
Globally, central banks are facing increasingly different domestic growth, inflation and market conditions. For investors however, much hinges on the extent to which domestic demand and inflation persistence continue to justify the tightening still
embedded in market pricing. Today we discuss that balance across the Fed, ECB and BoJ against the short-term technical backdrop for US Treasury yields, EUR/USD, the France–Germany spread and USD/JPY.
Global central bank decisions shift further towards tightening
As illustrated below, our global composite central bank indicator rose to 35% at the end of September, reinforcing the hawkish trend as the cumulative balance of rate hikes relative to cuts continues to rise across the central banks covered. Moreover,
while the Fed, ECB and BoJ are all expected to hold rates this week, against an otherwise hawkish backdrop, markets are likely to interpret this as a pause in implementation rather than any broader shift towards easing, with rates pricing still
pointing to a higher-for-longer policy stance. Against a backdrop of elevated energy prices, inflation dynamics remain central to the global policy outlook. While the Fed, ECB and BoJ face different growth conditions at home, the key question in each
case is how much further restraint may be needed to bring inflation under control without causing an undue hit to growth.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
First the Fed: Futures price an October hold, but the tightening cycle is expected to continue
Fed funds futures imply an approximately 87% probability that rates will remain unchanged on 28 October. A pause is therefore the clear market expectation, but at the same time the curve offers little evidence that investors view the tightening cycle
as finished. By December 2026, futures pricing implies roughly one full 25 bp increase relative to the current 3.75%–4.00% target range, which would take it to 4.00%–4.25%. It then prices in two further increases during 2027, with the next full
increment implied by March and another by July:

Growth dynamics offers little case for easing
While growth dynamics may have moderated recently, US activity remains resilient. The Atlanta Fed’s GDP nowcast, a real-time estimate of current-quarter growth ahead of the official release, stood at 3.5% on 27 October, down from around 5% in late
September, while the core PCE inflation nowcast rose to 3.15% from 3.0%, pointing to still-sticky underlying price pressures. For the Fed, that means softer growth, but not a backdrop weak enough to justify a rate cut. The advance third-quarter GDP
estimate and September PCE release, both due on 29 October after the Fed’s decision, will provide an early test of whether the data continue to support that market view.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
Inflation is not just being driven by energy: core pressures also remain sticky
As illustrated below, US headline CPI inflation rose to 3.74% in September, from 3.40% in August and 3.36% in July, with energy accounting for most of the acceleration. Energy’s contribution rose to 1.31 percentage points from 1.04 percentage points
in August. But the rebound was not only about energy. Core CPI inflation edged up to 2.56% from 2.45%, while core services remained the largest underlying contributor at 1.91 percentage points.
September therefore marked a break in the summer’s disinflation trend, even if the rise in core inflation was more modest than the move in headline CPI. With core components also firming, it is harder to dismiss the latest rise in inflation as purely
external, while persistent services inflation remains the main obstacle to a sustained return to lower inflation.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
Hiring momentum may be showing signs of slowing, but not in a way that yet signals a sharp deterioration in labour market conditions

The labour market offers some counterweight to the firmer inflation data. Non-farm payrolls rose by 29,000 in September, down sharply from 162,000 in August, while the unemployment rate edged up to 4.2% from 4.1%. The latest available JOLTS
data also pointed to softer labour demand, with job openings falling to 7.08 million in August from 7.34 million in July.
Taken together, the data suggest hiring momentum may be slowing, though not in a way that yet signals a sharp deterioration in labour market conditions. While the slowdown in payroll growth is notable, the rise in unemployment remains modest.
The next releases should help clarify whether this was a weak month or the start of a more sustained cooling trend, with September JOLTS due on 3 November and the October employment report on 6 November.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
US consumer sentiment has weakened, while longer-term inflation expectations remain elevated
As illustrated below, in beige, the University of Michigan’s consumer sentiment gauge fell to 48.1 in September, from 51.7 in August and 55.2 in July, reversing the improvement seen earlier in the summer and highlighting the widening gap between
still-resilient aggregate activity and households’ view of economic conditions. At the same time, five-year consumer inflation expectations, shown in red, edged up to 3.4% from 3.3% in August. Although still below May’s 3.9% reading, the increase
suggests that longer-term inflation concerns have not fully faded.
For the Fed, that makes the consumer backdrop less reassuring. Household confidence is weakening, but longer-term inflation expectations are not easing in step.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
The US 10-year Treasury yield is currently testing an important support area, while 5.37% remains the next upside reference point if the broader breakout stays intact
With the Fed expected to hold in the near term, while markets continue to price a relatively restrictive path further out, the recent pullback in the US 10-year Treasury yield from the 5.37% target can be read in two ways. It may reflect growing caution
over whether the economy can absorb restrictive policy for much longer, or simply a period of consolidation after an extended hawkish repricing. Either way, the move leaves the yield testing an important technical area rather than signalling a clear
shift in the broader rates narrative.
Technically, the yield is now testing key support at 4.93%–5.09%, including the rising 50-day SMA. A hold here would keep the broader breakout intact and leave 5.37% as the next upside reference point. A break below 4.93% would weaken the setup
and bring 4.81% into view, with the 200-day SMA near 4.53% as the next important support.

Euro-dollar: downside bias holds as relative policy expectations continue to support the dollar
The euro-dollar remains under near-term pressure, with relatively firmer US policy-rate expectations versus the euro area continuing to support the dollar, alongside broader macro and technical factors. From a technical perspective, the pair
continues to trade below former support at 1.1325 and within a descending channel, keeping the short-term bias tilted to the downside.
While oversold conditions may allow for a tactical rebound, the pair would need to reclaim 1.1325 to improve the technical picture and bring the 1.1413–1.1483 area back into view. Failing that, a break below 1.1113 would reinforce downside pressure
towards 1.1018–1.0906, while a move below 1.0906 would invalidate the current wave 4 scenario.

OIS pricing points to an ECB also on hold in October, with further tightening beginning to be priced from December onwards
The ECB face a similar policy pricing profile to the Fed: a near-term hold is strongly favoured, even as the curve continues to price further tightening over time. OIS pricing implies an approximately 95% probability of unchanged rates on 29 October.
The deposit facility rate currently stands at 2.50%, with the main refinancing rate at 2.65% and the marginal lending rate at 2.90%. By December 2026, and as illustrated below market pricing implies close to one additional 25 bp increase. By September
2027, cumulative pricing approaches three increases, equivalent to roughly one by end-2026 and two more during 2027.
As with the Fed, the hold itself is unlikely to be the main message. More important will be whether the ECB signals any change in its assessment of inflation persistence or the economy’s ability to absorb further tightening.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
In the Euro area, rising inflation complicates an already weak growth backdrop
Euro-area data point to an increasingly awkward mix for the ECB. Headline inflation rose to 3.8% in September, from 3.2% in August and 2.9% in July, while consumer confidence fell to -16.8 from -15.5 in August.
That leaves markets weighing firmer inflation against still-subdued household sentiment. Higher inflation supports the case for keeping policy restrictive, but weak confidence points to a softer demand backdrop and complicates the case for further
tightening. The next data releases will therefore matter for whether inflation pressures are broadening or remain largely energy-led. For the ECB, the key question is whether inflation persistence continues to outweigh signs of a weaker growth
environment.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
French sovereign risk: markets are pricing France as an outlier among European sovereigns
Alongside the inflation debate, sovereign spreads suggest that markets are treating France as a relative outlier within the euro area. At 158.5 bp, France’s spread is markedly wider than those of its A+ rated peers, Spain at 66.1 bp and Portugal at 52.9 bp,
and also above the spreads of lower-rated Italy and Greece, at 116.8 bp and 99.2 bp respectively.
That pricing points to investor concern that extends beyond the sovereign’s formal rating. Fiscal consolidation risks, political uncertainty and sensitivity around France’s debt trajectory all appear to be weighing on sentiment, even if spreads alone
cannot fully disentangle those factors from liquidity, technical positioning or broader market conditions.
For the ECB, this is distinct from the inflation story, but still relevant insofar as wider sovereign spreads can affect the transmission of monetary policy across member states – Even if recent remarks by Christine Lagarde at the IMF meetings
suggested no current intention to deploy the ECB’s Transmission Protection Instrument in support of France’s bond market.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
The France–Germany 10-year spread has widened further, with the move continuing to reflect a rising French risk premium
The France–Germany 10-year spread has moved sharply wider, reaching the extended wave 3 target at 1.54 and signalling a clear shift in dynamics. Momentum has strengthened further, with RSI breaking above a long-term resistance level in place
since 2011, underlining the significance of the move.
The key area to watch now is support at 1.13–1.29. As long as that zone holds, the breakout remains intact and the chart continues to point to a possible wave 5 target at 2.00.

OIS pricing points to a BoJ hold in October, with a year-end rate increase increasingly reflected in market expectations
The BoJ is also widely expected to hold rates in the near term, although markets continue to price a gradual tightening path thereafter. Little probability is attached to a move on 30 October, while December pricing incorporates around 0.78 of a 25 bp
increase.
The first full increase is priced by January 2027. Cumulative pricing then rises to 1.65 increases by March, 2.23 by June and 2.92 by September. These figures reflect the amount of tightening embedded in the curve, rather than a fixed schedule of future
policy decisions.
The October meeting will also include an updated Outlook Report. Its assessment of activity and inflation will help indicate whether the pace of normalisation implied by market pricing remains consistent with the BoJ’s own view.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
Recent macro data out of Japan are no longer surprising to the upside
As illustrated below, economic data out of Japan have recently become less supportive of near-term BoJ tightening. After exceeding 100 during the summer, the Citi Economic Surprise Index fell from 21.9 at the end of September to -8.5 on 23 October,
its first negative reading since March.
And in fact, it was the severe dual-miss in September trade data that illustrate the shift and took the CESI index into negative territory. Exports rose 16.7% year on year, but fell significantly short of the 20.1% consensus forecast, while the trade deficit
was wider than expected. That does not in itself signal contracting activity, but it does suggest the data are no longer outperforming expectations.
In other words, Japan’s data flow has become less supportive of near-term BoJ tightening, but not to the extent of signalling a broad deterioration in activity. Further normalisation is still likely to depend on the strength of domestic demand and the
persistence of inflation.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 27/10/2026.
USD/JPY: upside pressure remains in focus despite overhead resistance
USD/JPY remains capped for now by the 157.75–158.55 resistance cluster, defined by the 200-day moving average and a Fibonacci retracement level, while RSI momentum continues to struggle below descending resistance. Even so, the broader macro
backdrop remains broadly supportive of renewed upside pressure, with a relatively hawkish Fed and a still-cautious BoJ continuing to favour dollar-yen strength.
From a technical perspective, a break above 158.55 would strengthen upside momentum and bring 159.75 into view, followed by 161.35–161.95. On the downside, a move below 155.97 would point to renewed weakness and shift focus towards prospective
wave 4 support at 152.56.

The Equity quantitative grades
Our equity quant process is one by which we rank and score the stocks within our equity universe according to various short-term (i.e.
EPS growth) long-term (i.e. quality) and positioning (i.e. technical) characteristics, and on relative and/or historical basis as
appropriate. The various scores make up a stock’s total or ‘global' grade’:Growth Grade: The Growth Grade is a proprietary formula
made up of earnings revision momentum, past earnings growth, earnings stability, and current and long-term earnings growth.  A
grade above 55 is considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45 and neutral between 55 and 45.
EPS Grade: The EPS Grade is a proprietary formula consisting of current and forward EPS growth, change and surprise data.  An EPS grade
above 60 or below 40 is considered predictive for future out/under performance.Sales Grade: The Sales Grade is a proprietary formula made
up of current and next year’s sales momentum, past sales growth, sales stability, and current and long-term sales growth. A grade above 55 is
considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45, and neutral between 55 and 45.
Value Grade: The Value Grade is a proprietary formula made up of estimated P/E, P/B, P/S and P/CF ratios. 40% of the grade is based on
historical values and 60% on current market data.  A grade above 55 suggests a stock is cheap, below 45 expensive, and neutral between 55
and 45.
Quality Grade: The Quality Grade is a proprietary formula that focuses on the balance sheet (i.e. change in accruals, change in free cash flows
and profitability). A grade above 55 suggests a healthy balance sheet.Credit Grade: The Credit Grade focusses on the passive side of the
balance sheet.  It is divided into three sub-components to assess both short and long-term solvency.  A grade above 55 suggests a strong
capital structure, while a grade below 45 suggests a weak one.
Money Flow Grade: The Money Flow Grade is a proprietary formula that gives the accumulation/distribution based on the volume flows of a
stock. A grade above 55 indicates good money flow and a grade below 45 suggests weak money flow.Smart Sentiment Grade: The Smart
Sentiment grade is a contrarian indicator based on investor positioning measures such as the days-to-cover ratio, the put-call ratio, and the
short-interest ratio.  A weak grade suggests ‘too much’ optimism.
Relative Strength (RS) Grade: The RS grade measures the price momentum of a stock over its 1-year price performance.
Global Grade: The Global Grade is a weighted average of the Growth, EPS Sales, Value, Quality, Credit, Money Flow and Smart Sentiment
Grades.

The Exposure Optimisation Model
The PTS Exposure Optimisation Model (EOM) is a proprietary model designed to give tactical signals on the prevailing short-term
regional equity market regime. The model is applied to each market on a mutually-exclusive basis, with output expressed by way of
exposure scores (US; Europe; Japan and EM); in each case an average of underlying factors scores with dynamic weighting applied
according to the prevailing structural market volatility regime. Exposure score ratings are: very bullish (80-100%); bullish (60-80%);
neutral (40-60%); bearish (20-40%); and very bearish (20-0%). Factors are as follows:
Regime: A combination of technical indicators, moving averages and a Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance (CPPI) strategy, the regime
score is designed to reflect short-term market momentum in the region;
Valuation: Built around the forward price-to-sales ratio, the dynamic valuation score is based on a calculation that is re-set on bull/bear
market regime changes and calibrated to facilitate regional comparison;
Economics: The economics score draws on real economy activity data (i.e. heavy truck sales and shipping traffic), economic signals from
financial markets (i.e. yield-curves) and medium-term economic indicators (i.e. PMIs and unemployment data); 
Flows: The flows score draws upon both active and passive weekly EPFR fund flows data giving us an indication of risk appetite going into the
week ahead; 
Market Sentiment: The market sentiment score is built around classic regional market risk indices (i.e. VIX; ARMS) as well as soft survey data
(i.e. U.of Michigan Consumer Sentiment). The scoring system is applied in a similar fashion at both regional and market level, while at sector
level we refer only to the Regime, Flows and Valuation factors.

Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator that measures the speed and change of price movements. It ranges from 0 to 100 and
is typically used to identify overbought or oversold conditions in a market. An RSI above 70 suggests that a stock may be overbought, while an
RSI below 30 indicates that it may be oversold.
The Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD): A trend-following momentum indicator that shows the relationship between two
moving averages. It is calculated by subtracting the 26-period Exponential Moving Average (EMA) from the 12- period EMA. The result is the
MACD line. A nine-day EMA of the MACD, called the signal line, is then plotted on top of the MACD line, which can function as a trigger for
buy or sell signals. When the MACD crosses above the signal line, it is typically considered a bullish signal, and when it crosses below, it is
considered bearish.
Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis: Ichimoku Cloud is a popular technical analysis tool used to identify potential trends and
support/resistance levels in financial markets. It consists of several components, including the Kumo (cloud), the Tenkan-sen (conversion
line), the Kijun-sen (base line), and Senkou Span (leading span).
