Market fluctuations are merely buying time ahead of tomorrow’s escalation of 
developments that starts an active calendar of global risks straight through to next week. 
For now, equities are broadly higher, sovereign bonds are slightly richer across most 
benchmarks except France’s longer-end, oil is a touch cheaper and currencies are divided 
against the greenback. 
There isn’t a whole lot by way of fresh developments. US data risk is on tap. Israel’s 
election is happening but probably won’t reveal anything for a while.  
I would fade headlines on Canada-US trade negotiations in a fool-me-once, shame on 
you, fool-me-twice, shame on me sense. Canada is talking down talks, Trump and Hassett 
are talking them up, so this all sounds very reminiscent of the path to the breakdown in 
August. It’s the funny season in US politics and the GOP is learning on the hustings that 
apparently many Americans think a trade war with Canada is just plain nuts. Some of the 
other developments are downright bizarre, like the intimation that being able to buy 
blueberries from Canada might save Susan Collins. 
ISRAEL’S ELECTION COULD TAKE QUITE A WHILE TO SETTLE 
Will Bibi return for another term? Israel's election results could influence the direction of 
the war with Iran, among other factors. Polls run from 7am–10pm (1amET–4pmET). It may 
take quite a while to get the results, perhaps measured in days or longer, assuming that 
another election isn’t needed. That lengthy period includes vote counts but also jockeying 
to establish a workable coalition. Exit polls may provide a hint, but with the vote expected 
to be very close, they likely won’t reveal anything about the potential coalition.

WHAT TO MAKE OF AI FIRM’S DOOM CALLS?  
Anthropic––the gloomy AI poster child that posts high revenues but huge losses––was the latest to warn of an existential, catastrophic 
impact of AI on humanity when it issued its S-1 prospectus ahead of its IPO (here). It’s worth reading. This follows a warning from an AI 
researcher at the same firm a short time ago who warned of a significant risk that AI “could kill us all by the end of the decade.” Suddenly 
tomorrow’s Bank of Canada call doesn’t seem to matter versus grabbing a shovel to dig that bunker in the Canadian north. 
The substance of its warnings draws upon the following passages in the prospectus and note that the word ‘catastrophic’ appears no fewer 
than 10 times in melodramatic fashion. 
“In addition, advanced AI models, including our models, give rise to novel ethical and safety risks, including risks related to autonomy, 
manipulation, and the simulation of aspects of human psychology. These risks could result in unforeseen harms and may require the 
development of governance structures, safeguards, or mitigation strategies beyond existing AI safety frameworks, and there is always a risk 
that we will be unable to develop or implement them effectively.” 
“In addition, models, including our own, have exhibited, and may in the future exhibit, unexpected strategic or self-preserving behaviors, 
including attempts to resist shutdown, conceal or manipulate information, or generate outputs that could be interpreted as coercive, 
deceptive, or manipulative, including behavior resembling blackmail or efforts to influence human decision-makers to preserve the model’s 
continued operation or access.” 
“Even isolated or simulated instances of such behavior, whether occurring in testing or real-world deployments, and whether occurring with 
our models or models developed by others, could generate significant public concern, regulatory scrutiny, litigation risk, or reputational 
harm, regardless of whether such behavior results in actual harm.” 
“Concerns have been raised both within our organization and by other researchers, regulators, policymakers, and industry participants that 
sufficiently advanced AI systems could, if not developed, deployed, and operated with appropriate safeguards, oversight, and security 
measures, pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity, particularly if such systems were misused, replicated, weaponized, or allowed 
to operate with a high degree of autonomy.” 
“In addition, with the scale of harm that could result from a catastrophic AI-related incident, no single insurer, and potentially the insurance 
industry in the aggregate, may have sufficient capacity or expertise to underwrite insurance commensurate with such exposure.”

The message is clear—a leading firm in the sector is deeply concerned that its technology will wipe out humanity and perhaps itself through 
uninsured losses, but what the heck, get ready to buy its shares anyway! 
I’m inclined to listen with one ear to folks who know the technology, its pitfalls and rapidly developing risks, while maintaining healthy 
scepticism, nonetheless. You’d be foolish not to listen, but perhaps equally foolish to drink their Kool-Aid. 
There are two main reasons for being sceptical.  
One is that firms with leading technology in a highly fragmented industry that can see rivals quickly overtake the leader may have every 
incentive in the world to spook people in order to protect their leading if not dominant position. Scare the politicians and regulators so they 
restrict the technology, create economic barriers to entry and expansion, perhaps envelop the leading firm with a security blanket presently 
enjoyed by the defence industry against any liability for its products, and presto, the path to great fortune lies ahead. AI firms would hardly 
be the first to cajole policymakers into creating policies that serve their own interests. 
The second main reason draws upon the epic tendency of tech industry folks to come up with grandiose projections that routinely fail. Why 
believe them now? 
1. The Y2K disaster. 
2. “The cloud will eliminate the need for hardware.” 
3. The telecom “bandwidth shortage” — late 1990s 
4. “The Internet will destroy traditional media” — 1990s/2000s 
5. The “paperless office” — 1970s–1990s 
6. No one would want a phone with a touchscreen 
7. AI will eliminate 50% of white-collar jobs (2025) 
8. AI will expand white-collar jobs, same forecasters (2026) 
9. The PC would not become a mass consumer product (Digital Equipment Corp CEO, 1977) 
10. Kodak and digital photography. 
11. 3D television — roughly 2009–2013 
12. Netbooks would replace PCs 
13. The smartphone would destroy the PC much faster than it did 
14. Internet traffic forecasts — consistently too high 
15. “The metaverse is the next Internet” 
16. NFTs / “digital ownership” 
17. “SaaS means software margins can only go up” 
18. Moore’s Law would continue indefinitely 
19. The “AI will replace almost all software engineers immediately” thesis 
 If the world population hasn’t expired by the end of the decade, then we’ll put that one at #1 on the list.
