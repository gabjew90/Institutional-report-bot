 
A Beauty Contest With Many Unattractive 
Candidates: Are We Heading For Another Global 
Debt Crisis? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 02:30 AM 
Bond markets are asking questions again. And this time, the answers are not pretty. 
 
In early October, the US 10-year yield touched 5.34%, its highest since 2002. Japan's 10Y 
crossed 3% for the first time since 1996. The Bund hit its highest level since 2011. And France, 
well... France now pays the biggest risk premium since the euro crisis, with the OAT-Bund 
spread blowing out above 170bps on Oct 22, more than triple the roughly 55bps it traded at in 
February. 
Which is why Rabobank's head of macro strategy Elwin de Groot decided it was time for a 
global sovereign beauty pageant, which he aptly titled "Government debt sustainability: A 
beauty contest with many unattractive candidates"(available to pro subs). The question he sets 
 

 
out to answer is the one we have been asking for over a year: are we heading for another debt 
crisis? 
 
Regular readers know we have been pounding the table on the $340 trillion global debt pile 
since last October, and when France scrapped its pension reform a year ago we warned that 
global debt was about to go exponential: 
Fast forward one year and the debt is (a lot) bigger, the rates are higher, and the riots are in the 
streets of Paris. So let's see who wins the crown... and who gets voted off the stage. 
A Staircase That Only Goes Up 
De Groot starts with the obvious: public debt has risen in steps, with every shock (the GFC, the 
euro crisis, Covid, the 2022 energy crisis) absorbed by bigger deficits, and almost nothing paid 
down in the good years. According to the IMF, global public debt stood at almost 94% of GDP at 
 

 
the end of 2025 and is set to hit 100% around 2029, a level only seen after World War II. And 
that's before the bill for aging, defense, the energy transition and (now) AI comes due. 
 
For years none of this mattered because money was free and central banks hoovered up the 
supply. That ended in 2022, and 2026 piled on fresh pressure. Rabobank lists i) the Iran war 
and the Hormuz energy shock, which pushed the ECB to hike its deposit rate to 2.5% in June 
and September, ii) the AI capex boom, with tech companies issuing bonds at a record pace and 
competing with governments for the same savings, iii) fiscal concerns as investors demand 
more for lending to countries with no credible adjustment plan and iv) a "might makes right" 
geopolitical backdrop that raises questions about central bank independence. 
Readers will recognize point ii) as the "crowding out" theme we discussed just last week in the 
context of Goldman's FICC desk, and point iv) as a not-so-subtle nod to the White House and 
the Fed. 
Forget The Debt Ratio: It's All About r - g 
The most useful part of the note is de Groot's insistence that the headline debt ratio, on its own, 
is a lousy predictor of crises. Japan has run gross debt above 200% of GDP for years without 
blowing up; Ireland and Spain entered the euro crisis with debt below 40%. What matters is the 
direction of debt, and that boils down to the familiar debt equation: 
The debt level acts mainly as an accelerator or multiplier. Whether the ratio rises depends 
chiefly on two levers: the interest-growth differential, r − g, and the primary balance. 
Translation: when growth outruns the interest rate, governments can run primary deficits and 
still see debt ratios fall. That was the magic of the past decade. When the relationship flips, you 
 

 
get a snowball, and the higher the starting debt, the bigger the snowball. Rabobank notes that in 
one of its own calculations, the fiscal space created by a stable, high debt ratio disappeared 
with just a 0.3 percentage point rise in rates. Put differently, the free lunch was very fragile. 
And the buffer is gone: a decade ago, per the IMF, the global primary balance was more than 
1% of GDP stronger than needed to stabilize debt. That cushion has now almost disappeared. 
And The Ugliest Contestant Is... The United States 
This is where the beauty contest gets fun. Rabobank takes IMF data for 20 advanced and major 
emerging economies and computes the "fiscal gap": the structural primary balance minus the 
primary balance needed to keep debt stable. Red is ugly, green is pretty. 
 
The bottom row is the scorecard, and the US comes dead last at -3.7% of GDP, followed by 
China (-2.2%), Belgium (-2.0%), France (-1.9%) and Brazil (-1.8%). At the other end, Greece 
(+4.3%), Portugal, Japan, Canada and Spain look "strongest, despite high debt-to-GDP ratios" 
because primary surpluses or favorable growth keep them out of the danger zone. 
Yes, you read that right: Greece is the prettiest contestant on the stage. Only in 2026. 
As for the US, de Groot does not mince words, calling it the country with "the least comfortable 
numbers, but the greatest flexibility": a deficit north of 6%, debt above $40 trillion, roughly $1 
trillion in annual interest and an IMF projection of 142% debt/GDP by 2031. The only thing 
keeping it out of trouble is the dollar's reserve status and the depth of the Treasury market, 
which is why "pressure on Federal Reserve independence" is flagged as a key risk. We'd say 
 

 
it's the key risk, especially with the IMF already warning back in April that the Treasury market is 
prone to a "sudden repricing." 
Two more factors stretch the timeline before the pain shows up, for better and worse: 
●​ Maturities. Higher rates only bite as old debt rolls. In the Netherlands, nearly €150 billion 
of sub-1% coupon debt matures by 2032, pushing interest costs from 0.7% of GDP to 
almost 1.8% by 2035. 
●​ The bills trap. The IMF warns that nearly 20% of G7 and Spanish debt now matures 
within one year, after governments shortened maturities to save on term premium. That 
cuts today's interest bill... and maximizes tomorrow's rate sensitivity. 
France: The Textbook Case 
If there's one country that fits Rabobank's framework like a glove, it's France, which the note 
calls "a textbook illustration." The debt level (118% of GDP) is not exceptional. The direction is. 
The math is brutal: 
This year's deficit is about 5.4% of GDP, including 2.6% in interest costs, leaving a primary 
deficit of roughly 2.8%. Even if interest rates and growth were equal, the balance would need to 
improve by about 3% of GDP, more than €90 billion, to stabilize debt. Italy has much higher debt 
but already runs a small primary surplus, so its gap is far smaller. 
For context, the 2027 budget includes €54 billion in measures to get the deficit to 5%, which 
would slow, but not stop, the debt increase... and that's assuming it passes, which it may not 
since the National Assembly scrapped key savings measures this week. Oh, and France has 
missed its deficit target in three of the four years from 2023 through 2026, which helps explain 
this chart of chronic "surprises" vs. EC forecasts: 
 

 
 
Goldman agrees. The bank's European economists, in a note published this morning titled "Euro 
Area—Fiscal Policy Gears Up for the Electoral Cycle" (also available to pro subs) 
[MARKETDESK LINK], write that they "remain sceptical" France will hit next year's target and 
have raised their 2027 deficit forecast to 5.4% of GDP, i.e., no improvement at all. And as 
Goldman's rates desk put it on Friday, stabilizing French debt requires a primary surplus of ~1%, 
"something France has rarely come close to," rising toward 2% if funding costs converge on 
today's 5%+ OAT yields. 
 

 
 
Which brings us to Rabobank's most damning chart. Plot 10Y spreads vs. OIS against average 
credit ratings and France sits miles above the line at 163bps, wider than Italy (125bps) and 
Greece (113bps). In other words, French debt already trades at a level consistent with a much 
lower rating, below that of Greece. Or as the chart title puts it: "French debt trades as if it had a 
junk rating." 
 

 
 
Naturally, this is something we have been all over, from "Debt Crisis Back?" on Oct 1, when we 
noted that it had been a while since we had a European sovereign debt crisis, to "Sayonara, 
OATs" (Oct 5), which linked the French rout to Japanese repatriation, Morgan Stanley's verdict 
that the bond vigilantes have picked their first target, and last Wednesday's 24-year high in 
French yields. 
 

 
"Politics Is The Trigger" 
Rabobank's key insight is that debt crises rarely start with bad data. They start with politics: 
Greece's cooked books in 2009, Italy's Lega/5-Star government in 2018, Liz Truss in 2022 and, 
now, France's 2024-2026 run of snap elections, collapsing governments and the reversal of 
pension reform. Then comes the doom loop: 
Doubts about fiscal policy raise the risk premium. Higher rates increase interest costs and the 
deficit. Closing that gap requires larger spending cuts or tax increases, which are politically 
difficult and can weaken growth. That fuels further doubt. Debt that is sustainable at a 3% 
interest rate may become unsustainable at 6%, allowing markets to make their own forecast 
come true. 
Throw in rioters torching buildings, curfews across French cities, a far-left candidate now polling 
into the second round and an April 2027 presidential election, and you have a society that, in 
Goldman's words, is "not ready to accept that scale of adjustment." France "has the capacity to 
adjust," de Groot concludes; "markets question the political willingness." 
Spoiler alert: markets are right. 
The other worry is who holds the paper. Foreign ownership of French debt is still around 57% 
(down from roughly 60% in 2007), among the highest in the developed world, and foreign 
investors (hedge funds especially) tend to be the first ones out the door. 
 

 
 
Don't Hold Your Breath For Lagarde 
So will the ECB ride to the rescue? When the spread blew out last week, we expected the 
monetization demands to come back: 
Rabobank's answer: probably not, particularly in the near term. The bank gives four reasons: i) 
the selloff is "largely justified" by fundamentals, which is precisely what TPI is not designed to 
fight, ii) it's unclear France even meets TPI's fiscal conditions, iii) the market is still functioning in 
an orderly way, with no contagion and short-end yields comparatively low, and iv) intervening 
before a budget passes would be pure moral hazard. Lagarde herself said last week that the 
solution lies primarily with France itself. 
 

 
Even if things get disorderly, de Groot expects the sequence to be verbal reassurance first, a 
slower QT pace second, and targeted purchases only as a last resort, while an OMT program 
(which requires an ESM bailout) is "politically almost inconceivable" before the April 2027 
election. Which, frankly, sounds like a recipe for the spread to go wider first so that Paris (and 
Frankfurt) can have their "whatever it takes" moment later. 
The Escape Route Nobody Mentions Out Loud 
The most important paragraph in the entire note, however, is buried in the section on backstops. 
Because there is another, quieter way out of the debt trap, one that history shows is the 
preferred path: 
Under fiscal dominance, the central bank is pressured to keep rates low to protect the 
government even when inflation does not justify doing so. A related strategy is financial 
repression: forcing banks and pension funds to hold government bonds while keeping interest 
rates below inflation. High postwar debt ratios were reduced in large part through these 
mechanisms, with savers paying through inflation. For investors, this may be the largest 
long-term risk: not outright default, but a quiet erosion of bonds' real value. 
Rabobank notes this risk "appears more relevant in the United States than in Europe, but 
nothing is set in stone." Gold (and bitcoin) bugs, take a bow. 
A Slow-Motion Crisis... Until It Isn't 
So are we heading for another debt crisis? Rabobank's verdict: "Not imminently, but many 
countries have lost their fiscal room for maneuver." No major country has lost market access, 
maturities are long, and Europe has backstops that didn't exist in 2010. A euro-crisis-style panic 
would require a political rupture, contagion and a central bank that can't or won't step in. 
Instead, the base case is "a slow-moving crisis" in which interest costs eat an ever-larger share 
of budgets, crowding out investment, defense and social spending, fueling political tension that 
markets increasingly price. 
Moody's apparently agrees, at least for now. After the bell on Friday, the agency that every 
strategist expected to downgrade France... did nothing, keeping its Aa3 rating with a negative 
outlook, on the assumption that a deficit-cutting 2027 budget will pass and that funding costs 
and social tensions will ease. PM Lecornu took a victory lap on X, saying the decision defied the 
"pessimistic" forecasts. Our reaction was a bit more concise: 
 

 
Of course, as Rabobank points out, spreads tend to go up first and ratings go down later, and 
with French riots spilling into a second weekend, more strikes slated for early November and a 
budget that's being gutted on the floor of the Assembly, the "assumption" that tensions ease is 
doing a lot of heavy lifting. 
Our take: Rabobank is probably right that the crisis will be slow. But slow-moving debt crises 
have a habit of turning fast at the worst possible moment, and the politics, which de Groot 
himself calls "the key trigger," are moving in exactly the wrong direction in Paris, Madrid and 
Washington. And if the eventual "solution" is the one the note flags as the biggest long-term risk, 
i.e., central banks quietly inflating it all away, then the ugliest contestant in this pageant won't be 
France or the US. It will be the bondholder.
