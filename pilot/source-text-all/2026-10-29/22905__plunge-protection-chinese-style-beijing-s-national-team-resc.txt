 
Plunge Protection, Chinese Style: Beijing's "National 
Team" Rescues Stocks From A Bear Market 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 04:00 AM 
Earlier today, when we went through Hartnett's latest Flow Show, the "Enter The Dragon" 
edition, one curious footnote stood out: a $17 billion inflow into China equities, the largest since 
July, in a week when the CSI 300 was sliding toward a bear market. We noted that "we have a 
pretty good idea who is buying the cemetery" (a nod to the BofA client who joked that "if I 
bought a cemetery, people would stop dying"). 
Well, now we don't have to guess. 
According to Nikkei Asia, China's state-owned investment funds intervened in the stock market 
on Friday, one day after the CSI 300 fell more than 20% from its June high, "as investors 
increasingly question whether the country's much-hyped artificial intelligence sector can justify 
lofty valuations and offset broader economic troubles." And the market's reaction was exactly 
what you'd expect when a buyer with unlimited funds and zero price sensitivity shows up after 
lunch: the CSI 300 went from a fresh 15-month low in the morning to a 1.7% gain at the close, 
while Shanghai's tech-heavy STAR Composite swung from a 4.5% loss to a 3.7% gain. 
In other words, China's version of the Plunge Protection Team is back on the clock, and its own 
data say it has rarely been this busy. 
Friday Afternoon Miracle 
The tell, as always, was in the ETFs. According to Goldman's Asia desk (in its Friday regional 
wrap, available to pro subs), "state-backed 'national team' ETF buying supported the recovery, 
with turnover across nine favoured ETFs reaching RMB84bn ($12.6bn), after the market 
entered bear-market territory on Thursday." Growth names led the rebound, with ChiNext 
jumping 4.0% and the Shenzhen Component 2.8%.

The Biggest Month Of State Buying In Three Years 
Friday was the climax of a buying campaign that started more than a week earlier. On October 
15, as a global tech rout spilled into China, the E Fund CSI 300 ETF drew a record 18.8 billion 
yuan in a single session, "much of it in a late-session trading surge that coincided with the CSI 
300 paring its losses," according to Bloomberg. Bloomberg Intelligence's Rebecca Sin was 
blunt: "It is almost certain that the national team was behind the inflows." 
Goldman has been tracking the same flows, and its numbers are striking. In his latest China 
Weekly Kickstart (available to pro subs), Goldman's chief China equity strategist Kinger Lau 
devoted the entire "Charts of the Week" page to the national team, and the headline exhibit says 
it all: the top 10 national team favorite ETFs saw around RMB170 billion of net inflows in just 
two weeks, enough to trigger the bank's "NT buying signal" (net subscriptions more than two 
standard deviations above normal): 
 
Goldman's EM flows team made the same point in Friday's EM Weekly Fund Flows Monitor 
(also available to pro subs), writing that the inflows "offer signs of resumption in National Team 
buying, with month-to-date buying amounting close to the highest monthly level over the past 
three years." And that's with data only through October 22, i.e., beforeFriday's RMB84 billion 
afternoon:

Meanwhile, as Goldman's Sean Navin added in his weekend flows recap (this one ended with a 
question mark: "National Team?"), the private money is heading the other way: "According to 
GS Prime data, China allocations are approaching 1-year lows." Southbound investors, for their 
part, sold $2.2 billion of Hong Kong stocks last week, the most in one week since July. 
 

 
So hedge funds are dumping China, mainland retail is selling, Southbound is selling... and the 
state is buying everything they sell. Diversification at its finest. 
Sell High, Buy Lower 
The funny thing is that just nine months ago, the national team was on the other side of the 
trade. 
Regular readers will recall that back in April we reported that China's "National Team" dumped 
ETFs in Q1 to cool an overheating market: Central Huijin cut its stakes in several of the biggest 
ETFs below the 20% disclosure threshold, with Morgan Stanley estimating that the state sold 
about $80 billion in January and February, when turnover hit a record and the AI rally got a little 
too speculative for Beijing's taste. Bloomberg pegged the exit at $68 billion already by late 
January, and by July things had gotten so quiet that China's biggest ETF was a gold fund. 
Goldman's long-run chart puts the round trip in context. The national team has shifted toward 
ETF-led intervention since 2018, and the giant light-blue bar in 1Q26 is the January dump 
(Goldman's monthly series shows RMB935 billion of outflows from the favorite ETFs in January 
alone). The small bar on the far right is the buying so far: 
 
Say what you want about central planning, but on Goldman's chart the national team sold with 
the CSI 300 somewhere around 4,700 and is buying it back around 4,100, which is probably 
 

 
better timing than most hedge funds can claim this year. The question is whether a RMB170 
billion bid can do what a RMB935 billion offer did, only in reverse. 
Plenty of people on the ground doubt it. 
"Once They Ease On The Buying It All Comes Crashing Down" 
The most honest assessment came from a mainland fund manager. "The national team has 
been buying but the rebound has all been weak, once they ease on the buying it all comes 
crashing down again," Li Ying of Shanghai Zhenying Investment Management told Bloomberg, 
adding that margin calls may rise if declines persist. Leveraged investors clearly agree: 
outstanding margin debt fell 1% on Thursday to the lowest in a year. 
And the 3,700 line on the Shanghai Composite that another local fund manager told Bloomberg 
to watch on October 16, to see "if the 3,700 level... will hold thanks to such support by 
authorities"? Even after Friday's miracle rally the index closed at 3,690. 
The sell-side isn't much more optimistic. BNP's Liu called the state buying a "medium-term 
bullish signal," but warned it alone may not be enough to reverse short-term momentum amid 
weak sentiment globally. RBC Wealth Management's Nicholas Gwee blamed "insufficient fiscal 
stimulus and disappointing earnings," warned that volatility could persist into early 2027, and 
that "state-backed stabilization may not be enough to sustain a market recovery." Aberdeen's 
Xin-Yao Ng called state buying "only a temporary solution," because "at its core, China needs 
domestic consumption and investments to improve." 
Morgan Stanley's chief China economist Robin Xing went one step further and pointed at the 
real problem: "The key issue for Chinese companies is still profitability... Metrics such as return 
on equity (ROE) remain relatively weak." The bigger valuation overhang, he said, "remains 
China's weak domestic demand... when pricing power is weak and consumers are trading down, 
that inevitably weighs on earnings expectations and market sentiment." Which is exactly why 
Alibaba and Tencent, two of the biggest beneficiaries of China's AI boom, are each down more 
than 30% this year, and why CATL just plunged on a profit miss. 
We've seen this movie before. Back in January 2022, China's "National Team" rushed to save 
stocks after a bear market was signaled; in 2019 it backfired spectacularly and the ChiNext 
collapse continued as the national team abandoned investors. And in April 2025, during the

Eighteen months later, the yuan is at a 4-year high, but the playbook hasn't changed one bit. 
 

 
Propping Up The AI Trade 
What makes this round different is what Beijing is propping up. The selloff was led by China's AI 
darlings: optical transceiver makers Eoptolink and Suzhou TFC fell more than 5% Friday 
morning, and state-owned chipmaker SMIC lost 3%. During the lunch break, both Eoptolink and 
TFC rushed out exchange filings stressing their "strong" order backlogs, with Eoptolink touting a 
buyback of up to 1 billion yuan. By the close, everything was green, and SMIC's Hong Kong 
shares had gone from -4.6% to +1%. 
As Bloomberg noted last week, "keeping AI stocks buoyant could help Chinese companies raise 
the capital they need to compete with US rivals," which makes the national team the closest 
thing China has to a hyperscaler capex backstop. According to Goldman's desk, the single 
biggest contributor to Friday's Shanghai Composite gain was Cambricon, China's answer to 
Nvidia, which rose 5.5%. A few hours after the close, Cambricon reported its third-quarter 
numbers: 
That's a 44% revenue miss for the stock that led the rescue rally. Maybe the national team 
should pencil in a few extra ETFs for Monday. 
Beijing is also working the plumbing. On Monday, the CSRC approved the first batch of 18 
actively managed ETFs, widely seen as a way to attract long-term capital into equities, and as 
Goldman's Lau noted, the first products launched on October 23. Same day the national team 
showed up. What a coincidence. 
Buying The Cemetery 
None of this changes the macro picture that Hartnett laid out over the weekend: a CSI 300 down 
20% since June, a record-low 1.6% 10-year yield, a "consumer/investor buyer strike as China 
property bleeds" (more on that in today's Goldman deep dive into China's property bust, which 
keeps getting worse), and an economy exporting its way out by flooding the world with deflation. 
BofA itself argues that national team buying is more likely to establish a floor than ignite a 
sustainable rally, and on that point we agree with BofA, BNP, RBC, Aberdeen, Morgan Stanley 
and the guy at Shanghai Zhenying. 
A floor is precisely what Beijing bought on Friday: RMB84 billion of ETF turnover in one 
afternoon, near the biggest month of state buying in three years, and all it took was a bear 
market. What it didn't buy was a single extra yuan of household spending, a point of ROE or a 
better Cambricon quarter. With Goldman Prime clients at 1-year-low allocations, Southbound 
 

 
selling the most since July and margin debt at a one-year low, the national team is now the 
market's marginal buyer, and its last buyer too. We know how that ended in 2015, 2019 and 
2022: the rebound lasts precisely as long as the buying does. 
Then again, if anyone can make people stop dying by buying the cemetery, it's the Chinese 
Communist Party.
