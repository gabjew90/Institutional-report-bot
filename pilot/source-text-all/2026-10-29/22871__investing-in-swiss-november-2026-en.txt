Editorial
Beyond elections
Mark Haefele
Global Chief Investment Officer
Michael Bolliger, Ph.D.
Chief Investment Officer Switzerland
Dear readers,
Market volatility has risen significantly in recent weeks. 
Geopolitical risks, persistent inflation, concerns about the 
sustainability of government debt, and uncertainty ahead of the 
US midterm elections are unsettling investors. Surging artificial 
intelligence capital spending rates are also raising questions: Can 
this be monetized effectively, and will any revenues  generated be 
sufficient to justify past and future investment in AI infrastructure?
These concerns are balanced by encouraging signals, however. 
Leading economic indicators remain robust, and the reporting 
season currently under way is providing compelling evidence that 
many companies have continued to increase both revenues and 
profits. Overall, the economy is proving resilient and should support 
markets over the medium term. This points to a positive outlook, 
even if the risk of market corrections is likely to remain elevated for 
now. The main message for investors is to remain confident, while 
reviewing whether portfolios are still aligned with long-term 
objectives. If individual asset classes—or even individual stocks—
have become too dominant in a portfolio, or if risks are 
concentrated, the current environment offers opportunities to 
make targeted adjustments, in our view.
In this issue of Investing in Switzerland, we look ahead to the US 
midterm elections and what they could mean for investors. We 
highlight the opportunities and risks arising from the recovery in 
the Swiss economy and consider what the latest earnings trends 
imply for Swiss and global equities. Additionally, we explore the 
outlook for interest rates and Swiss franc bonds, the role of the 
franc and currency hedging, and the Swiss real estate market as it 
navigates robust fundamentals and mounting headwinds.
We hope you enjoy the read.
Mark Haefele
Global Chief Investment Officer
Michael Bolliger

Messages in Focus
For Swiss investors
Position for market upside
Markets are adjusting to higher bond yields, firmer 
energy prices, and persistent geopolitical risks, but 
earnings and economic activity remain resilient. 
Swiss investors should remain invested through diversified 
exposure to cyclical and structural growth opportunities, and use 
market strength to reduce concentrated single-stock positions. 
International diversification seems particularly important to us, as 
the Swiss equity market offers only limited exposure to growth 
themes such as technology. Higher rates and energy costs 
warrant selectivity, but do not justify a broadly defensive stance.
Position for a commodity upcycle
Renewed disruption to energy supply has 
highlighted the diversification value of commodities. 
Broad commodity exposure, including energy, can provide 
protection against a prolonged supply disruption and 
inflationary pressures. Tight physical markets also continue to 
support the case for a commodity upcycle, although allocations 
should be actively managed and sized for volatility.
Put cash to work
Cash provides liquidity for near-term spending and 
contingencies, but excess holdings can erode 
purchasing power and miss opportunities in markets. Swiss 
investors should determine how much liquidity they genuinely 
need, optimize returns on that cash, and establish a disciplined 
plan for the remainder. Those reluctant to invest immediately 
can phase into diversified portfolios. Given Switzerland’s low 
interest rate environment, high-quality dividend stocks, 
infrastructure investments, and select yield-oriented structured 
strategies may also be suitable for income-focused investors.
Invest in transformational innovation
AI investment remains a major driver of earnings 
and economic activity, in our view. Investors should 
diversify beyond a small number of market leaders, 
with opportunities spanning semiconductors, cloud platforms, 
software, power infrastructure, utilities, and industrial 
companies. As suitable investment opportunities in the Swiss 
equity market are limited, we prefer to look beyond the 
domestic market. Exposure should be balanced across the value 
chain and sized to reflect elevated valuations, execution risks, 
and potentially wide differences in company outcomes.
Diversify with alternatives
Alternative investments can broaden portfolio 
return drivers when public equity and bond markets 
are both sensitive to changes in inflation, interest rates, and 
geopolitical risks. Diversified exposure across hedge funds, 
private markets, infrastructure, private credit, and real estate 
may provide differentiated sources of income, growth, and 
relative returns. Investors should diversify across managers and 
strategies while carefully assessing liquidity, leverage, fees, 
transparency, and their capacity to hold long-term assets.

Deep dive
US midterm elections: Not a vote against US equities
Alessandro Bee
Economist
The midterm elections look likely to be a vote against the current 
US administration, but we do not view this as a vote against US 
equities in general. Three principles can help investors stay calm 
during elections: Do not vote with an investment portfolio, use 
diversification to prepare for all outcomes, and beware of 
supposed “no-brainers.”
The US midterm elections will take place on 3 November. The focus is 
on the US Congress, with all seats in the House of Representatives up 
for election, while one-third of Senate seats will be contested.
Good prospects for the Democrats
In the last two years, the Republicans have held a majority in both 
chambers of Congress. Polls suggest a political change of power is  
likely. We estimate the chances that the Republicans can defend the 
status quo at only 10%. We see a 40% probability that the 
Democrats will capture the House of Representatives, and a 50% 
probability that they will hold a majority in both chambers.
The interaction between the two chambers of Congress and the US 
President is complex. Any new laws must be approved by both 
chambers, after which the President has the power to veto them. 
Congress can, in turn, override a veto with a two-thirds majority in 
both chambers. The Democrats are very unlikely to win a two-thirds 
majority in the Senate next month, and won’t be able to neutralize 
the President’s veto in the next legislative period.
Political gridlock likely
We expect the Democrats to regain control of at least one chamber of 
Congress, but with too small a majority to override President Donald 
Trump’s veto power, which would lead to increased political gridlock.
So, while the Democrats likely won’t be able to implement their fiscal, 
energy, artificial intelligence, and housing policy agenda even if the 
election goes their way, they would have greater bargaining power. 
Demands for stricter oversight of big tech companies, more affordable 
housing, a halt to cuts in social programs, and more renewable 
energy investment would carry more weight in negotiations.
This also increases the potential for conflict over raising the debt 
ceiling, fueling market anxiety.
We do not expect major changes to US trade policy, as President 
Trump has spent the past two years shifting decision-making power 
in this area to the executive branch by invoking emergency laws.
Strong US technology stocks
Investors buying US equities are not really investing in US politics, but 
rather in the innovative capabilities of US companies. These companies 
have delivered convincing results in recent years. The benchmark S&P 
500 index has gained around 80% in US dollar terms over the past five 
years, and its performance over the same period in Swiss francs, at 
60%, has also been significantly better than that of the SPI (30%).

The technology sector, which dominates the US equity market, has 
been a key driver of these gains. Despite volatility and uncertainty, 
tech plays an integral role in our personal and professional lives, now 
as well as in the future. The US market enables investors to participate 
in tech innovation and related stock gains. This contrasts with the 
Swiss market, where technology accounts for less than 1% of the 
benchmark. In addition, the Swiss economy is traditionally closely 
linked to the European economic cycle and monetary policy. 
Investments in the US offer a degree of diversification against this risk.
Further important elections ahead
The midterms mark the start of a series of elections. In Europe, Spain, 
France, Poland and Italy all have elections scheduled by the end of 
2027. Moreover, Switzerland will vote for a new parliament in 
autumn next year.
Three considerations can help investors weather the upcoming 
elections with a degree of composure:
Beware of supposed “no-brainers”
Election platforms of individual parties or candidates usually indicate 
an affinity for certain topics, for example, the US Democrats’ focus 
on clean energy or the Republicans’ support for oil drilling and 
defense spending. However, after Democrat Joe Biden won the 2020 
presidential election, stocks in the green energy sector lagged, while 
shares of oil producers performed strongly. At the same time, leading 
defense stocks underperformed the broader market after Donald 
Trump’s election in 2016.
The reasons for this are that the link between individual parties and 
certain equity sectors had already been priced in before the elections, 
while stock markets are also impacted by other forces and economic 
or geopolitical developments. Any expected election outcome alone 
should therefore not be a sufficient basis for an investment decision.
Be prepared for every outcome
The economic policy consequences of elections differ across 
countries. The upcoming US midterms are likely to have little impact 
on economic policy. France’s situation could well be very different: In 
view of the tense debt situation and the differing, potentially extreme 
positions of key parties, the election outcome could lead to major 
changes. Additionally, recent history has shown that election polls 
can be wrong, as in the case of the Brexit referendum in 2016. It is 
important to structure portfolios in such a way that they can weather 
any election result.
Do not vote with an investment portfolio
As a general rule, voting is done at the ballot, not with an investment 
portfolio, which should focus on long-term goals. A strong 
preference for a particular political party may be emotionally 
understandable, but it can distort an investor’s judgment. It could 
lead to misplaced optimism or pessimism at precisely the wrong time.
In light of these three considerations, we believe broad diversification 
is the best way to sleep more soundly during the US midterms as well 
as the European elections, at least from an investment perspective.

Economy
Hurdles on the road back
Alessandro Bee
Economist
The Swiss economy is on the road back to full capacity utilization. 
Much of that distance has likely already been covered, but the 
coming quarters are likely to hold a few more pitfalls.
The assessment of the Swiss economy is now significantly more 
positive than it was in the summer. In the second quarter, the Swiss 
economy grew strongly by around 1.5% and closed the output gap. 
That said, an exceptionally strong increase in chemical and 
pharmaceutical exports is likely overstating the economic recovery. 
The purchasing managers’ indices for manufacturing and services 
have continued their upward trend, which began in the spring, into 
the autumn.
A solid global economy should support Switzerland’s return to its 
long-term growth trend over the coming quarters. For 2027, we 
expect Swiss economic growth of around 1.5%. This would put it at 
the lower end of our estimate for potential growth.
Hurdles for the Swiss economy
Geopolitical tensions in the Middle East pose a risk to the Swiss 
economy. Diesel prices and prices for natural gas traded in Europe 
have risen significantly. In addition, natural gas storage levels are low. 
A cold winter could lead to disruptions in the energy market and 
weigh on economic activity.
France’s public finances present another potential pitfall. The yield 
spread between 10-year French government bonds and German 
Bunds rose to more than 150 basis points in October—a sign that 
the market is concerned about France’s debt situation. A further 
sharp rise in French bond yields is not our base case, but it would 
weigh on economic activity in Europe.
A third potential pitfall could emerge in artificial intelligence. Doubts 
about whether AI can fully deliver on its promises could, for example, 
lead to data center construction being halted and weaker investment 
could weigh on global economic activity and Switzerland by extension.
Inflation stable
Inflation has been somewhat higher than expected in recent months 
and even rose to 1% in September. Uncertainty in the Middle East is 
likely to continue supporting inflation in the short term through 
rising energy prices.
If the oil market normalizes in 2027, energy prices should stabilize or 
decline, dampening inflation. At the same time, higher capacity 
utilization in the Swiss economy is likely to support inflation. Overall, 
we expect an inflation rate of slightly below 1% both this year and 
next year.

Swiss equities
Earnings growth defies risks
Stefan R. Meyer
Equity strategist Switzerland
Despite rising interest rates and political uncertainty, the global 
economic outlook is brightening, bringing broader momentum 
to corporate earnings. We recommend focusing investments 
on management quality and profitability. Tactically, we prefer 
mid-caps and quality cyclicals. 
Rising bond yields worldwide have weighed on equity market 
performance in recent months. Higher energy prices and government 
debt levels have pushed bond yields higher, particularly in the key US 
bond market, but also in Europe. In Switzerland, the rise in yields has 
been moderate, with the inflation rate still below 1% and government 
debt low. Rising bond yields tend to dampen discretionary consumer 
spending and capital-intensive corporate projects. They also weigh on 
price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios, as alternative investments such as bonds 
offer increasingly attractive yields. 
Swiss equity valuations correlate negatively with bond yields
Our analysis, based on data since 2003—that is, the period following 
the dotcom bubble—shows a negative correlation between the Swiss 
equity market’s forward P/E ratio and 10-year government bond 
yields. As the US is by far the world’s largest financial market, US 
interest rates serve as a global benchmark and are highly relevant to 
investors and companies across the world. Unsurprisingly, Swiss 
equities exhibit a negative correlation with US dollar bond yields. 
Over the period observed, however, this correlation has been only 
slightly negative.
The negative correlation with Swiss bond yields is much more 
pronounced. As Swiss government bond yields have risen slightly in 
recent months, the Swiss equity market has corrected moderately 
from its all-time high reached in mid-August. Based on the 
correlation trendline for Swiss bond yields and the current 10-year 
Swiss government bond yield of just under 0.6%, the fair P/E ratio 
for the Swiss Market Index (SMI) would be just under 17x. Based on 
current consensus earnings estimates for the next 12 months, the 
SMI’s P/E ratio is exactly at this level, indicating a fair valuation.
US midterm elections could put pressure on pharmaceutical 
stocks
The US midterm elections are likely to result in a less clear-cut 
political landscape in the US (see the focus article on page 5). This, 
in turn, could lead to greater regulatory uncertainty and somewhat 
more volatility in financial markets. That said, there is a degree of 
political consensus on issues including excessively high living costs, 
particularly in health care. From an equity market perspective, pricing 
pressure in the health care sector is likely to be a headwind, although 
rhetorical pressure could remain greater than actual pressure. This is 
likely to weigh on the near-term upside potential of pharmaceutical 
stocks. At the same time, technological leadership, industrial 
resilience, and higher defense spending are likely to remain 
important political priorities.

Economic tailwinds
Despite the risks mentioned, the economic outlook is improving, 
including for Europe, which has faced particularly weak market 
conditions over the past three years. In Switzerland, a key leading 
economic indicator—the manufacturing purchasing managers’ 
index—has now remained above the neutral 50 mark for seven 
consecutive months, pointing to an industrial recovery.
The first third-quarter results from Swiss companies reveal particularly 
strong market growth in regions such as Eastern Europe, Asia, and 
Mexico. Areas such as suppliers to the semiconductor industry and 
data center construction, beauty product manufacturers, and 
pharmaceutical contract manufacturers are also delivering strong 
growth. So far, higher interest rates have merely slowed the 
acceleration in global economic activity, for example in residential 
construction.
Robust earnings growth offers upside potential
While companies’ operating performance has recently proved 
robust—supported by structural investment trends and company-
specific measures to improve profitability—revenue and earnings 
prospects are gaining strength and breadth this year. This favors 
equity markets with above-average sensitivity to the economic cycle, 
such as the Eurozone. Within the Swiss equity market, it suggests a 
preference for more cyclical market segments. For the SMI, we 
forecast earnings growth of 11% in 2026 and 8% in 2027.
Mid-caps and quality cyclicals most attractive
We recommend focusing on management quality and profitability 
when investing. Tactically, we prefer Swiss mid-caps and quality 
cyclicals. For mid-caps, we expect stronger earnings momentum 
than for the SMI this year and next (investment theme 1). Within the 
mid-cap segment, we favor quality cyclicals, attractively valued 
growth companies, and companies benefiting from self-help 
measures to improve efficiency and profitability. Among cyclical 
stocks, we prefer innovation and quality leaders with attractive 
valuations (investment theme 2).
Over the longer term, we also recommend our investment theme 
“Swiss high-quality dividends,” comprising companies with attractive 
dividend yields or dividend growth (investment theme 3). As Swiss 
franc bond yields remain low, high-quality dividends with growth are 
appealing. Defensive companies with growing cash flows are a solid 
core investment (investment theme 4). Companies with particularly 
high returns on invested capital (ROIC) of over 15% offer quality 
valued over the long term (investment theme 5; see the table on page 
18). We like beneficiaries of structural growth in artificial intelligence, 
longevity, and energy and resources (investment theme 6).

Global equities
Steady under pressure
Ulrike Hoffmann-Burchardi
CIO Global Equities
Rudolf Leemann
Equity strategist
Markets have continued to rise despite higher bond yields, 
elevated energy prices, and geopolitical uncertainty. We maintain 
our Attractive view on global equities. Strong earnings growth 
remains the key support for markets. 
The resilience of equities is thanks to strong fundamentals, with 
healthy earnings growth across regions supported by solid investment 
spending, robust consumer demand, improving manufacturing 
activity, and the continued expansion of AI-related infrastructure.
In the US, economic growth remains resilient. Third-quarter earnings 
are expected to remain strong, with AI-related spending the largest 
source of earnings growth. Inflation remains above the Federal 
Reserve’s target, pointing to some further monetary tightening. But 
we do not expect higher interest rates to materially undermine 
corporate profitability or significantly slow economic activity. We 
maintain an Attractive view on US equities and continue to see 
further upside in the S&P 500 through the middle of 2027.
In Europe, recent market weakness reflects concerns over energy 
prices, higher interest rates, and widening sovereign spreads. We 
believe Europe is in the early stages of a cyclical earnings recovery 
following several years of stagnation. We maintain an Attractive view 
on the region and continue to forecast 15% earnings growth in both 
2026 and 2027 for the Eurozone.
Across Asia and emerging markets, higher oil prices, elevated bond 
yields, and a stronger US dollar have weighed on sentiment, but 
valuations already reflect much of this pessimism. We continue to 
expect the region to deliver among the strongest earnings growth 
globally, supported by AI-related investment and semiconductor 
demand.
AI-related risks as a source of volatility
Rapidly shifting sentiment around AI-related risks remains an 
additional source of volatility. On the demand side, continued 
evidence of enterprise productivity returns is essential to sustain AI 
adoption. Meanwhile, private capital funds a significant share of AI 
demand, making sustained access to it crucial. On the supply side, 
key watchpoints are power availability and data center permitting as 
potential bottlenecks, alongside resilient hyperscaler core-business 
cash flows to sustain AI capex. Higher rates and financing stress pose 
additional risks, but these are greater for new compute suppliers, 
while hyperscalers’ strong balance sheets continue to provide a buffer.
While we continue to monitor these risks, we believe fundamental 
indicators point to a positive outlook for equities. Investors should 
continue to use periods of volatility as opportunities to build 
exposure to long-term structural growth trends.

Interest rates and bonds
Limited upside risk for Swiss yields
Florian Germanier
Economist
Guillaume Schaller, CFA
Credit analyst
Confirmation of accelerating growth should pave the way for a 
normalization of SNB monetary policy in 2027, while upside risk 
for Swiss yields is limited. For CHF bonds, we continue to prefer 
intermediate maturities and high-quality issuers.
SNB likely to raise rates if recovery is confirmed
While the Fed and the European Central Bank (ECB) raised their 
policy rates last month, the Swiss National Bank (SNB) left its policy 
rate unchanged at 0% in September. At the same time, it raised its 
inflation forecasts slightly and highlighted the acceleration in Swiss 
economic activity. The SNB is likely to await confirmation of these 
developments before normalizing its monetary policy.
In our base case, we expect an initial rate hike in March 2027, 
followed by a further increase to 0.5% in June 2027. As financial 
markets have already largely priced in these rate moves, they imply 
only limited upside risk for longer-term Swiss yields. 
Moreover, global conditions argue against a sustained rise in bond 
yields. Elevated inflation risks could temporarily prompt central banks 
to adopt more restrictive monetary policy. However, growing concerns 
about global economic growth should support demand for defensive 
assets like government bonds, limiting further spikes in yields.
Current environment calls for selectivity
The CHF bond market has experienced relatively high interest rate 
volatility in recent weeks, while new issuance remained strong and 
spreads widened only marginally. Against this backdrop, the Swiss 
Bond Index (SBI) total return remains slightly negative year to date. In 
the short term, return prospects for CHF bonds remain limited, with 
yields to maturity ranging from around 1.3% to 1.5% in the SBI AA 
to BBB rating segment.
We expect the credit quality of the companies we cover to remain 
broadly solid through the third-quarter reporting season. However, 
still-tight spreads leave little room for disappointments, while 
uncertainty over global growth and the outcome of upcoming 
elections in several countries are likely to keep yield volatility elevated 
in the CHF bond market as well. This environment argues for cautious 
security selection; we continue to prefer intermediate maturities and 
quality within the investment grade segment. At the short end, 
selectively adding in credit risk can offer attractive additional yield.

Currencies
Asymmetric risks favor the Swiss franc
Florian Germanier
Economist
Constantin Bolz
FX strategist
Over a 12-month horizon, asymmetric risks favor the Swiss franc. 
High interest rate expectations abroad will be difficult to meet, 
while persistently high inflation could increasingly trigger growth 
concerns and strengthen the franc. Active currency hedging is 
therefore becoming more attractive.
USD and EUR supported in the short term
After EURCHF rose from below 0.92 to almost 0.95 in the second 
quarter as the interest rate differential between the ECB and the SNB 
widened, the franc has recently regained strength. This was primarily 
triggered by growing fiscal concerns in France, which weighed on 
the euro and increased demand for the franc as a perceived “safe 
haven.” In contrast, the franc has remained under pressure against 
the US dollar, as the US continues to benefit from higher interest 
rates and a robust economy.
In the short term, we believe the euro should regain some ground 
against the franc. Despite heightened fiscal uncertainty, we consider 
France adequately financed and do not expect a debt crisis. As 
concerns about French public finances ease, the Eurozone’s interest 
rate advantage over Switzerland should come back into focus. The 
ECB rate increase we expect in December should widen this 
advantage further and support the euro against the franc.
Much favors the franc over the medium term
Over a 12-month horizon, we expect a return to the long-term trend 
of lower EURCHF and USDCHF exchange rates. During next year, the 
SNB is likely to begin raising its policy rate, while the ECB and the Fed 
are likely to have reached the end of their rate-hiking cycles. The 
current interest rate differential should gradually narrow, reducing 
the tailwind for the euro and the US dollar.
Moreover, risks to the franc appear asymmetric. If inflation falls faster 
than expected, expectations for further rate increases by the ECB and 
the Fed should decline, supporting the franc. If inflation rises further, 
the interest rate differential could initially widen in favor of the euro 
and the US dollar. As inflation becomes more persistent, however, 
concerns about economic growth would also increase, benefiting the 
franc as a defensive asset. At the same time, the euro remains 
vulnerable to political and fiscal risks. We prefer to focus on currency 
hedging in this environment.

Real estate
Facing headwinds
Maciej Skoczek, CFA
Real estate analyst Switzerland
The Swiss real estate market benefits from robust fundamentals. 
However, rising interest rates, high valuations, and regulatory 
risks limit the upside potential of real estate stocks and funds. 
A neutral positioning is likely to remain appropriate.
The fundamentals of the Swiss real estate market remain robust. 
Accelerating economic growth should support employment and 
population growth over the coming quarters, benefiting demand for 
residential and office space. At the same time, construction activity 
remains subdued in key segments. This combination continues to 
create favorable conditions for rising rents and growing earnings at 
real estate companies.
Valuation gains likely to lose momentum
We expect the SNB to gradually raise its policy rate to 0.5% next 
year. Higher inflation expectations and the prospect of rising policy 
rates have already led to higher long-term yields in Switzerland. 
Against this backdrop, further reductions in discount rates appear 
unlikely, and future increases in property values should primarily 
result from rental growth. 
The fading tailwind for valuations is already evident in the stock 
market. From early September through 26 October 2026, Swiss real 
estate funds declined 1.2%, while real estate stocks were broadly 
unchanged. Year to date, real estate stocks remain up 2.6%, 
compared with a 2.5% decline for real estate funds. Given historically 
high valuations and continued brisk issuance through capital increases 
and new issues, we see upside potential as limited. Nevertheless, we 
continue to consider a neutral positioning in Swiss real estate stocks 
and funds appropriate, given their diversification benefits.
Regulatory risks return to focus
The regulatory environment is likely to remain uncertain. The submitted 
rental price initiative and a possible counterproposal from the Federal 
Council are likely to bring rental market regulation more firmly into the 
political spotlight. Restrictions on current practices for new lettings 
could dampen rental growth and earnings prospects for existing 
properties, weighing on their valuations accordingly. This would 
represent an additional price risk for residential real estate funds.

Equity selection: An assessment relative to a benchmark
Equity selections in Equity Preferences lists (EPLs) are assessments made relative to a sector/industry, country/regional or thematic benchmark. 
The chosen benchmark is disclosed on the front page of each EPL. It is also used to measure the performance of the EPL owner(s). Including 
a stock in the EPL constitutes neither a view on its expected, standalone absolute performance nor a price target. Rather, EPLs are meant to 
support the UBS House View, with the stocks included in them selected for their superior risk/return profiles.
Our selection is based on an assessment of the company’s fundamental outlook and valuation, the risks owning the stock entails, including material

managed futures (collectively, alternative investments). Interests of alternative investment funds are sold only to qualified 
investors, and only by means of offering documents that include information about the risks, performance and expenses of alternative 
investment funds, and which clients are urged to read carefully before subscribing and retain. An investment in an alternative 
investment fund is speculative and involves significant risks. Specifically, these investments 
1.	are not mutual funds and are not subject to the same regulatory requirements as mutual funds; 
2.	may have performance that is volatile, and investors may lose all or a substantial amount of their investment; 
3.	may engage in leverage and other speculative investment practices that may increase the risk of investment loss; 
4.	 are long-term, illiquid investments, there is generally no secondary market for the interests of a fund, and none is expected to develop; 
5.	interests of alternative investment funds typically will be illiquid and subject to restrictions on transfer; 
6.	may not be required to provide periodic pricing or valuation information to investors; 
7.	generally involve complex tax strategies and there may be delays in distributing tax information to investors; 
8.	are subject to high fees, including management fees and other fees and expenses, all of which will reduce profits.
Interests in alternative investment funds are not deposits or obligations of, or guaranteed or endorsed by, any bank or other insured 
depository institution, and are not federally insured by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, the Federal Reserve Board, or any 
other governmental agency. Prospective investors should understand these risks and have the financial ability and willingness to accept 
them for an extended period of time before making an investment in an alternative investment fund and should consider an alternative 
investment fund as a supplement to an overall investment program.
In addition to the risks that apply to alternative investments generally, the following are additional risks related to an investment in 
these strategies:
•	 Hedge Fund Risk: There are risks specifically associated with investing in hedge funds, which may include risks associated with 
investing in short sales, options, small-cap stocks, “junk bonds,” derivatives, distressed securities, non-U.S. securities and illiquid 
investments.
•	 Managed Futures: There are risks specifically associated with investing in managed futures programs. For example, not all 
managers focus on all strategies at all times, and managed futures strategies may have material directional elements.

involve risks associated with debt, adverse changes in general economic or local market conditions, changes in governmental, tax, 
real estate and zoning laws or regulations, risks associated with capital calls and, for some real estate products, the risks associated

•	 Private Equity: There are risks specifically associated with investing in private equity. Capital calls can be made on short no-tice, and 
the failure to meet capital calls can result in significant adverse consequences including, but not limited to, a total loss of investment.
•	 Private Credit: There are risks specifically associated with investing in private credit. This could include losses stemming from 
defaults on loans, which in significant adverse circumstances could result in a substantial loss of investment.
•	 Foreign Exchange/Currency Risk: Investors in securities of issuers located outside of the United States should be aware that even 
for securities denominated in U.S. dollars, changes in the exchange rate between the U.S. dollar and the issuer’s “home” currency

risks (such as political, economic or regulatory changes) that may not be readily known to a U.S. investor.
