 
"Supply Weakness Likely To Linger Into 2027": 
Refinery Outages Blow Past Goldman's Own Worst 
Case 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, OCT 26, 2026 - 07:40 PM 
Oil is sliding on Monday. The barrels people actually burn are not. 
Brent was down 1.4% at $115.04 and WTI down 2% at $101.24 in early New York trading, per 
Bloomberg, as Washington and Tehran stuck to their familiar scripts and top security officials 
from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey headed to Iran to try to cool things down. It is the usual 
Monday de-escalation trade, which in this war has had a habit of reversing by Friday's close. 
Goldman's commodities team, meanwhile, says the real story has moved downstream. In 
Sunday night's "Oil Products Tracker: Supply Weakness Likely to Linger into 2027" (available to 
pro subs), Filippo Cuscito, Daan Struyven and Yulia Zhestkova Grigsby lay out why refined fuel 
stays tight even as crude cools: 
Persistent refinery damage continues to constrain products supply, with Middle East outages 
around 1.6mb/d above seasonal norms and Russian outages 4.2mb/d above seasonal norms... 
Deferred maintenance is likely to prolong product supply weakness into 2027, with planned 
maintenance rising sharply in 2027Q2 after this year's unusually light fall maintenance season. 
In other words, the war premium never left the barrel; it moved to the refinery gate, the tanker 
charter and the pump, and none of the three gets fixed by a Monday headline out of Tehran. 
 

 
 
Bearish Crude, Bullish Everything Made From It 
The context matters here, because this is not a team of oil bulls. Just last week Goldman said 
Brent was carrying a war risk premium of nearly $25/bbl above fair value, and it still expects 
prices to slide to $90 by December and average $80 in 2027. That followed the bank's "nothing 
left to drain" warning on inventories earlier in October, and Friday's note from its trading desk 
laying out the two ways the war premium ends: "sell the news, or $120." 
Yet the same team that wants you underweight crude wants you long fuel. That is the tell. 
Goldman thinks the crude premium can bleed out while the products premium sticks, and 
Sunday's note explains why with four problems: freight, Gulf export logistics, broken refineries 
and a maintenance bill that comes due next spring. 
Not everyone thinks crude is done either. BofA's head of commodities Francisco Blanch told 
Bloomberg TV that if Iran goes back to hitting regional infrastructure, $120 a barrel is "probably 
on the cards" for the next few months. 
$24 To Ship A Barrel (It Was $3 A Year Ago) 
Start with the boats. Goldman writes that its global average crude freight rate "reached a new 
high of $24/bbl in October." A year ago that figure was about $3, which is why last week we 
 

 
described Goldman's freight teardown as "Seven Times Higher." Two consequences, per the 
note: 
●​ It is squeezing net margins for refineries that rely on long-haul crude imports (read: Asia 
and Europe). 
●​ It is pulling some product tankers into hauling crude, "reducing product shipping 
capacity." 
Translation: when it pays more to carry crude, clean tankers go dirty, and the gasoline, diesel 
and jet those ships would have carried stays where it was refined. High freight hurts crude 
buyers once. It hurts fuel buyers twice. 
Naturally, we were right on top of it. Regular readers know we have been tracking the tanker 
rate crisis since February 20, a week before the first shots of the war, and called every crude 
freight index printing a record on October 6. A day later, the long-haul routes went parabolic 
(and note this is route-level, well above Goldman's global average):

The Gulf Is Shipping Crude Again. Fuel, Not So Much 
The second problem is the Persian Gulf itself. Estimated crude exports are back near their 2025 
average, but refined product exports are not, and the gap is "supporting prices." Goldman's 
chart (chart source: Kpler, Goldman Sachs GIR) says it all: latest Gulf exports are running at 
92% of the 2025 level for crude (15.3mb/d), versus 54% for jet fuel, 34% for gasoil/diesel and a 
measly 10% for gasoline. 
 

 
 
Why the divergence? The workaround that got crude moving doesn't work for fuel. Per the note, 
the ship-to-ship transfers supporting crude flows "are harder to replicate for products, whose 
greater flammability and specification variability complicate transfer options outside the Persian 
Gulf." On top of that, bigger crude cargoes spread the fixed security bill (including crew 
compensation for strike risk) over more barrels. 
Put differently, the Hormuz shuttle was built for crude. If you are paying war-risk money per 
voyage, you load the biggest, simplest, least flammable cargo you can, and that is never a 
tanker of jet fuel. This is the flip side of the paradox we flagged when Goldman said Gulf exports 
had roared back: the barrels came back, the products didn't. 
Two Continents Of Broken Refineries (And Goldman's Worst 
Case Is Already Here) 
The third problem is the one that carries the note. Middle East refinery outages are running 
around 1.6mb/d above seasonal norms and Russian outages 4.2mb/d above seasonal norms. 
Goldman doesn't add the two (they are different regions and different refineries), but by our 
napkin math that is roughly 5.8mb/d of refining capacity offline beyond what is normal for this 
time of year. 
Why does that number matter? Because back in September, Goldman's Nikhil Bhandari warned 
of a "nightmare refining crisis" that could keep fuel prices high through 2027 (his full note is 
 

 
available to pro subs). His bear case, "Scenario 2," assumed Middle East and Russian outages 
stuck at 5.0mb/d above seasonal norms through the rest of 2026 and all of 2027, alongside just 
0.5mb/d of demand growth. A month later, on our rough math, actual outages are already 
running above Goldman's prolonged-disruption scenario. 
And Russia's number isn't getting smaller: Ukraine kept hitting Russian refineries even after 
Trump's proclaimed "energy ceasefire," and Washington is now reduced to importing Russian 
diesel under a Trump-Moscow fuel deal. Meanwhile the one big refining system with spare 
capacity has none left: US refinery utilization just hit its highest since 1998 per the EIA (as John 
Kemp flagged this morning). Running flat out is great for margins. It is also what you do right 
before you need a long maintenance shutdown. 
The Maintenance Bill Comes Due In Q2 2027 
Which brings us to the fourth problem, and the reason Goldman's timeline stretches into next 
year. This fall's refinery maintenance season has been unusually light: planned global 
maintenance is down 1.7mb/d, or 27%, year over year, "largely driven by postponed 
maintenance in the US." Deferred maintenance is not cancelled maintenance. It is a loan, and 
Goldman says it comes due in the second quarter of 2027, when planned outages rise sharply. 
The jump is biggest relative to seasonal norms in Asia ex-China, led by India and South Korea, 
two of the world's biggest product exporters. Here is the bank's Exhibit 2 (chart source: IIR, 
Goldman Sachs GIR; seasonal norm is the 2018-2025 median excluding 2020): 
 
Reading off the chart, the 2027 planned-maintenance line peaks at roughly 7.7mb/d around 
May, against roughly 6mb/d at this year's peak, and the Asia ex-China bar for Q2 2027 runs 
 

 
right off the top of Goldman's chart at well over 140% of its seasonal norm. Given their export 
role, Goldman warns high maintenance in India and South Korea "could reduce product exports 
in 2027Q2, particularly for diesel and jet fuel." 
And the kicker: "deferring maintenance increases risks of unplanned outages which can tighten 
product supply further." Think of it as skipping the oil change on a car you are driving at redline. 
America skipped it this fall, India and Korea take their turn next spring, and the Russian and 
Gulf plants that are already damaged still have to be fixed in the meantime. 
No Cushion Anywhere 
All of which would matter less if there were inventories to lean on. There aren't. Goldman says 
high-frequency product stocks "remain low across major hubs, leaving limited buffers against 
additional supply disruptions": 
●​ US gasoline and diesel stocks are still below their historical range. 
●​ China's gasoline and diesel inventories are down 8% and 11% year over year, and 
Singapore middle distillates are down 44%. 
●​ At Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Antwerp, jet fuel stocks have resumed drawing and are down 
56% year over year, because local refiners keep prioritizing diesel. 
That last line tells you which shortage Europe's refiners fear most: they would rather run out of 
jet fuel than diesel. And when every refiner maximizes diesel, something else gets squeezed, 
which is exactly the logic behind Goldman's gasoline trade (more on that below). It is also why 
the G7's emergency release, the one that came after Trump's diesel threat worked, barely 
dented the curve. As we noted at the time:

Demand Is Already Cracking. It Hasn't Helped 
The demand-destruction crowd has a point, and Goldman concedes it. Global average retail fuel 
prices are around $223/bbl, which is "weighing on demand." Goldman's nowcasts put European 
oil demand and US gasoline demand down 2% year over year, and jet demand is soft too, with 
November up only 73kb/d year over year and 3% below trend. 
 

 
So demand is doing what the textbooks say it should. The problem is that supply is doing what 
the war says it should. The thing that is supposed to bring fuel prices down is already in the 
data, and the stocks are still drawing. 
Where demand destruction does show up is in gasoline. Per Bloomberg's Oil Barometers, the 
Nymex gasoline crack fell to $27.94/bbl on Friday, its lowest since April, while the heating oil 
(diesel) crack sat at $98.45, roughly 3.5x the gasoline crack by our math. The Brent curve, 
meanwhile, is not buying the demand scare at all: the December-December spread hit 
$29.98/bbl, the strongest backwardation on record on a rolling basis. 
Why Goldman Wants Summer Gasoline, Not Diesel 
Which brings us to the trade. Goldman first recommended buying European gasoline for June 
2027 back in mid-September, in a note titled "High Diesel Prices Cause High Gasoline Prices," 
when it also closed a European diesel spread for a potential 45% gain on the view that diesel 
already priced in a lot of disruption. As we wrote at the time, the diesel crisis is setting up the 
next gasoline squeeze: refiners switching output to diesel tighten gasoline, where "less elevated 
price levels leave room for sharp price upside." On Sunday, the bank doubled down: 
Against a highly uncertain supply outlook for the Mideast and Russia, we reiterate our 
recommendation to be long 2027 European summer gasoline to hedge further geopolitical 
escalation. 
The logic stacks up neatly. Summer 2027 is when Asia's deferred maintenance peaks, when 
driving season starts, and when any refinery that limped through this winter on skipped 
maintenance is most likely to break. Gasoline is the cheap leg today (at least on the US side, 
where the crack is the lowest since April), so it is the cheapest way to own the products 
squeeze. Note the word Goldman chose, though: it is a hedge, not a forecast, which is what you 
call a position when you want to be right without having to be early. 
Crude Can Be Talked Down. Refineries Can't 
The last word goes to Goldman's own summary of the backdrop: the "tight fundamental 
backdrop and high crude and products risk premia keep global average retail fuel prices 
elevated at around $223/bbl." 
Our take: Monday's dip in crude is the market pricing a deal. The products market is pricing the 
plumbing, and the plumbing is worse than Goldman's own worst case: outages above its 
prolonged-disruption scenario, freight at a record, Gulf product exports at a fraction of normal, 
 

 
inventories at hub lows and a maintenance wall arriving in Q2. There are two ways this goes. 
Either a durable peace deal lets Russian and Gulf refineries get repaired before next spring and 
the summer gasoline hedge expires worthless, or - more likely - the deal headlines come and 
go, the refineries stay broken, and the bill shows up at the pump right on schedule for driving 
season, with OAT spreads and 5% Treasury yields already giving central banks enough 
headaches without an energy shock to go with them. 
Crude can be talked down by a Monday headline. You can't talk a refinery back online.
