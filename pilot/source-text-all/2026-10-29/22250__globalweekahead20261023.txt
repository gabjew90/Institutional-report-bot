Pay Now, Less Later 
Warning—a short introduction has merit considering what lies ahead in this publication! Much of the content pulls together core arguments 
that explain the Bank of Canada outlook in response to varied queries for a single deeper dive that draws on client presentations, meetings 
and publications. The crux of the issue is to pay a little more on borrowing costs now so as to lower the risk of paying vastly more in future 
alongside bigger risks to the economy. 
A packed week includes multiple central bank decisions—including majors like the Fed, BoC, ECB and BoJ—alongside several elections in 
Brazil, Israel, and the Canadian province of British Columbia. The UK budget and a wave of global GDP and inflation figures may also pose 
significant market risk. So could earnings, with 168 S&P500 firms releasing including more of the ‘Magnificent 7’, such as Amazon 
(Thursday), Alphabet (Wednesday) and Microsoft (Wednesday). Their reports may be key to wavering risk appetite toward tech stocks. 
Fifty-eight TSX firms also refresh earnings estimates. 
It will also be the final full week of campaigning ahead of the November 3rd US midterm elections as poll mapping onto scenarios for the 
House and Senate are firmed up, and we could see potential trial balloons ahead of Canada’s November 5th federal budget. 
BANK OF CANADA PREVIEW—TO HIKE IS MORE FORGIVING THAN TO HOLD 
The Bank of Canada is expected to hike its overnight rate by 25bps on Wednesday alongside a full suite of forecasts and communications. 
Where and When to Find It 
The statement will appear here, the Monetary Policy Report with the forecasts will be here, and Governor Macklem’s opening remarks will 
appear here, all at 9:45amET. The press conference held by Macklem and SDG Rogers will be available at 10:30amET here, or at CPAC (link 
tbd), or on other news media sites and Bloomberg at live <go>. The usual recommendation is to run more than one of them simultaneously 
given frequent tech and/or translation issues. 
The Path Forward 
Don’t expect terribly clear forward guidance. Macklem hates explicit guidance and tends to speak in cryptic, somewhat cagey ways, at least 
ever since he overreached on forward guidance in the pandemic. The BoC will let its forecasts do the talking. 
Updated Projections 
We’ve already heard from Macklem that the BoC will raise its growth and inflation forecasts; he said so at the IMF retreat in Bangkok. Chart 1 
compares the BoC’s July forecasts for GDP to our current forecast; we think they’ll need to revise up 2026–28 but are unsure of how much 
in relation to our views.  
Chart 2 compares inflation forecasts and here too 
we think they’ll revise up materially but by how 
much and over what time is uncertain.  
A caution is always that our forecast embeds our 
known 100bps of hikes and the BoC’s projections 
will embed an unknown and unpublished policy 
rate trajectory. The BoC does not provide explicit 
forward guidance like the FOMC, or Norges Bank 
etc. Yet absence monetary tightening, we would 
project higher inflation over a longer period.

Low Surprise Risk 
A hike is about 85%+ priced. At the time of writing, it isn’t clear what the trimmed consensus is because about half of the domestic banks’ 
economics shops have stuck their heads in the sand. There is, however, a definite gulf between being 85% priced in markets versus waffling 
economists.  
Is a surprise hold instead of a hike possible? Yes, it has happened in the past. It has never happened under Governor Macklem when it has 
been priced as high as now.  
It would be more awkward not to hike. One reason is that it would ease financial conditions at an awkward juncture for inflation risks and 
would likely do so by driving lower borrowing costs than what is priced and a weaker Canadian dollar. Another is that it could make the BoC

So Why Hike? 
Scotiabank Economics has been forecasting a 2026H2 hike since last November as a reversal of that Fall’s two insurance cuts that were 
expected to work through to firmer growth, as happened. It’s not just about higher commodities. A Taylor Rule approximation of the policy 
rate indicated that this was an undershoot of where the rate should have been, thereby positioning the reductions as insurance cuts that 
would likely be taken back when they worked later on. And they did work which I’ll come back to. 
We delayed that hike to 2026Q4 back in January 
and have been there since. In choosing either 
October or December, our guidance on meeting 
dates has for many weeks been that October is a 
‘live’ meeting and we formalized the October call 
at the start of this past week. There should be no 
surprises about our long-held guidance and 
overall consistency. Recall by contrast that 
markets and other strategists and economists 
were debating and pricing rate cuts from Q4 last 
year throughout much of this year. 
One key reason to hike is that all measures of 
inflation are roughly on or above the 2% target 
and so if they’re data dependent, then on that 
count alone they have cause to go. Headline CPI 
is at 3.1% y/y. Trimmed mean CPI and weighted 
median CPI are running at 1.8% y/y and 1.9% y/y 
respectively but these measures are not spot y/y measures. They are rather slow-moving m/m weighted contributions summed up to a 
slow moving, lagging metric that takes too long to identify turning points. Traditional core CPI (ex-food and energy) is at 2.1% y/y. CPI 
excluding the either most volatile components and indirect taxes is up by 2.2% y/y.  
Fresher price signals look at these measures on m/m SAAR terms and after applying some 
smoothing via a three-month moving average. On that basis, total CPI is at 3.6%, traditional 
core CPI is 2.6%. CPIX is 2.5%, CPI ex-8 is 2.2%, weighted median is 2.7% and trimmed mean is 
2.6%. Chart 5 shows the trimmed mean and weighted median measures.The evidence on 
inflation is crystal clear across a whole suite of measures taken together—inflation is on or 
above target and most likely the latter with clear trend evidence of pass through into broader 
price pressures.

You May Dislike the Alternative More than Hiking Now 
The BoC has to be less confident that it has imminent inflation risk under control within the 
horizon of lagging monetary policy effects on the economy and inflation. At an absolute 
minimum there is a strong case for taking out insurance against upside risks to inflation. 
There is added reason for taking out insurance on its ability to forecast inflation. Chart 17 
compares actual inflation to successive MPR forecasts; the BoC routinely underestimates 
inflation and misses inflection points. It remains to be seen whether a wholesale revamping of 
its models that has been underway will improve upon this track record. 
The BoC did not do that in the pandemic. It waited until it felt it had all the answers while 
denying there was an inflation problem for far too long. The result is in the history books.  
People may not like higher rates now. Yet repeating the same mistake would raise the odds of far bigger rate hikes later to counter far 
higher inflation after letting the problem fester and build upon itself.

So, if you don’t want a hike this week or if the Bank of Canada whiffs, then frankly, don’t complain when you face the higher probability of

The Bank of Canada is Probably Already Behind 
So why is the Bank of Canada still sitting at the low end of neutral and at a real policy rate that is around zero if not negative depending on 
the measure of inflation or expected inflation? If you’re on or above the 2% inflation target and slack is shutting with rising upside inflation 
risk, then policy should be acting now if not having already hiked. The case for being behind the curve is based upon solid inflation tracking 
and the lagging effects of monetary policy adjustments that can take 6–18 months to have most of its effects.  
Growth prospects are another consideration. Even with 100bps of tightening in our forecast we are projecting real (inflation-adjusted) GDP 
growth of about 2¼% in 2027. Absent such tightening we would be pushing into the 2½–3% range. You might think great! But the Bank of 
Canada’s estimated potential rate of growth in Canada’s economy is 1.1% in 2026 and 1.3% in 2027 as per the July MPR pending any future 
updates. If the BoC did not hike in a material way, then the economy would probably transition toward further toward excess demand and 
greater inflationary pressures. 
Common Myths 
Now for some myths about tightening that are sometimes advanced. Some of them are reminiscent of the same myths that were promoted 
by some throughout the pandemic. 
One is a repeat of the pandemic line from other forecast shops that the BoC shouldn’t hike because inflation is supply-driven and monetary 
policy can’t control supply shocks. It should therefore look through this type of inflation. The counter is that the BoC’s job is to equalize 
supply and demand through the net impact on slack such that it doesn’t matter if inflation is generated through a negative supply shock or 
a pick-up in demand or both. The BoC has to target balanced conditions and does so by pulling on the levers that exert influence upon 
interest-sensitive demand drivers. 
A second myth is that you can’t hike because you’ll damage the demand side. Yet that’s the whole point! There is no immaculate 
disinflation. You’ve got to break some teacups to use the same metaphor I used in the pandemic’s inflation surge. To repeat the point 
above, if there is no tightening of monetary policy, then inflation risk would be higher. 
A third myth is that housing would be crushed and that could sideswipe demand for skilled labour. This is worth a few counter points: 
• 
The worst damage that you can do to housing is to allow inflation to rip and then slam the 
brakes on later. Hiking earlier than later could mitigate the risk of even higher rates later on 
and with it more damage to housing. Homebuilders need to understand that the worst risk 
to their market is a repeat of runaway inflation. 
• 
Short-term drivers of economic activity—like consumer spending, housing and short-term 
government spending—remain dominant as shares of GDP in ways that harken back to the 
1980s (chart 18). The Carney administration is trying to pivot growth drivers toward longer-
term investment-led growth that will need resources. That would be made more difficult 
by maintaining such high shares of activity going into shorter-term spending.  
• 
Not much can be done about oversupply in condos in any event; that has to be worn off 
over time and it’s not a rates issue versus shaking off prior greed.  
• 
I still think that the Carney administration will be raising immigration targets—this time 
more so in permanent, highly skilled categories—given reports of skilled labour shortages 
among small businesses, the need for specialized labour, and as the massive overshoot in 
temps works off and gets assimilated.  
• 
Besides, the 5-year mortgage rate is driven by the 5-year GoC yield that has risen but at about 3.65%% remains low in real terms even 
if not as low as the pandemic-generation recalls.

A fourth myth is that you can’t cut because the job market is tanking. First, inflation is job #1 and the job market and wage-inflation 
connections are not strong enough in the face of inflation’s many and complex drivers to thwart inflation risk. Second, I still don’t believe 
the jobs numbers (here). They asked us to believe that public sector jobs in health and education sectors in Quebec were cut by tens of 
thousands in the middle of an election campaign. There are quirks to the Labour Force Survey that merit downplaying the reading. 
Attempts at scraping the internet for job cut announcements reveal a few thousand nationwide at most and not just in a single month. I 
wouldn’t be surprised to see a rebound the next month. Further, the Feds are smothering the shock to jobs in some sectors with recent 
support announcements that may or may not be wise over time. 
Fifth on the myths list is that you can’t hike until you have clarity on trade policy risks. Here too there are several counters: 
• 
One counter is that you would never hike rates if the threat of misguided US trade policy lurked around the corner because it is likely to 
remain misguided while inflation risk creeps higher and higher.  
• 
Even if Canada signs an agreement with the US, it would always be a concern that the US signature’s value has been deeply diminished 
by the Trump administration.  
• 
Another counter is that trade policy risks may raise inflation risks; within reason, controlling inflation is the BoC’s job, while dealing with 
the adjustments is more of a job for fiscal and regulatory policies.  
• 
Another counter is that it’s highly unlikely Trump self-immolates the US economy with 50% auto tariffs on Canada that would destroy 
his own auto sector given the high cross-border integration of supply chains.  
• 
Another counter is that Canada has been afraid of the Sword of Damocles felling the nation’s businesses and leaders for years, yet 
exports have been remarkably resilient via offsets such as US growth, CAD depreciation and high commodities. There is a higher bar 
set against the gloomsters on trade after basically six quarters of export resilience dating back to ‘Liberation Day.’ in April 2025. 
A sixth myth is that market volatility should mean standing pat on rates. Yet volatility is the one constant in markets. The TSX is only about 
3% off its peak, and the S&P 500 is about 2% off the high. Sovereign bond yields seem to be rapidly reversing some of the sell-off in rates 
with the US 10-year now down 3bps from the high and the Canadian 10-year yield now down by under 20bps. A large, sustained stock 
market sell off could change the picture, but we’re nowhere close to such concerns. 
AI-DRIVEN INFLATION IS JUST BEGINNING IN CANADA 
Here’s something you won’t find in any Phillips curve model of Canadian inflation just as it wasn’t to be found in any comparable US model. 
AI-driven inflation has only just begun to show up in Canada. Just as the Federal Reserve feels it must raise rates in part to address this type 
of price shock, the Bank of Canada will probably come under pressure to follow suit.  
This could be a further source of upward pressure on our existing inflation forecast that requires 100bps of tightening to drive inflation 
toward about 2½% y/y by the end of next year and about 2¼% into 2028. That forecast does not yet incorporate a meaningful impact from 
AI-driven inflation. If so, then our 100bps of forecast interest rate hikes is likely light. 
Key is the relatively sudden onslaught of data centre development—including hyperscalers—and plans for an enormous increase in 
planned gigawatts over coming years. From about 300 now, there are plans to build hundreds more data centres in Canada. Numbers of 
centres are not the best guide, however, since they don’t power-weight the size and scope of the facilities. That’s why mega- or gigawatts 
are preferred. By this yardstick, data centres will soar as shown in two recent estimates from independent sources in Jay Parmar’s chart-of-
the-week on the front cover of this publication. Colliers (here) estimates that Canada has about 1.415GW of commissioned data centre 
capacity now, particularly in Ontario and Quebec. They estimate 5.5 GW of planned capacity, led by Alberta but also with BC and 
Saskatchewan participating. Researchers at Toronto’s York University led much of the work on tracking Canadian data centres (here). 
Hyperscalers are part of this, including Meta’s 1 GW facility in Alberta. Bell Canada’s AI Fabric centre in Saskatchewan is another 300MW. 
We’ve already known for some time that producer price inflation across electrical components has been picking up (chart 19). Ditto for the 
US and more powerfully so.

UK BUDGET—FISCAL PRESSURES PUT BANK SURCHARGE IN PLAY 
My colleague Jay Parmar explains in this section that in the UK, attention turns to Chancellor John Healey’s first Budget on Wednesday 
under the new Andy Burnham government, against a difficult backdrop of elevated borrowing costs relative to peers (chart 34), an energy-
driven growth shock and mounting cost of living pressures. The central challenge will be balancing market demands for fiscal discipline, 
with debt elevated and borrowing running ahead of OBR projections, against pressure for household relief and no further tax increases on 
businesses. That balancing act has become harder as weaker growth and higher inflation following the Middle East war are estimated to 
have reduced fiscal headroom to £10–20 billion, from £23.6 billion following Chancellor Reeves’ earlier consolidation. 
The room for manoeuvre therefore looks limited 
given existing spending commitments, including 
higher defence spending targets, alongside 
pressure to provide new support for households. 
The government may, however, have some 
flexibility on capital spending under the fiscal 
framework. Further revenue raising measures are 
likely, but an already elevated tax burden (chart 
35), alongside pledges not to raise income tax, 
National Insurance, VAT or capital gains tax, 
narrows the options. One possibility is an increase 
in the bank surcharge from 3% to as high as 8%. 
Spending restraint offers an alternative, though 
commitments to maintain the costly pension 
“triple lock” limit the scope for cuts, leaving welfare 
spending as potential area for savings. 
With fiscal space limited and the government 
unlikely to want to risk adding to the BoE’s inflation challenge, the Budget is therefore likely to favour targeted, temporary support rather 
than a large fiscal package. This would limit the risk of exacerbating inflationary pressures, pushing gilt yields higher and raising debt-
servicing costs, which would further erode the government’s fiscal room.

Bank of Canada (BoC): The Bank of Canada is expected to deliver its first rate hike since July 2023 on Wednesday, with markets pricing an 85% chance of a 25bp 
move following Governor Macklem’s hawkish comments and the latest inflation and BoC survey data. Macklem noted that higher oil prices are “having a positive 
effect on growth” and reiterated that the bank expects to raise its inflation forecast in the October MPR. He further added that policymakers will assess whether 
inflation is returning to 2% or whether rate hike is needed to alter its path. With inflation still above 2% on average and commodity prices elevated, price pressures 
remain a concern. The policy statement, MPR and Macklem’s opening remarks are due at 9:45am ET, followed by the press conference at 10:30am ET. Federal Open 
Market Committee (FOMC): After delivering a 25bp hike in September and guiding toward another increase this year, the FOMC is expected to pause on Wednesday, 
with markets pricing a further 25bp hike in December. The minutes showed most participants leaning toward another hike by year-end, but with no commitment to 
an October move. Further patience may also be warranted ahead of the November 16 “field hearing” of Warsh’s task force examining AI and its impact on 
productivity. The FOMC will release its policy statement at 2pm ET, followed by Chair Warsh’s press conference at 2:30 ET.
European Central Bank (ECB): The European Central Bank is expected to stand pat on Thursday after a 25bp hike in September, with markets pricing a 70% chance 
of another hike in December. At its last meeting, the ECB judged that “inflation is set to remain well above target for an extended period,” prompting markets to price 
nearly two additional hikes by year-end. Since then, however, financial conditions have tightened, with President Lagarde noting that higher long-term rates “will slow 
growth and reduce pass-through by more than projected in our September exercise.” Policymakers have also noted limited evidence of second-round inflation 
effects, consistent with soft core inflation in September. Still, elevated energy prices leave upside risks to inflation and downside risks to growth. With more inflation 
and Q3 growth data, US midterm elections, and Middle East uncertainty ahead of December meeting, policymakers have room to pause and reassess the outlook 
alongside an updated set of forecasts. Focus at this meeting will be on Lagarde’s press conference and the ECB’s wage tracker, due the following week, which 
continues to signal firm wage growth next year.
Banco Central de Chile: The Central Bank of Chile is expected to keep its overnight rate unchanged on Tuesday and retain a meeting-by-meeting approach. While 
uncertainty around the Middle East conflict and oil prices remains elevated, consecutive slowdowns in economic activity and a higher unemployment rate should 
keep the bank on hold as it assesses the risk of a prolonged domestic slowdown. Core inflation remains steady at 3.2%, while two-year inflation expectations are well 
anchored at 3%. This supports a cautious stance, balancing upside inflation risks from a potential energy shock against downside risks from weaker domestic activity. 
Banco de la República de Colombia: After a series of unexpected rate decisions by the Central Bank of Colombia, the consensus is now betting on a hold, with the 
bank expected to leave its lending rate unchanged at 12.25% on Friday. At its last meeting, the bank delivered a surprise 25bp hike, backed by four members, while 
two voted for no change and one favoured a 50bp increase. The bank left the door open to further tightening, if needed, depending on the fiscal adjustment process, 
the impact of El Niño and the recovery from the August earthquake. However, despite still-elevated inflation and inflation expectations, weaker economic activity, 
peso appreciation and uncertainty over fiscal adjustment measures amid IMF talks could favour a pause as policymakers seek greater clarity on the outlook.
Bank of Japan (BoJ): After raising rates by 25bp at its last meeting, the Bank of Japan is unanimously expected to pause on Friday, while continuing its gradual policy 
normalisation. However, Governor Ueda struck a more hawkish tone at September's press conference, highlighting a shift in the policy objective from lifting 
underlying inflation to stabilising it around 2%, now that underlying inflation is “very close” to target. He also noted that the risk of inflation overshooting the target 
has increased, citing the Middle East war, strong AI-related demand and the weak yen. Producer prices are already elevated, while medium- to long-term inflation 
expectations have picked up. Still, despite retaining guidance for further rate hikes as economic and financial conditions evolve, Ueda signalled no urgency for back-
to-back moves. Attention will therefore centre on the updated inflation and growth forecasts, followed by Ueda’s press conference for his assessment of whether 
underlying inflation has reached 2% and the extent of the risk of an overshoot. Reserve Bank of New Zealand (RBNZ): The RBNZ is expected to deliver a third 
consecutive 25bp hike on Tuesday, with markets pricing an 80% probability. Since September, stronger activity, weaker dollar, and persistently elevated energy 
prices support further gradual withdrawal of monetary stimulus. Q2 GDP rose 0.2% q/q versus the bank’s forecast for flat growth, while Q1 was revised slightly higher, 
pointing to diminishing spare capacity. Labour market growth also strengthened, while Q3 data showed broadening price pressures, with core inflation rising to 2.9% 
after six quarters at 2.5%. Another 25bp hike therefore appears likely before the bank pauses to assess the medium-term inflation risks, including uncertainty around 
energy prices and El Niño. Bank of Thailand (BoT): Consensus unanimously expects the BoT to hold its repo rate unchanged on Wednesday and maintain a data-
dependent stance, with the next move potentially going “either way.” Despite a recent pickup in inflation, Governor Vitai Ratanakorn has indicated that Thai inflation 
remains manageable, reducing the need to follow other central banks’ tightening paths. The Governor further added that the BoT is also likely to preserve policy 
space as a buffer against a potential economic downturn.
