China’s High-Tech Push: Support for Output, Less for Employment 
 
 
China’s policymakers have long prioritized industrial upgrading and technological 
self-reliance as central to the country’s development strategy. From the launch of “Made 
in China 2025” in 2015 to the emphasis on high-tech industries and AI in the 15th 
Five-Year Plan (FYP) for 2026–30, policy support has focused on strengthening domestic 
capabilities and global competitiveness in strategic industries, including 
semiconductors, advanced robotics, biotechnology, AI and related digital technologies 
(Exhibit 1). 
This agenda has become more urgent amid intensifying technological competition with 
the US. Recent policy signals on advancing the integration of technological and industrial 
innovation underscores policymakers’ intention to accelerate the commercial application 
of innovation and AI adoption. Meanwhile, the government has placed renewed 
emphasis on the broader “Six Networks” initiative – covering water networks, new-type 
power grids, computing-power networks, next-generation communications networks, 
urban underground pipeline networks, and logistics networks – as a means of supporting 
near-term investment while addressing longer-term infrastructure, resilience, and 
innovation needs. 
For investors, the key questions are whether high-tech sectors can generate sufficient 
growth to offset the continuing drag from the property downturn, how far this 
expansion translates into employment and household income, and how large a 
near-term boost the “Six Networks” initiative, especially data center construction, could 
provide to investment and growth.   
In this report, we build on our previous analysis of high-tech manufacturing by 
broadening the scope to include new energy vehicles (NEVs) manufacturing and 
telecom, IT and software services sectors. This expanded scope captures key parts of 
the AI supply chain, from semiconductors and computing equipment production to 
cloud computing and software services. Using an updated input-output framework, we 
estimate these sectors’ contribution to real GDP growth and assess their implications for 
the labor market. We also size investment across the “Six Networks” during the 15th FYPs 
and gauge the growth impact from China’s data center construction.

Stylized Facts on China’s High-tech Sectors 
China’s high-tech sectors have expanded rapidly over the past decade. Our expanded 
definition covers high-tech manufacturing—including computers, communications and 
electronics; electrical machinery, general and special equipment, pharmaceuticals and 
medical products, instruments and meters, and rail, ship and aircraft manufacturing—as 
well as new energy vehicle manufacturing and telecom, IT and software services. This 
scope broadly aligns with the NBS definition of high-tech sectors. 
The manufacturing industries in this group accounted for around 38% of industrial 
production in 2025, up from 33% in 2021 (Exhibit 2). Over this period, their real output 
grew by an average of 8.3% per year, compared with 5.7% for headline industrial 
production. Investment in our expanded high-tech sectors, including both 
manufacturing and services, also grew faster than investment in the broader economy. 
FAI in these sectors increased by an average of 8.4% per year, compared with 4.7% for 
total FAI, raising their share of total investment from 13% in 2021 to 18.4% in 2025. 
Together, these high-tech sectors directly accounted for 16.4% of GDP in value-added 
terms in 2025, around 6pp more than a decade earlier. 
However, these sectors have a much smaller employment footprint, accounting for only 
10.8% of total employment in 2023, the latest year available, compared with 10.4% in 
2018. Their composition also varies substantially across industries. Computer and 
communications equipment manufacturing accounts for the largest share of production 
and investment within the grouping but only 2.5% of employment, while telecom, IT and 
software services account for the largest share of employment (2.7%). These differences 
suggest that a larger contribution to production does not necessarily translate into a 
proportionate increase in employment. The implications for household income also

High-tech Sector’s Transmission to Growth and the Labor Market 
Relative to the property sector, high-tech sectors’ growing economic importance brings 
greater exposure to foreign demand and does not by itself necessitate a shift toward 
consumption-led growth. 
China’s latest input-output table shows that high-tech sectors rely primarily on domestic 
investment and external demand1. Only around 20% of spending on high-tech products 
and services comes from consumption by households and government, compared with 
roughly 70% for the overall services sector (Exhibit 3, left). Instead, nearly half of 
demand comes from purchases for investment, such as equipment and software, while 
foreign demand (i.e., exports) accounts for the remaining third. In this respect, the 
relatively small consumption share resembles that of the property sector, where 
demand is also dominated by investment, including households’ purchases of new 
properties. 
The main difference is high-tech’s much greater exposure to foreign demand, 
particularly within high-tech manufacturing industries, where exports account for nearly 
half of final demand. A shift in activity from property toward high-tech therefore gives 
exports a greater role in China’s growth outlook, even though high-tech sectors’ own 
export dependence declined somewhat2.

High-tech manufacturing has also become a more important contributor to China’s 
export performance. These industries’ share within nominal goods exports increased by 
around 4pp between 2018 and 2025, while their gross exports rose by approximately 
2pp of GDP over the same period (Exhibit 3, right). The shares have risen further in 2026, 
although that is mainly driven by higher prices from AI-related inflation in those 
high-tech sectors than in the rest of the economy. 
Taken together, exports will be an important factor in sustaining high-tech production 
growth, alongside continued domestic investment. This reliance may partly explain 
policymakers’ lackluster policy support for boosting household consumption. 
 
Beyond their reliance on domestic investment and exports, high-tech sectors generate 
less domestic value added and support fewer jobs per yuan of final spending than either 
the property sector or the overall service sector. We estimate that every RMB100 of final 
spending on high-tech products and related services generates around RMB73 of 
domestic value added, including the value created by these industries and their 
domestic suppliers throughout the supply chain, with the rest leaking abroad through 
imported inputs. This compares with roughly RMB90 for the property sector – including 
construction and real estate services - and RMB92 for the overall services sector (Exhibit 
4, left). The shortfall reflects high-tech sectors’ greater reliance on imported inputs, 
meaning a larger portion of spending leaks into production abroad. Reducing import 
reliance is likely one of the reasons behind policymakers’ intensified efforts to invest in 
home-grown high-tech development. Going forward, rising self-sufficiency in areas such 
as semiconductors will lift the domestic value-added share over time. 
The smaller domestic value-added share also limits how much spending translates into 
labor income. Each RMB100 of final spending on high-tech output generates around 
RMB33 of labor compensation across these industries and their domestic suppliers, 
compared with RMB40 for property and RMB46 for the services sector. Relative to 
property, the gap mainly stems from greater import leakage, as workers in both sectors 
receive a similar share of domestic value added at around 45%. By contrast, the services

meaningfully. The combined contribution of property and high-tech sectors should

A key component of computing network investment is the build-out of data centers. 
Under the “East Data, West Computing” strategy and the broader coordination of 
computing resources and power, energy-rich hubs in Western China are becoming 
increasingly important for capacity expansion. Ulanqab, in particular, is emerging as one 
of the Asia-Pacific region’s largest and fastest-growing AI-computing clusters. Our 
equity analysts estimate that China will account for nearly 20% of global data-center 
capacity additions between 2025 and 2030 (Exhibit 7, left).  
To estimate the growth impact of this build-out, we use our equity analysts’ projections 
for additions to China’s live data-center capacity. Investment is broadly divided between 
physical infrastructure—including buildings, power-supply systems, and cooling 
equipment—and computing hardware, notably servers and AI chips. As a growing share 
of new capacity is designed for AI inference workloads, computing hardware has 
become a much larger component of total data-center investment, compared with 
traditional cloud data centers, where physical infrastructure accounted for the bulk of 
spending. Unlike many other economies, where a meaningful share of electronic 
components is imported, China’s policy focus on technological self-sufficiency likely 
means that a larger share of chips used in domestic data centers will be sourced locally 
especially for state-funded data centers6. This should limit import leakage and raise the 
domestic growth multiplier associated with the investment cycle. Overall, our estimates 
suggest that data center build-out could add around 40bp to the average level of China’s 
GDP between 2026 and 2030.
