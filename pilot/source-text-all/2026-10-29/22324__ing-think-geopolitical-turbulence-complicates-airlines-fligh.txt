Geopolitical turbulence complicates
airlines’ flight paths for 2027
Global air travel growth is set to rebound to 5% in 2027, but airlines face
tougher conditions. Demand remains resilient, but the fuel price shock
will spill over into 2027, while new aircraft supply remains constrained.
This creates divergent fortunes across airlines, segments and regions
The aviation industry now faces a growing challenge to deliver profitable growth as capacity remains
constrained, costs rise, and geopolitical risks intensify
The fuel-price shock will continue into 2027. Jet fuel could account for close to
35% of airlines’ operating costs in 2026, up from 27% in 2025. Tight refinery
capacity and low inventories are likely to keep prices elevated even if oil prices
ease.
European air fares will have further to rise. Hedging initially protected many
European airlines, but that cover will progressively expire during 2027. US fares
have already adjusted more quickly because American carriers tend to hedge
less.
Budget airlines are particularly exposed. Fuel represents a larger share of their
costs, while price-sensitive passengers make it harder to pass on the full
increase. Premium carriers have more scope to raise fares without significantly

Ticket prices are unlikely to return to early-2026 levels. Even if fuel becomes

The Middle East disruption has reshaped global traffic flows. Traffic in the

particular stands out. Asia’s share of the global aircraft fleet is expected to rise
from roughly one-third to around 45%, with India standing out: its fleet could

2026 has turned into the most turbulent year for the airline industry since the pandemic. Once

the year and its effects will spill over into 2027, too.

Cost shock from Middle East disruption most impactful
Air traffic to and from the Middle East has taken a significant hit as a result of the war,
which has led to airspace closures and passengers shifting to other destinations due to safety
concerns. And this comes on top of the Russian airspace, which complicates operations. Traffic
in this pivotal region on the East-West corridor halved in March and April and was still 15%
lower year-on-year in August 2026. Before the war, the Middle East accounted for 9.5% of
global revenue passenger kilometres (RPKs). On balance, the setback in the region shaved
some 2-3 percentage points off global growth with additional impacts elsewhere.
Despite a sharp increase in jet fuel prices, airline response remained constrained, with most
measures limited to cancelling certain unprofitable routes rather than undertaking broader
capacity reductions. All in all, we still expect around 1% global passenger growth in 2026, but
this obviously falls short of the structural 4-5%.
It remains unclear when sustainable peace and stability will return to the Middle East, but Gulf
countries retain ambitious growth plans for hubs such as Dubai and Abu Dhabi, anticipating
strong feeder growth from the expanding middle class in India, the world’s most populous
country.

Rebound to 5% growth expected in 2027, but it needs to be
profitable growth
Airlines enter 2027 in a much more difficult operating environment. Jet fuel prices remain
elevated, geopolitical and security risks persist, and aircraft maintenance and delivery
constraints continue to limit growth. Disruptions are also becoming more frequent, partly due
to more extreme weather. In this environment, the performance gap between airlines is
widening.
Yet air travel demand has proved remarkably resilient despite the turmoil and higher ticket
prices. You might think geopolitical and safety concerns would keep travellers on the ground,
but it appears they more often opt to switch destinations. In Europe, flight traffic had already
moved out of contraction before the summer, pushed by countries like Spain and Italy. This
resilience was evident across all global regions outside the Middle East, with particularly strong
growth in emerging aviation markets such as Africa and Latin America.
Economic growth is expected to pick up in 2027, while the propensity to travel remains strong.
Against this backdrop, we expect global passenger growth to rebound to around 5% in
our base case.

European carriers benefited from the setback on the Middle
East-Asia corridor
Travel patterns changed after the Iran war started, while Middle Eastern carriers and transfer
hubs were hit hardest by airspace closures. European carriers such as Lufthansa and Air
France-KLM benefited from the disruption, partly cushioning the impact of surging fuel prices.
Traffic in the Europe-Asia corridor was still up more than 12% year-on-year in August, while
the Middle East-Asia corridor was down almost 12% following much steeper declines in March-
May. But with the conflict persisting and renewed security incidents, the impact could extend
into 2027.

Cost shock hit profitability, but for some airlines more than
others
Jet fuel is often the largest or second-largest cost item for airlines. In 2025, it accounted for
27% of total operational costs on average. This share could move towards 35% in 2026 as a
result of persistently high jet fuel prices and elevated crack spreads, caused by the loss of
Middle Eastern supplies and tight global availability.
Further geopolitical developments remain highly uncertain. Even if the Middle East conflict is
resolved and oil prices retreat, tight refinery capacity and depleted inventories are likely to
keep middle-distillate prices, including jet fuel, elevated well into 2027.

Fuel hedging provides less cushioning in 2027; fares must
respond
Airlines employ very different strategies to protect themselves against fuel price shocks, using
derivatives based on crude oil or jet fuel. European airlines generally hedged 40-90% of their
jet fuel consumption, cushioning the initial impact of the price shock.

However, the Middle East conflict has lasted too long to avoid a significant impact. Existing
hedges gradually roll off, increasing exposure to prevailing market prices. Ryanair, which
remains (80%) hedged until the second quarter of 2027, has already warned of significantly

Outside, the picture is mixed, but hedge ratios are generally lower, and airlines in the US and
China, for instance, don’t hedge at all. This means that two airlines operating similar routes
can face very different fuel bills.

Fares already sharply higher in the US, less so in Europe
The fuel price shock will increasingly feed through into higher fares. US airlines’ practice of
retaining relatively high exposure to fuel price volatility resulted in an almost immediate pass-
through, with ticket prices rising solidly in the months after jet fuel prices started to rise. It is
also easier to raise prices when domestic competitors face a similar cost shock.
The situation is different in Europe, where hedging provided more initial protection. This also
means fares may still need to rise significantly if fuel prices fail to fall. Coverage gradually
expires as 2027 progresses, leaving airlines increasingly exposed to higher spot prices.

More factors are driving up costs, particularly in Europe
Jet fuel is the clear outlier this year, but wage and maintenance costs are also rising faster
than the average price level. Borrowing costs and aircraft leasing rates remain another factor,
although the impact depends on individual airlines’ strategies and financial structures.
European airlines also face rising costs from the European Union Emissions Trading System
(ETS) for flights within the European Economic Area. In addition, fuel suppliers are required to
blend sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) under ReFuelEU Aviation, starting at 2% and rising to 6%
by 2030*. SAF still trades at a significant premium. This puts a price on emissions, and in
combination, this adds some $300 per tonne of jet fuel in 2026 (BNEF, ING Research).
Meanwhile, France and the Netherlands have either raised or plan to raise ticket taxes,
particularly on long-haul flying, while Germany has scaled its levy back and Sweden has
abolished it.

High fuel prices put more pressure on low-cost carriers
Low-cost carriers are particularly exposed because fuel accounts for a larger share of their cost
base, and profit bases are more vulnerable. High hedge ratios initially cushion volatility, but
these hedges gradually roll off. The more challenging environment is already visible in the
performance of several European low-cost carriers and will remain an important theme in
2027 if fuel prices stay elevated.

Premium carriers have more room to manoeuvre. Demand for premium seats (business,
premium economy) is less price-sensitive, providing airlines with greater scope to pass on
higher costs. Both United Airlines and Delta Airlines reported little impact after price increases
of 15-20% in the second to third quarters.
Passengers are booking later, but airlines continue to indicate a strong propensity to fly,
especially in premium and premium-economy classes. A minority also accounts for a large
share of flights. In the Netherlands, for example, 25% of people account for 75% of journeys.
These frequent travellers tend to be less price-sensitive.
There has therefore been some demand destruction wiping out part of the underlying growth.
So far, though, the propensity to travel has remained resilient.
Aviation remains profitable, but margins hit a post-pandemic
low
The fuel shock has significantly affected margins. On average, profits in the global industry are
expected to drop meaningfully below 5%. Airlines including Ryanair, Southwest Airlines and
AirAsia have scaled back capacity on unprofitable routes as elevated fuel prices persisted
longer than expected.
According to global aviation consultancy Cirium, around 1% of global air travel capacity will be
removed heading into the Northern Hemisphere winter of 2026-27. In less mature markets,
maintaining volumes while passing on the higher fuel bill is more difficult. Airlines may also
prioritise less fuel-efficient, older-generation aircraft over capacity growth.
Industry headwinds could become a catalyst for further consolidation, ranging from minority
stakes to full takeovers.
Fares could retreat if fuel prices ease, but don't expect too
much
Dynamic and algorithm-based pricing is becoming increasingly common. This helps airlines
optimise already historically high load factors (84% in 2026 so far) but can also contribute to
greater fare volatility, especially as passengers book later amid uncertainty.
If fuel prices ease in 2027, fares could drop back. Yet relatively strong demand, constrained
aircraft availability and general inflation mean a return to early-2026 fare levels looks unlikely.

Cargo benefits from high-tech demand and supply-chain
volatility
Air cargo continues to perform well as a fast transport option in an increasingly volatile and
less predictable world. Volume growth remained almost 5% year-to-date, while freight rates
remained attractive for carriers.
While e-commerce drove much of the growth in recent years, digitalisation and large-scale AI
investment increasingly support shipments of high-value technology products. Strong cargo
performance helped cushion pressure on airline results in 2026. European carriers also
benefited from reduced available freight capacity from Middle Eastern competitors.
Freighter capacity received a boost during the pandemic through passenger-to-freighter
conversions, but that boost has largely run its course. New-generation A350 and Boeing 777
freighters should add capacity in the coming years.

India is becoming a powerhouse for future airline growth
Air travel is strongly correlated with GDP and household income. As Asia’s middle class
expands, the potential customer base for air travel swells. For future aviation demand, this
income effect is even more important than population growth alone.
Younger generations also appear keen to travel, adding to the potential in countries such as
India and Indonesia. Increasingly internationally dispersed families provide another structural
source of demand.
India stands out here. Strong economic growth, rising household incomes and a huge
population offer strong potential. Airlines are positioning themselves accordingly, illustrated by
historically large aircraft orders from IndiGo and Air India. India’s aircraft fleet is expected to
grow by around 8% annually on average over the next two decades.

Supply-side developments
Aircraft scarcity keeps capacity tight and lease rates elevated
Supply constraints have been curbing aviation growth since the post-pandemic recovery and
remain a major factor. The share of stored aircraft dropped below 10% by the end of 2025, its
lowest level since 2019. Even several A380s (the largest aircraft) have returned to service.
More than half of the global commercial aircraft fleet is leased. Tight capacity is consequently
reflected in relatively high used-aircraft values and lease rates.
Airbus and Boeing had a combined record backlog of around 15,000 aircraft by mid-2026.
Production and deliveries continue to lag order intake after seven consecutive years of
subdued deliveries from 2019 onwards. Demand for new-generation aircraft remains strong,
as illustrated by major recent orders from Avolon and Turkish Airlines.
Production could finally return to pre-pandemic levels in 2027. Chinese manufacturer COMAC,
meanwhile, is making progress in China in Asia, but Western certification remains distant.

Why aircraft supply remains constrained
Production has still not caught up with pre-pandemic levels and is unlikely to do so in
the next five years. Aircraft manufacturing is recovering, but delays and large backlogs
push delivery dates into the 2030s. Airlines are therefore forced to keep older
aircraft flying for longer.
Suppliers are still struggling to ramp up. Six years after the sharp pandemic downturn,
aviation supply chains have still not fully normalised. Stringent safety and certification
requirements mean manufacturers rely on a limited number of specialised suppliers.
Engines and sophisticated cabin components still have long lead times, while skilled
workers remain scarce.
Engine issues continue to affect available capacity. The peak impact of the Pratt &
Whitney inspection programme after an identified production issue has passed, but
checks and maintenance continue to constrain parts of the A320neo-family fleet and
absorb scarce maintenance capacity.

Delivery catch-up is crucial for both capacity and efficiency
New aircraft are critical not only to accommodate growth, but also to improve efficiency. The
average age of the global fleet has increased from around 13 to 15 years since 2019 due to
production constraints.
This matters increasingly as older units require more maintenance at a time when skilled
personnel and maintenance capacity are scarce; they also consume significantly more fuel
and therefore emit more.
Boeing is finally regaining altitude supporting deliveries
Boeing is finally making a sustained comeback following an unprecedented period of
disruption stemming from the 737 MAX accidents, the pandemic and subsequent production
and quality problems. It received certification for its 737 MAX 7 and also the 737 MAX 10
recently. Boeing plans to increase production for the family from 38 to 47 a month.
Higher 737 production should support deliveries in 2027 and gradually bring pre-pandemic
production levels back into sight. This is hugely important for Boeing-dependent airlines such
as Ryanair and Southwest. Progress on certification of the 777-9 is also important for long-haul
fleet renewal.
Fleet renewal is currently the most important decarbonisation
lever
Air travel continues to grow, and associated emissions tend to continue to rise further as well.
Sustainable aviation fuels are labelled as an important industry pathway to decarbonise, but
globally, blend rates still hover below 1% in 2026.
The closure of airspaces, too, has created inefficiencies and extra emissions over the last few
years, undermining gains elsewhere. Around 63% of the global fleet still consists of previous-

The challenge is to deliver profitable growth in a more
disruptive world
For airlines, the challenge heading into 2027 is therefore not simply finding sufficient demand.
Relatively poor punctuality, with only around 61% of flights departing on time in 2026 year-to-
date, is a signal.
The globally operating industry faces more turbulence and uncertainty amid intensified
geopolitical risks. The challenge is to deliver profitable growth in a world of persistently
constrained capacity, higher costs and more frequent disruption.
