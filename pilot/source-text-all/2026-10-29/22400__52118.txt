3Q 2026 Review 
As of September 30, 2026 
• Strong growth: Global G D P is projected to be 3% for 2026, led by a strong U S economy. The U S economy 
continues to show economic resiliency led by positive G D P growth (2.2% annualized in Q2 2026). Strong earnings 
led by A I -related business investment, manufacturing, and consumer spending are the key economic drivers. 
• Inflation persistence: Inflation remains persistent and “sticky,” above the Federal Reserve’s 2% target rate. High 
energy, freight, and food costs are sustaining inflation and weighing on consumers. 
• U S Fed: Inflation has overtaken the labor market as the immediate monetary policy concern. The Fed raised rates 
by 0.25% on September 16, 2026, with F O M C members and the markets anticipating further rate hikes. 
• Labor Markets:  Despite a weaker than expected September U S jobs report, the U S labor market is relatively 
strong with low unemployment (4.2% in September). 
• Artificial Intelligence (A I ): AI continues to fuel equity markets through strong capital expenditures (capex). A I 
is also impacting fixed income markets, as hyperscalers issue record levels of debt in 2026, providing the Treasury 
market with competition. 
• Fixed Income Markets:  Yields continue to rise over inflation fears, Fed policy, and geopolitical concerns. 
• Portfolio diversification: For investors, diversification provides opportunity to participate in different asset classes 
as market strength expands beyond top-tier tech into international and emerging markets, 
 small caps, and 
alternative assets. Diversification may also help manage the impact of inflation and volatility associated with 
geopolitical concerns and potential fiscal policy changes resulting from the upcoming U S mid-term elections.

The Consumer continues to drive G D P growth 
Consumer spending regains momentum in August 2026 as 1-year ahead inflation expectations increase 
• Personal income advanced by a modest 0.2% month-over-month (m/m) in August, below market expectations for a 0.5% gain. After adjusting for inflation, taxes, and transfers, real 
disposable personal income growth was flat. 
• Consumer spending rose by 0.8% m/m, roughly in line with expectations. Higher prices – especially for gas – accounted for some of the increase, but the gain remained sizeable even 
after adjusting for inflation, with real spending up 0.6% m/m – the largest monthly gain since March 2025. 
• With spending outpacing income, the personal saving rate fell to 4.1% in August from 4.6% (revised up from 3.0%) in the prior month. The personal saving rate was notably higher 
throughout 2026 after incorporating the revisions to household income, though it's still showing a downward trend through 2026.

Lower jobless claims point to Labor Market resiliency 
Job openings to unemployed ratio has returns to a historical balance 
• U.S. initial jobless claims fell to 197,000 for the week ending October 3, 2026, hovering near a 57-year low and signaling continued resilience in the labor market. 
• The trend in low initial jobless claims points to the continued “low hire, low fire” job environment. 
• Low initial claim figures reflect minimal corporate layoffs, even as overall job creation has cooled and the national unemployment rate sits at 4.2%. 
• The jobs openings to unemployed ratio currently stands at 1.01. In recent months, the ratio has hovered around 1.0 to 1.1 unemployed persons per job opening. 
• A ratio at or below 1.0 means jobs and workers are closely balanced or that openings match/exceed job seekers (indicating a tight labor market). A ratio above 1.0 means there are 
more unemployed workers than available jobs (indicating labor market slack).

Income continues to drive fixed income returns 
Due to inflation, geopolitical, and fiscal concerns, selectivity remains key 
• In the third quarter of 2026, fixed income markets experienced a sharp sell-off and rising yields as persistent inflation, geopolitical energy shocks (such as conflict in Iran), and a 
hawkish 25-basis-point rate hike by the Federal Reserve in September pushed interest rates higher. 
• Short-term, intermediate, and long-term yields have all increased, with longer-term rates reaching multi-decade highs. 
• The key today, however, is that "real yields" (meaning yields adjusted for inflation) are rising alongside nominal yields. In other words, higher yields are not just the result of inflation 
but instead reflect positive growth trends and heavy spending in areas like AI.

The yield curve rises 
Escalating geopolitical tensions, inflation, A I expenditures, and monetary policy are driving yields higher 
• In 2026, U.S. Treasury yields are experiencing notable volatility due to shifting Federal Reserve expectations and inflation concerns. Benchmark yields started the year lower, hit mid-
year highs, and then fluctuated as markets continued to debate the path of interest rates. 
• Since the start of the Iran War, the 10-Year Treasury yield has increased by 132 basis points, reaching 5.29% as of 9/30/2026. 
• Concurrently, the 10-year U S Treasury term premium—the extra compensation investors demand for holding long-term bonds instead of rolling over short-term investments—has also 
increased. 
• U.S. real bond yields (nominal yields minus inflation expectations) are rising because resilient economic growth, heavy capital expenditures for artificial intelligence infrastructure, and 
massive government debt supply are driving a structural repricing of interest rates.

As inflation rises, stock-bond correlation is increasing 
Bonds are a weaker hedge when inflation rises 
• The correlation between stocks and bonds is largely shaped by inflation, monetary policy and the degree of macroeconomic shocks. In low and stable inflation environments, 
macroeconomic shocks are the biggest driver. Stocks fall on weak growth, while bonds rally on rate cuts, leading to negative correlations. However, in inflationary or stagflationary 
periods, both asset classes may decline together, pushing correlations positive. 
• Across the five S&P 500 pullbacks since 2024, bonds only helped in two of them, although those happened to be two of the deeper declines. 
• In the other three, an inflation scare drove the selloff (tariffs, oil, Iran) and bonds fell right alongside stocks.

Floating Rate Bond Investing 
Potential for portfolio diversification, income potential, and helping to protect against rising interest rates 
Floating Rate Bonds seek to offer: 
• Helping to Protect Against Rising Interest Rates: Unlike fixed-rate bonds, which lose value when rates rise, floating rate bonds adjust their coupon rates 
periodically (e.g., every 3 to 6 months) to reflect current market rates, often resulting in higher income. 
• Reduced Interest Rate Risk (Lower Duration): Because the interest rate resets, these bonds have limited price volatility, making them a more stable investment 
compared to fixed-rate bonds when interest rates are volatile or increasing. 
• Hedge Against Inflation: As inflation forces central banks to raise rates, the income from floating rate bonds increases, helping investors maintain purchasing 
power. 
• Higher Income Potential: When interest rates are rising, floating-rate notes can provide higher payouts compared to fixed-income alternatives. 
• Portfolio Diversification: These instruments often have low correlations with traditional asset classes, such as stocks and government bonds, providing stability to 
a diversified portfolio. 
• Higher Recovery Rates: Many floating-rate bonds are senior secured loans, which historically offer better protection with 60-70% recovery rates in the event of 
default, compared to 40-50% for high-yield bonds.

High Yield Bond Investing 
Potential for high yields, lower sensitivity to interest rates and equity-like returns with lower risk 
• 
High Yield Bonds aim to provide: 
• 
Higher Yields (Income Generation): They provide superior coupon payments to compensate for higher credit risk, making them an excellent choice for income-focused investors. 
• 
Lower Sensitivity to Interest Rates: High-yield bonds generally have shorter durations, meaning their prices are less likely to fall when interest rates rise compared to higher-
grade bonds. 
• 
Diversification and Reduced Volatility: These bonds often have lower correlations with other fixed-income assets and equities, which can enhance a portfolio's overall risk-adjusted 
returns. 
• 
Equity-Like Returns with Lower Risk: While they offer higher returns similar to stocks, they are typically less volatile and less prone to severe market swings. 
• 
Capital Appreciation Potential: If the issuing company's financial health improves, the bond's value can rise, offering both high income and capital gains. 
• 
Senior Position to Equity: In the event of bankruptcy, high-yield bondholders are generally paid before equity shareholders, offering a safety net compared to owning stocks
