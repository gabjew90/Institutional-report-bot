On 3 November, Americans will cast their first nationwide 
verdict on Donald Trump’s second term, and the Presi-
dent has invited them to treat the vote as a referendum 
on himself. Twenty-one months in, the record is sharply 
divided. The administration has largely done what it said 
it would do on the border, taxes and regulation, yet it has 
fallen well short on the promise that mattered most to 
voters: making everyday life more affordable. A Washing-
ton Post-ABC-Ipsos poll conducted on 9-13 October puts 
Trump’s approval at 33%, the lowest of this term, with 
only 28% approving of his handling of the economy. The 
same survey gives Democrats an eight-point lead on the 
generic congressional ballot, and an AP-NORC poll found 
that just 17% of Americans approve of his management 
of the cost of living. For investors, the scorecard matters 
mostly as a guide to which policies are likely to last and 
what a divided Congress could change.
Promises kept on the border, taxes and 
regulation
The border is where the administration has made the 
most progress. According to the Department of Home-
land Security, Border Patrol recorded 98,708 apprehen-
sions at the southwest border in fiscal year 2026. That is 
roughly 95% below the annual average of the Biden years 
and the lowest level since the late 1960s. Nationwide 
encounters, at 379,243, were the lowest on record. US 
Customs and Border Protection (CBP) reports that about 
250 miles of new barrier have been built since January 
2025, supported by USD $34.3bn committed to “Smart 
Wall” projects. The wall itself is unfinished, but the flows it 
was meant to stop have largely dried up.
Source: US Customs and Border Protection
Deportations have also increased sharply, with ICE arrests 
averaging more than 1,400 a day in July, according to 
federal records reported by Bloomberg Government, more 
than four times the roughly 300 daily arrests  recorded at 
the end of the Biden administration. The cost has been 
political and human, from confrontations in US cities to 
labour shortages in construction and agriculture. One 
pledge has also clearly failed: the Supreme Court blocked 
the executive order ending birthright citizenship in June.
On taxes, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the 2017 
individual tax cuts permanent and added targeted relief 
through deductions for tips, overtime and seniors. These 
items did not become fully tax-free as promised on the 
campaign trail, but the substance was delivered, at a 
price. The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimates 
that the law adds USD $4.7tn to deficits through 2035 
once interest and macroeconomic effects are included. 
It also projects the fiscal 2026 deficit at USD $1.9tn, or 
5.8% of GDP. The administration disputes these figures 
and argues that stronger growth will pay for the cuts.
Deregulation has gone further than promised. The White 
House Office of Management and Budget counted 218 
deregulatory actions against five significant new rules 
in fiscal 2025, well beyond the ten-for-one target. The 
US has left the Paris Agreement for a second time. The 
Environmental Protection Agency has rescinded the 
endangerment finding that underpinned federal green-
house-gas rules, and much of the Inflation Reduction 
Act’s clean-energy credit structure has been scaled back.
Energy output tells a more nuanced story. The “drill, baby, 
drill” agenda has kept production at record levels, with 
crude output forecast at 13.9 million barrels a day in 2026 
and marketed natural gas production at a record 122.5 
billion cubic feet a day, according to the US Energy Infor-
mation Administration (EIA). Per the EIA, LNG exports are 
expected to average 17.6 billion cubic feet a day in 2026, 
nearly 50% more than in 2024, after the export licensing 
freeze was lifted on day one. Yet US shale was already 
reaching record highs under the previous administration, 
and crude growth has since slowed.

peace

“Ending inflation on day one” was the centrepiece of the 
2024 campaign. Instead, headline consumer prices rose 
0.6% in September alone and 3.7% year on year, accord-
ing to the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), up from 2.9% 
when Joe Biden left office. Energy drove much of the 
increase, with gasoline up 9.1% in the month and account-
ing for more than half of the overall rise. Core inflation 
also exceeded expectations at 0.3% month on month, 
suggesting that price pressures extend beyond oil. The 
Federal Reserve’s September projections put 2026 
PCE inflation at 3.7%. Tariffs and the construction boom 
fuelled by artificial intelligence had already added to infla-
tionary pressures before the war with Iran amplified them.
The pledge of gasoline below $2 a gallon has gone into 
reverse. The conflict that began in late February and the 
near-closure of the Strait of Hormuz triggered the largest 
oil supply shock on record. Brent hit an intraday high of 
$126.41 on 30 April and is back above $110. Pump prices 
averaged $4.33 a gallon in September, above the previous 
September record of $3.83 set in 2023. Trump himself 
has conceded that relief will probably not come before 
the election. Electricity, which he promised to halve 
within 18 months, is about 18% more expensive than at 
his inauguration, according to the EIA, as grid investment 
and data-centre demand push bills higher. To cushion the 
shock, the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, which he vowed 
to refill, has been drawn down to its lowest level since the 
early 1980s, according to Department of Energy data.
Borrowing costs have moved in the wrong direction too. 
Mortgage rates were supposed to fall back towards 3%. 
Freddie Mac data showed that the 30-year fixed rate aver-
aged 7.4% in mid-October, higher than in January 2025. 
Far from cutting as the President demanded, the Federal 
Reserve raised its policy rate in September to a range of 
3.75-4.00%, marking its first hike in more than three years. 
Long-dated Treasury yields have climbed alongside infla-
tion and deficit concerns.
Trade is another disappointment. Tariffs are at their highest 
level in generations, yet the trade deficit has not narrowed 
structurally. According to the Bureau of Economic Anal-
ysis (BEA), the goods and services deficit widened to 
USD $105.6bn in August. The year-to-date gap is 19.9% 
smaller than in 2025, but that comparison is mislead-
ing, since 2025 was distorted by companies stockpiling 
imports ahead of the tariffs. The legal foundation has 
also weakened. In February, the Supreme Court ruled 
6-3 that the International Emergency Economic Powers 
Act (IEEPA) does not authorise tariffs, striking down the 
“reciprocal” and fentanyl-related duties and opening the 
door to refunds. The White House has since turned to 
a temporary global surcharge under Section 122 and to 
sector-specific duties under Sections 232 and 301. Tariff 
policy therefore now rests on narrower legal footing and 
faces greater scope for a legal challenge.
The promise of peace has fared worst. The 24-hour deal 
for Ukraine never materialised, and the war is now in its 
fifth year. Despite repeated trips by envoys Steve Witkoff 
and Jared Kushner, the Kremlin still demands Donbas 
territory its army has not taken, and trilateral talks remain 
unscheduled. Meanwhile, the candidate who promised to 
“stop wars” launched one with Iran. Initially presented as 
a matter of weeks, the conflict is approaching its eighth 
month, with US casualties and a cost the Pentagon esti-
mates at more than USD $40bn.
The grey zone of growth, factories and 
markets
The economy has not delivered the promised “explo-
sion”, but it has proven resilient. BEA data show that GDP 
grew at an annualised rate of 2.2% in the second quarter. 
The Atlanta Fed’s nowcast points to faster growth in the 
third, keeping the US ahead of its G7 peers. The main 
engine, however, is capital spending on AI rather than the 
promised broad-based revival. The labour market is also 
cooling, with the BLS reporting only 29,000 new jobs in 
September and unemployment rising slightly to 4.2%.
Source: US Department of Labor
Manufacturing is a matter of perspective. According 
to the BLS, factory employment is up 72,000 from its 
December 2025 low, and the Institute for Supply Man-
agement’s manufacturing index has signalled expansion 
for nine consecutive months. Measured from inaugu-
ration day, though, factory jobs are roughly flat at about 
12.7 million, according to the BLS. Spending on new plant 
construction has also fallen sharply despite large head-
line investment pledges. The sector may be turning, but a 
renaissance remains some way off.
Wall Street has largely looked past the politics. The S&P 
500 set a record close of 7,818.93 on 6 October  and is 
up about 14% this year. The gains are narrow, however. 
AI-linked companies now account for close to half of the 
index’s market capitalisation and have driven around two-
thirds of this year’s rise. The rally owes more to technol-
ogy order books than to Washington’s policy mix.

Markets and the midterms
The pattern is consistent. President Trump has delivered 
on the pledges he could enact by executive action or 
through a single reconciliation bill, and has fallen short 
where outcomes depend on markets, geopolitics or the 
Fed. Supporters argue that the Iran war and the AI-driven 
power crunch were shocks no president could have 
avoided, and that the border and tax record will pay off 
over time. Critics respond that the war and the tariffs were 
deliberate choices whose burden fell on the very voters 
who had been promised relief.
Several implications follow for the market backdrop. 
Inflation has become the binding constraint, and with 
headline CPI close to 4% and the Fed tightening, the 
prospect of rapid rate cuts has receded. Fiscal risk now 
looks structural. Permanent tax cuts, lower tariff revenue 
after the Supreme Court ruling, and the cost of the war all 
point to wider deficits and increased Treasury issuance, 
which tends to put upward pressure on long-dated yields. 
Finally, a divided Congress would be more likely to freeze 
policy than reverse existing policies. Should Democrats 
win the House, as polls suggest, oversight and budget 
battles would intensify, but tax law would likely remain 
unchanged. Trade, immigration and regulation would 
remain largely under the president’s control.
Conclusion
Twenty-one months into his second term, Trump’s record 
falls into two distinct categories. Where the President 
controlled the outcome directly, including the border, 
taxes and regulation, he largely delivered. Where results 
depended on prices, central bank decisions or foreign 
adversaries, the pledges have mostly gone unmet, and 
inflation, fuel costs and two unresolved wars now dom-
inate voters’ concerns. The November 3 vote will not 
rewrite this scorecard. It will instead determine how much 
of the next two years Trump spends governing, and how 
much defending his record.
