◼ AI changes how we operate but fails as the cyclical determinant of spending and 
hiring in this cycle – except among Asian exporters 
◼ Equity market is not the economy – but the Fed supports both 
◼ Recession is far off (2029?) but inflation is here and the Fed will lag 
The AI revolution changes everything, but for employment and spending, including money 
spent to build data plants, AI fails to rise to a meaningful enough level to reshape the current 
US business cycle. Further, recession is far from imminent -- nominal GDP is running at around a 
7% SAAR with the 10Y at 5%, the funds rate at 3.88%, and no shortage of capital looking to be 
placed. That AI-related borrowers are now paying spreads better reflective of risk/reward is hardly 
reason to sound alarms (unless your business model is based on zero cost of funding). If every AI 
bond defaulted, which will not happen, the amount outstanding still pales next to the mortgage 
market and all its derivatives in 2007. Point is that money remains cheap and plentiful, and the 
Fed still believes only a modest course correction is needed to return inflation to target –they 
will lag current events until forced otherwise. We at TSL have long written that the Fed’s 
perception, duly priced in by the market, will be proven very wrong. We have been on the higher 
inflation / higher interest rates side of the story for a long time (years, in fact, pre-Covid for me) 
and we remain there. Recession shows up when interest rates are pulled well ahead of nominal 
growth and stay there, and fiscal policy meaningfully tightens through spending cuts and/or tax 
hikes. Accepting that recipe for a downturn, my guess is 2029 is the recession year. It is way too 
soon to say what that recession will look like – although it is always interesting to speculate. 
Recession can arrive sooner if the rest of the world forces the US to pay much higher real rates 
for importing capital. Always the near-term risk, and signs of non-dollar investor disquiet abound, 
but for real yields to soar to levels that are contractionary there needs to be something much 
more compelling, of sufficient size, for global capital to acquire, other than gold.  
AI data plants garner a lot of the news, deservedly so, but its impact on overall private 
nonresidential construction spending is minimal and hence unlikely to be a critical 
determinant of the business cycle. Census categorizes these plants as office buildings (no idea 
why) and real spending on office construction increased by $28bn Y/Y for the 12 months ending 
in August (Chart 1). Private power plant spending is up $5.4bn (I am assuming there is a 
relationship). The office spending number is large, but it peaked at $37bn Y/Y for the 12 
months ending last November. To put this in context, real dollar office spending was 
increasing around $60bn/year in the late 1990s. More to the point for the current business 
cycle, the current pace of spending fails to meaningfully offset the $188bn decline in 
manufacturing construction that or $219bn decline in total nonresidential private construction.

Switching to corporate spending on equipment and intellectual products, real dollar 
purchasing of software and related equipment has steadily risen as a percent of real final 
sales of domestic product and has pulled up overall corporate real spending on equipment and 
intellectual property. (Chart 2) Since the end of the 08-09 recession, overall capital spending as a 
percent of real final sales (revenue proxy) has risen, in real terms, from 10% to 14.7% -- the half-
way point occurred in 2019, when spending averaged around 12.5%.  Software spending is now 
4% of final sales (in real terms) up from 1.4% in 2012. An addition to real GDP growth, but this is 
more of switch in what business buy than a leap in overall spending that multiplies employment. 
The impact on employment is, in fact, underwhelming, but not so the impact on foreign suppliers. 
The AI revolution is being imported – and this is where any reversal would be felt.

Business capex (excluding structures) was 1.075% contributor to real GDP growth in Q2 (4Q 
MA), above the long-run median but hardly record-breaking. (Chart 3) The median contribution 
in the late 1990s was 1.15% -- a capital deepening era that boosted real growth and, in turn real 
yields. What distinguishes the current period from prior ones when real capex increased its 
contribution to the expansion is that real GDP growth today is slowing – underscoring, again, 
that the multiplier from AI-related capex is truly less than advertised. It is also important to note 
that the economy is only in year one of recovery, post its non-recession recession in 2025, 
meaning that other contributors to real GDP growth are expected to accelerate in the year ahead. 
On the production (goods) side, the AI revolution is being imported. There is no sign of an AI 
surge in orders of shipment of computers and electronic products (includes semi-conductors) 
relative to the non-defence total (Chart 4).

To underscore this point, the AI revolution coincides with a surge in imports of advanced 
technology (Chart 5). 
The lack of impact of AI spending on high-tech products for domestic production extends to 
labour, but also including services. Since 2002, growth in employment in computer services has 
grown much fast than total private employment (excluding health services), especially right out of 
COVID, but employment in these services has been falling since 2022 (Chart 6). While 
employment in computer related employment has been shrinking, employment in nonresidential 
private construction (AI data plants) has essentially kept pace with total employment. In the 
post-COVID AI spending boom, there is no evidence of a related surge in employment in goods 
or services – an AI bust is, in turn, unlikely to create a surge in unemployment.

Payroll tends to follow profits, so in this regard there is guarded optimism that spending on 
labor by AI related firms will increase in the year ahead (Chart 7). Once again, though, in terms 
of an AI bust busting the demand for labor, the data do not support the notion. 
 
As for inflation, construction costs are inflating, but far less so than at the COVID peak for supply 
disruption (understandable), but the PPI for materials and components for manufacturing (which 
is not where AI dollars are flowing) are inflating faster. As for AI deflating prices, keep in mind that 
food at home, energy, housing, and medical services are near 70% or CPI. Legal and financial 
services combine for 0.4% of CPI. AI will change a lot, be a deflationary force in much of our 
world, but far from enough to reorder the price structure. Unless, of course, one considers the 
likely trend destruction of income for the upper end of the “K” and the resultant trickling down 
that hurts gig workers.

AI related firms are borrowing a lot of money, spreads are widening (they should widen), and 
here the overall risk from this increased borrowing sits with the economy needing to import 
more capital as a percent of GDP. When total net investment (net of replacement) is running a lot 
higher as a percent of GDP than net domestic saving (private saving + government dissaving), 
real yields rise to attract capital – with the Fed balance sheet acting as ballast against too great 
of a rise. With Warsh effectively backing away from that responsibility, real yields have been on 
the rise and threaten to rise further if private borrowing accelerates accelerates and there is no 
relief in the pace of government borrowing. (Chart 9). Herein lies the risk – weaker hands now 
owning US dollar assets decide they need greater compensation for their holdings. Real yields 
rise or the dollar falls, or some combination. If the dollar falls, inflation rises – undercutting the 
Fed’s absolute conviction that inflation trends lower next year, duly reflected in market pricing. To 
date, however, business net investment is running at 3% of GDP, the median beginning 2003. 
In other words, AI is borrowing more, total net investment show no great surge. Is AI crowding 
out? I doubt it, it is more like this is the new shiny thing to borrow money to invest in. Every cycle 
has its shiny new toy. AI is important and transformative to how the economy operates and 
prices goods and services (over time), but to think it has upended and extended the current 
economic cycle along with capital markets (other than equity prices) is a thought too far.

In sum, AI is transformative to how we operate but not to the business cycle, not yet anyway. 
The boom/bust potential rests in the equity market much more so than in real spending flows. 
As for the equity market, interest rates remain supportive of growth and will remain so as the Fed 
will take its time truing up policy rates to inflation, and there is plenty of cash around to lend. If 
equities did crash the Fed would ease, and market participants know that. The internet 
boom/bust was an equity event, not so much one for the business cycle. In terms of spending 
flows, the internet continued to be absorbed into everyday life. Further, this time around, AI-
related firms make money. Equity markets are, today, repricing to find a normalized forward PE 
for AI firms – 15 is too low, 50 is now too high. This is not a bust; this is a functioning market. 
The Fed, based on recent comments from Warsh, see AI similarly – important but not a key driver 
of employment and inflation. It is hard to find the AI impact on inflation today. The FOMC still 
sees AI as this great disinflationary force – it is for some services but not for the broader 
economy, unless one wants to take into account the eventual impact of lower income trajectories 
for high-skilled jobs. While that impact is coming, recession is only on the horizon – where it 
always sits. I put the timing as 2029 because the US is a long way away from the FOMC ramping 
up policy rates to get them well ahead of nominal growth. The “tell” that they shifted focus to be 
more aggressive would be a 50bp hike in Dec, to make up for the skip this week. A near-term 
reversal in AI spending will fail to reverse the economy (even if such a reversal was to occur), 
especially with the Fed keeping the economy afloat.
