Executive summary
Investors in data centres and the power ecosystem supporting them face a difficult question: is capacity being built 
ahead of AI demand that never arrives? This question is not easy to answer, not least because many of the projections 
available are based on supply-side signals, such as chip shipments and building pipelines. They are easy to track, but 
they only describe what the industry is building, rather than what users will need. 
In this paper we have taken a different, demand-based approach, modelling demand from how the technology is 
actually used. We attempt to address this question from the perspective of generative AI – in our view the subset of 
the broader AI discussion that is most relevant and attracts most investor time and discussion. Our key findings are 
as follows.
AI-driven power demand growth is real and significant. Demand is forecast to grow ninefold by 
2040, underpinned by generative AI. Generative AI's electricity use rises from about 91 terawatt-hours 
(TWh) today to roughly 857 TWh in 2040 even with significant hardware and software efficiency gains 
modelled. In our high case demand reaches 1,219 TWh, thirteen times what it is today. 
Investment opportunities abound. Building the data centre and required power infrastructure will 
require some $US4.5 to $US5.6 trillion by 2040, or roughly $US300 to $US375 billion a year. 
Agents are the future. The proliferation of AI as agents used for tasks currently performed by 
humans is the critical next evolution. Demand growth is driven far more by intensity of use than 
breadth of take-up. In our base case heavy users are about a quarter of the total by 2040 yet account 
for some 70% of the electricity behind inference. Behind that is agentic use: the model works through 
tasks on its own rather than answering a person, so its output is no longer bounded by what a human 
can process. As an example, the fast-developing world of humanoid robots – not within the scope of our 
paper – would add further upside to our power demand forecasts.
A more predictable but less flexible load matters for developers and grid owners. Generative AI 
at work (“inference”) consumes more electricity over time than generative AI training, and this is 
a more predictable but less flexible draw on power use. Inference rises from under 60% of demand 
today to over 85% by 2040 in our base case. It follows the users and usually answers on demand, 
resulting in use that tracks the working day and week, whereas training tasks can be queued but draw 
power more erratically and more flexibly. 
AI-driven infrastructure demand has been hotly debated over the past few years and continues to capture investors' 
imagination across the world. Our bottom-up work leads us to conclude that the growth will be significant and real. 
Permitting and environmental impact may continue to influence the path, and there will be difficult questions ahead 
on grid availability and behind-the-meter solutions. Nonetheless, large amounts of infrastructure capital will be needed 
to meet the demand generated by this new technology. The even harder question for infrastructure investors is 
whether that power can be delivered where and when the load asks for it.

Few forces are reshaping the outlook for electricity demand as quickly as 
artificial intelligence and, within it, one workload in particular: generative AI.  
It is the technology behind large language models and the tools built on 
them, and it behaves quite differently from the machine learning that has 
run in data centres for years.
It is also where the money has gone. Global private AI investment tripled between 2022 and 2025, from $US113 
billion to $US345 billion, and generative AI accounted for around 70% of that increase.1 That is already changing 
load forecasts, grid plans and generation investment across much of the world. Those assets are financed over long 
horizons, so the question for anyone building or funding them is not just how fast capacity can be built, but how much 
electricity AI will need over the life of the asset, often the next ten to twenty years. 
Today's projections mostly forecast supply. They extrapolate from what can be counted: power contracted, data 
centres announced, chips shipped. That data is timely and it is real. But they describe what the industry is preparing 
for, not what will be consumed. Chip shipments are the clearest case. Hyperscalers order well ahead of demand, and 
there is a backlog, so shipments show what suppliers expect to sell rather than what users will need. That gap is why 
the risk of oversupply is a live question. Forecasting supply therefore has several shortcomings.
•	 Is the demand real? Supply-side estimates measure commitment rather than consumption, so they cannot tell a 
build-out that demand will fill from one that has run ahead of it. They cannot, therefore, answer the fundamental 
question of whether we are in an investment bubble. 
•	 Which AI? They cannot separate demand from generative AI that started the current boom from predictive and 
older machine learning workloads. That matters because the two behave quite differently: the older workloads 
grow steadily and predictably, while generative AI is the volatile and rapidly growing part the capital is exposed to. 
Blending them makes the total look steadier than the decision facing an investor.
•	 Training or inference demand? Supply-side estimates cannot distinguish the two, though each places quite 
different demands on a network and on the assets serving them.
This paper takes a new approach and models demand, building projections from the bottom-up fundamentals of how 
the technology is actually used: how many people use generative AI and how heavily; how much each request asks 
of a model; and what serving that request costs in energy. For training, we start from what the leading laboratories 
spend on compute. Training is a deliberate choice about how much to spend, so that spending is the demand, not a 
proxy for it.

Our analysis gives a considerable spread: 857 TWh in 2040 for our base case, within a range of 464 to 1,219 TWh 
(Figure 1). That range reflects the genuine uncertainty about the technology's long-term trajectory, but our analysis 
also offers a view of the drivers behind it, which is where the more interesting findings are:
•	 Intensity drives the growth, not headcount. How many people use AI matters far less than what they use it for, 
with a small group of heavy and increasingly machine-driven users carrying most of the load. 
•	 The load will likely become more predictable but less flexible. AI electricity consumption divides between training 
(building a model) and inference (using it), and the two behave differently. Inference follows the users and usually 
answers on demand, tracking the working day and week, whereas training can be queued but draws power more 
erratically. In our base case inference rises from under 60% of generative AI demand today to more than 85% by 
2040, so the load becomes more predictable but less flexible as it grows larger.
•	 The demand is real and meeting it will require substantial amounts of capital. Even where adoption stalls and use 
stays light, demand rises around fivefold by 2040; in the base case ninefold, and at the top of our range thirteenfold. 
Continuous draw goes from around 10 GW today to between 53 and 139 GW. Adding that much load in fifteen 
years is not primarily a generation problem – it is a question of where it lands, how quickly it can be connected, and 
whether the firming and transmission assets arrive with it.

What generative AI is and where its electricity goes
Generative AI is software that learns patterns from vast amounts of data and uses them to produce new content, text, 
images, audio, video or code, in response to a prompt. The system doing the learning is called a model, and the models 
behind most of the current interest are large language models (LLMs): ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude are all built on 
them. Images and video come from a different kind of model, which is why we measure them separately. Providers do 
not offer a single model but several, ranging from small and fast to large and capable: Claude comes as Haiku, Sonnet, 
Opus, Fable and Mythos, Gemini as Flash and Pro. A short question is usually handled by a smaller model, while harder 
work goes to a larger one that costs far more energy to run. Underneath, everything they do is computation running 
on specialised chips, and that computation is what draws power.
A model's life has two stages, and each runs its own computation (Figure 4):
•	 Training – Building a model. A neural network is exposed to enormous datasets until it absorbs their patterns. 
For any one model this is a one-off, but across the industry it never stops. In 2025, Epoch AI recorded six frontier 
models and more than a hundred notable ones.2 The real figure is higher still, considering smaller releases fall below 
the counting threshold as well as failed runs and experiments not reported. Compute in a run is measured in floating 
point operations (FLOPs). A frontier run now involves around 1026 of them; an ordinary laptop running flat out would 
need something like a million years to get through that many. 
•	 Inference – Continuous usage. Every prompt and response is processed in tokens, the small chunks of text a model 
reads and writes. (A token is roughly three-quarters of a word. Images, audio and video convert into far more.) Each 
token is a small parcel of computation, individually tiny, but repeated billions of times a day and never stopping. 
Increasingly, that output is not read by a person at all. Agentic systems, where a model is given tools and a goal 
and calls other models in a loop, consume their own output as the input to the next step. That removes the ceiling 
human attention places on response length.

The two stages behave very differently. Training is a fixed cost: a model is built once and can then serve any number 
of queries. Inference is a marginal cost: every query adds computation of its own. As the user base grows and each 
user asks more of the model, the fixed cost is spread across more use while the marginal cost multiplies. That is why 
inference is the share that grows, and why it is the measure this paper is focused on.
Each token processed and each training run carries an energy cost, so total energy consumption is pulled in two 
directions by two different forces. Volume growth pushes it up: more models trained, increased intensity of use, and 
more users all increase energy consumption. Efficiency, on the other hand, pulls it down. The impact of efficiency gains 
is greater than most people realise – the energy behind an average token falls by more than 95% by 2040 in our base 
case. Total demand still rises, because volume grows faster still. The question is not which force wins, but by how much 
volume outpaces efficiency, and that margin is the thread running through the rest of this paper.

Key terms 
Model: The trained generative AI system itself, a large set of parameters learned from data that turns an input 
into an output. Providers offer families of models sized for different jobs: Claude comes as Haiku, Sonnet, Opus, 
Fable and Mythos, Gemini as Flash and Pro. Larger models reason better but cost more electricity to run, so the 
energy behind a request depends on which model answered it.
FLOP (floating-point operation): FLOP measures the total amount of computation performed. This is the 
standard metric for comparing the scale of training runs across different labs and models. A frontier training run 
in 2025 uses on the order of 1026 FLOP (a 1 followed by 26 zeros).3
Token: A token is the basic unit of text that a large language model (LLM) processes. Roughly, one token equals 
about three-quarters of a word.4 When you type a question into ChatGPT, the system converts your text into 
tokens, processes them, and generates output tokens as a response. Every token processed consumes a small 
amount of energy, so the total number of tokens processed globally directly drives electricity demand.
Agentic AI: An agent is a model given a goal and a set of tools, working over many steps rather than answering a 
single prompt, with each step's output feeding the next. A normal answer is bounded by what a person will read; 
an agent's is bounded only by time and cost.

Both digital infrastructure and power assets are financed over long time 
horizons. For anyone building or financing them the question is not how fast 
data centres go up in the next few years, but how much demand generative 
AI will place on them over the life of the asset, often the next ten to 
twenty years. 
Most estimates we have seen build projections from supply: power contracted, projects in the pipeline or chips 
shipped.5 Estimates can be constructed via these trackable items because the data exists. But announced pipelines 
thin out quickly beyond a few years and a chip backlog reaches only as far as the orders. A handful of very large 
campuses are planned a decade ahead, but most capacity is not, so beyond the near term a projection has to rest on 
something else. That makes them forecasts of what will be built, not of what will be needed. 
That is the gap we have tried to fill. Building from how the technology is used rather than from what is being installed 
measures the right variable. It also enables the estimate to run as far as the behavioural assumptions can be defended. 
And by forecasting demand it can be set against the build-out as an independent check on supply rather than a 
restatement of it.
Two further advantages follow.
•	 It isolates generative AI, the segment the capital is chasing, from what headline data-centre figures blend in. As 
Section 1 set out, that is where the new capital is concentrated, and where the overbuild question sits. Assessing 
whether the build-out is justified therefore means looking at generative AI on its own, rather than at a total that 
would have grown regardless. Our inputs are people, prompts and the output those prompts generate, so cloud 
computing, storage and other kinds of AI cannot enter the estimate. That distinction will get harder to observe as AI 
is embedded into ordinary software, but the unit we measure does not change.
•	 It enables us to separate inference from training. The two draw power quite differently and place different 
demands on generation and the grid, as we discuss in Section 5.
Figure 5 sets out the building blocks. Our analytical framework can be split into two halves, each anchored on what can 
actually be observed, and added together at the end.
•	 On the training side, we start from disclosed compute spend at the major laboratories, scale that up to cover the 
market as a whole, and infer electricity consumption based on the market price of a unit of rented compute.
•	 On the inference side, we start from how many people use AI and split them by how heavily they use it. We then 
build up how often each group prompts a model and how much output each prompt generates. That output is split 
again by the kind of work being done, since a reasoning request costs far more energy than a simple chat.6 It is then 
converted into electricity using published measurements. Images and video are counted separately, per image and 
per second of clip respectively.
The two are anchored differently because demand reveals itself differently in each. Inference shows up in usage; 
training is a decision about how much compute to buy, so what reveals it is spending. Neither is a supply measure. Our 
spend figure is the value of compute consumed within the year, not capital expenditure on hardware.

Our analysis runs three scenarios – low, base and high – to span the range of plausible outcomes. Each pairs a coherent 
set of assumptions about how widely AI is adopted, how intensively it is used, how quickly efficiency improves, and how 
fast training spend grows. They are best read as three internally consistent cases rather than as statistical confidence 
bounds around a central estimate. The headline figures throughout this paper refer to the base case. Full mechanics 
are in the Appendix.
•	 The base case: More people using AI, and each using it far more. Adoption reaches around 70% of the world by 
2040, comparable to global internet penetration today, helped by generative AI being absorbed into search, office 
software, and websites, so that users are reached without choosing to adopt anything. Requests get longer and 
harder, with output lengths rising nearly sixfold, and more of that work runs through agents, where the output is fed 
straight back in as the next step rather than read by a person. Training spend follows currently published forecasts 
through the rest of this decade, then slows as the build-out matures. Efficiency keeps improving, but the pace 
slows as chips approach their physical limits and the easiest algorithmic gains are exhausted. By 2040 the base case 
projects the volume of work rising around 400-fold, and electricity demand around ninefold. 
•	 The low case: Adoption stalls, and use stays light. This scenario describes a bounded role for AI. Adoption 
reaches only 55% of the world by 2040, well short of internet penetration today. Usage intensity stays modest 
and agentic use spreads more slowly than in the base case. Training spend plateaus by the late 2030s. Efficiency 
also improves more slowly, on the view that a smaller market draws less investment in chips and software alike. AI 
proves useful but never ubiquitous: by 2040 the volume of work rises around a hundredfold and electricity demand 
around fivefold.
•	 The high case: AI reaches nearly everyone, and machines do the asking. This scenario assumes pervasive 
agentic use, with AI reaching around 90% of the world's population by 2040. Outputs lengthen sharply as they are 
written for machines rather than people. Training spend roughly triples by 2030. Efficiency improves faster here 
than in either other case but is still comfortably outrun by volume: by 2040 the volume of work rises more than a 
thousandfold and electricity demand thirteenfold.

The wide range reflects the very real uncertainty around adoption, usage intensity and efficiency fifteen years out. It 
bounds the ceiling, and the ceiling is an interesting number. In the high case generative AI reaches about 3% of global

intensity and efficiency into one coherent world and the inference and training halves are then paired scenario for 
scenario. The high case assumes the fastest efficiency gains as well as the heaviest use. A true upper bound, pairing 
the heaviest use with the slowest efficiency, would sit above our high case. We do not model it, however, on the 
view that the same investment that drives heavy use also funds the efficiency gains, so the two should not move in 
opposite directions.

where we found that even pushing every video assumption against us at once, which nothing today supports, leaves 
it at around a fifth of demand in our base case.

Comparing our figures with published forecasts
Very few forecasters project generative AI on its own, and we have found no projection of it to 2040. Published 
estimates are drawn wider: some cover all AI data centres, others all data centres, with generative AI one component 
of each. Of what is available, the most useful test is what our figures imply about generative AI's share of the data-
centre estate, set against what others assume. 
Against the IEA's forecast for all data centres, our base case suggests 39% by 2030 (Figure 9). McKinsey is the only 
major forecaster we have found that splits generative AI out separately, and its projection has generative AI at around 
40% of total data-centre capacity demand by 2030 (Figure 10). The two are not strictly like for like, since McKinsey 
measures data-centre capacity in gigawatts rather than electricity demand in TWh, but we both arrive at the same 
share despite being built on entirely different foundations: ours from user behaviour upward, McKinsey's from 
capacity downward.

The level of power demand: Efficiency versus volume
The level of demand is set by a race between the rising volume of computation and the falling energy cost of each unit 
of it. That the volume will rise is close to certain. The open question is whether efficiency falls quickly enough to hold 
the total in check.
Two forces push the energy cost of a token down:
•	 Hardware. Each generation of AI chip performs more computation per watt than the last. On Epoch AI's data, the 
energy efficiency of leading machine-learning hardware has improved by a factor of roughly 1.3x per year over the 
past decade, though the pace is easing as chip design approaches physical limits (Figure 12).
•	 Software. Better model architectures, together with techniques such as quantisation,7 distillation8 and the use 
of smaller task-specific models steadily cut the computation needed for a given result. Our base case assumes 
software efficiency gains arriving at roughly 25% a year today, decaying to 10% by 2040 as the stack matures and 
algorithmic headroom thins.9

Set against these gains, model growth pushes back. Frontier models have grown to hundreds of billions of parameters 
up from millions just a decade ago. Greater capability generally means more computation per token: if a chip is twice 
as efficient but the model running on it is twice as large, the energy behind a token has not changed. Together with 
a shift towards more demanding tasks such as reasoning, that absorbs much of the gain. Hardware and software 
improvements alone would cut the energy behind each output token around 125-fold by 2040; the net fall is around 
21-fold.
A further improvement happens outside the chip, as data-centre overheads fall with better cooling, better power 
usage effectiveness (PUE)10, and higher utilisation. Those gains narrow as PUE approaches its practical floor and 
utilisation approaches its ceiling, but our base case still has electricity per unit of AI output falling around 28-fold by 
2040. Google's disclosure that its median Gemini prompt now draws 0.24 watt-hours, 33 times less than a year earlier, 
is the same story at the level of a single query.11 
Yet none of this is enough to offset volume growth. Over the same fifteen years the volume of tokens rises 399-
fold, against a 28-fold fall in the electricity behind each one. That leaves a fourteenfold rise in the power behind text 
inference, and a ninefold rise in total demand once training and multimodal content (image and video) are included. 
Efficiency does the great majority of the work here, but cheaper output invites more of it, and volume grows faster 
than efficiency can absorb. This is the Jevons paradox12 in arithmetic (Figure 13).

Volume: Determined by agentic AI 
The volume that efficiency has to race against depends less on how many people use AI than on how intensively 
each of them does. The gap between a light user and a heavy one runs to more than 200-fold today, narrowing only 
gradually to about thirtyfold by 2040. Efficiency determines how far the energy cost of each unit falls. 
Our base case generative AI adoption path runs ahead of the internet's. Indexed to the year each technology reached 
around 15% of world population, it stays above the internet's own path throughout, reaching 70% of the world at year 
fifteen where the internet reached 60% (Figure 14). That is consistent with what we have seen so far: generative AI 
has diffused faster than almost any technology in history, and adoption is increasingly passive, embedded into search, 
office software and websites so that users are reached without choosing to adopt anything.13
Even so, adoption is not where the majority of the growth comes from. Of the 399-fold rise in tokens from 2025 to 
2040, a wider user base contributes a factor of 4.8x. The other 83x comes from each user generating more (Figure 15). 
Reasoning models write longer answers and agentic systems consume their own output as the next input.
That last mechanism removes a limit that used to hold. By 2040 a heavy user in our base case generates around 
3.9 million output tokens a day – some 190 hours of reading. These are not volumes a person consumes. The heavy 
segment is really a measure of agentic activity, which is why per-user intensity grows 83-fold while the user base 
grows less than fivefold. Indeed, we are already seeing the first clear sign of it: Microsoft reports 2.3 million GitHub pull 
requests associated with AI agents in March 2026, a 28-fold rise in ten months.14
Ours is therefore an intensity range. The question for an investor is not whether AI is widely adopted, but how 
demanding each user turns out to be.

Video: The wild card
Video is where our estimate could most easily be wrong. The per-second energy behind a generated clip is the least 
settled measurement we use, with published estimates of the energy behind a second of generated video differing by 
an order of magnitude.15
We estimate video accounts for about 2% of inference electricity today, despite being far more energy intensive per 
unit than text. A six-second clip draws roughly 34 times the energy of a text query. But for every clip generated there 
are some 2,700 text queries, and the gap is structural: a text response is produced for one person and used once, while 
a video is produced once and watched by many. Video generation scales with how much is made, not with how much 
is watched.
Within the range we can defend, the numbers stay small. Our high case assumes around 2 billion minutes of video 
generated a day by 2040. Stress-testing the two assumptions that matter most does not change the conclusion. 
Generating three times as much video takes it from 22 to 70 TWh, and halving the rate at which per-clip energy falls 
takes it to 75 TWh, in both cases under a tenth of total demand (Figure 16). Only if both hold at once does video 
become material, at 242 TWh or a fifth of the total, and nothing today supports that combination. 
Two things could change this picture. The first is AI displacing filmed content, and it is already happening in some 
pockets: in China, more than 95% of the 128,000 micro-dramas16 launched in the first quarter of 2026 were AI-
generated.17 But this is bounded, for the reason above: content made once and watched by many does not need much 
generating. The second would not be bounded. If video came to be generated for each viewer rather than made once 
and shared, generation would scale with how much is watched rather than how much is made, and the numbers above 
would be orders of magnitude too low. Nothing we can point to suggests that is imminent. It is also beyond what we 
can model, and we have not attempted it.

Training is bounded by capital, not by use
Training is a deliberate choice by laboratories about how much to spend, so that is where we start. We estimate global 
spend on compute based on what the leading laboratories disclose, then convert that spending into electricity. 
What varies between our cases is how fast that spending grows. The base case has it growing at about 20% a year to 
2030, the low case at half that rate and the high case at a quarter above it. All three slow thereafter, on the view that 
the current phase is a build-out rather than a steady state, easing to 3% a year in the base case by the late 2030s. That 
takes training electricity from 39 TWh in 2025 to between 52 and 220 TWh by 2040 (Figure 17).
We do not model training from compute growth, which would mean extrapolating the historical trend. Compute 
growth is not really a trend at all. It is a choice the labs make each year, guided by how much capital they can raise 
and justify. The trend is also unlikely to hold to 2040, since continuing it would run into constraints on power, chip 
manufacturing and data well before then.18 Modelling the spending directly is the more practical route.

The implications for infrastructure are about how much power is needed, 
but even more about where, when and how steadily. The world can generate 
what generative AI will need. In our base case it reaches about 2.2% of global 
electricity by 2040. 
That might seem like a modest share, but it sits on top of the demand growth already coming from electrification, 
industry and heating, and it arrives faster than any of them. Getting that power to the right places, at the moments it 
is needed, is the harder problem. It also becomes more difficult as the load grows, because the part of it that can wait 
is shrinking. Meanwhile the growth is increasingly driven by machines rather than people, which changes not only how 
much electricity is needed but when.
What this means in investment requirement terms
Our base case implies around 120 GW of installed data-centre capacity by 2040, some 106 GW more than today. This 
will cost roughly $US4.5 to $US5.6 trillion to build, or $US300 to $US375 billion a year.19 About 40% of this is the data 
centre itself plus the generation to power it and the grid to connect it: land, shell, cooling plant, electrical systems, 
substations, generating capacity and transmission. The other 60% is IT equipment such as servers and networking. Our 
figure is the cost of the capacity added by 2040 in today's cost terms and takes no account of IT equipment turning 
over during the period, so it likely understates the total investment requirement.
Demand is large and durable but not a threat to global supply
On our base case, generative AI reaches about 2.2% of world electricity consumption by 2040, and 3.1% at the top 
of our range, measured against a global supply that grows substantially over the same period. That is a large load in 
absolute terms, but it is achievable if sufficient capital can be mobilised.
A natural question is whether efficiency gains simply offset the growth in volume. They do not. Without the 
improvement we assume, 2040 demand in our base case would be around 230 times today's. Even with it, demand 
rises ninefold. Cheaper output has consistently stimulated more demand, so the energy behind a single query has 
shrunk while total consumption has climbed. We have seen this before in cloud storage: the cost of storing a gigabyte 
fell by roughly 87% between 2009 and 2022, and the world responded by storing six times as much.20 Moreover, even 
if demand disappoints, it does not disappear. In our low case it still rises around fivefold by 2040, to about 464 TWh, 
growing every year of the period. It describes a world where AI use stays light, not one where the load goes away.

Not all AI load is created equal and the difference matters for the grid
Generation adequacy assumes power can get where it is needed, when it is needed. In practice it must be built in 
specific places, connected through networks that take years to permit and build, and delivered at the moment the load 
asks for it.
All three of those factors depend on what kind of load it is, a point ignored in much of the literature on AI and its 
power demand implications. Training is patient but erratic. It can wait and doesn’t need to be done close to its users, 
but its draw swings from hour to hour with little regular pattern. Inference is demanding but more predictable. It must 
answer the moment it is asked, yet its load follows a steady daily and weekly rhythm, troughing before dawn, peaking 
in the late afternoon, easing at the weekend (Figure 18).
That is why the split matters. Published estimates cover all AI, or all data centres; they rarely separate generative AI 
from other AI workloads, let alone training from inference within it. Ours does both, and shows inference rising from 
57% of demand today to around 87% by 2040 in our base case. For a network operator or a site developer, what 
matters is the training and inference split rather than whether the tokens came from a chatbot or from a spreadsheet 
that quietly called a model. Splitting the estimate is what makes the discussion possible.

Furthermore, there may also be a geographic dimension to this. Training concentrates where the model developers are, 
which today means overwhelmingly the United States, with China running a parallel ecosystem of its own. A frontier 
run needs one very large site, and it will be built close to the firm doing the training rather than close to anyone using 
the result. Inference does not work that way. It follows the users, and increasingly the rules governing where their 
data may be processed, as sovereignty requirements and sector regulation push serving capacity into the markets 
it serves. As inference comes to dominate the load, the direction of travel is toward a more geographically dispersed 
one, with more of it landing in Europe and other regulated markets rather than in the few countries where training 
clusters today.
Agentic use could break inference's link with human behaviour
The load shape for the inference data centre in Figure 18 is one driven by human behaviour. It tracks the working week 
because a person is waiting at the other end of each request. Agentic use would likely sever that link as it proliferates, 
and would do so in two ways at once:
•	 The first is level. Demand stops being bounded by how much a person can read or type, which is what allows per-
user intensity in our analysis to grow 83-fold while the user base grows less than fivefold.
•	 The second is shape. Work that runs unattended has no reason to follow the working day. In principle that flattens 
the profile and raises utilisation, which suits a grid: a flatter load needs less capacity behind the same energy. In 
practice a price signal would do much of that work on its own, since load chasing the cheapest hour bids that hour 
up until it is no longer the cheapest. 
Generative AI providers already discount work that can wait, and some are beginning to shift it around grid conditions, 
but these arrangements are at an early stage.21 Not all work can wait, either. Many users would rather pay for an 
answer now, and some requests lose their value if delayed. Where the load cannot be steered, flexibility can be built 
around it instead: on-site generation, storage, or a connection sized for the swings rather than the average. That is 
familiar ground for infrastructure investors, and easier than waiting for AI pricing to follow the grid.

We built a proprietary model, which forecasts annual electricity consumption from generative AI and agentic 
workloads worldwide to 2040. The model is primarily conceived as two independently anchored halves, inference 
(serving models) and training (building them), as an attempt to address electricity drawn from the grid, including the 
cooling and power losses of the data centre around the chips.
1. Scope
We focus our work on generative and agentic AI only – text, image and video generation, plus the full training cycle 
including pre-training, fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and post-training. It excludes everything else running on AI 
accelerators: recommendation and ranking, advertising models, computer vision, speech, classical machine learning at 
scale and GPU-based high-performance computing. 
Given our narrower approach, it is plausible readers might find our numbers differ from published headline “AI 
electricity” estimates out there. It is not practical to review all of these published estimates. In preparing this work, 
however, we found that practitioners define “AI electricity” in a wide variety of ways, on both the consumption and 
the supply sides. That topic probably warrants a separate discussion in itself. Our scope is rather limited in this regard 
to the activities mentioned, which, as explained in the main body, we have chosen to focus on for their commercial 
relevance and high impact. We also note the need for further work in other aspects of “AI” in the industry that is 
beyond the remit of this paper.
2. Modelling – the two halves are anchored differently
To conceive our approach simply, we see inference as bound by how much work people ask the models to do: put 
simply, how many people use AI, how often, and how much output each request generates. Training is the other half 
and we approach it slightly differently. To us it appears bound by the money available to spend on compute. Each half 
is anchored on industry disclosure and informed by academic measurement. We believe there is rather limited overlap 
between the two areas, and simple mathematical sums might suffice to give a high-level view. 
The asymmetry of the two anchors matters. A work-based anchor carries no price, so efficiency gains must be 
applied to it directly, whereas a spend-based anchor already embeds them in the falling cost of compute. Applying 
an efficiency improvement to both halves would raise the question of double counting and whether we can precisely 
delineate the interaction between the two.
2.1 Inference: from users to electricity
We estimate a user base simply via population multiplied by the share of it using AI, split across three segments by 
how intensively they use it: casual, moderate and heavy. Cultural intricacies and geographical realities obviously will be 
factors that are too complicated for us to fully scope in the current remit of our work. To help ourselves conceptualise, 
we imagine a casual user might be represented by a delivery driver using generative AI in private life, a moderate 
user an accountant or lawyer for whom it is a routine work tool, and a heavy user a developer calling an API, a quant 
researcher running research workflows, or any professional whose work now runs continuously through an LLM.
AI Users(year) = Population(year) × AI Diffusion(year) × Segment Mix(segment, year)
We observe that the mix of AI usage shifts toward the heavier segments over the horizon. As models become more 
capable, more work can be delegated to them. As generative AI loses its novelty, using it becomes part of how work 
gets done rather than something people try. Our model anticipates users move up the segments as a growing share of 
what they do runs through a model. 
Inference volume is measured in output tokens, the text a model generates. Each segment has its own query frequency 
and output length, both rising over time.
Output Tokens(year) = ∑ segments [ Users × Queries per User per Day × 365 × Output Tokens per Query ]
We see tokens split across five task archetypes: chat, reasoning, image understanding, video understanding and 
code completion. The same token costs materially different amounts of energy depending on the work behind it. 
As reasoning grows as a share of the total, it is reasonable to expect average intensity rises on top of the growth 
in volume.

In our approach to modelling energy levels, we refer to Jegham et al. (2025), which include the energy a model uses to 
read a question as well as to write the answer.22
Energy per Output Token = Base Energy per Token × Task Multiple ÷ Efficiency Index
The efficiency index compounds three annual rates: hardware gains, software gains, and an offset for models 
growing larger.
Efficiency Index(year) = Π years [ (1 – Hardware Gain) × (1 – Software Gain) × (1 + Model Growth) ] 
The steps above give the energy used while chips are doing useful work. The electricity a data centre actually draws 
is higher, for two reasons: chips spend some of their time switched on but idle, and the building itself uses power for 
cooling and power conversion, measured by power usage effectiveness (PUE).
Electricity = Compute Energy × Idle Adjustment × PUE
For image and video generation, we model energy per image and per second of video. Consumer volumes grow with 
the user base and enterprise volumes on their own path. Energy per second of video shares the hardware and software 
gains used for text, with an offset for rising resolution, frame rate and clip length.
Video Electricity = Videos per Day × 365 × Clip Length (s) × Wh per Video-second × Overhead
2.2 Training: from spend to electricity
Our approach to modelling training demand is informed by the compute spend disclosed by the large pure-play 
generative AI laboratories, scaled up to a global figure by an assumed coverage share. Using pure-play laboratories 
keeps non-generative training out of scope.
Global Training Compute Spend = Observed Laboratory Compute Spend ÷ Coverage Share
Training Electricity = Global Training Compute Spend × Energy per Dollar × PUE
Energy per US dollar is the electricity a dollar of computing buys: the power a chip and its server draw, divided by what 
an hour of that chip costs to rent. We hold it constant, assuming chips get cheaper to rent at roughly the rate they get 
more efficient to run. A 5% variation in this parameter moves the 2040 total in our base case by 0.63%.
The spend figure is the cost of computing used during the year, as if rented, not the cost of buying the machines, so it 
should not be compared with reported capital expenditure, which is several times larger. Its starting level is built from 
what the laboratories disclose; only its growth rate comes from a published forecast.

5. Principal limitations
•	 All three scenarios start from the same estimate of energy per token. That estimate is inferred rather than metered, 
and its source carries an uncertainty of roughly a factor of two. An error there would move every scenario together, 
so our Low–High range does not capture it.
•	 The model has no economic layer. It estimates how much electricity a given level of use requires, not whether that 
use will be paid for. If the value of generative AI disappointed, nothing inside the model would slow demand; that 
judgement enters only through the choice of scenario.
•	 Our boundary will become harder to observe. We count tokens generated, whatever product they sit behind. As 
generative AI becomes embedded in other software, it will be harder to separate from other data-centre demand in 
published figures, and so harder to check our estimates against them.
