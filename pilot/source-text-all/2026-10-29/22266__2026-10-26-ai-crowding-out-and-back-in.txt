AI CROWDING OUT... AND BACK IN
Dario Perkins
26 Oct 2026
There has been a lot of talk about “crowding out” recently, but not in the sense of government
spending replacing private demand. Indeed, these days people focus on crowding out in reverse –
where strong investment demand from private tech companies is making it costlier for
governments to borrow, pushing up interest rates and squeezing activity in the rate-sensitive parts
of the economy (residential construction, durable goods demand and housing). 
Is there any truth to this? Sort of...
1/7
It won’t surprise readers to learn that I don’t like the “crowding out”
thesis. (Is there anything I like?) The textbook version is bad because it
is a fake, loanable funds model of the world. There is a fixed amount of
funding available, so stronger demand for capex mechanically raises
interest rates, squeezing other activities. In reality, the financial system
is stretchy, and flexes in response to new investment opportunities.
That’s why the cost of capital often drops during economic booms.

2/7
This is relevant to the recent debate because it isnt true that more tech
issuance has mechanically driven yields higher. In fact, the thesis is full
of holes, including: (i) the fact there is no statistical relationship
between security issuance and yields; (ii) the reality that many buyers of
government paper have mandates that prevent them from buying risky
tech securities; and (iii) the fact that AI-related debt is trading at a
discount over other corporate bonds, and spreads have widened. 

3/7
But there is a softer version of the crowding out thesis that makes a
little more sense. This isn't based on loanable funds, but the idea that
the AI boom is a form of demand stimulus that is boosting inflation and
forcing the Fed to keep interest rates higher. And those higher borrowing
costs are damping activity in rate sensitive parts of the economy.
Unsurprisingly, Jason Furman –  long-time fan of the BOGUS idea that
the US would be in recession without AI – is especially fond of this idea.
4/7
It is true the AI buildout is net inflationary. We wrote about this earlier in
the year – before it became fashionable. The boom is raising energy
costs, creating supply bottlenecks in tech supply chains, and bolstering
both the PPI and the CPI. But the effect isn't large. It pains me to write
this because it’s the most sellside macro cliché in the world – but the
impact is around 0.3%pts on the CPI (more than that on the PPI). In my
defence, that is just math, not some dodgy forecasting model.

5/7
Interest rates would probably be a bit lower without the AI boom, but not
massively – because, as we have been saying for over a year, the macro
footprint of the AI buildout really isn't as large as everyone thinks. When
you see tech exports suddenly adding 20%pts to GDP in Korea and
Taiwan, that is a clear sign that most of this American stimulus is
leaking out of the US economy in the RoW.  These are the worst
investment multipliers in history.

6/7
What’s more important is that the AI boom is changing PERCEPTIONS
about the monetary stance. We see this on two dimensions. First, until
recently, the stock market seemed unstoppable. That gave the
impression that financial conditions (badly measured) were loose.
Second, the AI buildout has supported construction activity. Job gains
in the non-residential construction sector have concealed losses in the
residential sector. This has convinced officials that r* is higher than they
thought. 
7/7
So the crowding out thesis, while overblown (like everything in modern
memenomics) is not totally unfounded. What i find more interesting,
however, is the prospect of crowding IN. The Ai boom will eventually
end. When it does, as we examined in the latest Macro Picture, the Fed
will cut interest rates aggressively. And that is why the downturn will be
relatively mild - because there is plenty of scope to crowd in non tech
demand. That's another reason why the next crash won't be like
2008/09…
