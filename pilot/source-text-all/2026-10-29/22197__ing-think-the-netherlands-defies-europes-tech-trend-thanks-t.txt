The Netherlands defies Europe’s tech
trend thanks to ASML
While Europe has lagged the US and Asia in capitalising on the global
tech boom, the Netherlands stands out as an exception. ASML and its
semiconductor equipment give the Dutch economy a unique position in
the global AI investment boom, with an economic footprint that could
reach 2.2% in 2026
ASML in Veldhoven. The tech company is one of the most important suppliers of machines for the
semiconductor industry
The Netherlands stands out in Europe for its ability to benefit from the global
tech boom thanks to ASML and the semiconductor-equipment cluster.
ASML has become a macroeconomic force in its own right: its direct contribution
could reach 1.6% of Dutch value-added in 2026.
Its true economic footprint is even larger. Including domestic suppliers and
investment, we estimate ASML’s contribution could reach 2.2% in 2026.
The benefits go beyond current GDP: ASML’s highly productive ecosystem may
generate wider productivity spillovers across the Dutch economy.
Success also creates concentration risk. ASML’s growing weight means
semiconductor swings can increasingly affect Dutch economic growth.

The Dutch economy is profiting from the strong global tech
cycle
The Netherlands is an exception to Europe's difficulty in capturing the full benefits of the global
tech boom. According to the IMF's latest World Economic Outlook, the Netherlands is among
the countries most exposed to the global AI investment cycle through trade, a trend that is
boosting growth across the economies involved.
This is predominantly due to the strength of computer chip equipment maker ASML and the
wider semiconductor ecosystem surrounding it. ASML has rapidly become the largest
European company by market capitalisation and is becoming a dominant force in the Dutch
economic outlook. In fact, changes in turnover expectations have become valuable insights for
the Dutch near-term economic outlook.
Mario Draghi has warned that Europe risks becoming trapped in older technologies. Yet thanks
to ASML and the ecosystem around it, the Netherlands occupies a pivotal position in the AI-
driven technology boom and is reaping substantial economic benefits from it.
In this note, we quantify the ASML impact on the Dutch economy and discuss its implications.
We argue that ASML’s importance to the global economy has helped cushion the impact of
shocks such as the energy crisis and recent trade tensions in the Netherlands. We also argue
that it is set to boost the Dutch economic outlook, and while it comes with certain
vulnerabilities, it is a key driver of modernisation and productivity growth in the years to come.
Share of machinery in the Dutch economy has accelerated in
the last 10 years thanks to ASML
Already familiar to industry experts and increasingly recognised by the broader public, ASML is
a Dutch company and the world's sole producer of leading-edge lithography tools essential for
advanced semiconductor manufacturing.
Developing these advanced technologies requires substantial R&D expenditure. As there are
not yet competitive alternatives to its most advanced lithography tools, this gives ASML a
strong position in price negotiations. That makes ASML quite profitable. However, customer
Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company has recently shown some reluctance to adopt
ASML’s newest technology, initially viewing the tools as too costly, highlighting that the

company’s pricing power has its limits.
Nevertheless, ASML generates substantial value-added (see below for our methodology on
how we estimate the direct value-added contribution and the wider impact for the Dutch
economy). While the company is a multinational, its main production and R&D activities are
based in the Netherlands. As a result, it is mostly Dutch GDP that is boosted by the expansion
of ASML.[1] 
ASML's rise is clearly visible in industry data. Since 2017, the share of machinery in total Dutch
gross value-added has increased. Trends like the introduction and increasing popularity of
smartphones, cloud-based services, social media and the demand boom in (ICT) goods during
the Covid lockdowns have been very beneficial for semiconductor-related businesses, including
producers of semiconductor lithography systems. This trend has accelerated with the rise of
large language models and the broader AI investment cycle, lifting the machinery industry's
share of the Dutch economy to around 2.7% in current prices and 2.9% in constant prices by
2025.

Half of the Dutch machinery industry is ASML
Admittedly, ASML is not the only machinery company in the Netherlands, and not all
machinery expansion can be attributed to just this business. Other large tech companies in the
semiconductor equipment manufacturing cluster include ASM International and BE
Semiconductor Industries. Other well-known Dutch companies active in the wider machinery
industry include VDL Groep (e.g. food, packaging, and agricultural robots, also an important
supplier to ASML), Vanderlande (e.g. logistics systems) and Lely (e.g. cattle milking robots and

ASML alone is on track to account for 1.6% of the Dutch
economy
For each euro of turnover, ASML creates about 50 cents of value-added with its own activities,
of which we (perhaps somewhat conservatively) assume that 40 cents can be assigned
domestically, which is slightly less than the 42 cents of the total machinery industry.
We therefore estimate that the direct contribution of ASML to Dutch GDP has risen from a
modest 0.1% in 1995 to a substantial 1.3% in 2025. That is significant for an economy with a
high development level and a high degree of diversification. Based on ASML’s own expectations
(of which we take the midpoint of €44.5bn) and our own economic growth forecasts, this may
well be 1.6% in 2026.

suppliers are located around Veldhoven, where ASML's headquarters are, making the broader
value chain key to the company's success.

In the absence of ASML-specific data, we investigate the indirect impacts by looking into
intermediate inputs and investment of the Dutch machinery industry, using input-output
tables.
About half of the gross intermediate inputs come from the Netherlands, half from abroad. For
each euro of total intermediate consumption by the machinery industry/ASML, about 20 cents
of domestic value-added is created. This means that intermediate consumption by the total
machinery industry has an impact of 0.8% of total Dutch value-added. If we scale this to the
size of ASML, this means the domestic impact of intermediate consumption by ASML is 0.3% of
the Dutch economy in 2025 (and is forecast at 0.4% in 2026). This is still quite sizeable.
The machinery industry sources a large share of its inputs from within the sector, although
many of these suppliers are located abroad. For ASML, these include both strategic partners
and external suppliers, such as ZEISS SMT in Germany, as well as subsidiaries like Cymer in San
Diego. Other important suppliers include wholesale trade, metal products manufacturing, and
professional services such as legal and management consultancy. Within the Netherlands,
however, temporary job agencies are among the biggest beneficiaries of the sector's activity,
alongside other firms in the machinery industry.

Impact via investment, especially R&D
The second indirect contribution to the economy is the domestic value-added content of gross
capital formation. While the impact of ASML and the broader machinery industry through
investment is smaller than that of their own production and supply-chain activity, it remains
significant enough to have a meaningful effect on the Dutch economy. For 2025, we estimate
the contribution through investment at 0.2% for ASML and 0.1% for the broader machinery
industry. For ASML, we expect this contribution to remain at 0.2% in 2026.
This captures the economic impact generated beyond the investment that is done within
machinery/ASML. Own in-house R&D is not included in this estimate since it is already included
in the direct impact and would otherwise be counted twice. From our analysis based on input
tables, we can conclude that when it comes to sourcing, R&D investment is the most
important capital asset type for the sector: on average, this was about 57% of gross capital
formation. The second- and third-most important are other machinery and equipment and
computer software and databases, followed by buildings and computer hardware.
Adding the direct impact and the two indirect impacts, we estimate the total impact of the
machinery industry and ASML on the Dutch economy at 3.7% in 2025 (and forecast it for ASML
at 2.2% in 2026). While this falls short of the 3.8% contribution Nokia made to the Finnish
economy in 2000 (excluding indirect effects), it is nevertheless highly significant.

Wide economic benefits
The gains are likely not limited to the output and employment of the machinery industry.
Aggregate productivity levels in the Netherlands would probably also be lower without the
presence of this export giant. Not only are ASML's own activities highly productive, but joint
research by the CPB and Statistics Netherlands also shows that firms supplying exporters tend
to be among the most productive companies in the Dutch economy, second only to the
exporters themselves.
Moreover, international case-specific evidence suggests that the benefits of hosting a globally
competitive high-tech champion may extend beyond its direct value-added and purchases
from domestic suppliers (see here for an example). Participation in the supply chain of a
leading multinational can improve productivity, scale, management practices and the ability
of suppliers to attract new customers. These spillover effects are difficult to quantify, but they
suggest that ASML's contribution to long-term Dutch prosperity may be larger than what can
be observed in national accounts alone.
Should the Netherlands worry about a Nokia effect?
Despite the wide economic benefits, the strong dependence of value-added on one industry
and one company in particular can also be a vulnerability for the Dutch economy. What may
now be a strength that other countries envy may later turn into an economic weakness. As a
large part of ASML’s business is known to be cyclical, macroeconomic swings may be

exacerbated and shocks may be limited not only to cyclical fluctuations but can be more
permanent as well. This is despite the fact that ASML is increasingly becoming a services
provider for its installed base. These services are typically less cyclical, with lower utilisation by
customers also providing opportunities to undertake maintenance and upgrades.
Even global market leaders can lose their edge when markets change, as Nokia's difficulties
following the advent of smartphones illustrate. At its peak around 1998-2000, the share of
Nokia in Finnish GDP was close to 4%, but in 2011 it had fallen to 0.5%. OECD analysis suggests
that the prolonged contraction of Finland's computer sector was closely linked to Nokia's
decline following the rise of smartphones, and may explain at least 30% of the productivity
slowdown in the rest of the Finnish business economy during 2009-13. The estimate is based
on industry-level effects rather than Nokia specifically, but it highlights how disruptive a shock
to a dominant firm can be, even within a highly developed technology ecosystem.
But the Netherlands is a more diversified economy than Finland was at the peak of Nokia’s
strength. Our estimate of a 2.2% total impact on the economy of ASML so far is still well below
the 3.8% (excluding indirect effects) Nokia once contributed in 2000 as a single company to
the Finnish economy. The diversification is evident in other thriving high-tech sectors like
pharma and IT services, which are also contributing substantially to growth this decade.
Geopolitical fragmentation brings risks to both markets and
critical inputs for chip machines
The Dutch machinery industry’s deep integration into global trade is a source of strength, but
also creates a vulnerability to geopolitical fragmentation. For ASML, this is currently the case
outside the European Union, as semiconductor manufacturing is predominantly based outside
Europe. Both ASML and the broader machinery industry depend heavily on continued access
to foreign markets and to highly specialised inputs from abroad. IMF analysis shows
substantial geographic concentration on both sides: export demand is particularly exposed to
disruptions involving the US, Germany, China and South Korea, while the supply of several
critical machinery inputs is concentrated in a relatively small number of countries.
These risks are no longer hypothetical: US restrictions already limit exports of ASML’s most
advanced machines to China, while further measures, including the proposed Match Act, could
constrain Dutch exports further. At the same time, our analysis of Eurostat input-output data
shows that foreign intermediate inputs for the Dutch machinery industry are particularly
dependent on Germany, the rest of the world, including Taiwan and South Korea, and the US.
Geopolitical tensions can therefore hit the sector from both directions: by restricting where
Dutch machinery can be sold and by disrupting access to the critical inputs needed to produce
it.
For the Dutch economy, this brings a downside risk to growth. Export market restrictions can
weaken foreign demand, while disruptions to critical inputs can constrain domestic production

specialised firms to source the best inputs internationally and sell their products globally. The
same international specialisation that has made the Dutch high-tech sector so successful
therefore also makes it vulnerable when geopolitics interferes with trade.
High-tech is reshaping the Dutch economy, but can growth
continue at this pace?
Since the mid-2010s, ASML has evolved from a highly successful technology company into a
business of genuine macroeconomic significance for the Netherlands.
Its direct and indirect contribution to Dutch value-added is now large enough to influence
national developments and has helped the Dutch economy benefit much more than countries
around it from the global AI investment boom. ASML also strengthens the European tech
ecosystem, for instance through its partnership with imec, its collaboration with TNO on the
photonic chip pilot line, and its investment in Mistral AI. These initiatives could support new
technologies and future growth opportunities.
For the years ahead, the question is whether the Dutch economy can continue to
accommodate the rapid growth of the chip-machine-making cluster. The Dutch economy has
shown signs of strain in recent years as limits to talent, space and other supply-side
constraints are the main source of concern for future growth. In 2024, the Dutch government
decided to invest €2.5bn to improve the business climate for the sector, but overall concerns
around talent availability and specific bottlenecks in the economy that we wrote about
repeatedly (see for example here, here and most recently here in Dutch) remain.
Despite challenges, we do expect that the growth of the ASML cluster will remain an important
structural driver for economic growth in the Netherlands.

Methodology:
Based on details that are only publicly available for 2025, we estimate the gross value-
added (in current prices) of ASML’s own production (the “direct impact”) by subtracting
sourcing spent from net sales. To produce a time series for 1995-25, we collect the net
sales and gross profit margin from ASML’s annual accounts based on the GAAP
methodology. For the 2026 forecast, we use the midpoint of ASML’s own projections
available in the most recent quarterly press release. We then work with constant
ratios. We assume that in each year the ratio of value-added or intermediate
consumption (depending on what data is available) to the gross margin is the same as
in 2025.
Since not all the value-added of ASML can be attributed to the Netherlands, we assume
that 80% ends up in Dutch value-added. This is equal to the unweighted average share
of the Netherlands in R&D full-time equivalents and profit before taxation, based on
the Public Country-by-Country Report 2025 and Tax Report 2025. Note that in official
national accounts, Statistics Netherlands (CBS) may attribute a different share to the
Netherlands than we do, as it has access to detailed microdata that is not publicly
available.
To determine the indirect impacts on Dutch domestic value-added, we assume that
the Dutch machinery industry’s intermediate consumption and gross capital formation
are representative for ASML. This is a strong assumption, but it allows us to provide a
more complete picture of the wider impact of the company. We use input-output,
supply and use tables in current prices for 2010-25 by Statistics Netherlands. These
data are better suited to accurately track the impact of other Dutch industries than
international input-output, supply and use tables. Again, we extrapolate with constant
ratios for 1995-09 and 2026. We apply the so-called Leontief inverse to ensure that
indirect effects via all industries are taken into account, not only direct purchases. For
further insights into international linkages, including those via third countries,
Eurostat’s 2023 FIGARO input-output tables were used.
For investment by the machinery industry, we used data by Statistics Netherlands on
gross capital formation by capital asset type for 1995-23. Since input-output tables
provide no data on which industry produces which type of productive capital, we make
our own assumptions.
