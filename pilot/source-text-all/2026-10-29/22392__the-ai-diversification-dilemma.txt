The AI diversification 
dilemma
Managing concentration risk across public and private markets. 
The AI investment boom is creating a new concentration challenge for global 
portfolios. What began as a technology-led equity story has become a much broader 
investment cycle, drawing growing amounts of capital into public and private 
markets. For investors, the risk is that portfolios diversified across asset classes and 
geographies are becoming more dependent on the spending decisions of a handful of 
technology companies.
The scale of the opportunity is considerable. As we outlined in Investing across the 
AI Supercycle, we estimate that $5.5 trillion to $6.0 trillion will be required through 
2030, with around 80% financed through capital markets, predominantly credit. As 
that financing expands, so does concentration. 
This creates a dilemma for investors. Reducing exposure to the largest technology 
companies means taking underweights against increasingly concentrated equity 
benchmarks, potentially sacrificing returns if AI investment continues to accelerate. 
Maintaining those holdings while adding AI-related credit and infrastructure adds 
exposure to the same cycle.
We think investors should retain conviction in the AI theme while becoming more 
selective about where they take that risk. 
AI’s expanding reach is changing portfolio risk
AI’s growing influence across capital markets makes concentration harder to 
identify through traditional measures. Issuer and sector limits remain important, 
but they do not always capture the interconnectedness of holdings across different 
asset classes.
Consider a hyperscaler held through an equity allocation and an investment-
grade credit portfolio. The same company may guarantee the lease supporting a 
privately placed data centre bond and serve as the anchor tenant in an infrastructure 
investment. Each position has different contractual rights and risks, but its financial 
strength and investment plans influence all four.

The dependencies extend further. AI capex supports semiconductor revenues and 
construction activity, as well as demand for electricity and the repayment capacity 
of privately financed projects. A slowdown in spending could therefore affect 
businesses with very different sector classifications.
This concentration is already visible at the index level. The Magnificent 7 
contributed around 52% of the S&P 500’s total net earnings growth in 2024, and 
without their contribution, 2023 earnings growth would have been negative.1 
Geographic diversification is also becoming less straightforward. Three Korean 
and Taiwanese chipmakers accounted for nearly one-third of the MSCI Emerging 
Markets Index at the end of September.2 An allocation intended to reduce 
dependence on U.S. equities may therefore introduce substantial exposure to the 
same global technology cycle. 
Measuring how much AI risk a portfolio owns
Our equity research team examines companies’ direct and indirect revenue exposure 
to AI, including dependencies several stages removed from the largest platforms. 
Extending that analysis across public and private markets and establishing a 
portfolio-wide AI exposure budget based on the sources of revenue and repayment 
can help manage the risks. 
The analysis begins with a slowdown in capital expenditure by hyperscalers. 
Chipmakers could face weaker earnings alongside lower valuation multiples, while 
lease-backed data centre bonds could experience spread widening as investors 
reassess tenant creditworthiness and collateral values. These effects can then be 
aggregated across holdings, net of any offsetting gains in assets with independent 
return drivers and expressed as a single figure in percentage points of total 
portfolio value.
The analysis also considers a more disorderly scenario, where correlations rise, 
liquidity deteriorates and losses across AI-linked assets compound. Investors 
can assess the results to understand whether the portfolio carries excessive 
exposure to the cycle.
Finding returns beyond the buildout
Finding diversifiers is becoming harder as the AI investment cycle reaches into 
more industries. Even industrials and commodity producers are becoming more 
dependent on expectations for data centre demand. 
We see opportunities in European and Japanese industrial companies benefiting 
from AI adoption, often at more attractive valuations than U.S. technology leaders. 
Investors should differentiate between businesses using AI to improve productivity 
and those whose revenues depend heavily on supplying the infrastructure. 
A related distinction applies to physical infrastructure. We favour capital-intensive 
businesses with hard-to-replicate assets and limited obsolescence risk, including 
power grids, baseload generation and cooling systems. These assets typically carry 
high replacement costs and relatively inelastic demand that can provide a hedge 
even as they benefit from AI-driven growth.

The theme also has a geographic dimension. Non-U.S. markets offer an investible 
universe of airports, toll roads, utilities and midstream pipelines, where AI-driven 
energy demand has yet to be fully priced in, often at more attractive entry points 
than U.S. counterparts.3
We also see opportunities in European financials and selected old-economy 
businesses, including packaging, where earnings are driven by different sources 
of demand. In private markets, core middle-market lending to established old-
economy businesses (such as wastewater management and commercial landscaping) 
alongside health care real estate and grocery-anchored retail can provide exposure 
to cash flows less reliant on AI investment. 
For investors benchmarked against global equities, these allocations may 
underperform if technology leadership continues. Our framework can help analyse 
that potential cost against the protection offered during an AI slowdown. We favour 
accepting some benchmark-relative risk to build exposure to independent sources of 
return, while retaining meaningful participation in AI. 
Getting paid to finance the AI buildout
The financing requirements of the AI investment cycle are creating opportunities 
across public and private credit, but investors are becoming more discriminating 
about the risks they finance. As of July, nearly 80% of data centre securities issued 
since early 2025 were quoted at wider spreads than at issuance, while some planned 
bond sales had been postponed or withdrawn.4 The repricing creates opportunities 
for selective lenders, provided financing terms offer adequate compensation for 
construction risk and sufficient creditor protection.
A single data centre can be financed through unsecured corporate bonds, lease-
backed vehicles or privately placed project bonds, with investment grade data centre 
bonds trading 50 to 200 basis points wider than their tenants’ unsecured debt.5 
Some of the premium compensates for construction risk and illiquidity, as well as 
uncertainty over residual asset values. Strong counterparties and lease guarantees 
can provide additional creditor protection, particularly where debt amortises over 
the contracted lease term. 
Those protections are only as strong as the tenant. While the largest technology 
platforms have relatively conservative reported leverage, more than $1 trillion of 
leases not yet commenced and approximately $1.7 trillion of purchase commitments 
point to substantial future obligations.6 Circular financing arrangements 
create further dependencies, allowing stress in one part of the AI ecosystem to 
affect counterparties elsewhere. Recent weakness in the debt of lower-rated AI 
infrastructure providers, including bonds financing data centres leased to those 
companies, illustrates how concerns about tenant creditworthiness can spread 
across financing structures. 
Execution risk also deserves attention. Power constraints and local opposition 
could delay project completion, increasing financing costs and putting pressure on 
returns. Construction delays may also allow tenants to terminate agreements under 
certain contractual provisions, leaving lenders exposed to projects without the 
anticipated cash flows.

We favour select privately placed lease-backed and project bonds where 
compensation adequately reflects these risks. These structures can offer more 
attractive terms than unsecured exposure to the same tenant, but investors should 
recognise that better compensation addresses pricing, not correlation as the 
underlying exposure to hyperscaler capex decisions remains.
Positioning for the next phase
In public credit, we favour companies with resilient balance sheets and financing 
structures that provide adequate compensation for concentration. Investment-
grade utility operating companies and selected energy and power infrastructure 
financing offer opportunities where contractual cash flows can support debt service. 
As outlined in our recent research, Investing across the world’s largest opportunity 
set, we also see selective opportunities among established providers of power 
management, cooling and network infrastructure, where demand extends beyond 
individual hyperscalers. 
Emerging market debt offers another source of return less directly tied to the 
AI investment cycle. While emerging market equity benchmarks have become 
concentrated in Asian technology companies, sovereign bond returns reflect 
a broader range of economic and policy developments. Latin American local 
currency debt illustrates this, with returns supported by domestic monetary policy 
and currency appreciation rather than technology earnings.7 Selective EM debt 
exposures provide diversification of return drivers, with our preference for where 
real yields are attractive and underlying fundamentals are healthy. 
Liquidity is also a consideration for investors with substantial private market 
allocations. Refinancing needs and potential calls on capital could coincide across 
investments linked to the same technology counterparties. We would place weight 
on financing resilience and the ability of underlying assets to generate cash flows 
through a slower investment cycle.
Some of the warning signs are already emerging. Pressure is particularly evident in 
lower-rated borrowers and projects still under construction. We see this as a reason 
for greater selectivity within credit rather than a retreat from the investment cycle. 
Our view would change if these pressures spread beyond the more vulnerable parts 
of the market. Spreads on hyperscalers’ debt have also widened,8 although higher 
rates and heavy issuance appear to have played a greater role than concerns about 
their underlying credit quality. 
A sustained deterioration driven by weaker fundamentals, particularly alongside 
reductions in capex guidance or slowing cloud revenue growth, would suggest 
a broader reassessment of AI demand. We would also watch whether rising 
correlations across AI-linked assets begin to undermine the diversification investors 
expect from holding them in different parts of a portfolio. 
Conversely, sustained cloud revenue growth and stronger evidence of AI 
monetization would reinforce the investment case, although continued technology 
leadership could deepen concentration in benchmark portfolios. 
As investors set next year’s allocations, understanding a portfolio’s exposure to the 
AI cycle could be critical for investment success.
