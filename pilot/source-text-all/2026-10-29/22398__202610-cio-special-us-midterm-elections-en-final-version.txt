U.S. midterms: Ten questions investors 
should be asking
 
From gridlock and government spending to AI and bond yields, we explore what the 2026 U.S. 
midterms could mean for markets
 
IN A NUTSHELL 
— Predicting the midterm elections correctly does not guarantee the right portfolio decision. 
What matters is how the result compares with what the market has already priced in.
 
— Equities have often weakened ahead of elections and recovered afterwards, but individual 
cycles vary considerably, while high
-yield spreads show little consistent independent election 
effect. Fundamentals and the wider macro backdrop typically matter more 
for markets. 
— Recounts and legal challenges in tight electoral races are a near
-term risk, but perhaps the 
biggest short -term market impact is likely to emerge beyond Congress, at the state and local 
level through regulation, for example of AI infrastructure.
 
 
Introduction 
Washington pundits and policymakers have an old habit: declaring that an election sent a clear message, then spending 
months arguing about what it 
was . Markets tend to react rather more quickly.  
 
On 3 November 2026, Americans will elect all 435 members of the House of Representatives and vote in 35 Senate races, 
including two special elections.
1 Voters will also choose 39 state and territorial governors, alongside members of numerous 
state legislatures and holders of many other state and local offices. By the time markets open on 4 November, investors will 
have to decide whether any of these resu
lts warrant a change in their expectations for growth, inflation, earnings or the 
U.S. 
dollar. With that in mind, we have structured our
 election outlook around ten questions investors should be
 asking .  
1 / What is the market expecting from the 2026 midterms?
 
The starting point is not simply who wins, but what markets already expect. Current forecasts favor Democrats taking control 
of the House, although surprises remain possible. Even correctly predicting the political result does not necessarily lead to
 
the right investment decision. What matters is whether the outcome is better or worse than investors had anticipated and 
how markets interpret the result. 
 
 
The 2016 presidential election offers a useful reminder. Markets initially fell as Donald Trump's surprise victory became cle
ar, 
but the decline had more than reversed by the end of the following trading day as investors turned their attention to the 
prosp ect of corporate tax cuts. Getting the politics right and getting the market right 
can be two different things.

2 / Could the biggest surprises come from outside Washington?
 
Yes. Investors will naturally focus on control of the House and Senate, but some of the most interesting signals could come 
further down the ballot. Americans will also elect governors, state legislators and numerous local officials. 
 
 
That matters economically. State and local officeholders can influence electricity supply, transmission lines, planning deci-
sions and permits for data 
centers . A result that barely moves a broad equity index could therefore still matter significantly 
for individual companies 
awaiting project approval s. 
3 / Can investors rely on the usual midterm patterns?
  
History can be a useful guide, but it should not be treated as a rule. The president’s party has 
usually lost House seats at 
midterm elections, and 2026 currently looks consistent with that pattern.
2 But this election is unusual because President 
Trump is serving a second, non
-consecutive term. That is rare within U.S. history – the only other example would be 
Grover 
Cleveland . Arguably, the closer modern paral lel are presidents serving a second, consecutive term . Within this smaller subset 
of midterms , there is a much 
stronger effect known as 
the “six -year itch ,” w ith  the president’s party almost always losing 
support . 3  
 
Markets have patterns
, too: U.S. equities have often weakened ahead of midterms and recovered afterwards. But these 
patterns tell us what happened previously, not what caused it. Investors should therefore treat historical precedents as usef
ul 
reference points, rather than pred
ictions. 
4 / Would divided government be good for markets?
  
Not necessarily. Conventional Wall Street thinking tends to view divided government positively 
because political gridlock can 
make major legislative changes harder to achieve. But historical experience does not provide such a simple conclusion. Re-
search 4 finds higher average excess equity returns under unified than divided government. 
 
 
More importantly, fewer laws need not mean less political uncertainty. Congress would still need to agree 
to annual spending 
bills, leaving scope for disputes over government funding
 and potentially a government shutdown
. A constrained White 
House could also rely more heavily on other policy tools. For investors, the relevant question 
in our view is therefore not 
simply whether Washington becomes gridlocked, but what policymakers do in response.
 
5 / Why could the midterms matter for AI and data centers?
   
Because the politics of AI is increasingly also about physical infrastructure. Data cent
ers need electricity, grid connections 
and local infrastructure, and state and local governments can influence planning and permitting decisions. 
 
 
If election results are interpreted as reflecting local concerns about data cente
rs, local legislators could respond by scrutiniz-
ing permits more closely, asking developers to contribute more towards infrastructure or seeking to protect households from 
rising power costs. That could turn politics into an earnings issue for 
some beneficiaries of the AI buildout. 
 
 
But investors also need to look at the other side of the equation. If AI begins to increase productivity more broadly, that 
economic impact could prove more important for equity valuations than the political debate
 surrounding data centers .

6 / Does political gridlock mean greater fiscal discipline?
    
Again, not necessarily. A divided Washington 
could make it difficult to pass large new fiscal programs, but much 
of the 
existing federal spending would 
likely continue. Interest costs would continue to accumulate, while compromises over annual 
funding could preserve existing commitments or add spending without resolving the longer
-term debt problem.  
 
This distinction is particularly important for U.S. Treasuries. Investors will need to consider what the election means for f
uture 
deficits, debt issuance and the yields required to attract buyers of government debt. Gridlock can make policy change harder.
 
It cannot, by itself, fix the fiscal arithmetic
. 
7 / How s hould investors treat the first election -night results?    
With caution. If control of the Senate depends on one or two very close contests, the apparent outcome could change as 
more ballots are counted. Exit polls are estimates, while close races can involve late
-arriving ballots, recounts, litigation and 
differing state certification procedures. 
 
 
Markets, however, do not necessarily wait for the final result
. Previous research shows that equities, interest rates, oil and 
the U.S. dollar can move as the perceived probability of a political outcome changes. That makes it especially important to 
distinguish new information from election
-night noise . 
8 / How might election results affect the investment outlook?
     
Election results do not come with trading instructions. The chain that matters is: what had markets expected, what surprised 
them, how does that change political incentives and policy, and what does that eventually mean for earnings, inflation, 
interest rates and risk premia?
  
 
For 2026, that means looking beyond the simple question of which party controls Congress. Investors will need to consider 
the consequences for tariffs, fiscal policy, AI infrastructure and bond yields, while deciding which election
-night develop-
ments repre sent lasting economic signals and which are primarily political noise.
 While the lat ter could certainly include 
recounts and legal challenges in tight races, 
it is worth keeping in mind that there is less at stake at midterms in general,
 
than in presidential elections . Senate control could potentially come down to a single
 seat but even that would have limited 
investment implications. As a result, we would expect politics 
to return to business as usual, at least as far as markets are 
concerned. In other words, 
investors appear likely to 
focus on the decisions the newly elected officials promise to make, 
rather than who actually won.

midterms?     
For equity investors, the key is to 
distinguish between market
-wide and company
-specific effects. History offers some en-
couragement, but also an important warning. The S&P
 500 has typically experienced some weakness in the months ahead 
of midterm elections before recovering in the following year.
5 Yet, returns over the 12 months following individual midterms 
have varied considerably , suggesting that the average is no 
particularly useful guide as to what comes next. Historical returns 
also differ depending on the political configuration: 
the most relevant comparison is 
under Republican presidents since 19
53. 
For these admittedly small samples
, average annual S&P 500 returns were positive 
for all combinations of control of Con-

 when a Republican president faced Congres-

 both the House and the Senate

An election result may dominate the headlines 
without materially changing the outlook for the broader equity market, where earnings, productivity, monetary policy and

10 / What should bond investors take away from the 2026 
midterms?     
For bond investors, the election result is only the starting point. The bigger question is whether it changes expectations fo
r 
growth, inflation, government borrowing and ultimately interest rates. Political uncertainty can also influence the extra com-
pens ation investors demand for holding longer
-dated bonds. That means the implications may emerge gradually rather than 
on election night. Investors should therefore look beyond which party controls Congress and focus on whether the resulting 
policy mix change s the expected path of inflation, Treasury yields and the 
price of longer -term government debt. 
 
Credit markets tell a similar story. Across the six midterms for which Bloomberg Global high
-yield spread data are available 
since 2002, election outcomes show little consistent independent effect on spreads. Instead, post
-election moves appear to 
have bee n driven more by the prevailing credit cycle and wider macroeconomic developments, such as 
the second round of 
Quantitative easing (QE) in 2010, the oil shock in 2014 and 
the Federal Reserve ’s tightening in 2018. With such a limited 
historical sample, the macroeconomic outlook and sector
-specific risks may matter more
, though in years to come, a larger 
sample might well reveal some interesting patterns
. 
Conclusion      
Elections make headlines, but not every political shift changes the investment outlook. The lesson from these ten questions 
is simple: look beyond who wins and ask what, if anything, changes for growth, inflation, earnings and interest rates
, shifting 
market expectations . History can be a useful guide, but it’s not a rulebook. For investors, separating meaningful economic 
signals from political noise may ultimately matter more than correctly predicting the result
.

Glossary 
AI infrastructure comprises the physical facilities and systems needed to develop and operate artificial intelligence, including data centers, 
electricity generation, power grids and network connections.
 
The credit cycle is the expansion and contraction of access to credit over time and is usually connected to the business cycle.
 
A data center is a facility containing computing equipment used to store, process and transmit digital information.
 
The Democratic Party (Democrats)
 is one of the two political parties in the United States. It is generally to the left of its main rival, the 
Republican Party. 
Fiscal policy describes government spending policies that influence macroeconomic conditions. Through fiscal policy, the government at-
tempts to improve unemployment rates, control inflation, stabilize business cycles and influence interest rates in an effort 
to control the 
economy. 
A government shutdown occurs when funding legislation is not approved in time, requiring some government activities to stop temporarily.
 
High -yield bonds are issued by below
-investment -grade -rated issuers and usually offer a relatively high yield.
 
The United States House of Representatives is a legislative chamber consisting of 435 Representatives, as well as non
-voting delegates from 
Washington, D.C. and U.S. territories. Representatives are elected for two
-year terms and each state's representation is based on population 
as measured in th e previous Census. 
Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services is rising and, subsequently, purchasing power is fall
ing. 
Interest rate is the proportion of a loan that is charged as interest to the borrower, typically expressed as an annual percentage of the l
oan 
outstanding. 
Monetary policy focuses on controlling the supply of money with the ulterior motive of price stability, reducing unemployment, boosting 
growth, etc. (depending on the central bank's mandate).
 
Political gridlock is a situation in which disagreement between political parties makes it difficult to pass legislation.
 
Productivity measures how much economic output is produced for a given level of inputs (such as capital and labor).
 
Quantitative easing (QE) is an unconventional monetary
-policy tool, in which a central bank conducts broad
-based asset purchases.
 
The Republican Party (Republicans)
, also referred to as Grand Old Party (GOP), is one of the two major political parties in the United States. 
It is generally to the right of its main rival, the Democratic Party.
 
The risk premium is the expected return on an investment minus the return that would be earned on a risk
-free investment. 
The S&P 500 is an index that includes 500 leading U.S. companies capturing approximately 80% coverage of available U.S. market capitali-
zation. 
The spread is the difference between the quoted rates of return on two different investments, usually of different credit quality.
 
A tariff is a tax imposed by one country on the goods and services imported from another country.
 
Treasuries are U.S. government debt securities, including Treasury bills, notes, bonds, and inflation
-protected securities. 
The U.S. Federal Reserve , often referred to as "the Fed," is the central bank of the United States.
 
The United States Congress 
is the legislature of the federal government. It is comprised of the Senate and the House of Representatives, 
consisting of 435 Representatives and 100 Senators.
 
United States Senate is the upper chamber of the U.S. Congress, composed of 100 senators, two from each state, elected to staggered six
-
year terms. 
Valuation attempts to quantify the attractiveness of an asset, for example through looking at a firm's stock price in relation to its e
arnings. 
Yield refers to the income generated by an investment, such as interest or dividends, usually expressed as an annual percentage of 
its value.
