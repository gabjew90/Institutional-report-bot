U.S. Midterms: 
When Politics Misprices 
Fundamentals
While markets may trade the midterms on headlines, the opportunity lies 
beneath them: where policy shifts can change industry economics—and 
where political noise may push prices away from fundamentals. Aerospace 
and defense, health care, and AI infrastructure show how differently that 
tension can play out.
Key takeaways
	
■The midterms are a policy signal, not an endpoint. The outcome may provide insight into the direction, 
durability and practical constraints of future policy.
	
■Not every political development has the same investment significance. Its impact can vary within a sector 
based on competitive positioning, project or program funding, and exposure to potential regulatory change.
	
■Separating perception from fundamentals. Our teams look to identify stock-specific risks and 
opportunities by distinguishing shifts in political expectations from policy changes that can meaningfully 
alter earnings and long-term economics.

1. Aerospace & defense
The signal
Unified Democratic control of Congress could increase uncertainty around defense appropriations, selected 
program timing and spending priorities A split Congress could raise the risk of an extended Continuing 
Resolution (CR) to start CY2027, temporarily holding base defense funding near prior-year levels. 
The economics
A prolonged CR could delay new program starts and contract awards and push revenue or cash flow timing to 
the right. Multi-year frameworks can improve visibility, but the economic benefit reaches prime contractors and 
suppliers only as agreements become funded contracts and production rates rise.
The disconnect
Funding headlines could pressure defense broadly even when long-term national security demand remains intact. 
Programs supported by signed contracts, established backlogs or enduring mission priorities may have materially 
less economic exposure than newer starts that depend on the next appropriations cycle.
Our perspective
Temporary budget friction 
can create dispersion 
between delayed revenue 
and impaired demand. The 
opportunity may be greatest 
where the market discounts 
an intact program because 
of timing uncertainty, while 
businesses dependent on 
the next budget decision 
warrant greater caution.

2. Health care
The signal
The election may signal shifts in coverage, reimbursement, drug pricing, and research funding, but the direction 
and economic impact will vary significantly by subsector. Providers, managed care, biopharma, and life science 
tools should not be viewed as a single political trade.
The economics
Providers face policy exposure through coverage, utilization, and uncompensated care; MCOs through 
membership, reimbursement, and medical costs; and biopharma through Medicare and Medicaid drug pricing. Life 
science tools face indirect R&D funding risks, while medtech depends partly on provider utilization.
The disconnect
Broad health care headlines may move subsectors together despite different policy exposure. Life science 
tools face less direct risk, biopharma has limited downside beyond current drug-pricing law, and sweeping MCO 
proposals such as Medicare for All remain unlikely to be implemented.
Our perspective
Policy changes affect health 
care subsectors differently, 
creating opportunities when 
broad market reactions 
overstate the impact on 
a company’s earnings and 
long-term fundamentals.

3. AI infrastructure & data centers
The signal
The elections may reveal the strength and geographic reach of resistance related to power costs, grid access, 
permitting, zoning, water use, and community impact. Because these concerns cross party lines, state and local 
outcomes may prove as consequential as the federal result.
The economics
Greater resistance could raise development costs, reduce tax benefits, require on-site power solutions, or delay 
interconnection and construction. Projects with secured power, established approvals and local support should be 
less exposed than developments still dependent on future grid capacity or political backing.
The disconnect
Political headlines could pressure the broader data center ecosystem even when near-term projects already have 
power and approvals. The greater economic risk may lie with later-stage developments whose returns still depend 
on future grid capacity, permitting, tax incentives or community acceptance.
Our perspective 
Political constraints may 
increase the scarcity value 
of secured capacity while 
shifting spending toward 
on-site power, microgrids, 
grid upgrades, and 
connections between 
existing facilities. The 
opportunity is not only 
identifying delayed projects, 
but also the businesses 
positioned to benefit as 
development adapts.

What’s unlikely to change
The administration’s broader industrial policy priorities—including national security, AI, and domestic manufacturing—
are likely to remain intact. The midterms may shape how those priorities are implemented, not reverse their direction. 
For investors, the key is identifying where political change could alter execution, timing or underlying economics.
Bottom line
	
■Markets will trade the election. We look for the disconnects it creates.
	
■The result matters when policy expectations change the earnings path, not merely the political narrative.
	
■Policy can create tailwinds, but durable opportunities arise when they reinforce competitive strength rather 
than substitute for it. Active investors can capitalize when political perception diverges from company 
fundamentals.
