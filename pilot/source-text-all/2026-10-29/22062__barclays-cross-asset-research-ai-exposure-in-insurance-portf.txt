AI Exposure in Insurance Portfolios
– Just the Start
We quantify AI exposure at life insurers and identify the
structures through which insurers are taking risk in the
growing sector. We think the overall risk is low currently
because the allocations are small, but we expect them to
grow.
Equity View Summary
• Exposure remains manageable today: Current digital infrastructure holdings appear
modest and generally high quality, with conservative underwriting providing additional
comfort around the underlying risk.
• Concentration is higher at select insurers and exposure is growing: FG, CRBG, and Athene
screen above peers, while strong recent purchasing suggests allocations are increasing
quickly.
• Comfortable today, but worth monitoring: Current balance-sheet risk appears
manageable, though total exposure is likely understated; a significant deterioration in
hyperscaler credit would likely matter more through broader recessionary fixed-income stress
than through direct digital infrastructure exposure alone.

• Risk tolerances are unclear: We expect insurers to increasingly discuss how they manage AI

experienced limited impairments to their credit portfolios overall, and there is no reason to

Life Insurers' Exposure to Digital Infrastructure by Company 
We are fielding a significant increase in investor interest around life insurers’ exposure to data
centers and the broader digital infrastructure ecosystem. To size the risk, we leveraged U.S.
statutory filings to identify relevant holdings at insurance operating companies and estimate
exposure at the consolidated company level. Our primary takeaway is that exposure remains
relatively modest today, with digital infrastructure representing approximately 1.4% of bond
portfolios within our peer set. The exposure equates to roughly 24% of statutory surplus as of
2Q26, falling to approximately 15% after our estimated adjustment for funds withheld assets.
Importantly, credit quality also appears strong: 99% of the digital infrastructure exposure we
analyzed across our broader peer group is currently investment grade, while 68% is NAIC Class
1, or effectively rated A- or better. Meanwhile, the ABS transactions we reviewed generally have
LTVs in the 40-60% range, providing additional comfort around the underlying risk. That said,
exposure is growing quickly, with purchases during 1Q26 and 2Q26 contributing to a meaningful
increase in holdings. On our funds-withheld-adjusted measure of digital infrastructure exposure
relative to surplus, FG (39%), CRBG (33%), and Athene (28%) screen highest versus a peer
average of roughly 15%, although these exposures remain relatively small compared with other
higher-risk asset categories we have previously analyzed.
Overall, we are comfortable with the risk currently on life insurer balance sheets, but believe the
exposure warrants continued monitoring given the pace at which new money is being allocated
to the sector. Our analysis also likely understates total exposure because it does not fully
capture digital infrastructure investments held through commercial mortgage loans or limited
partnership/alternative investments. More broadly, if investors believe the hyperscalers
themselves face significant credit risk, we would argue that the more relevant concern for life
insurers is likely a broader recessionary fixed-income stress associated with such an outcome,
making our prior recessionary and 2000-2002-style credit stress work particularly relevant.

Defining data center versus digital infrastructure exposure: We define data center exposure
across three primary categories: (1) ABS financing data centers & hardware, (2) CMBS financing
data center properties and structures, and (3) corporate debt issued by data center operators.
Our broader digital infrastructure definition includes these same exposures but expands the
corporate debt bucket to include hyperscalers and businesses adjacent to the data center and
broader AI ecosystem, including GPU and semiconductor manufacturers. In other words, our
digital infrastructure universe includes (1) ABS financing data center hardware, (2) CMBS
financing data center properties, and (3) corporate debt issued by hyperscalers, chipmakers,
data center operators, and other AI-infrastructure-adjacent businesses. This broader definition
captures exposures to hyperscalers such as Alphabet, Meta, and Amazon, although we view
these companies as sufficiently large and systemic that material credit deterioration would
likely coincide with a much broader economic and corporate-credit stress scenario. As a result,
investors particularly concerned about these exposures should also consider the implications
under a broader recessionary scenario, such as our 2000-2002 credit stress analysis.
Nevertheless, we believe the narrower data center and broader digital infrastructure definitions
provide a useful framework for isolating and tracking the industry's direct exposure to the
growth in AI and data center investment. 
Overall, we view current allocations to digital infrastructure and data center-specific
investments as manageable and see limited cause for concern at current exposure levels. Digital
infrastructure exposures as a percentage of total investments sits in the lower single digits
range for all of the companies we analyzed, with most sitting within the 1-2% range. Of these
investments, the vast majority are investment grade with about 1% sitting in the high yield
bucket. Excluding ancillary corporate debt including hyperscalers like META or AMZN, which are
less likely to face serious credit issues in the face of a more modest economic slowdown
scenario, most insurer exposures to AI infrastructure investments are still just roughly 1-2% of
total general account balances with 99% rated investment grade.

Reinsurance Adjustment Considerations: Our methodology requires an important adjustment
for reinsurance. We identified individual holdings using Schedule D of insurers’ statutory
statements, which provides security-level investment disclosures. The principal limitation of
this approach relates to the industry's use of reinsurance, particularly funds withheld
structures. In many of these transactions, the cedent continues to hold the assets supporting
the reinsured liabilities on its statutory balance sheet, while the economic performance of those
assets is effectively passed through to the reinsurer, subject to the reinsurer satisfying its
obligations. As a result, simply aggregating Schedule D holdings can overstate the cedent's
effective economic exposure to digital infrastructure. Ideally, we would exclude the specific
digital infrastructure assets whose economics are transferred to reinsurers, but insurers
generally do not provide a security-level schedule identifying those assets. We therefore make a
pro rata adjustment based on total funds withheld assets relative to total bonds. We
acknowledge that this is necessarily a relatively crude estimate and will look to refine the
methodology as companies provide more granular disclosure, but we believe it provides a
reasonable approximation of insurers’ retained economic exposure today.

AI-Related Investments at Insurers - Balancing the Risks and
Opportunities
Risks
• Concentration could reduce portfolio diversity: At approximately 1.5% of the life sector's
total investment portfolio (~$75bn of ~$6.0trn), we do not think AI-related securities pose
concentration risks, even using a broad definition of the exposure. At current allocations, the

• Exposure to rating agencies with shorter track records: AI debt investments also expose
the insurance industry to smaller rating agencies with more limited track records. Some of
these rating agencies focus on niche sectors and products, such as data center
securitizations. For example, DBRS rates 36% of the DC ABS/CMBS investments in the life
insurance industry as of June 30. This compares with S&P at 29%, KBRA at 22%, and Moody's

ratings.
Opportunities
• Structural and ratings advantages: Insurers have a higher penetration of the securitized DC
market than of DC investments in the corporate bond market because the former offers a
wider breadth of deals with higher ratings. There could be other reasons, including structural
protections in securitizations.4  The structured credit market has always afforded investors
exposure to highly rated securities, given waterfall mechanics that prioritize cash flow and
value to senior creditors of the securitization. This is the case with DC ABS and CMBS
securities (Figure 17, Figure 18), most of which are rated A to AAA (NAIC 1). By count, most DC
bonds in the IG corporate bond market are rated BBB (NAIC 2), but insurers get most of their
exposure to this asset class through one large deal, which is 'A' rated (Beignet, which is an
OWL/META JV).
• Yield opportunity: Historically, 'yieldy' investments in the A-range have been particularly
attractive to life insurers from the perspective of return on regulatory capital. Many data
center securities in the securitized market fit that description. This is one of the reasons why
we expect AI investment exposures in the insurance industry to continue to grow.
° As of June 30, data center investments rated in the A category (i.e., A1, A2, A3) held by US
life insurers had a weighted average book yield of 5.5%. This was about 100bp higher than
the overall book yield of all A-rated debt investments owned by US life insurers at 2Q26
(4.6%). This highlights the excess spread opportunity AI-related credit investments offer
insurers, relative to other parts of their general accounts.
