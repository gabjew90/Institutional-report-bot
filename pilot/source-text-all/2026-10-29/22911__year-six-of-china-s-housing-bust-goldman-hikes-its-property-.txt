 
Year Six Of China's Housing Bust: Goldman Hikes 
Its Property Drag Estimate As Beijing Kills Off 
Presales 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 27, 2026 - 02:20 AM 
On Sunday we laid out why Hartnett thinks China is now the biggest risk to global earnings: 
domestic demand is on strike, so Beijing is doing what it always does and exporting its way out, 
flooding the world with deflation in the process. And at the heart of that buyer strike sits the one 
sector that has been bleeding for half a decade, and which BofA's strategist flagged in his 
"Biggest Picture": property investment is still down 20% YoY, record-low bond yields 
notwithstanding. 
 

 
 
Well, as if on cue, Goldman's China economists just published a long, sobering update on 
China's original sin, and the punchline is in the title: the downturn is "deeper than previously 
anticipated." 
In their latest Asia in Focus note (available to pro subs;  Goldman's Yuting Yang and Lisheng 
Wang start by admitting that the bank's forecast for this year was too optimistic: 
China's unprecedented property downturn is now in its sixth year. While we had projected the 
property sector's drag to real GDP growth to narrow somewhat from last year to this year, most 
housing activity indicators have been much weaker than expected so far this year. In addition, 
the recent policy announcement to shift from presales to completed-home sales implies tighter 
developer funding and lower property investment over the next few years. 
In other words, the drag was supposed to shrink in 2026, and instead it got bigger. Goldman 
now sees property subtracting 1.8pp from China's real GDP growth this year, up from 1.7pp in 
2025 and well above its previous estimate of 1.5pp. The bank still thinks the drag fades from 
here, which is something we'll come back to, because the path there involves a few more years 
 

 
of construction collapse, a land-sales revenue implosion and, quite possibly, a nationwide 
property tax. 
Worse Than Expected, Again 
Start with the numbers. In January-September, land sales revenue fell 28% YoY, new home 
starts dropped 25% and property FAI declined 21%, all of which, per Goldman, "were weaker 
than we anticipated at the start of this year." The bank's January forecasts look positively quaint 
next to the outturns: 
 
And these are declines on top of declines. As Goldman notes, most activity indicators, such as 
new home starts, sales and property FAI, are now down 60-90% from their 2020-21 peaks, with 
new home starts leading the way at more than 80% below the top. Five years after Evergrande, 
 

 
the founder of which was sentenced to life in prison in August, China's housing market is a 
fraction of what it was: 
 
This won't surprise regular readers: we noted last November that Beijing was preparing yet 
another property stimulus package as the housing crisis entered year six, followed weeks later 
by "Evergrand-er" as Vanke's bonds imploded. Goldman, for its part, was warning of 
"accelerated property price declines" across Chinese cities back in July 2025, around the same 
time that UBS was pushing back its recovery timeline. Each stimulus round bought a headline; 
none bought a floor. 
And just last Tuesday, The Market Ear showed that real estate FAI has collapsed to under half 
its 2019 level, even as exports nearly doubled. Which is a good place to bring in last week's 
GDP print: 
 

 
China's growth "bounced off three-year lows" in Q3, as Reuters put it, because "roaring exports 
offset consumers' tight grip on their wallets." One analyst quoted in the piece summed it up 
nicely: growth is "export- and state-led... and the property sector shows no floor." 
Here's some napkin math (approximate, of course): if property is subtracting 1.8pp from growth 
and the headline is running around 4.5%, then the rest of China is growing at something like 
6%+. Which tells you exactly how much heavy lifting the export machine is doing, and why 
Europe is now planning further curbs after China's September trade surpluses with Germany 
and France each jumped more than 200% YoY. 
Killing The Presale Model (And The Cash Flow With It) 
So why did Goldman cut again? Partly the data, and partly a policy change that most of the 
West barely noticed. On August 28, Beijing announced a sweeping shift away from selling 
homes before they are built: developers can now only presell once a building's main structure 
has topped out, and mortgage proceeds go into supervised escrow accounts that developers 
can't touch until the project is completed (per the SCMP). Shenzhen became the last tier-1 city 
to roll out its detailed rules on October 15, capping residential deposits at 3% of the purchase 
price. 
On paper it protects buyers from the nightmare of paying for apartments that never get built, 
which is what turned Evergrande into a national scandal. In practice, as Nomura bluntly put it at 
the time, "deferring the release of funding after home completion effectively dismantles the 
financing function of presales." Translation: the cheapest source of developer funding in China, 
namely homebuyers' money, just went away. 
The land market noticed immediately. According to Caixin, land transaction volume across 70 
major cities fell 36% in a single week after the directive, and Beijing suspended a major 
Chaoyang district land auction to rewrite the bidding conditions. 
Goldman's property team has since slashed its forecasts across the board "by factoring in the 
impact of recent policy changes on developer cashflows." The scale is something else: from 
2025 to 2035, they expect new home starts to drop another 30%, completions another 46%, 
property FAI another 61% and new home sales another 70%. And residential land sales 
volume? Down more than 90%. 
 

 
 
For the macro team's own baseline, land sales revenue, new home starts, new home sales and 
property FAI fall another 20%, 20%, 10% and 17%, respectively, in 2027, after drops of 30%, 
22%, 8% and 20% this year. That's two more years of double-digit declines in a sector that has 
already lost most of its activity. 
Goldman also made a couple of "technical adjustments" to its model, and they are more 
revealing than the name suggests. The first one deals with China's zombie construction sites: 
years of funding shortages have left many projects "suspended or terminated," but these "stuck" 
inventories still show up in the NBS floor-space-under-construction data, flattering the official 
decline. Since 2023 the official series and the model based on new home starts have gone their 
separate ways, so Goldman replaced the official number with its own: 
 

 
 
Put differently, a chunk of China's "construction" consists of buildings nobody is building. 
The second adjustment is even better: Goldman used to calculate local governments' land sales 
revenue from the NBS series on developer land purchases, but the NBS has stopped publishing 
it, so the bank switched to the Ministry of Finance's land revenue series. When the data get ugly 
enough, the data tend to disappear (we've seen this movie before with youth unemployment). 
Either way, the trend is unmistakable: government land revenue peaked above RMB 8 trillion in 
2021, was roughly halved by 2025, and on Goldman's projections falls to not much more than 
RMB 1 trillion by 2030: 
 

 
 
From 30% Of GDP To 8% 
Here is the big picture. At its peak in 2010-2021, Goldman estimates property accounted for 
around 30% of China's GDP. By 2025 that was down to roughly 15%, and the bank expects it to 
keep shrinking until it stabilizes "at around 8% from 2030 onward, gradually reducing the 
sector's systemic importance to the Chinese economy." 
 

 
 
Which is the silver lining Goldman is selling: once the sector is small enough, it can't hurt growth 
very much. The math is simple enough. A sector that is 30% of GDP falling 20% a year is a 
catastrophe; a sector that is 8% of GDP falling 20% a year is a rounding error. And that's why, 
even after the downgrade, Goldman expects the drag to narrow by around 0.5pp to 1.3pp in 
2027, and to "largely fade by 2030": 
 

 
 
We'd note that Goldman's previous forecast also had the drag narrowing this year; instead it 
widened. Recovery in China's property sector has been "next year" for about five years now. 
The Wealth Effect: Half Of Household Assets, Still Shrinking 
The 1.8pp direct drag also isn't the whole story. Property is more than half of Chinese household 
assets, so when home values fall, consumers stop spending, which is exactly the "consumers 
balk" problem in Reuters' GDP headline. Goldman's chart shows urban housing wealth 
shrinking steadily since 2023 while household deposits kept rising, a picture of a population that 
has stopped buying homes and started hoarding cash instead: 
 

 
 
Goldman's rule of thumb is that each RMB 1 change in property value moves annual household 
consumption by RMB 0.01 in the same direction. Add that indirect channel to the direct one and 
the total hit was around 3.4pp at the 2024 trough. The good news, per Goldman, is that the 
negative wealth effect "peaked in 2024-25" and should fade by the early 2030s... assuming 
nationwide existing home prices bottom in 2030, which is what the bank's property team 
expects: 
 

 
 
Read that again: the optimistic scenario has home prices falling for another four years. 
Two Chinas: Shanghai Recovers, Everyone Else Waits 
There is a sliver of good news. Goldman expects that in top-tier cities such as Shanghai and 
Shenzhen, where inventory is already low, "home prices may begin to recover soon." That's also 
the bull case making the rounds this week: Citi's Griffin Chan said the evidence "increasingly 
points to stabilising and potentially rising home prices in top cities," according to the SCMP, 
which noted that Shanghai just became the first city to post positive YoY growth in secondary 
home prices (+0.8% in September). Even Citi, however, expects most tier-two cities to lag. 
Goldman is far more blunt about everyone else: 
But for the rest of the country – particularly in lower-tier cities with still-significant inventory 
overhang – prices may continue to decline over the next few years. 
 

 
Which means that for the hundreds of millions of households outside Shanghai and Shenzhen 
whose savings are locked in apartments, the bleeding continues. Good luck getting them to 
spend. 
The Bill Comes Due For Local Governments 
And then there's the part that should worry Beijing the most. For two decades, land sales were 
the ATM of Chinese local governments. Goldman's third implication, in full: 
Based on developer cashflows, our property research team projects more than 90% decline in 
residential land sales volume from 2025 to 2035. This suggests persistent local government 
financing pressures — especially in regions more dependent on property-related revenue — 
requiring alternative revenue sources or spending cuts to ensure fiscal sustainability. We 
therefore see scope for a nationwide property tax expansion toward the end of the 15th 
Five-Year Plan (FYP; 2026-30) or during the 16th FYP (2031-35). 
So the "solution" to a property bust that has wiped out a big chunk of household wealth is... to 
tax the property that's left. Beijing has floated a national property tax on and off for more than a 
decade and shelved it every time, for the simple reason that nothing scares homeowners in a 
falling market like a new annual bill. Goldman concedes as much, noting that the timing "will 
likely depend on housing market conditions and domestic market confidence," i.e., not anytime 
soon. 
Meanwhile, as Reuters notes, tight local government finances "contributed to a 7.2% fall in 
fixed-asset investment in January-September." The fiscal channel is where the property bust 
stops being a housing story and becomes a growth story, and spending cuts in the provinces 
don't show up in the headline GDP print until they do. 
Where All That Excess Capacity Goes 
Which brings us back to Hartnett. If households won't spend, developers can't borrow and local 
governments can't sell land, all that productive capacity has to go somewhere, and it's going 
overseas. China's high-tech exports hit a record $147 billion in September, up 63% YoY, with 
total exports up 36%: 
 

 
 
We said as much almost exactly a year ago, when Xi was calling for trade "liberalization": 
Twelve months later, the middle class is still waiting, and Goldman just told them to wait until 
2030. Hartnett's conclusion follows directly: the biggest risk to global EPS is "China exports 
deflation," and with the CSI 300 down 20% since June and in a bear market that's too broken to 
buy and too oversold to short, local investors clearly aren't betting on a domestic rebound either. 
Or, as one of his clients put it: "If I bought a cemetery, people would stop dying." 
A Slower Bleed Is Still A Bleed 
Goldman's bottom line is reassuring, on its face: the property drag "should gradually diminish 
over the coming years" as the sector shrinks into irrelevance. That's probably true in the narrow 
arithmetic sense. A sector heading from 30% to 8% of GDP eventually runs out of room to fall. 
We're less sanguine. The bank has now had to raise its drag estimate in the very year it was 
supposed to start shrinking, the government has just pulled the funding rug out from under 
developers, existing home prices aren't expected to bottom until 2030, and the land revenue 
that funds local governments is headed for a 90%+ collapse in volume. That's a recipe for five 
more years of weak consumption, fiscal austerity in the provinces, and an even bigger export 
push, which is great for China's GDP target and terrible for every manufacturer competing with it 
(just ask the Germans). And in a world where only 6% of fund managers fear a hard landing, a 
 

 
China that has to export its way out of a housing bust for the rest of the decade is maybe the 
most underpriced deflationary shock out there. 
Then again, on Goldman's numbers, the shrinking will continue until morale improves.
