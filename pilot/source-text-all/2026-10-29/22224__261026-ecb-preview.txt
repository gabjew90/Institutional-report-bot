ECB PREVIEW: ON HOLD AMID TWIN SHOCKS 
Sticking to the hike-pause pattern: Surging energy prices and mounting French risks. Facing two shocks at 
the same time, the European Central Bank (ECB) will likely stay put this Thursday. But probably not for long. 
As the ECB has already reacted twice to higher energy prices by raising its policy rates by 25bps each in June 
and September, the additional increase in headline inflation to a three-year high of 3.8% yoy in September 
suggests that the ECB will do so again shortly, most likely upon presenting new staff projections for growth 
and inflation at its meeting on 17 December after pausing on 29 October. This Thursday, the ECB will likely 
indicate that the recent further spike in oil prices has tilted the risks to inflation even more to the upside than 
seemed to be the case in September. Despite financial risks focusing on France, we do not expect the ECB to 
change the pace at which it reduces its balance sheet. The ECB will continue to let maturing bonds from its 
previous asset purchase programmes roll off its balance sheet without reinvesting the proceeds. Nor will the 
ECB offer direct support for France – see ECB: how to react to the creeping French crisis (23 October 2026). 
And once again, ECB President Christine Lagarde will likely dodge the question as to when precisely she will 
leave office next year.
France: What if? Despite mounting problems, France is not (yet) close to a full-blown crisis. In case of a 
dramatically worsening crisis, the ECB could use its tools such as liquidity injections into short-term funding 
markets to prevent them from seizing up and/or deploy its “Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI)” to 
shield other countries such as Italy from contagion risks. However, the ECB has no mandate and no appetite 
to fund French fiscal follies. In our view, the ECB would only support France if Paris returns to more sustain-
able policies. Recent market moves seem to mostly reflect France’s worsening fundamentals. Of course, if 
the French yield spread were to blow out in a disorderly fashion and by far more than the fundamentals may 
warrant, the ECB may try to constrain that with a cautiously worded verbal intervention. In our view, we are 
not close to such a situation. 
Inflation headache: Although Germany’s three-month fuel tax rebate will likely subtract 0.1ppt from Euro-
zone inflation from October to December, the ongoing rise in oil prices has tilted near-term inflation risks 
decisively to the upside. At current oil prices, inflation may even briefly breach the 4% threshold in coming 
months. And as household prices for heating gas will likely go up in 2027, Eurozone headline inflation looks 
set to stay above 3% at least until spring next year. The ECB may indicate this Thursday that, at current energy 
prices, average inflation in 2027 could be closer to the ECB’s adverse scenario of 3.2% instead of the baseline 
forecast of 2.5% which the ECB presented on 10 September. 
Four reasons to stay put this week: Like most other pundits, we expect the ECB to leave its policy rates 
unchanged on 29 October and repeat the message of a “data-dependent and meeting-by-meeting approach”: 
1.
Since 8 September, that is just before the last ECB meeting on 10 September, 10-year yields govern-
ment bond yields have surged by 78bp for France and 38bp for Italy whereas German 10-year Bund 
yields are again close to where they were six weeks ago. To the extent that the French creeping cri-
sis is affecting the outlook for growth and inflation in the Eurozone as a whole, the ECB needs to 
take that into account. Higher risk premia in long bond yields reduce the perceived need for the 
ECB to dampen domestic demand by raising rates at the short end. Various ECB speakers have ad-
mitted earlier this month that higher yields will restrain economic activity. 
2.
Judging by recent US statements, the situation around the Strait of Hormuz may or may not change 
after the US midterm-elections on 3 November, possibly by renewed US attacks on Iran followed by 
negotiations. The ECB may want to wait for more clarity on that count.

3.
Amid elevated political uncertainty within the Eurozone and beyond, the drop in the Composite 
Output flash PMI from 53.1 in September to a four-month low of 51.9 in October could be a precur-
sor to a more noticeable loss of growth momentum in the Eurozone. Better to wait for more evi-
dence. 
4.
The ECB has delivered its two rate hikes so far this year upon discussing new staff projections for 
growth and inflation. We see no clear reason for the ECB to break that pattern. 
A case for higher rates? Despite the extended surge in headline inflation above the ECB’s 2% target, the case 
for further rate hikes is less convincing that it may appear at first glance, in our view. With its policy tools, a 
central bank cannot prevent the direct and indirect impact of external shocks such as the de-facto closure of 
the Strait of Hormuz on energy prices. With long and variable lags, a central bank can only control home-
grown inflation by influencing domestic demand. Eight months after the Iran war sparked a major rise in 
energy prices, we still find little evidence of second-round effects such as a price-wage spiral or broader 
domestic inflationary pressures. Instead, the hit to the purchasing power of consumers stemming from ele-
vated energy prices seems to keep a lid on domestic price pressures. In its October survey of purchasing 
managers, S&P noted that the rate of output price inflation even slowed in October despite a major surge in 
input price inflation due to higher energy and transport costs. Although consumer expectations of one-year 
inflation have edged up to 3.1% in September from 3.0% in August and from 2.9% in July according to the 
ECB’s monthly survey, they remain far below the 4% registered in March and April, that is right after the start 
of the Iran war. These expectations tend to follow energy prices. Relative to the surge in energy prices, they 
remain fairly well anchored. If oil prices decline over the course of next year, as we expect, and if the impact 
has started to become visible at the time of the ECB’s March 2027 meeting, the ECB will likely end the hike-
pause pattern at the time and stay put throughout next year.
Changes at the top: The European Union is trying to guard itself at least somewhat against the risk that right-
wing Marine Le Pen may win the French presidential election in April/May 2027. As part and parcel of that 
process, Lagarde looks set to leave office early next year, probably in January, so that her successor could still 
be appointed while the staunchly pro-European centrist Emmanuel Macron still resides in the Elysee Palace. 
However, it would be a surprise if Lagarde were to announce a firm day for her departure this Thursday. 
EU leaders will likely try to fill the three vacancies on the ECB’s Executive Board that will open up in 2027 as 
a package no later than the EU summit of 17-18 December. According to various media reports, Germany will 
soon nominate a candidate to replace Isabel Schnabel as the board member in charge of market operations 
but not claim the presidency or the position of chief economist. For now, Klaas Knot from the Netherlands 
and Spain’s Pablo Hernandez de Cos remain the favourites to succeed Largarde. In addition to the usual is-
sues of monetary policy competence and regional balance on the board, the question who may be best placed 
to deal with potential financial turbulences will likely play a key role in the choice of new ECB leaders. As a 
former chair of the G20’s Financial Stability Board (FSB), Knot may have an edge in this respect.
