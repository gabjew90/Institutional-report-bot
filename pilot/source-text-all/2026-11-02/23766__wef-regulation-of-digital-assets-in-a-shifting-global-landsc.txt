Regulation of Digital Assets in a Shifting Global Landscape

Foreword
Digital assets are entering a new phase. What 
began largely as the development of a novel asset 
class is becoming part of the infrastructure through 
which money, securities and collateral move. This 
shift is bringing digital assets into the centre of 
debates about financial stability and the future 
structure of finance.
The conversation is unfolding amid geopolitical 
tension and renewed competition over the 
architecture of the global economy. The G20 has 
placed digital assets within broader discussions 
of financial modernization and cross-border 
coordination. In the United States, efforts to move 
forward on the CLARITY Act reflect the need for 
a statutory framework for digital asset markets 
and clear responsibilities for financial regulators. 
Digital asset regulation has become intertwined 
with monetary sovereignty and control over critical 
financial infrastructure.
Regulatory decisions will influence which forms of 
digital money, tokenized securities, collateral and 
financial intermediation can develop at scale. They 
will also affect where liquidity develops and whether 
new digital networks strengthen or fragment the 
existing financial system.
These issues become especially important as digital 
assets shift from individual products to market 
infrastructure. Regulatory coverage and technical 
viability are not sufficient without legal certainty and 
common standards across institutions.
This creates a significant challenge for policy-makers. 
Digital networks operate internationally, while 
regulation and supervision remain primarily national. 
Jurisdictions will take different approaches, reflecting 
their institutional structures and policy priorities. The 
objective is to establish sufficient consistency and 
cooperation to support safe cross-border activity 
while preserving legitimate national choices.
This report, developed jointly by the World 
Economic Forum and Boston Consulting Group, 
examines how jurisdictions can approach that 
challenge. It draws on an analysis of regulatory 
developments across 35 jurisdictions and 
extensive engagement with regulators and market 
participants. As well as identifying a set of regulatory 
decisions that jurisdictions will need to address, it 
recognizes that the appropriate sequencing and 
institutional response will differ across markets.
We are grateful to the public- and private-sector 
leaders whose experience and perspectives shaped 
this report. We hope it contributes to an informed 
discussion at a time when decisions on digital 
assets are influencing the resilience of the global 
financial system.

Executive summary
Digital asset regulation is now at the core  
of financial system design.
Digital assets are moving from a stand-alone 
market into the infrastructure of finance. Their 
significance increasingly lies beyond the value of the 
asset class itself and is rooted in their potential to 
change how money, securities and collateral move 
through the financial system. As this integration 
advances, regulation also takes on wider strategic 
significance: digital money and settlement networks 
can influence currency use, capital flows, sanctions 
enforcement, market competitiveness and the 
degree of influence jurisdictions retain over financial 
activity conducted through global digital networks.
The report sets out a regulatory playbook for 
jurisdictions navigating this transition. Its nine 
dimensions define the conditions required for digital 
finance to function safely and at institutional scale:

The framework shifts the regulatory question 
from whether a product is regulated to whether 
the surrounding financial system can support 
institutional use.
There is no single regulatory sequence for building a 
credible institutional market. Jurisdictions start from 
different legal systems, financial structures, policy 
objectives and supervisory capabilities. Authorities 
can use this playbook to identify the most material 
risks and constraints, sequence priorities as adoption 
develops and determine where existing rules are 
sufficient and where they need to be adapted. Cross-
border growth will depend less on identical rules than 
on sufficient trust in regulatory outcomes, alongside 
deliberate choices about where international 
integration creates value and where stronger 
domestic or regional control remains important.
Making the regulatory framework usable in 
practice requires more than rule-making. Common 
standards are needed to avoid fragmented 
interpretation, comparable activities should receive 
similar treatment across regulatory perimeters and 
supervisory expectations must evolve with evidence 
from real-world use. Industry and regulators need to 
work together to make that happen.
If regulation helps make digital asset markets viable 
at scale, value creation and competitive advantage 
will shift across the sector. Firms that benefit from 
today’s frictions will face pressure even without 
launching digital asset products. Advantage will 
increasingly rest on scarce capabilities, such as 
liquidity, risk bearing, trusted control, distribution 
and market access. Traditional institutions and 
digital-native firms will compete more directly  
across the same parts of the value chain.

Introduction
Amid growing geopolitical fragmentation, 
this report offers jurisdictions a practical 
playbook for navigating the evolving 
regulation of digital assets.
Digital asset regulation has expanded rapidly during a 
time of financial fragmentation and a more contested 
post-Bretton Woods order, yet jurisdictions continue 
to differ in the activities they prioritize, the legal 
frameworks they use and the extent of regulated 
institutional participation. The policy challenge 
increasingly concerns how digital money, tokenized 
assets, intermediaries and infrastructure fit into the 
wider financial system and interact across borders.
Against this backdrop, the World Economic 
Forum’s Centre for Financial and Monetary 
Systems, together with Boston Consulting Group, 
developed this report to help regulators navigate 
an increasingly complex and interconnected 
landscape. It builds on the Forum’s previous 
work, including the Digital Currency Governance 
Consortium, Pathways to the Regulation of Crypto-
Assets: A Global Approach and Digital Assets 
Regulation: Jurisdictional Approaches.
Scope
Digital assets span three broad categories: 
cryptoassets, digital money, including stablecoins, 
tokenized deposits and central bank digital 
currencies; tokenized financial instruments, such 
as securities, funds and other real-world assets. 
This report focuses on the regulatory questions 
that arise as these forms of digital finance develop 
across payments, capital markets and financial 
infrastructure.
The report provides a modular playbook that 
jurisdictions can apply from different starting points, 
reflecting their market structure, policy objectives, 
supervisory capacity and exposure to digital asset 
activity. It also examines how domestic approaches 
interact across borders and how regulatory choices 
affect monetary sovereignty, market structure and 
critical financial infrastructure.
Research methods
The report draws on analysis of regulatory 
developments across 35 jurisdictions, interviews 
with 50 public- and private-sector experts, 
multistakeholder workshops and a community of 
more than 100 participants from the public and 
private sectors and academia.

Market size alone no longer captures  
the significance of digital assets
Cryptoassets dominate the digital asset market 
today, but the more consequential long-term shift 
may be taking place elsewhere: digital money 
and tokenization are beginning to change how 
established financial markets operate. Digital 
assets span three broad areas – cryptoassets, 
digital money and tokenized real-world assets – 
each at a different stage of maturity and adoption. 
Cryptoassets represent approximately $3 trillion in 
market capitalization and a revenue pool estimated 
at $90 billion.1 Stablecoins have reached roughly 
$300 billion in circulation, while tokenized real-world 
assets are approximately $30 billion. Yet current 
size understates the scale of the markets that could 
ultimately be affected. In progressive scenarios, 
tokenized real-world assets could represent 
approximately 16% of global investable assets  
by 2035, equivalent to around $88 trillion.2
Market size captures only part of digital assets’ 
significance. Cryptoassets have created new 
instruments, markets and intermediaries, while 
digital money and tokenization increasingly bring 
digital infrastructure into established financial 
activity. Tokenization and distributed ledgers can 
change how securities, funds and collateral are 
issued and recorded, controlled, traded and settled, 
mobilized as collateral and serviced throughout their 
life cycle. The significance of tokenization reaches 
beyond the creation of another investable asset 
category; it changes the infrastructure through 
which existing financial activity takes place.

Institutional adoption is emerging where digital 
formats improve existing market functions
Early institutional examples show the shift taking 
shape in investment products, settlement and market 
infrastructure. The BlackRock US Dollar Institutional 
Digital Liquidity Fund (BUIDL) reached approximately 
$2.6 billion as of July 2026, bringing cash and US 
Treasury exposure into tokenized investment and 
settlement workflows.3 The Depository Trust and 
Clearing Corporation (DTCC) has demonstrated 
how securities held within established market 
infrastructure can be represented in tokenized form 
and used in digital market transactions, creating a 
bridge between conventional securities infrastructure 
and tokenized markets.4 Hong Kong’s digital green 
bond issuances have applied tokenization across 
issuance, settlement and life-cycle management in

the integration of tokenization into established 
investment, settlement and market infrastructure.

AI agents are creating a new source of demand for programmable financial infrastructure 
B O X 1
Agents can already initiate and execute 
transactions directly, challenging payment and 
financial infrastructure designed around human-led 
interactions. Tokenized assets and programmable 
digital money offer machine-readable ownership, 
permissions and settlement, making them well-
suited to this emerging mode of economic activity. 
Scale will depend on whether that integration 
creates tangible economic value. Lower transaction 
and servicing costs, reduced settlement friction, 
broader access, greater transparency and more 
efficient use of collateral can justify migration from 
established infrastructure that technical feasibility 
alone cannot. The relevant test is whether digital 
formats improve outcomes enough to warrant 
investors, issuers and other market participants 
changing established market processes.
Migration is also eroding the boundary between 
traditional finance and digital asset-native markets. 
Digital asset firms are entering regulated roles 
across banking, custody, brokerage and payments, 
while established financial institutions are bringing 
tokenized products, digital money and blockchain-
based settlement into existing businesses. The 
relationship is increasingly one of interdependence: 
digital-native firms rely on banking, custody, 
distribution and institutional access, while traditional 
institutions draw on digital asset infrastructure, 
liquidity and specialist capabilities. Rather than 
developing as parallel financial systems, the 
digital-native firms and traditional institutions 
are increasingly becoming part of the same 
market architecture.
Infrastructure choices will determine how far 
digital assets reshape financial markets
Different infrastructure models embed different 
assumptions about participation, governance 
and where financial functions are performed. 
Permissioned networks restrict participation to 
approved entities and operate under defined 
governance arrangements. They can materially 
improve issuance, settlement, collateral movement 
and asset servicing while preserving many 
established institutional roles, resulting in substantial 
operational change without a fundamental shift 
in market structure.
Permissionless networks are open to broader 
participation and function without a single 
controlling operator. Widespread adoption could 
imply a deeper structural shift by allowing assets, 
liquidity and applications to interact across shared 
networks rather than keeping functions within 
controlled institutional environments, potentially 
redistributing market access, intermediation 
and control.
The distinction between permissioned and 
permissionless networks is not binary. Both 
architectures can coexist, while regulated access, 
identity and control layers can develop around 
open networks. The extent of structural change 
will depend on how much of today’s institutional 
model is preserved and modernized, and how 
much moves into shared infrastructure. These 
infrastructure choices also carry implications for 
sovereignty and can influence the degree of control 
jurisdictions retain over settlement, critical financial 
infrastructure and financial data as markets become 
more digitally interconnected.
As digital assets scale, they affect three core 
dimensions of sovereign influence: monetary control, 
infrastructure oversight and financial enforcement.
Digital money can shift monetary influence
Control over money is tested when private digital 
money changes what people hold, use and 
which currency they settle in. Dollar-denominated 
stablecoins create a new channel for digital 
dollarization, allowing users outside the US banking 
system to hold and transfer dollar-linked value 
through global wallet and payment networks. 
In markets characterized by currency volatility, 
limited payment infrastructure or capital controls, 
this can accelerate usage away from domestic 
currencies and reduce authorities’ oversight over 
monetary flows. Stablecoins, tokenized deposits 
and tokenized money market funds can also make 
balances more mobile, changing the behaviour 
of funding, liquidity and monetary transmission 
under stress.

Early regulatory responses to digital assets were 
largely defensive, reflecting a market that was retail-
heavy, volatile and predominantly crypto-native. 
As activity has become more institutionalized, 
jurisdictions have increasingly moved towards 
formal regulatory frameworks, licensing regimes and 
supervisory models.
Regulatory maturity varies across  
the digital asset stack
Analysis conducted for this report across 35 
jurisdictions and more than 25 common digital 
asset activities shows that regulatory maturity is use 
case-specific. A jurisdiction may be advanced in 
one part of the digital asset stack while remaining 
less developed in another. Switzerland, for 
example, has an established legal and regulatory 
framework for tokenized securities and DLT market 
infrastructure, while pursuing reforms to its existing 
regulatory treatment of stablecoins.8 The key 
concern is less to do which jurisdiction is “leading” 
overall and more about which parts of the digital 
asset stack each jurisdiction is ready to support.
Across the jurisdictions reviewed, more than  
70% regulate digital asset activity itself rather 
than relying primarily on the legal form of the 
asset. This reflects the fact that digital assets can 
combine functions that traditional frameworks 
often treat separately, including issuance, custody, 
transfer, settlement, trading and asset servicing. 
An activity-based approach enables regulation to 
follow what market participants do and the risks 
they create, rather than relying primarily on how 
a token is labelled.
Different legal and supervisory routes can still 
produce credible regulatory outcomes. The 
effectiveness of a regime depends less on whether 
rules sit in banking, securities, payments or stand-
alone cryptoasset law than on whether they are 
clear, enforceable and usable in practice. Indonesia 
illustrates how regulatory architecture can evolve 
over time: cryptoassets were initially supervised 
by the commodities regulator before responsibility 
shifted to the financial services regulator, supported 
by dedicated rules for digital financial assets 
and cryptoassets.9
Different regulatory architectures can support 
institutional participation when they provide clear 
obligations, identifiable supervisory accountability and 
sufficient legal certainty for firms to operate at scale.
1.3	 Regulation of digital assets has accelerated, 
but is fragmented in its approach
Control over digital financial infrastructure  
is becoming strategic
As financial activity moves onto new digital 
infrastructure, control over the underlying 
infrastructure becomes a strategic policy issue. 
Settlement networks, distributed ledgers, cloud 
infrastructure, interoperability services and critical 
data providers can determine how markets operate, 
who has access and where authorities retain 
oversight and intervention rights. Greater reliance 
on infrastructure operated outside a jurisdiction 
can create dependencies on foreign providers, 
legal regimes and governance structures. Policy-
makers need to consider not only the resilience of 
individual services but which critical infrastructure 
and dependencies they are prepared to rely on as 
digital markets scale.
Digital assets require new financial  
enforcement points
Banks, payment companies, brokers and custodians 
have historically served as key control points for 
financial crime and sanctions enforcement. With 
digital assets, these control points are spread across 
issuers, custodians, exchanges, wallet providers and 
network infrastructure, changing where authorities 
can monitor, restrict or freeze activity. Effective 
enforcement increasingly depends on identifying 
which actors retain control over assets and 
transactions, including across borders.
Together, these shifts make digital asset regulation 
part of a broader sovereignty agenda, shaping 
how jurisdictions retain influence over monetary 
conditions, critical financial infrastructure and the 
enforcement of financial rules.

Regulators continue to pursue familiar objectives, 
including financial stability, market integrity, 
consumer protection, financial crime prevention and 
resilient infrastructure. Digital assets make the route 
to those outcomes more consequential, because 
regulation increasingly shapes the structure through 
which financial activity is conducted.
Regulatory choices determine which activities 
regulated institutions can carry out, which forms 
of digital money can be used for settlement, 
which assets can be held or pledged as collateral, 
what infrastructure firms can rely on and where 
accountability sits when risk materializes. The 
regulatory perimeter is also a market boundary, 
shaping where liquidity, investment and 
infrastructure develop.
Different areas of the digital asset landscape 
raise different regulatory questions. Cryptoassets 
and crypto-native activities are distinct from the 
modernization of existing capital markets through 
tokenized securities, funds and collateral. In the latter 
case, the underlying economic exposure may remain 
unchanged while issuance, ownership, transfer 
and settlement move onto new infrastructure. The 
regulatory task is to distinguish risks inherent in 
the asset or activity from those introduced by the 
infrastructure through which it is delivered.
This distinction is central to technology neutrality. 
Financial regulation was built around the institutions, 
processing cycles and record-keeping structures 
of the financial system in which it developed, so 
applying the existing rules can preserve those 
assumptions even where the rules are formally 
technology-neutral. The objective is to preserve 
outcomes such as stability, integrity and investor 
protection while allowing different technological  
and market architectures to deliver them.
The regulatory mandate extends beyond bringing 
digital asset activity inside the perimeter. It is to 
create conditions under which digital money, 
tokenized assets, intermediaries and infrastructure 
can operate together in a legally reliable, institutionally 
usable and operationally safe financial system.
Institutional markets require legal, prudential, 
infrastructure and market conditions to work 
together across the full transaction life cycle. 
Settlement cannot be relied on where legal 
treatment stays uncertain. Assets cannot be held 
safely where custody treatment is unclear, and they 
become uneconomic to hold or pledge under the 
wrong prudential rules. Liquidity, meanwhile, never 
forms if infrastructure stays fragmented.
Regulatory permission is only one condition for 
adoption. Tokenization must also create sufficient 
value for investors, issuers and market participants 
to justify migrating from established infrastructure. 
Regulation cannot create that utility, but it can 
determine whether legal, prudential and market 
structures allow it to be realized.
The nine dimensions are distinct, but most cannot 
be solved in isolation, because a gap in one can 
make progress in another unusable in practice. 
Supervisors also need enough information across 
all nine dimensions to observe activity, identify 
emerging risks and confirm regulatory outcomes are 
being achieved. Supervisory visibility is a cross-
cutting requirement rather than a separate pillar.

Nine dimensions of an effective digital asset regulatory framework
F I G U R E 1
Core market rules
1
Regulatory perimeter,  
classification and accountability
Challenge: Digital asset regulation must establish 
what activity is being performed, which actors are 
accountable and where supervisory responsibility 
sits. Classification cannot rely solely on a static label 
attached to a token, since an asset can perform 
different economic functions or acquire different 
characteristics over its life cycle.
Key considerations: Regulation should follow the 
economic function and activity being performed. 
The framework must establish the classification of 
the asset, the entities accountable for issuance, 
custody, transfer, settlement or trading, the 
supervisory authority responsible and the licensing, 
disclosure, attestation and audit obligations that 
follow. Tax-reporting obligations for regulated market 
participants should also be evident. Clear boundaries 
around roles, regulatory responsibility and applicable 
obligations enable firms to build compliant operating 
models into the rules rather than around them.
Digital asset markets also change where regulatory 
control points sit. Traditional financial regulation 
has largely relied on regulated intermediaries as the 
principal points of accountability. In digital markets, 
execution and control can be distributed across 
intermediaries, protocols and shared infrastructure. 
Regulatory obligations should remain anchored 
in regulated activities and identifiable points of 
control, while accountability for protocols, smart 
contract developers, validators, oracles and other 
infrastructure participants remains an evolving area 
of policy. Neutral protocols, open-source software 
and network participants should not become 
subject to regulation solely because regulated 
activity occurs on the infrastructure they support.
Direct obligations become relevant where an 
infrastructure provider itself performs a regulated 
financial function, operates a controlled network 
or becomes systemically important. Beyond these 
cases, authorities can define the conditions under 
which regulated firms may rely on the infrastructure 
rather than regulate every infrastructure 
participant directly.
Scale changes the regulatory question. Issuers, 
intermediaries or infrastructure that become 
sufficiently important to market functioning may 
warrant designation and enhanced requirements 
reflecting their systemic relevance. Cross-border 
oversight then depends on cooperation and 
information sharing among the relevant authorities.

2
Legal certainty and enforceability
Challenge: Digital assets can function technically 
while remaining difficult to rely on legally. Institutional 
markets require clarity that digital ownership, 
contractual rights and settlement outcomes are 
recognized and enforceable under applicable law.
Key considerations: On-chain execution alone is 
insufficient for institutional settlement, and technical 
execution must translate into legally enforceable 
ownership and settlement outcomes. Legal 
frameworks should establish the enforceability of 
smart contracts, the evidentiary or authoritative 
status of on-chain records and the point at which 
digital execution constitutes legally final settlement. 
The same certainty applies to custody arrangements, 
insolvency treatment, priority of claims and, where 
relevant, the tax treatment of economically equivalent 
digital and traditional instruments.
Digital ledgers can create tension between technical 
finality and legal remedies. Transactions recorded on 
DLT are generally designed to be final and resistant 
to unilateral alteration, which can conflict with legal 
rights to correct errors, enforce court orders or satisfy 
data-protection requirements. Frameworks must 
provide a workable response to fraud, error, court-
ordered intervention and data-protection obligations, 
making clear which legal rights prevail and how they 
can be enforced in practice.
3
Custody and client asset protection
Challenge: Digital asset custody must protect 
clients both legally and technically. The defining 
issue is that legal ownership, technical control and 
the practical ability to move or recover an asset can 
sit with different parties.
Key considerations: Client asset protection 
rests on clear rules for segregation, safekeeping, 
protection from creditor claims and insolvency 
treatment, supported by appropriate operational, 
capital and liquidity safeguards. Digital custody 
adds specific governance considerations around 
private keys, transaction authorization, custody and 
recovery arrangements.
Legal protection is insufficient if assets cannot be 
accessed or recovered after key loss, technology 
failure or failure of a service provider. Legal 
responsibility and practical control should align so 
that client assets remain identifiable, protected and 
recoverable under stress.
4
Infrastructure
Challenge: Digital asset infrastructure must be 
sufficiently reliable for regulated firms to depend 
on it and sufficiently interoperable to support 
functioning markets rather than isolated pools  
of assets and liquidity.
Key considerations: Effective regulatory 
treatment of digital asset infrastructure needs to 
address resilience, technical interoperability and 
market interoperability.
Resilience. Networks, bridges, oracles, smart 
contracts and other shared components can 
become critical points of failure. Reliance on these 
components calls for standards on governance, 
availability, recovery, cybersecurity, change 
management and settlement integrity. Pricing, 
collateral and market integrity mechanisms must 
also remain effective under stress.
Technical interoperability. Common standards in 
messaging, token interfaces, smart contracts, 
identity, data and connectivity allow assets and 
information to move between systems without 
bespoke integration for every connection. 
Interoperability also extends throughout the asset 
life cycle, including ownership recognition, life-cycle 
events and compliance information.
Market interoperability. Technical connectivity alone 
does not create a market. Parallel traditional and 
tokenized markets need consistent ownership 
records, valuation, market access, execution 
certainty, collateral treatment and reliable pricing 
if the same economic asset is to move between 
traditional and tokenized markets without 
fragmenting liquidity or settlement outcomes.
Network architecture affects how these outcomes 
can be achieved. Permissioned networks generally 
provide clearer participant identification, governance 
rights and intervention mechanisms, while more 
open networks offer broader connectivity and 
composability while distributing control across a 
wider set of actors. Hybrid models increasingly 
combine the characteristics of both. The regulatory 
test is whether institutions can meet expectations 
for accountability, privacy, financial crime controls, 
settlement integrity and resilience under the 
architecture they use.
Regulation should remain technology-neutral while 
defining the minimum outcomes that infrastructure 
must deliver for regulated institutions to rely on it.

Digital money and  
settlement assets
Challenge: As stablecoins, tokenized deposits and 
central bank digital currencies develop in parallel, 
regulators must determine which instruments can 
perform which monetary and settlement functions, 
who may issue them and using which safeguards. 
Similar payment or settlement functionality does  
not make these forms of money economically or 
legally equivalent.
Key considerations: Stablecoins depend 
on private issuer, reserve and redemption 
arrangements; tokenized deposits remain liabilities 
of regulated banks; and central bank digital 
currencies provide a direct claim on the central 
bank. These differences shape credit exposure, 
redemption certainty, access to liquidity support 
and the obligations attached to each instrument.
Regulation also has to anticipate a market in which 
different forms of digital money coexist and value 
moves among them according to the function 
required, whether settlement, liquidity, yield or 
access to central bank money. Safe convertibility 
and interoperability can support this movement 
while preserving the distinct legal and prudential 
characteristics of each instrument.
For stablecoins, reserve standards should address 
the quality and liquidity of reserve assets, at par 
redemption, portfolio transparency and independent 
attestation. Yield is particularly consequential 
because it can alter both the economic function 
and regulatory perimeter of an instrument. Clear 
thresholds are important where remuneration, 
reserve management or associated services begin 
to resemble deposit taking, investment or lending.

Monetary sovereignty  
and financial stability
Challenge: Digital money and tokenized markets 
can change the speed and channels through 
which monetary and financial stress is transmitted. 
Continuous transferability, real-time redemption 
and automated treasury processes can compress 
liquidity and crisis timelines, while new forms of 
money can affect currency use, bank funding and 
monetary transmission.
Key considerations: Authorities require oversight 
of how digital money circulates among institutions, 
issuers, asset classes and jurisdictions, including 
reserve backing, redemption flows and effects on 
bank funding. Greater transparency can strengthen 
supervision, but it may also enable depositors and 
counterparties to observe stress as it develops, 
potentially accelerating confidence-driven outflows.
Four transmission channels are particularly relevant:
Collateral mobility. Tokenized securities and collateral 
can move across venues and operating hours more 
continuously, affecting central bank approaches to 
eligible collateral, custody, settlement finality and 
liquidity provision.
Settlement architecture. The form of money used in 
systemic settlement affects the singleness of money, 
particularly where private money becomes central to 
the activity of capital markets.
Bank funding. Stablecoins, tokenized deposits 
and tokenized money market funds can make 
balances more mobile and rate-sensitive. Large-
scale migration from deposits into fully reserved 
instruments could also alter bank funding and  
credit creation.
Interest rate transmission. If digital money markets 
reach sufficient scale, policy rates may have to 
anchor a broader and more continuously operating 
set of money-like instruments and yields.
Jurisdictions that rely on capital flow management 
measures face an additional challenge where 
digital assets create new routes for cross-border 
movement or reduce the visibility on which existing 
controls depend.

tokenized assets can enter institutional balance 
sheets, funding and collateral markets on 
economically viable terms. Existing frameworks 
already address capital, liquidity and intraday risk,

greater collateral mobility and new infrastructure 
dependencies in ways that existing calibrations may 
not fully capture.

security does not, by itself, alter the underlying 
economic exposure or legal claim. Recent US 
regulatory clarification applies this principle 
explicitly, treating eligible tokenized securities 
and their non-tokenized equivalents consistently, 
including for recognition as financial collateral. 
Incremental technology or infrastructure risks can 
be captured through the relevant risk management 
and supervisory requirements.10

continuous markets. The financial system provides

to preserve traceability and enforcement capability

Public ledger data can allow funds to be 
traced across multiple transfers and support

advantage weakens where identities cannot be 
reliably linked to wallets or activity moves through 
obfuscation mechanisms or systems that cannot

The Travel Rule is one mechanism for preserving 
the link between transactions and identity. An anti-
money laundering (AML) standard developed by 
the intergovernmental Financial Action Task Force 
(FATF) and implemented in many jurisdictions 
requires institutions to exchange identifying 
information on the sender and beneficiary of a 
digital asset transfer. Its effectiveness is more 
limited where transfers involve self-hosted wallets 
or jurisdictions without equivalent obligations.
Risk assessment is also less binary. A wallet may 
receive assets that passed through a sanctioned 
or illicit address several transactions earlier, making 
transaction proximity, attribution and behavioural 
signals central to decisions on review, restriction, 
freezing or reporting.
Intervention rights differ across assets and actors. 
A stablecoin issuer may be able to freeze a token 
if that capability is embedded in the asset, while 
self-custodial assets or assets exchanged into other 
instruments may move beyond that control point. 
Effective enforcement depends on whether the 
relevant actor can identify the activity and has the 
legal authority and technical ability to intervene.
Recent European Union (EU) sanction measures 
have extended restrictions beyond conventional 
financial institutions to cryptoasset service 
providers, decentralized trading platforms, specific 
digital currencies and third-country platforms 
used for circumvention. This reflects the fact that 
enforcement increasingly follows the points at which 
digital assets can be exchanged, transferred or 
moved beyond effective control.13

Market integrity and  
investor protection
Challenge: Digital asset markets combine familiar 
conduct risks with market structures in which 
trading, information and execution are distributed 
across multiple venues and networks. The regulatory 
challenge is not simply whether the infrastructure 
functions but whether markets operating across it 
remain fair, transparent and resistant to abuse.
Key considerations: Surveillance must extend 
beyond individual trading venues. Manipulative 
activity can move between centralized platforms, 
derivatives markets, decentralized protocols and 
on-chain transfers, while digital asset markets 
operate continuously and across jurisdictions. 
Effective surveillance increasingly depends on 
connecting order-book activity, transactions and 
relevant on-chain data rather than monitoring each 
venue in isolation.14
Comparable data is a prerequisite for cross market 
oversight. Common identifiers and standardized 
order and transaction records enable firms and 
supervisors to connect activity involving the same 
asset across venues and reconstruct trading 
patterns. Without linkable data, fragmentation 
across networks and platforms can also disrupt 
supervisory visibility.
Market structure can create concentrated conflicts 
of interest. Digital asset groups may combine venue 
operation, brokerage, custody, lending, market-
making and proprietary activity within the same 
organization. Governance, disclosure, segregation 
and conflict management arrangements become 
particularly important where these combinations 
create incentives to disadvantage clients or 
distort execution.15
Programmable execution creates new channels 
for familiar forms of abuse. Transaction ordering, 
manipulation of oracle inputs and other protocol 
mechanics can influence prices or execution 
outcomes without resembling a conventional 
manipulative order. The relevant regulatory test 
is the economic effect: equivalent misconduct 
should be captured whether it arises through 
trader behaviour, platform design or control over 
transaction sequencing.16
Investor protections should travel with the 
token. Tokenization changes the format and 
infrastructure through which an asset is issued, 
held or traded, but it does not by itself change 
the underlying economic exposure. Where that 
exposure is equivalent, investors should retain 
comparable standards of suitability, disclosure, 
valuation, liquidity management and recourse. 
As assets move across platforms and jurisdictions, 
interoperability and recognition arrangements 
should preserve those protections rather than  
allow them to fall away at the point of transfer.

Cross-border scale rests on comparable 
outcomes and credible supervision
Cross-border scale depends on whether institutions 
can trust regulatory outcomes produced outside 
their home jurisdiction. This trust must cover 
the legal, prudential, conduct and supervisory 
outcomes equally. The objective is sufficient 
comparability for assets and activities to operate 
across jurisdictions while allowing domestic legal 
and supervisory structures to differ.18
Comparable standards make outcomes easier to 
assess across jurisdictions. Where another jurisdiction 
already delivers sufficiently comparable protections 
for a given activity, recognition or equivalence can 
cut out duplicative process. Supervisory cooperation 
provides the information and oversight needed to 
sustain that reliance over time.19
Recognition works best when calibrated to the 
activity involved. A jurisdiction may place confidence 
in another market’s custody or tokenized securities 
framework while applying different conditions 
to stablecoins, settlement assets or critical 
infrastructure. The real question is what outcomes 
another authority can credibly deliver and supervise, 
and where reliance on those outcomes is sufficient 
to justify market access.
Recognition also has to preserve the protections 
attached to the asset as it moves. Legal rights, 
investor protections, compliance obligations and 
settlement outcomes should remain effective across 
platforms and jurisdictions. Technical connectivity 
creates limited institutional value if those protections 
become uncertain at the point of transfer.
The case for coordination strengthens as  
markets become more interconnected. Activity 
can migrate towards jurisdictions or entities where 
standards or enforcement are weaker, allowing 
vulnerabilities in one part of the system to create 
exposure elsewhere.20

As rules clarify, regulators and market 
participants need to build the standards 
that make them work in practice
Digital asset rules often leave firms with discretion 
over implementation; some of these choices 
determine whether a wallet, counterparty, asset 
or dataset is acceptable across institutions.
For example, in financial crime controls, firms may 
share the same obligation to identify illicit exposure 
but differ on the depth of transaction history 
reviewed, the treatment of indirect exposure and 
the interpretation of blockchain analytics, so the 
same wallet or transaction may be accepted by 
one institution and rejected by another. The Travel 
Rule creates a related operational challenge where 
firms need compatible methods for identifying 
counterparties, exchanging required information and 
assessing transfers involving self-hosted wallets.
Comparable issues arise wherever institutions rely 
on one another’s data, controls or valuations to 
complete a transaction, including the treatment of 
asset and counterparty data, collateral valuation, 
transaction messaging and controls applied to 
shared infrastructure. Where common standards 
are still immature, firms can reach materially 
different conclusions on the same risk, whether 
in sanctions screening, operational resilience or 
transaction acceptance; once those judgements 
affect counterparties, assets or infrastructure, the 
differences can also fragment market activity.
Firms and infrastructure providers are best placed 
to develop common thresholds, data formats 
and control methods, with regulators supporting 
convergence where inconsistent implementation 
begins to constrain market scale.
Friction with wider legacy regulatory 
frameworks can block viable digital asset 
models or push activity towards lighter regimes
Even where market practice converges, institutional 
adoption can be constrained when digital asset 
activity meets existing banking, securities, 
payments, prudential and financial crime rules. One 
source of friction is that many of these rules assume 
conventional trading, record-keeping or settlement 
processes even where new infrastructure does not 
materially change the underlying economic risk.
In the EU, the DLT Pilot Regime allows authorities 
to grant qualifying DLT market infrastructures 
targeted exemptions from specified trading and 
settlement requirements, subject to safeguards 
that preserve the objectives of the existing rules, 
including investor protection, market integrity 
and financial stability.22
Regulatory arbitrage can also emerge across 
provider types. The same activity may attract 
different capital, liquidity or control requirements 
depending on whether it is performed by a bank, 
payment firm or digital asset-native provider. 
Some differences reflect genuine balance sheet or 
business-model risks; where they do not, materially 
lighter treatment can distort competition and draw 
activity towards the less demanding regime.

Digital asset supervision requires authorities 
to connect on-chain activity with traditional 
supervisory data
With digital asset activity moving into live markets, 
supervisors need visibility into activity that extends 
beyond the regulated entity. Firm records capture 
only part of the risk when transactions involve 
self-hosted wallets, smart contracts, protocols and 
multiple blockchains. In financial crime supervision, 
the FATF identifies four capabilities that are 
becoming more important: understanding smart 
contract functionality; tracing activity across chains; 
using blockchain analytics effectively; and assessing 
risks from peer-to-peer transactions through self-
hosted wallets.23
The supervisory toolkit is starting to adapt. Project 
Pyxtrial, developed by the Bank for International 
Settlements (BIS) Innovation Hub and the Bank 
of England, tested whether supervisors could 
use integrated data on stablecoin liabilities and 
backing assets to monitor whether issuers remain 
fully backed. This points towards a model in which 
supervisors source and analyse market data directly 
alongside information reported by firms.24
Supervisory mandates can fragment the picture 
further. Custody, trading, payments, settlement 
and technology infrastructure may sit within the 
same transaction chain, while different authorities 
see only parts of it. Effective oversight depends 
on information and supervisory judgement moving 
across those boundaries.
When activity reaches scale, supervisory  
findings provide evidence that rule-making and 
pilots cannot fully anticipate. Recurring control 
failures or unintended effects can be addressed 
through clearer expectations or targeted 
changes without repeatedly reopening the 
wider regulatory framework.

Conclusion
The central regulatory challenge is to preserve 
the properties that underpin trust in the financial 
system, including legal certainty, sound settlement, 
resilient infrastructure, market integrity, consumer 
and investor protection, and financial stability.
Regulators will increasingly need to distinguish the 
objective of a rule from the mechanism historically 
used to achieve it. Many existing requirements 
around intermediation, settlement cycles, custody 
and record-keeping reflect the financial architecture 
in which those rules were designed. Where new 
infrastructure can achieve the same regulatory 
objective through different means, rules should be 
applied in ways that reflect the new architecture 
without weakening the safeguards they are 
intended to provide.
Greater interconnectedness across markets, 
infrastructure, data and digital money will also 
require regulators to make clearer choices about 
reliance and control. They will need to determine 
where reliance on foreign regimes and external 
infrastructure is acceptable and where domestic 
oversight, capability or control should be retained  
to protect resilience and sovereignty.
Regulatory choices made now will determine 
whether digital assets entrench new fragmentation 
and dependencies or support a more integrated, 
trusted and resilient financial system.

Appendix: 
Regulatory baseline
To assess these frameworks, this report draws on an 
original analysis of 35 leading jurisdictions currently 
regulating digital assets. The findings inform the 
regulatory baseline set out in this report and point to 
several trends shaping the current landscape.
Regulatory trends shaping the current landscape
Full market bans remain rare, and regulated 
participation continues to expand. Only China 
maintains an explicit ban; other jurisdictions 
constrain participation through narrower channels, 
including restrictions on regulated financial 
institutions or the absence of a comprehensive 
licensing regime, as in Mexico and Saudi Arabia.
Regulation is shifting from an asset-based 
approach to an activity-based one. More than 
70% of jurisdictions in the sample now regulate 
based on the activity being performed rather than the 
form the asset takes, consistent with the activity-
based principle this report argues should anchor 
effective supervision more broadly. This reflects 
the fact that digital assets can combine functions 
that traditional frameworks often treat separately, 
including issuance, custody, transfer, settlement, 
trading and asset servicing. An activity-based 
approach enables regulation to follow what market 
participants do and the risks they create, rather than 
relying primarily on how a token is labelled.
Regulatory maturity varies across the digital 
asset stack. A jurisdiction may be advanced in one 
part of the digital asset stack while still developing 
rules elsewhere. Switzerland, for example, has an 
established legal framework for tokenized securities 
and DLT market infrastructure; its stablecoin 
framework remains under development.25 The 
relevant comparison is therefore by activity, not by 
jurisdiction overall.
Regulatory maturity does not depend on where 
the rules sit. Digital assets may fall under existing 
banking law, a stand-alone regime built specifically 
for them or some hybrid of the two. What 
determines effectiveness is not which structure 
a jurisdiction chooses but whether that structure 
delivers clarity and credible supervision in practice.
Several jurisdictions are actively recalibrating 
their regulatory stance. Türkiye, Nigeria, Ghana 
and Argentina have moved towards more formal 
licensing or registration regimes and defined 
supervisory requirements, while India has tightened 
AML registration and enforcement for virtual digital 
asset service providers without yet establishing a 
comprehensive market framework.
Regulated digital asset markets are broadening 
beyond the most established centres. Bahrain, 
Kazakhstan and South Africa have each developed 
licensed frameworks for digital asset activity, 
expanding the group of jurisdictions seeking to 
attract regulated market development beyond 
centres such as Singapore; Hong Kong SAR, 
China; and the United Arab Emirates.
Core activities, trading, custody and payments 
are largely brought within the regulatory 
perimeter. Trading venues, custodians and 
payment providers are supervised in more than 
75% of jurisdictions surveyed, typically alongside 
AML and counterterrorist financing requirements.
Stablecoin frameworks are formalizing quickly. 
As stablecoins expand beyond crypto-market 
settlement into payments and treasury use cases, 
jurisdictions are introducing clearer requirements 
for issuance, reserve assets, redemption and 
operational resilience. Treatment of yield and reward 
mechanisms remains less consistent, reflecting 
continuing differences over when a stablecoin 
begins to resemble a deposit, investment product 
or other regulated claim.
The effectiveness of a regime depends less on 
whether rules sit in banking, securities, payments 
or stand-alone cryptoasset law than on whether 
they are clear, enforceable and usable in practice. 
Indonesia illustrates how regulatory architecture 
can evolve over time: cryptoassets were initially 
supervised by the commodities regulator before 
responsibility shifted principally to the financial 
services regulator, supported by dedicated rules  
for digital financial assets and cryptoassets.26
