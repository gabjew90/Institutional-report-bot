Wind of change 
 
EUR inflation-linked strategy: The inflation data pack for August 
(preliminary numbers so far) evidenced the steady increase in core 
inflation, again. Our inner-core inflation measure stood at 4.6% in July, 
and was probably not far from 5% in August. 
 
In the near term – H222 – we expect core inflation to continue to rise, 
from the end of the “9-euro-ticket” operation in Germany and from 
more pass-through of energy prices into core HICP. In Q422 and Q123, 
another wave of wage increases will also feed in. 
 
Breakevens plunged last week (10Y down c.20bp, 2Y down c.100bp) 
due to the collapse in natural gas prices but also (in our view) due to 
high expectations with regards to the coming EC meeting on energy 
regulation, scheduled on 9 September. Even if our conviction level is 
low, our overall impression is that the market is placing too much hope 
on this meeting: a European energy reform is incredibly complex and 
likely cannot be implemented quickly. 
 
Strategy-wise, the second half of September could be a nice window for 
a tactical rebound in EUR breakevens: after the ECB meeting, EC 
meeting and French linker supply, we expect strong linker extension 
(c.0.28Y) and another super-strong core inflation number at month-end. 
 
We do not have any open trades at the moment. We closed our last 
long inflation trade (1Y inflation swap) in June. The P&L in H122 was 
165bp, for 11 trades and an 82% hit ratio. 
 
US inflation-linked strategy: We provide our usual CPI preview this 
week. We expect headline CPI to come out at 8.1% YoY, 40bp lower 
than in July. The Bloomberg consensus is not formed yet.  We expect 
core CPI to be around 6.0-6.1% YoY, ie, 15bp more than last month, 
mostly from the housing components (OER and rents) again. 
 
If our take is correct, this would be another CPI print with core in the 
6% YoY area, which is much more than the Fed can tolerate. That said, 
the declines in energy inflation and core inflation which we expect after 
August are encouraging points (from a risk sentiment perspective).

 
The inflation data pack for August (preliminary numbers so far) evidenced the 
steady increase in core inflation, again. Our inner-core inflation measure 
stood at 4.6% in July, and was probably not far from 5% in August. 
 
In the near term – H222 – we expect core inflation to continue to rise, from 
the end of the “9-euro-ticket” operation in Germany and from more pass-
through of energy prices into core HICP. In Q422 and Q123, another wave of 
wage increases will also feed in. 
 
Breakevens plunged last week (10Y down c.20bp, 2Y down c.100bp) due to 
the collapse in natural gas prices but also (in our view) due to high 
expectations with regards to the coming EC meeting on energy regulation, 
scheduled on 9 September. Even if our conviction level is low, our overall 
impression is that the market is placing too much hope on this meeting: a 
European energy reform is incredibly complex and likely cannot be 
implemented quickly. 
 
Strategy-wise, the second half of September could be a nice window for a 
tactical rebound in EUR breakevens: after the ECB meeting, EC meeting and 
French linker supply, we expect strong linker extension (c.0.28Y) and 
another super-strong core inflation number at month-end. 


Back to the August flash HICP release 
Eurozone headline inflation came out at 9.1% with core at 4.3% in August 
(preliminary data), in line with our most recent update published on Tuesday 30 
August: Eurozone inflation: August HICP preview and update. 
The big picture remains in our view the strength in core inflation, which has 
increased by c.30bp every month since January, with the notable exception of 
June 2022 (stagnation around 3.75%), when the price for public transport 
services was slashed in Germany. 
Our inner-core HICP measure, which excludes the most volatile and non-
macroeconomic related subcomponents within core inflation has been steadily 
increasing since the start of the year, and in our view, this is not over. A few 
details here: 


 
The increase in our inner-core measure has been remarkably steady 
since the start of the year, starting from 2.55% YoY (January) and reaching 
4.6% in July. We believe inner-core stood around 4.9% YoY in August. Over 
the last six months, the monthly acceleration has always stood between 25bp 
and 45bp, Figure 1. 


electricity and natural gas prices) will cause the same effects (higher core 
goods inflation and gradual spillover into services from firmer wages, with

Another update in our inflation forecasts 
Near-term adjustments 
Like every week, we update our inflation forecasts. Here are a few changes. 
1. We marginally adjusted our take for energy inflation in Germany, taking stock 
of (1) the natural gas levy expected for October; (2) the 7% VAT cut also 
expected for October; and (3) the recent increases (June, July and probably 
August) in natural gas prices. In our view, while the natural gas levy 
(+EUR0.24 per kW/h) has attracted a lot of attention, the impact would be 
limited (around 15% MoM on natural gas HICP in October in our view). Bear 
in mind that natural gas HICP in Germany has already increased by c.50% 
since the start of the year, therefore a 7% increase again in October (+15% 
from the levy maybe, partly offset by -7% from the expected VAT cut) would 
not be a game changer. All in all, we now have natural gas HICP at 88% YoY 
at year-end, with electricity at 46% YoY, for what it is worth. 
2. We also had a look at the “klimaticket” initiative in Germany, which may 
follow the “9-euro-ticket” operation from September. The “klimaticket” would 
be a public transport pass with a monthly price of possibly EUR69. Some 
press sources mentioned a cost for the government of around EUR2bn per 
year for this measure (compared with EUR2.5bn per quarter for the “9-euro-
ticket” operation which will end in four weeks). This suggests the “klimaticket” 
would be far less relevant (HICP-wise) than the current operation. On our 
side, we now assume that transport services HICP is 80% back to normal 
from September. 
3. In Spain, we took into account the expected 15% VAT cut in Spanish natural 
gas (from 21% to 6%) announced by PM Pedro Sanchez on Thursday2. Do 
not expect a massive impact here. Natural gas is 1.5% of Spanish HICP, in 
turn the 15% VAT cut would shave off 22bp from Spanish HICP, and around 
2.5bp from Eurozone HICP. 
4. In Spain, the prices for public transport will also be strongly reduced from 1 
September to year-end. The Spanish media provided a few practical 
examples3. On our side, we assumed a 50% reduction in two-third of the 
HICP rail basket (“Passenger transport by air” and “Combined passenger 
transport’) from September to year-end. This would shave off c.3.5% from

Beyond these multiple small things, we continue to see how the European 
governments try to deal with the spike in inflation: it is mostly about 
accompanying measures: VAT cuts, discounted prices on public transport, 
rebates on fuels. These measures will likely end (they are extremely costly) once 
energy prices ease (from H223 or 2024?). In turn, inflation may well stand 
stronger than the macro economy would suggest at the 2024 horizon, implying 
that Eurozone inflation may remain higher than 2% into mid-2024 (this is what is 
priced in by the current 1Y1Y forward). 
The 9 September EC meeting (on electricity reform) 
An emergency meeting for a reform of the European energy system is scheduled 
on 9 September in Brussels. Here is our utilities analyst’s thoughts on this, 
published in his weekly publication Utilities AWARE: A Week ARound Energy - 
Sailing the Roaring Forties! published on Wednesday 30 August: 
“Time for a change in the European energy market design? After Spain, 
Portugal, Italy and France, new voices are joining calls for structural 
reforms of the European energy market. [...]. Germany, Belgium and 
Austria, among others, have been vocal in recent days while EC 
President Ursula von der Leyen called for “emergency intervention” 
in electricity markets to bring down soaring prices. She also 
indicated that the EC is working on an emergency intervention and a 
structural reform of the electricity market. An agreement has yet to 
be reached between EU countries, especially as discussions remain 
tense and have been on-going for several months (see: The regulation 
path). Indeed, various proposals are on the table and target a price 
no longer reflecting (at least only) the marginal cost of producing 
electricity. Options include: 
(1) decoupling the gas price from the price of electricity;  
(2) capping the gas price, as is currently in place in Iberia; and  
(3) setting a price based on the average cost of producing electricity.  
The Czech Republic, which holds the rotating EU presidency, will hold 
an urgent meeting of energy ministers on 9 September “to discuss 
specific emergency measures to address the energy situation”. Echoing 
those growing voices, French PM Elisabeth Borne warned of the energy 
rationing risk this winter, urging companies to establish energy-saving 
plans while she indicated that companies will be hit first in the event of 
rationing. Borne also said over the weekend that the government will 
continue with measures aimed at curtailing the effect of higher energy 
prices. According to Bruegel, France has already pledged EUR45bn 
worth of measures to shield households and companies from higher 
energy prices between September 2021 and July 2022, the third largest 
amount after Germany (EUR60bn) and Italy (EUR50bn). The combined 
figure across the EU-27 amounts to EUR236bn so far.” 
Our take: decoupling electricity prices from those of natural gas will probably be 
extremely complex and time consuming, therefore the reform could take a lot of 
time to be designed and implemented. That said, the energy crisis is so intense 
that there could be surprises (like option 2?).  
Our base case would be for more measures taken at individual country level for 
now (rather than a large-scale EU reform for now), but our conviction level is low, 
due to the multiple potential scenarios. In any case, the approaching date of the 
meeting (next week) probably accounts for some of the weakness (sub 5Y) in the 
European inflation market.

Strategy 
First of all, do not forget there was the monthly roll in Eurozone inflation swaps on 
1 September, as the 1Y reference point changed from May 2023 to June 2023. 
Our inflation profile suggested a 60bp roll, the 1Y actually moved by 65bp on 1 
September morning. 
Energy prices have been exceptionally volatile in August, with namely the price of 
natural gas (Dutch TTF reference) up 80% before returning close to the starting 
point. This of course created massive moves in inflation swaps and breakevens, 
all the more in the context of reduced liquidity in August. 
To get rid of the energy influence (or at least try to), we focus on a relatively 
neutral time sample, energy-wise. We therefore consider the 17 August to 2 
September period (Figure 4). Over these two weeks or so, inflation swaps moved 
as shown in Figure 5, once correcting the 1Y for the 65bp roll of 1 September.

Key takeaways: 
 
1Y down 80bp (down 145bp if not roll-adjusted). This is a very strong dive, 
probably related to (too high?) expectations of lower electricity (and or 
natural gas) prices due to a possible reform of the energy sector in Europe.

 
The 1Y1Y forward lost 30bp again, from 2.35% to 2.05%. This makes sense 
considering that the markets see the 1Y1Y as a proxy of a recession risk 
(regardless of whether this makes sense or not). Considering the almost 10% 
decline in European equity prices since mid-August and the hawkish central 
banks’ speeches at the Jackson Hole symposium, this should come as no 
surprise. 
 
The 2Y2Y lost c.10bp. This is a similar story as the 1Y1Y, with a more limited 
magnitude.  
 
The 5Y5Y and longer forwards actually behaved fairly well, posting around 
5bp gains. This is a solid performance, considering the fact that the market 
mood turned more negative and linker supply started to return.  
Looking ahead over the coming month, we expect the following: 
 
An ECB rates decision not far from expectations, with a 75bp hike now 
becoming the likely base case. More in the ECB Focus Preview: have your 
hike and eat it too, 2 September. 
 
Still major uncertainty on the regulatory outlook of natural gas and the 
electricity outlook in Europe, even following the 9 September EC meeting. 
 
Core inflation increasing more at end-September (around 4.5-4.6%) 
illustrating the fact that core inflation may have little to do with economic 
activity for now, being actually driven by the past increases in energy prices. 
 
Linker supply returning more in the coming weeks (Germany, then France, 
then Italy), which is negative to breakevens all else equal. 
 
On a more positive note, probably a strong index extension on end-
September, with EUR17.7bn of BTPei Sep-23 leaving the 1Y+ indices. We 
expect the same index extension as in September 2020, ie, +0.28Y, see 
Figure 6 (two years ago, EUR17bn of BTPei Sep-21 had left the 1Y+ 
indices). 
 
Equities probably doing better than the -10% performance observed over the 
past two weeks.

Bottom line, we have a slightly positive stance on Eurozone breakevens,

French linker supply and the ECB meeting, we expect strong linker index 
extension and possible upside surprise in core inflation at month-end) could be a 
nice window for a tactical long in 10Y breakevens. 
Of course – and as usual – energy would be a key driver as well. No point in 
going long inflation if natural gas and or crude oil price is suffering at this point.

Core goods 
We expect core goods inflation to decline again, from 7% to 6.85% YoY. Core 
goods inflation has already declined very significantly since Q1, as it peaked at 
12.3% YoY in February. 
The key reason is the stabilisation in used car prices, which implied that used 
cars CPI collapsed from 40% YoY (January and February) to the single-digits 
level in June and July. 
Beyond the specific case of used cars, the base effects are now very negative for 
most core goods, as the MoM levels recorded in H221 were very strong and are 
now leaving the YoY calculation. This is the case for ‘households furnishing and 
supplies’ (Figure 10), ‘new cars’ (Figure 11), ‘used cars’, and ‘Recreation 
commodities’.  
In turn, the decline in core goods inflation can now be qualified as ‘broad-based’, 
mostly from the negative base effects. Core goods inflation, still above 6% YoY, 
remains well above the historical average, but we still assume that it will fall to 
negative YoY level at some point next year, mostly from the negative base effect 
from ‘used cars’.

Food inflation remains influenced by similar factors as core goods, namely higher 
input costs and logistic costs as well as supply disruptions on a global scale. The 
main difference is the base effect: core goods inflation (MoM) was extremely

Energy inflation 
Our model points towards a strong decline in energy inflation, from 33% to 24% 
YoY. In detail, we have fuels strongly down, electricity somewhat stable, and 
natural gas up. 
We namely have motor fuels (CPRPENCM Index in Bloomberg) falling from 
44.9% to 27.7% YoY from a combination of base effect and of actual decline in 
August (-11% MoM). 
Conclusion: if our take is correct, this would be another CPI print with core in the 
6% YoY area, which is much more than the Fed can tolerate. That said, the 
declines in energy inflation and core inflation which we expect after August are 
encouraging points (from a risk sentiment perspective).
