SCIB Macro & Markets Chartbook - On Hold, but Not 
Done Hiking
Antonio Villarroya: antvillarroya@gruposantander.com | (+34) 91 257-2244 
Central banks took center stage again this week, although not because of any policy changes: the FOMC (Wednesday), the ECB (Thursday) and the
BoJ (Friday, our thoughts here) all left rates unchanged. Although all three were widely expected to remain on hold, the harder part was explaining 
why they chose not to hike already this week, with markets pricing in further tightening at each of their December meetings. Elsewhere, the UK 
Budget (our thoughts here) and Lula’s victory in Brazil also kept investors’ attention. Looking ahead, next week’s midterm elections in the US 
(Tuesday) and BoE’s meeting (Thursday) will be the key events.

1. Holding rates amid a “heck of a family fight”
On Wednesday, the FOMC left rates unchanged. The overall message could hardly be considered hawkish, while Chair Warsh remains adamant 
about providing as little forward guidance as possible. In that vein, the Fed will reduce the number of policy-setting meetings per year, while his 
explanation for not hiking already this week, despite persistently high inflation, was somewhat lacking. Accordingly, the US front end rallied (see lh 
chart). But the combination of a less hawkish message, persistently elevated long-term inflation expectations and a resilient economy (3Q26 GDP 
growth of 3.4% SAAR) drove a bull steepening of the UST curve, reinforced by safe-haven flows into government bonds (see right-hand chart). Our 
call remains for one final hike in December, followed by a hold throughout 2027 — much less easing than currently priced (see lh chart) — a view 
reinforced by this week’s FOMC meeting.

2. ECB: see you in December
On Thursday, the ECB also left rates unchanged, but clearly hinted at another instalment in its tightening cycle in December, which is also our 
base case. Besides Lagarde’s early departure (more on this below), on the monetary policy front, with oil prices becoming increasingly less relevant 
for short-term monetary policy (lh chart), two aspects increase our confidence in our call that the ECB will hike rates by less than expected by the 
market: (1) its characterization of EUR inflation: “underlying inflation has remained broadly stable so far and wages do not show a material 
response at this stage” and (2) the (new) mention of the impact of tighter financing conditions likely weighing on inflation and growth, as we 
discussed last week. Nevertheless, the inflation outlook remains challenging. Market pricing suggests that inflation may not return sustainably to 
the ECB’s 2% target, at least until mid-2028 (rh chart), but we will keep a close eye on next week’s Eurozone inflation data.

3. ECB: Frankfurt’s great reshuffle
Following Lagarde’s announcement that she will step down early, on 7 February 2027 (rather than 31 October 2027), attention has turned to her 
successor as ECB president. Polymarket currently puts Pablo Hernández de Cos, former Bank of Spain governor and current BIS general manager, 
in the lead with a 34.5% chance, ahead of former Dutch central bank governor Klaas Knot at 29%.
In addition, Philip Lane’s term as chief economist ends in May 2027, while Isabel Schnabel has brought forward her departure to January 
2027, from December. This means three senior ECB positions will turn over within a few months. The most likely outcome of this is that euro-
area governments will negotiate the appointments for these positions as a package, balancing nationality, geography, gender and political 
considerations across the three key posts.

4. Brazil’s 2R surprise unwinds most of the post-1R move
Contrary to market expectations following the first round of voting, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won the second round of Brazil’s presidential 
election last weekend securing 51.8% of the vote vs. 48.2% for Flavio Bolsonaro (from 45.2% vs. 47% in the first round).
The result triggered a sharp reversal in Brazilian assets:
• Long-end government bond yields rose sharply (+230bp in 10y rates), more than correcting the rally seen after the first-round results (lh 
chart).
• The Ibovespa (equity) index fell by c.18% over the week, also more than unwinding the post-first-round rally (rh chart).

5. Earnings beats narrow, Equities under pressure
The S&P 500 is at about the half-way mark of this earnings season with 288 companies (weighing c.50% of the index’s market cap) already having 
reported 3Q26 results. We highlight that this earnings season, so far, has seen the lowest aggregate earnings positive surprise (3%) in over 
eight quarters (lh chart), and most narrow positive surprise on revenues (1.6%) since 1Q25, with modest upgrades to earnings guidance, which has 
weighed on investor sentiment. Despite aggregate net income from companies that have already reported growing 25% YoY (rh chart) vs. its 11% 
10-year average, this has failed to beat investors’ lofty expectations.

6. US midterms up next: losing a chamber is not that unusual
Next Tuesday, the US midterm elections will keep most of market’s attention, as the Republicans are expected to lose their majority in Congress. 
We highlight that since 2006, every president entering the midterms with unified control of Congress has lost at least one chamber. In fact, the 
last four midterms with unified control (2006, 2010, 2018, and 2022) all resulted in losses: three House-only, and one involving both chambers. 
Interestingly, G.W. Bush (2002) was the last president to gain control of a chamber, retaking the Senate. But in 2006 he was also the last president 
to lose both chambers. In his previous administration, Trump lost control of the House in the midterms.

7. BoE preview: A hike to douse the spark, not extinguish a fire
We firmly expect the BoE to hike Bank Rate by 25bp next week, catching up with the earlier moves from the Fed and ECB, in line with market 
pricing (97%). We think this should be framed as a risk-management move against inflation persistence rather than the start of a sustained hiking 
cycle, and for the MPC’s tone to lean against the 84bp of subsequent tightening priced over 2027. That puts the UK’s peak overnight rate c.40bp 
higher than the US’s – a remarkable swing from 5bp lower at the start of this month.
We also expect the MPC will follow the FOMC and ECB’s lead in holding rates at the following meeting, in December, but market pricing 
implies a 40% chance of back-to-back moves. The MPC should also echo the ECB’s key messages that “tightening financial conditions are doing a 
lot of work for it” and “there is no sign of second-round effects [yet]”. The very high peak has become firmly entrenched in the market and will take 
sustained effort from the MPC to bring down, but that should be the direction of travel.

8. UK Budget: good news for gilts, as the tough fiscal work is left for the future
The chancellor’s pitch in his first Budget was to help households and businesses through what the government hopes is a temporary energy shock, 
without abandoning fiscal discipline. The package seemed to tick all the boxes at the headline level, which is what mattered for gilt investors this 
week, but the tough and important fiscal choices were left for next year. See our reaction note for full details on the policy choices and gilt remit.
The cash details were a relief for the gilt market, and the entire curve rallied by c.5bp over Wednesday and Thursday combined. The gilt issuance 
target for this fiscal year increased by £4.4bn and next year’s estimated financing needs by £10bn, less than we had assumed. Furthermore, the 
DMO has over-delivered so far this year, so the average pace of gross issuance over the next five months will be 24% lower than over the last 
seven. The picture is even better for gilts on a net basis: two large redemptions in 1Q27 mean negative net issuance next quarter (even before 
considering coupon flows).
