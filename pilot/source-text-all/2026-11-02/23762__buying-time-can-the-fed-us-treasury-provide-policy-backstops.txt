In Summary 
 
• From private-led to public-led financial stability. For four decades, the recycling of 
foreign savings into US assets delivered low rates, abundant collateral and private 
liquidity creation for countries around the globe. Yet as foreign demand for US Treasury 
securities has weakened over recent years, stability has come to rest increasingly on public 
backstops from the US Federal Reserve (Fed) and, increasingly, the US Department of the 
Treasury. In effect, their interventions stabilize a highly leveraged system in which most risk 
assets sell off when rate volatility rises. 
• A first generation of tools, led by the Fed, managed abundant liquidity. After the 2008 
Global Financial Crisis, quantitative easing (QE), was complemented by a raft of tools that 
created a policy-rate corridor, with Interest On Reserves Balances (IORB) serving as a 
ceiling and Reverse Repo Program (RRP) as a floor, along with the Foreign and 
International Monetary Authorities (FIMA) repo facility and central-bank swap lines to 
contain funding volatility and backstop offshore dollar demand. This framework relied on 
low inflation until Covid changed the macro backdrop. 
• Now, a second generation, increasingly led by the Treasury, manages frictions. Since 
the Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) crisis in March 2023, T-bill supply has surged from about 
USD3.8tn to USD6.7tn, reflecting deficit financing and the rebuilding of the Treasury's cash 
balance as well as debt-management choices, and has drained the RRP from its USD2.6tn 
peak in April 2023. Alongside the Fed's Standing Repo Facility, the toolkit also includes a 
shorter debt maturity, buybacks, bank balance-sheet relief and the GENIUS Act. Together, 
these have acted as a form of “indirect QE”: by reducing the duration supply available to 
private investors, they lowered 10-year yields by an estimated 25bp even as the Fed 
implemented quantitative tightening, although term premia still rose. But this has come at 
the cost of higher financing costs over the long term and shorter debt horizons. 
• The structural forces behind fragmentation keep strengthening. Rising defense, energy 
and industrial financing needs are keeping more savings at home for those countries that 
used to recycle their excess cash into the US capital markets. On top of this, reserve 
managers are progressively diversifying away from the USD, and the marginal foreign 
buyer of Treasuries is no longer central banks, but price-sensitive, leveraged investors 
domiciled in offshore centers. As a consequence, the volatility of long-term Treasury 
inflows has risen to about 3.7%, against a 2.6% average since 2009, and Treasuries 
increasingly sell off alongside equities. Beyond higher inflation uncertainty, this is eroding 
the safe-asset status of US debt securities. 
• Policy backstops buy time, but they cannot reverse the shift. In our base case, 
fragmentation points to higher term premia, a weaker USD over the medium term, more 
domestic bond markets and a larger role for gold.

Buying time: Can the Fed & US Treasury provide 
policy backstops in a fragmenting dollar system

The global financial system has gradually transitioned from a regime driven by foreign capital recycling and 
private credit creation to one increasingly dependent on domestic balance sheets and central bank backstops. 
As foreign demand for US Treasuries weakened and bank lending slowed, fewer USDs were recycled abroad 
through traditional channels despite persistent global demand for USD funding. This growing mismatch between 
USD demand and USD supply has forced the Federal Reserve to evolve: from a central bank focused on broad 
liquidity provision to one actively managing the distribution of liquidity across the system, ensuring that funding 
reaches the balance sheets that need it, when they need it. Said differently, the monetary system has gradually 
switched from a private led financial stability, to gradually a public led financial stability. Understanding why this 
evolution took the specific form it did requires taking a deeper look to how we can estimate the degree of financial 
stability. 
Financial stability, in this framework, is best understood through the lens of rate volatility. In a financial system 
characterized by high debt levels and widespread leverage, rate volatility becomes the central variable because it 
directly affects funding stability. When interest rates become volatile, funding costs and funding availability become 
more uncertain. As uncertainty rises, leveraged investors are forced to reduce risk, creating deleveraging pressures 
across asset classes, from equities and credit to commodities and foreign exchange. In that sense, a large share of 
capital market assets behave as if they were highly sensitive to rates volatility: their valuations and financing 
conditions implicitly rely on a stable interest-rate environment. The negative correlation with the MOVE index in 
Figure 1 partly reflects a common risk-off factor, but the mechanism runs through funding: in March 2020 and April 
2025, spikes in the MOVE index came with wider funding spreads, the unwinding of leveraged Treasury basis and 
swap-spread trades. As a result, higher rate volatility tends to increase cross-asset volatility and the risk of systemic 
deleveraging. Faced with this vulnerability, policymakers, especially in the US,  expanded their role from managing 
liquidity crises to containing rate volatility itself. This de facto “public volatility management” has helped preserve 
funding stability and prevent destabilizing deleveraging spirals. This evolution went through two generations: the 
first led by central banks, and the second led by treasuries and debt management offices (DMOs).

The first generation of public intervention on financial stability was marked by the creation of new monetary 
policy facilities. By conducting QE, the Fed injected vast amounts of cash into the banking system, which initially 
sat idle and unremunerated on bank balance sheets. To manage these excess reserves, the Fed introduced the IORB 
as a ceiling and the RRP as a floor, effectively building a corridor to steer short-term rates while giving institutions a 
tool to park and price their excess cash. This was a direct response to the growing gap between Treasury issuance 
and foreign purchases (Figure 2): the Fed created the cash banks needed to absorb those Treasuries, preventing 
the funding stress (Figure 3) that would otherwise emerge from an imbalance between cash and collateral in the 
repo market.

agreements were established  between central banks to exchange currencies at a pre-defined rate. Those can 
backstop the price of foreign USD on the FX swap market, and ease USD shortages within the private sector.

Together, these tools reshaped monetary policy. Their stated aims were to steer short-term rates and contain 
funding stress; in effect, they also supported the USD's global status, sustaining low rates and a strong currency 
despite weakening fundamentals, while ensuring that the domestic sector had enough liquidity to finance the US 
fiscal deficit. This has been possible due to low level of inflation that facilitated public money creation. The shock 
surrounding the halt to production globally to prevent the spread of the Covid-19 virus changed this dramatically. 
A second generation of new monetary policy tools appeared after Covid with a growing footprint of the 
government, whose debt management came to have QE-like effects. The return of inflation after the Covid shock 
fueled by supply chains disruptions, fiscal stimulus and geopolitical tensions, forced the Fed to hike rates and 
implement quantitative tightening (QT) in 2022. But QT, added to a hiking cycle, increased financial stability risk. 
Backstops with indirect, QE-like effects helped offset the undesirable effect of monetary policy on liquidity in an era 
of elevated inflation. The main instrument was the standing repo facility (SRF), which the Fed describes as a 
backstop in money markets to support monetary-policy implementation and smooth market functioning.  While QT 
drains reserves from banks, this repo facility enables banks to borrow reserves on demand and thus cap the upside 
risk on short term rates that IORB can't do.  
With the Silicon Valley Bank crisis in March 2023 financial stability management increasingly moved from the 
Fed to the Treasury. In order to finance large deficits and rebuild its cash balance at the Fed (the Treasury General 
Account, TGA) after the June 2023 debt-ceiling resolution, the US Treasury significantly increased bill issuance. This 
drained much of the USD2.6tn (April 2023) of excess liquidity parked in the Reverse Repo Facility (RRP) while raising 
the stock of US T-Bills outstanding from roughly USD3.8tn to nearly USD6.7tn today. By shortening the weighted 
average maturity (WAM) of government debt, the US Treasury effectively reduced duration supply available to 
private investors, creating a duration-extraction effect with QE-like consequences, which Miran and Roubini (2024) 
call Activist Treasury Issuance (ATI). They estimate that this issuance mix lowered 10-year yields by 14-40bp, with a 
central estimate of around 25bp1. By lowering duration supply, debt management thus reduced pressure for 
Treasury bond unwinds, credit spread widening and broader risk-asset deleveraging, helping keep financial 
conditions under control. This is, however, a gross effect of the maturity mix: it does not net out the much larger 
overall supply needed to finance the deficit and rebuild the TGA, and term premia rose over the same period. Debt 
management therefore dampened, rather than prevented, the rise in long-term yields. Bills also function as near-
money instruments, creating a money-supply channel that partially sterilizes QT, particularly when financed by 
balances leaving the RRP. In parallel, the review of the Basel III regulation on the Enhanced Supplementary 
Leverage Ratio (eSLR) freed up bank balance-sheet capacity. This supported Treasury demand and preserved 
funding market stability, while buyback operations improved market liquidity by removing less liquid off-the-run 
securities from the market, thereby tightening the bid-ask spread and directly supporting the market functioning. 
 
Stablecoins emerge as a new channel of demand for Treasury bills. Through reserve requirements embedded in 
the GENIUS Act, stablecoin issuers have effectively been transformed into institutions akin to money market funds 
that issue private digital money backed predominantly by public assets. As stablecoin adoption grows, every 
additional USD of reserve-backed stablecoins translates largely into additional demand for Bills and other 
Treasury-backed money market instruments. This helps facilitate the Treasury's strategy of shortening the maturity 
profile of government debt, while simultaneously strengthening the liquidity and resilience of money markets. In 
effect, the GENIUS Act's reserve rules channel part of the growth of the digital USD ecosystem into demand for near-
money government collateral. The Act is primarily a payments and consumer-protection framework, however, and 
this support for Treasury funding is a by-product of its design rather than a stated aim. Stablecoin issuers already 
hold close to $200 billion of Treasury bills, making them a rapidly growing, if still modest, source of demand for 
front-end government debt. As reserve-backed stable coins continue to expand, private digital money creation is 
largely transformed into additional demand for short-term government liabilities, reinforcing liquidity in money 
markets and supporting Treasury funding needs. According to the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, 
stablecoin issuers' holdings of Treasury securities could approach $400 billion by 2030 if current adoption trends 
persist. Against a stock of about USD6.7tn of bills, this remains a real but small source of demand, around 3% today 
and about 6% by 2030 at today's stock. Part of it is also not net new: stablecoins partly displace money market fund 
holdings and bank deposits rather than adding to total demand for government paper.

Taken together, Active Treasury Issuance, RRP drains, lower weighted average maturity, regulatory relief, 
GENIUS Act and buybacks operate as a de facto form of yield management, designed to suppress duration risk, 
reduce rates volatility and smooth the transmission of tighter monetary policy. We can observe a direct link between 
the reduced rates volatility and the bills issuance (Figure 4). In practice, the Treasury has become an increasingly 
active player in managing the financial cycle. This activity, however, has come at the cost of structurally higher 
funding expenses for the government, and a reduction in the time horizons that may exacerbate the magnitude of 
potential future shocks.

Central banks are buying time, but the structural forces behind capital flow fragmentation is growing stronger. 
The U.S. is becoming increasingly reluctant to provide global public goods at the expense of domestic objectives, 
while encouraging allies to assume a larger share of defense and infrastructure spending. At the same time, 
governments across advanced economies face rising financing needs linked to the energy transition, industrial 
policies, infrastructure modernization and defense expenditures. As a result, capital is increasingly incentivized to 
remain within domestic markets rather than being exported abroad. Reserve allocation data (Figure 5) reflect this 
trend: the decline in the USD's share has not benefited any single alternative reserve currency but has instead been 
distributed across a broader set of assets, including the renminbi, the CAD and AUD, and increasingly gold. The 
logical consequence is a transition toward a more fragmented, safety oriented and domestically financed global 
economic order. In this context, new monetary policy facilities should be viewed as tools that help manage the 
financial instability generated by this transition rather than to reverse it.

The structural fragmentation has changed the composition of Treasury market buyers. Treasury holdings 
registered in offshore financial centers such as the Cayman Islands, Ireland, and Luxembourg have grown three to 
four times faster than total foreign holdings, as shown by figure 6. These jurisdictions primarily serve as domiciles 
for hedge funds, asset managers, and other leveraged investment vehicles that use Treasuries as collateral 
instruments for relative-value strategies. The marginal foreign buyer of US government debt has therefore shifted 
away from sovereign balance sheets and toward private, leveraged capital, which is more price sensitive2. While 
central banks typically accumulate reserves with long investment horizons and limited sensitivity to short-term 
market fluctuations, demand from leveraged investors depends on funding costs, volatility, and relative returns. 
Combined with the lower weighted average maturity of US debt, this shift increases market vulnerability. Instead, 
treasury market stability now rests mainly on the policy backstop described above, rather than on investors’ 
confidence in the soundness of USTs.

The consequences of this transformation are increasingly visible in Treasury capital flows. Since 2021, the 
volatility of long-term Treasury inflows has risen sharply, reaching approximately 3.7%, well above its long-run 
average of 2.6% (Figure 7). Volatility in short-term Treasury flows has also increased, although to a lesser degree. 
The widening gap between long- and short-duration flow volatility suggests that duration-sensitive capital, 
precisely the type supplied by leveraged and offshore investors, has become a growing source of instability. When 
the marginal holder of long-duration sovereign debt is a hedge fund financing a basis trade, rather than a central 
bank managing reserves, Treasury demand inevitably becomes more sensitive to funding pressures, margin calls, 
and shifts in market sentiment.

The fragmentation dynamic is raising doubts about the safe asset status of US Treasury bonds. The US Treasury 
is progressively losing part of its exceptional status as the dominant global safe asset. This is reflected in higher term 
premia, weaker hedged returns for foreign investors and the increasing importance of liquidity considerations 
during periods of stress. The changing relationship between Treasury yields and equity markets provides another 
illustration of this evolution. Historically, Treasuries tended to benefit from safe-haven inflows during equity selloffs, 
but episodes of rising yields alongside falling equity prices have become increasingly frequent. This shift is not, on 
its own, evidence of fragmentation: a positive stock-bond correlation is also what an inflation-uncertainty regime 
produces, as in 1970-1990, when inflation surprises pushed equity and bond prices down together.  
 
What points beyond inflation is the behavior of indicators that isolate the safe-asset premium, such as negative 
swap spreads (Figure 8), falling convenience-yield estimates and the rise of the term-premium component of long 
yields rather than of expected rates. Taken together, this growing procyclicality suggests that Treasuries are 
increasingly competing for scarce capital, rather than automatically benefiting from their traditional safe-asset 
status.

The world is moving from a regime characterized by abundant savings and capital exports toward one 
characterized by higher investment needs and greater competition for capital. In this environment, rising term 
premia should be interpreted as the reflection of a broader structural transition. Central banks increasingly 
recognize this reality. The objective of the new facilities is to ensure that the transition happens gradually, rather 
than through disorderly deleveraging. They can smooth it but they cannot reverse its direction. 
 
The common thread behind these developments is the gradual transition from a monetary regime supported by 
abundant global savings toward one characterized by greater capital scarcity, growing financial intermediation 
friction and increasing capital-flow instability. In such an environment, the role of policymakers is, in practice, no 
longer limited to managing interest rates. Increasingly, it extends to managing the speed of capital reallocation and 
preserving the stability of money, funding and sovereign bond markets. The expansion of the monetary-policy 
toolkit, together with the growing interaction between governments and central banks, suggests that the next 
monetary regime may be characterized by more active management of liquidity, foreign-exchange markets and 
capital flows. In this sense, the evolution of recent policies appears consistent with framework: as global financial 
integration constrains monetary autonomy, policymakers increasingly rely on interventions designed to stabilize 
capital flows and financial conditions. Ultimately, this will reach a discussion questioning the freedom of capital 

9 
 
flows. The August 2026 "Yen-tervention" offers a useful illustration of this evolution. By only communicating about 
liquidity provision and foreign-exchange stabilization, policymakers helped limit the risk of forced Treasury sales, 
liquidity shortage and as a result, more widespread financial instability. More broadly, such interventions suggest 
that the purpose of modern monetary policy is increasingly shifting from the management of money itself toward 
the management of the consequences of global capital movements. Ultimately, central banks are not reversing the 
structural forces behind the growing instability. They are buying time for the financial system to adapt to it. 
 
The current policy mix inevitably invites comparisons with financial repression and fiscal dominance. Debt 
management is actively shaping market structure, regulation is channeling private balance sheets toward 
sovereign debt, and the boundary between monetary and fiscal policy is blurring. But the analogy has limits. 
Financial repression sought to keep real borrowing costs artificially low to erode the real value of public debt; fiscal 
dominance described a central bank subordinated to deficit financing. Regime emerging here is different in nature: 
it is less about suppressing the level of rates and more about containing their volatility. Sovereign bonds are no 
longer merely financing instruments — they are increasingly the core collateral on which the entire financial 
system's stability depends, furthermore they are re-used several times and form “collateral chains” into the repo 
market, which create a new procyclicity between money markets and capital markets that was not existing before. 
Managing their volatility is not a policy choice but a structural necessity. What is taking shape is therefore not a 
return to financial repression, but something new: a de facto public volatility-management regime, in which the form 
if interventions deployed aim to preserve market stability and prevent systemic deleveraging. 
 
The implications of this global fragmentation may be deeper than expected, and in our base case they begin 
with a structurally weaker USD over the medium term. Two converging dynamics point in that direction. On one 
hand, central banks have been gradually diversifying their foreign exchange reserves away from the USD, whose 
share in global official reserves has been trending lower for two decades. On the other hand, there is a growing 
political willingness to depreciate the currency in order to strengthen US competitiveness. Together, these forces set 
the stage for a major rebalancing between surplus and deficit countries, channeled through an appreciation of 
surplus economies' currencies, primarily in Asia. These countries will increasingly need to finance their domestic 
investment requirements, reallocate trade flows and, ultimately, redirect their savings inward. Over shorter 
horizons, tariffs, foreign investors' hedge ratios and the pace of repatriation by surplus countries can push either 
way: tariffs and risk-off episodes can support the USD, while higher hedge ratios and faster repatriation would 
accelerate its decline. Depreciation is most likely if reserve diversification and surplus-country repatriation continue 
together; a renewed US growth and yield advantage would delay it. 
 
The domestication of bond markets and the rise of gold in a world of fragmented safety. This rebalancing carries 
a direct consequence: surpluses that were once recycled into foreign assets and above all into US Treasuries are 
increasingly retained domestically. Bond markets are becoming local rather than global. This is the mirror image of 
the reserve diversification observed in foreign exchange: the same savings that once flowed into USD assets are 
now being absorbed at home. In addition, no single asset is likely to replace US Treasuries as the global safe asset. 
Instead, each sovereign market becomes the reference safe asset for its own financial ecosystem a world of 
fragmented safety. In this context, gold emerges as the structural beneficiary: it is the only neutral reserve asset in 
a system where no sovereign bond can claim universal status.  
 
Lower liquidity, higher term premia, greater volatility. Finally, the fragmentation of bond markets reduces the 
global circulation of savings. As cross-border liquidity becomes increasingly constrained, convenience yields decline 
on the back of growing supply, and market depth deteriorates. This weakens the system's ability to absorb shocks, 
resulting in structurally higher volatility across asset classes. A multipolar world without a dominant monetary 
hegemon is therefore, by construction, a more fragile financial system, and a more interventionist one by the public 
sector.
