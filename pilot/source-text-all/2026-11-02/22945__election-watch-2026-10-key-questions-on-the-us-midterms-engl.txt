Election Watch 2026: 10 key questions on the 
US midterms
Election Watch

• We expect the 2026 midterm elections to yield 
a divided government, with sweeping policy 
changes constrained by Senate rules, party divisions, 
presidential veto power, and already wide federal 
deficits.
• AI oversight and data center permitting will be 
consequential policy issues after the election, 
with the buildout expected to continue as strong 
demand supports investment, while state and local 
rules shape project timing and location.
• There is a path to a 2027 debt ceiling agreement, 
but political divisions could delay a deal and 
heighten market volatility, adding uncertainty to 
a bond market already facing persistent deficits and 
heavy Treasury issuance.

1. What is the most likely outcome of the 2026 
midterm elections?
We assign a 50% probability to Democrats winning both 
chambers, 40% to Democrats winning the House while 
Republicans retain the Senate, and 10% to Republicans 
retaining both. The size of any majority will depend 
on election-night performance. Democratic Senate 
control would shift committee leadership, investigations, 
nominations, and judicial confirmations. It would also 
open a path to budget reconciliation, although Senate 
rules, party divisions, and the presidential veto would 
constrain legislation.
2. Could the result shift market expectations for 
2028?
Yes, if the result differs meaningfully from expectations. A 
strong Democratic showing could raise the market-implied 
probability of a Democratic president in 2028 and bring 
future tax, spending, and regulatory priorities into view. A 
Republican sweep could extend the current policy agenda 
and reduce expectations for congressional investigations, 
fiscal compromise, and tighter regulation. The size and 
composition of either majority would shape the signal for 
2028.
3. How could the election shape fiscal policy and 
Social Security reform?
Divided government and already-wide federal budget 
deficits will likely rule out another sweeping fiscal package 
like the 2025 tax law. Social Security will gain importance 
as its projected 2032 trust-fund depletion approaches. 
Senators elected in November will participate in the reform 
effort, and the issue will likely feature prominently in the 
2028 presidential campaign. A bipartisan commission 
could frame the choices. A likely compromise would 
rely more on new revenue than benefit cuts, potentially 
applying the payroll tax to earnings above current income 
limits and adding other taxes.

4. Why do deficits and the 2027 debt ceiling matter 
for bonds?
The 2027 debt-limit negotiation could produce an 
agreement that softens scheduled social-program cuts and 
raises defense spending, reversing expected narrowing 
of budget deficits and increasing Treasury supply. A late 
agreement could also increase market volatility. We still 
expect inflation to moderate, the Fed to finish raising 
rates, and Treasury yields to settle lower. Current yields 
provide attractive income, although persistent federal 
budget deficits and heavy issuance support greater caution 
toward the longest maturities.
5. Will trade policy and tariffs change after the 
election?
The executive branch will retain broad authority over 
tariffs, regardless of who controls Congress. Democratic 
majorities could increase hearings and oversight, while the 
courts could constrain the administration’s use of specific 
tariff authorities. Canada and Mexico will focus on the 
USMCA review, while Asia will watch technology controls. 
Washington may continue to pressure Europe to take a 
tougher stance toward China as Europe addresses its large 
and growing trade deficit with China.
6. Could AI regulation and data-center opposition 
derail the investment cycle?
Under divided government, House Democrats would likely 
increase oversight of AI companies, while the Senate’s 
60-vote threshold would limit broad federal legislation. 
A narrower bipartisan package could speed data-center 
permitting, protect ratepayers, and require developers to 
bear more grid-upgrade costs. State and local rules will 
still influence project timing and location. We expect the 
buildout to continue because AI demand remains strong, 
capital spending is supporting growth and earnings, and 
bipartisan support for technology investment makes a 
material slowdown unlikely.
7. How have stocks and bonds behaved during 
midterm years?
Midterm-year averages have limited value as trading 
signals because each cycle brings a different economic 
backdrop. In 2026, the usual year-two weakness in stocks 
gave way to record highs as AI investment and profit 
growth drove returns. Bonds tracked the historical pattern 
more closely: yields rose in the first half, and volatility 
increased before the election. Inflation, Fed expectations, 
and fiscal concerns drove those moves.
8. Why do state elections matter to investors this 
year?
Democrats could gain two to four governorships and 
narrow the Republican advantage in state trifectas. Thirty-
six governor races and 164 statewide ballot measures will 
shape taxes, housing, health care, education, energy, and 
infrastructure. Governors and legislatures will set different 
approaches to affordability, permitting, public investment, 
and regulation. Successful state initiatives can spread to 
other states and influence federal policy debates.
9. How exposed are municipal issuers to federal 
spending cuts?
Federal transfers provide roughly one-third of state 
revenue, ranging from 20%–39% across states. 
Permanent Medicaid and other safety-net funding cuts 
would require budget and program adjustments, with 
credit impacts varying by federal dependence, obligations, 
and fiscal flexibility. Strong reserves, taxing authority, 
balanced-budget rules, and phased implementation of 
the One Big Beautiful Bill Act should support adjustment. 
States could also reduce funding to cities, schools, transit 
systems, and hospitals, spreading pressure across the 
municipal market.
10. What will matter most for long-term investors as 
Election Day approaches?
Growth, earnings, inflation, and interest rates will matter 
most. Divided government will likely constrain major tax 
and spending legislation, although budget negotiations, 
the debt ceiling, trade policy, and regulation could still 
affect the outlook. We expect economic growth and 
inflation to slow, the Fed to finish raising rates, and bond 
yields to settle slightly lower in 2027. Strong earnings will 
likely continue to support equities. Policy changes may 
warrant portfolio adjustments if fundamentals materially 
change.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
