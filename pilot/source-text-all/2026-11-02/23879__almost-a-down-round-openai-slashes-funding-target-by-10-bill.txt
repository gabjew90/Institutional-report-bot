 
"Almost A Down Round": OpenAI Slashes Funding 
Target By $10 Billion As AI Rout Spoils "Last 
Chance" Pitch 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 31, 2026 - 04:25 PM 
So much for the last chance. 
Just last month OpenAI, fresh from delaying its IPO well into 2027, was pitching a $30 billion 
raise at a $1.4 trillion valuation, billing it as investors' "final chance" to buy in ahead of its IPO. 
Tonight, the FT reports that the ChatGPT maker has slashed the target for the round by $10 
billion as it "scrambles to close a deal" that has been overshadowed by the AI sell-off in public 
markets and by the imminent IPO of its arch-rival Anthropic. OpenAI is now seeking about $20 
billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation, according to multiple people with knowledge of the matter. 
And it gets worse: the deadline for investors to commit was today, and that "first close" is now 
expected to bring in less than $20 billion, after some investors lowered their allocations to the 
round. 
We had a one-line summary of the FT's scoop the moment it hit: 
Readers will recall that last week we wrote that "Haircut Season" had spread to OpenAI, after 
the company trimmed its valuation target from $1.4 trillion to $1.25 trillion when Morgan 
Stanley's wealthy clients greeted the deal with what one person called "a bit of a thud." At the 
time, the FT warned that OpenAI "may ultimately raise less than $30bn depending on investor 
interest," and we translated that as the $30 billion is the ceiling. Well, we now know where the 
floor is (for now): one third lower. 
A Third Less Money At An 11% Lower Price 
Here is the round as it stands, according to the FT: 
 

 
●​ A consortium of UAE investment groups, including state-backed tech fund MGX, is 
expected to be the largest participant, and as part of the deal OpenAI gets an option to 
lease newly developed computing power in the region. 
●​ Founders Fund, Coatue, Sequoia, Andreessen Horowitz and Masayoshi Son's SoftBank 
(the same SoftBank selling junk bonds at soaring yields to fund its OpenAI habit) also 
plan to participate. 
●​ A subsequent retail offering will let wealthy individuals buy in at $1.25 trillion, which 
"could expand the round to $20bn or so." 
Read that last bullet again: the "about $20 billion" headline number depends on the same 
rich-client channel that delivered "zero clamouring" just one week ago. Which probably explains 
the hedge. 
Put it in context and the shrinkage is stark. OpenAI raised a record $122 billion in March; it was 
pitching $30 billion in early October; it will now be lucky to close $20 billion: 
 
Some napkin math (approximate): $20 billion at $1.25 trillion buys investors roughly 1.6% of 
OpenAI, against roughly 14% in March. A company that "has plans to spend multiples" of 
March's $122 billion on compute, as the FT puts it, and that is on the hook for some $1.5 trillion 
of compute commitments, is now passing the hat for loose change by its own standards. 
Technically $1.25 trillion is still a 47% markup to the $852 billion of March, and OpenAI pointed 
to a rapid increase in revenue since July to justify the near-doubling. But that revenue story 
already took a hit when OpenAI told investors its run rate was approaching $50 billion (Oct 8), 
some $20 billion below what had been circulating, and at $1.25 trillion the company still trades 
at ~25x that figure. Against the price OpenAI itself put on the table a month ago, this is an 11% 
 

 
markdown on a third less money. One more turn of the screw and the word "down" stops 
needing the "almost" in front of it. 
Everyone Is Waiting For Anthropic... Which Just Got Its Own Bad 
News 
The FT's other culprit is Anthropic, which is set to go public next month at a valuation of "$1.5tn 
or more" and is "absorbing both investor capital and focus." In other words, for the first time 
OpenAI's private price tag sits below what its rival is expected to fetch in public, and investors 
would rather wait and see how Anthropic prices before writing checks to the competitor: 
 
Then again, the Anthropic IPO is not exactly a victory parade either. Earlier this month some of 
Anthropic's own investors marked its valuation down to $1.5 trillion from the $2 trillion penciled 
in, and just this afternoon the FT reported that Blackstone has told Anthropic it may not secure 
an investment-grade rating after the IPO, and is instead expected to land a "high" junk rating. 
We had a question: 
(Those commitments are laid out in all their glory in Anthropic's updated IPO prospectus.) 
Goldman's credit strategists saw this coming, sort of: in their latest Global Credit Trader 
(available to pro subs), Amanda Lynam's team writes that "we expect BBs to absorb the brunt of 
future AI-related financing." High junk, meet BB. 
So the two frontier labs that were supposed to anchor the AI IPO trade now look like this: one is 
cutting its private round by a third, the other is heading into its listing with a junk balance sheet. 
Everyone wants to be in the room for the IPO; fewer and fewer want to pay up before it. 
 

 
$4.5 Trillion Of Dry Powder... And Nobody Had $10 Billion 
The timing is especially awkward for the sell-side's "there's plenty of money" camp. On Friday 
morning, just hours before the FT story, Goldman software analyst Gabriela Borges published a 
note titled "What we learned this week: AI Supply/Demand, Infrastructure & Financing" 
(available to pro subs), arguing that the funding gap is smaller than feared: 
The amount of financing needed may be less than what investors expect, in part because of 
levers to financing like BYO-hardware or vendor prepayments, in part because the installed 
base will generate cash as it matures, and in part because of non-traditional sources like the 
$19 tr private markets, across all strategies (which includes $4.5 tr of capacity to be put to work, 
per Preqin) or Nvidia's $500bn financing pool. 
Maybe. But $4.5 trillion of private-market dry powder and the single most famous private 
company on earth just came up $10 billion short. The Goldman note does concede that the 
bank's own credit strategists see room for "another $500bn of IG issuance" from the 
hyperscalers, while warning that "the scale and scope of AI-related gross debt extends well 
beyond the hyperscaler universe." 
That distinction is the whole story. Investors will still lend to the landlords, but they are getting 
pickier about the tenants. Microsoft drew a staggering $115 billion of demand for its jumbo bond 
deal this week (per Bloomberg, flagged in JPM's Market Intel briefing), while OpenAI, which 
rents its compute from Microsoft, Oracle and the rest, can't fill a $30 billion equity round. 
Goldman's Delta One head Rich Privorotsky made the same point from the trading floor in 
Friday's "What Matters Today" (available to pro subs), after Amazon's first ad miss in seven 
quarters and trailing FCF of negative $42 billion: 
Core businesses financing this extraordinary investment cycle are coming under greater 
scrutiny... The bigger issue is increasingly funding and returns. 
Last week, Privorotsky put it even more bluntly: "The assumption of effectively unlimited cheap 
capital to fund this buildout is being challenged. You either cut capex, delay deployment or 
increasingly fund through equity." OpenAI just tried the third option, and found out what the 
equity market charges these days. 
The Tape Did The Negotiating 
As one of OpenAI's backers told the FT, they "kicked off the fundraising process amid 
enthusiasm for the AI trade but had been caught out by a sharp reversal in the market." That's 
 

 
one way to describe Friday, when the Nasdaq 100 fell 3.5% and briefly entered correction 
territory, Intel suffered its biggest drop on record and Nasdaq 100 members had already erased 
more than $1.2 trillion in market cap in October before the opening bell. 
The plumbing is no healthier. Goldman's Tony Pasquariello notes (available to pro subs) that the 
bank's flagship momentum basket has already logged 39 one-day moves of more than 5% in 
2026, more than in any year since at least 1999 except 2020 (42), and ahead of 2000 (32). 27 
of those 39 were selloffs. Meanwhile JPM's Nikolaos Panigirtzoglou found that retail poured 
record amounts into leveraged ETFs in recent weeks to buy the AI dip, which is exactly the 
cohort Nomura's Charlie McElligott warned this afternoon faces a massive $35 billion levered 
ETF dump into the close. And on the credit side, the AI CDS market has become the hottest 
trade on Wall Street, with Barclays now publishing field manuals on how to short it. 
Even JPM's Andrew Tyler, who remains "Tactically Bullish," conceded in Friday's briefing (also 
available to pro subs) that the AI trade "remains crowded with exceptionally high sentiment bars 
to clear." OpenAI just failed to clear one of them. 
The Last Chance Before The Next Last Chance 
None of this was supposed to happen on this timeline. Back in September, Sam Altman told 
Fortune that an IPO would be "ill-timed" and wouldn't happen until 2027, and the plan was 
simple: raise one last private round at a rich markup, let Anthropic test the public waters, then 
list from a position of strength next year. As we noted at the start of the month, OpenAI was 
"happy to wait well into 2027" while Anthropic was in a "far bigger rush to reload capital." 
Four weeks later, the "final chance" round is a third smaller, 11% cheaper and leaning on Gulf 
money that comes bundled with Gulf compute, the same sovereign wealth funds that we have 
long described as the AI trade's lender of last resort. Meanwhile OpenAI still has to fund some 
$1.5 trillion in compute commitments, which means this is far from its last trip to the capital 
markets before an IPO that is now at least a year away. 
The bull case is that Anthropic prices well in November, AI stocks bounce into year-end (the 
outcome most of Goldman's credit clients concede is likeliest, even though "few have positions 
on for this move") and OpenAI's next raise goes off at a premium. The far more likely outcome, 
in our view, is that the next round gets priced off whatever Anthropic trades at after its "high 
junk" IPO, and that the word "almost" disappears from the down-round headlines. As we have 
been warning since last October, the AI buildout runs on other people's money, and as of 
tonight, other people want a discount.
