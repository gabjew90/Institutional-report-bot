GLOBALcycle points to 3.7% global GDP growth in 3Q 
We re-launch our GLOBALcycle, a real-time coincident indicator to track the underlying 
strength of the global economy. Our main takeaway is that our model points to resilient 
global GDP growth, somewhat stronger than what is implied by aggregating our house 
country forecasts. Despite higher US rates, the Iran war and other well-documented 
risks, the model points to global GDP growth of c.3.7% in 3Q26, vs 3.4% implied by our 
economists' individual forecasts for the countries included in the model. Overall, growth 
resiliency remains the order of the day. 
Growth looks broad-based across regions at the moment 
For developed economies in aggregate, GLOBALcycle points to 3Q growth around 2.2%, 
broadly in line with the 2.1% implied by BofA economics country forecasts. The model’s 
signal is weaker than our house forecasts for the US, while it’s more constructive in the 
Euro area and Japan, though we would discount the magnitude of these divergences. For 
EM, GLOBALcycle indicates somewhat higher growth at 4.6% vs BofA forecasts at 4.3%, 
with a generally positive gap across most countries, including China. 
Tracking the global economy in real time 
GLOBALcycle combines mixed-frequency data to generate real-time Economic 
Conditions Indicators (ECI) by country. The ECIs can nowcast current-quarter GDP 
growth at the country level, which can then be aggregated into a global growth signal. In 
developed markets, we cover the US, Euro area, Japan, UK, Canada, and Australia. In EM, 
we track Brazil, India, China, Indonesia, Korea, Poland, Türkiye, South Africa, and Mexico. 
With this re-launch, we enhance our methodology to reduce forward-looking bias and 
better reflect the changing composition of global GDP over time. 
The model tracks global GDP closer than alternatives… 
Our GLOBALcycle correlates more closely than commonly used alternatives for tracking 
global GDP growth, such as OECD leading indicators and PMIs. In some cases, our model 
is about as accurate as the Bloomberg consensus. Yet, unlike the Bloomberg consensus, 
which is observed in practice only shortly before the GDP release, our approach allows us 
to track global growth in real time as macro data becomes available. 
… and can signal shifts in global asset prices 
By tracking the global economy, GLOBALcycle should also be expected to exhibit 
statistical relationships with asset prices. We show that a stronger GLOBALcycle 
indicator is associated with higher global equity returns, higher short-end US rates, a 
flatter curve, more elevated commodity prices, stronger EM FX, and a weaker dollar. 
Importantly, these asset price variables are not a model input. We also show that 
stronger momentum in the GLOBALcycle indicator signals higher equity prices, a weaker 
dollar, and stronger EM FX. But in rates, momentum signals higher long-end rates and a 
steeper curve.

 A timely take on the global economy 
Why do we need another measure of activity? 
A timely reading of macroeconomic activity in real time based on higher frequency 
indicators is useful for at least two reasons. First, a bottom-up approach can serve as an 
additional input in forecasting. Second, once an aggregate measure is constructed, it can 
act as a sanity check for a given set of forecasts. Does the bottom-up approach coincide 
with or differ from these forecasts? Moreover, can such an indicator provide a valuable 
signal about asset prices?  
In this report, we re-launch our model of coincident indicators to track the global 
economy in real time. The framework, based on the methodology of Auroba-Diebold-
Scotti, combines mixed-frequency data, including weekly, monthly, and quarterly 
releases, to construct country-level Economic Conditions Indicators (ECIs). These 
indicators are then aggregated to track the global economy. We also enhance our 
methodology to avoid forward-looking bias and account for the changing composition of 
global GDP over time. We intend to publish the GLOBALcycle at least quarterly. 
Filtered, high-frequency data rolled into a common signal 
Our ECIs are designed to capture a latent economic variable that represents the 
underlying state of economic activity. While this latent variable is continuous, it is not 
directly observable. Instead, it is inferred from a broad set of economic indicators 
released at different frequencies, which are jointly filtered by the model to extract a 
common signal representing current economic conditions in each country. 
Nowcasting GDP growth as data becomes available  
The ECIs are intended to nowcast current-quarter GDP growth at the country level based 
on the information available at any point in time, and can be aggregated for the global 
economy using GDP weights. In developed markets, we cover the US, the Euro area, 
Japan, the UK, Canada, and Australia. In EM, we include Brazil, India, China, Indonesia, 
Korea, Poland, Türkiye, South Africa, and Mexico. This covers close to 80% of global GDP 
in PPP (Purchasing Power Parity) terms. 
Positive/negative values for ECIs suggest growth above/below trend 
The ECIs should be interpreted as standardized z-scores. By construction, the indicators 
are centered around zero, with positive values signaling above-trend economic growth 
and negative values indicating below-trend growth. The evolution of the indicator over 
time is equally informative: an upward-sloping ECI points to an acceleration in economic 
activity, while a downward-sloping ECI suggests a deceleration. 
What our indicators have told us over the past decade 
Looking at what GLOBALcycle indicators would have signaled over the past decade is 
informative. For example, they show us the framework could provide useful signals even 
when uncertainty is high, with previous seemingly overly optimistic signals ultimately proving 
founded. We walk back through different key events of the past 10 years and conclude that 
the framework would have been helpful in tracking the cycle and cutting through noise. 
Stronger DM vs EM growth in Trump 1.0 and in the post-Covid boom 
Exhibit 1 shows the GLOBALcycle indicator for the global economy, as well as developed 
(DM) and emerging markets (EM) going back to 2015. The indicator signaled stronger 
DM vs EM growth during the first Trump administration and the trade war, as well as 
exiting from the pandemic, when China growth disappointed. Before the pandemic, the 
model was starting to show below-trend growth globally, but whatever the economic 
cycle had for us back then was abruptly truncated by the pandemic. 
Resilient growth has been the norm since 2024 
More recently, at the global level, GLOBALcycle only indicated below-trend growth after 
global central banks embarked on aggressive tightening in 2022-2023. Since 2024,

GLOBALcycle looked through Liberation Day uncertainty 
GLOBALcycle would have looked through the uncertainty shock after Liberation Day. At

From ECIs to GDP nowcasts 
GLOBALcycle signals GDP growth at the country level. Running a series of regressions of 
each country’s quarterly GDP growth on the average level of its respective ECI, we can 
generate fitted values for GDP growth. To estimate GDP growth in real time for the 
current quarter, we take the average value of the ECI since the beginning of the quarter. 
From single-country to global 
Aggregating the ECIs using PPP-GDP weights yields an aggregate ECI for the global 
economy, or our groups of developed (DM) and emerging market (EM) economies. In 
turn, we use these aggregate ECIs to nowcast global, DM and EM GDP growth in real 
time. Interestingly, the aggregate indicators become more precise than for each 
individual country, which we consider an advantage of using these measures. 
What belongs in the mix of data? 
At the weekly frequency, the inputs of the model are typically stock indexes (though not 
in the US), which we intuitively interpret as capturing the flow of news still not showing 
in most economic activity data. For monthly variables, we use variables like retail sales, 
industrial production, employment data, or exports, among others depending on the 
country. Lastly, at the quarterly frequency we look at GDP releases. 
Two methodological improvements 
Relative to our prior methodology (last published in 2022), we make two important 
enhancements. First, we now run the framework with expanding samples, to minimize 
forward-looking bias in constructing each country-level ECI. This allows for a more 
precise tracking of real-time accuracy. Additionally, we also account for the changing 
composition of Global GDP over time when constructing our global indicators, using 
rolling weights for PPP-GDP.

support growth globally. Our GLOBALcycle indicators point to global growth reaching 
3.7% in 3Q26, somewhat stronger than the 3.4% rate implied by aggregating our 
economics team’s forecasts for the countries included in the model.

GLOBALcycle can signal asset price moves 
Across asset classes, we run a series of contemporaneous and leading regressions and 
find statistically significant relationships between our GLOBALcycle indicator and asset 
prices (Exhibit 3). This link is most significant for global equities, commodities, oil prices, 
US front-end rates, and EM FX. We also find a weaker but still significant relationship 
with the DXY and US 2y10y slope. 
A stronger GLOBALcycle signals the expected moves 
We also find the expected signs in the regression coefficients. As one could expect when 
the global economy is growing above trend, the higher our GLOBALcycle turns: (i) global 
equity returns are stronger; (ii) US rates (especially 2y) sell off, and the curve flattens; 
(iii) commodities and oil pick up; and (iv) EM FX appreciates, and the dollar weakens.

Looking at alternative indicators 
There are several indicators which are frequently used to track economic activity, 
including both public data as well as other BofA proprietary signals. For instance, the 
OECD produces a series of leading indicators at the country (and sometimes regional) 
level which can be aggregated and measured against global GDP growth aggregates.  
Alternatively, we have other BofA proprietary indicators, which were not specifically 
designed to track global GDP growth, but which may indirectly be correlated with it 
given their nature. These include the Global Wave, the Truckload Diffusion Indicator, the 
Memory indicator, or the Industrial Momentum Indicator. There are also certain macro 
variables, like Korean and Taiwanese exports, which are broadly considered to be leading 
indicators of the global cycle. 
Our framework tracks GDP more closely than others 
In terms of “nowcasting” or providing an estimate for the upcoming quarterly GDP print, 
we find our framework tracks GDP more closely than many alternatives. Furthermore, it 
has the advantage of letting us view the signal in real time (as opposed to e.g. OECD 
leading indicators, which come with a lag). Exhibit 4 shows the correlations between 
global GDP growth and several alternative indicators. 
While positive correlations abound, showing most indicators by themselves contain some 
signal, we find an overall stronger relationship between global growth and our 
GLOBALcycle indicator. In fact, as we will show, for some countries our framework has a 
similar level of accuracy to the Bloomberg consensus estimate. But while we get the 
Bloomberg consensus shortly before the GDP release, we can update our ECIs in real time. 
GLOBALcycle works best for global growth aggregates 
As outlined above, we can use our framework at the individual country level as well as 
for global aggregates. In relative terms, Exhibit 5 shows that GLOBALcycle is most 
precise at the aggregate level, for global GDP growth, DM GDP growth, and EM GDP 
growth (despite EM growth being more volatile). 
We use the median absolute deviation between observed GDP growth and our nowcasts 
as measure of fit, because we want to avoid outsized shocks like Covid or the Global 
Financial Crisis from driving results. Beyond the global aggregates, the model tends to 
work better at the individual level in developed than emerging economies, but this is 
expected given the more volatile nature of growth in EMs.

The GLOBALcycle methodology 
To develop our indicators for global growth aggregates, we start by constructing single-
country ECIs for our set of developed and emerging economies. Across developed 
economies, we include the US, Euro area, Japan, UK, Canada, and Australia. In turn, for 
emerging economies we consider Brazil, India, China, Indonesia, Korea, Poland, Türkiye, 
South Africa, and Mexico. This covers close to 80% of global GDP in PPP terms. 
At the country level, we maintain employ the same methodology we have used in the past, 
based on Auroba, Diebold and Scotti (2009). We focus here on a brief intuitive explanation, 
while we provide the model equations in the Appendix: Econometric methodology. For 
each country, we use a set of country-specific variables, which are released at the 
quarterly, monthly, or weekly frequencies depending on the variable in hand. Exhibit 6 and 
Exhibit 7 show the set of variables used for each country and their frequency. 
At the weekly frequency, the inputs of the model are typically stock indexes, which we 
intuitively interpret as capturing the flow of news still not showing in most economic 
activity data. For monthly variables, we use variables like retail sales, industrial 
production, employment data, or exports, among others depending on the country. 
Lastly, at the quarterly frequency we mostly look at (prior) GDP releases. 
A set of “real time” country-level indicators 
This set of variables is constantly updated, and new information is added to the sample 
as they are released. In turn, at each point in time these variables at mixed frequency are 
“compiled” so that the framework can always make use of the latest data released, 
regardless of its frequency. In this sense, we have a “real time” methodology.

Minimizing forward-looking bias 
While we leverage our previous methodology at the country level, we make some 
important methodological changes. At the country-level, we make sure to minimize 
forward-looking bias in the construction of each country-level ECI, which makes any 
measure of fit a more accurate tracking of real-time accuracy. For this, we estimate our 
ECIs using an expanding window, such that past values of the ECI are not altered by 
future information which was not available in real time (except for data revisions). 
Constructing ECIs for global growth aggregates 
With the series of individual country-level ECIs in hand, we can then develop aggregate-
level ECIs for global, DM, and EM growth. Since each country’s ECI is a z-score, they are 
already normalized. 
We construct aggregate-level ECI by computing a PPP GDP-weighted average of 
country-level ECIs included in each aggregator (Global, DM, EM). This is economically 
intuitive, as it gives the most weight to the largest economies in the group. We use 
rolling GDP weights to account for the shifting composition of global GDP over time.

Does GLOBALcycle lead asset prices? 
We infer statistical relationships from a univariate regression between our GLOBALcycle 
indicator and the monthly return of each asset class. However, we aim to diminish 
potential concerns about endogeneity by using the first lag of our GLOBALcycle 
indicator as an instrument. 
We consider the contemporaneous and leading specifications as follows: 
 
 
 
 
Our regressions run an expanding sample from 2000 to December 2024, except that we 
exclude observations for the pandemic period 2020-2021. Exhibit 27 reports the 
regression results. Overall, we find statistically significant relationships between our 
GLOBALcycle indicator and most asset classes. 
We find statistically significant relationships… 
Across asset classes, we find the statistical relationship is most statistically significant 
for global equities, commodities, oil prices, US 2y rates, and EM FX. We also find a 
weaker but still significant relationship with the DXY and US 2y10y slope. 
… and intuitive results across asset classes 
We also find the expected signs in the regression coefficients. As one could expect when 
the global economy is growing above trend, the higher our GLOBALcycle turns: (i) global 
equity returns are stronger; (ii) US rates (especially 2y) sell off, and the curve flattens; 
(iii) commodities and oil pick up; and (iv) EM FX appreciates, and the dollar weakens. 
GLOBALcycle is significant both contemporaneously and as leading indicator 
While the fraction of variation in returns explained by GLOBALcycle is not exceptionally 
large, the statistical relationships outlined are significant. Interestingly, our findings 
suggest that GLOBALcycle contains useful information not just as a contemporaneous 
variable but also in a leading sense. While the proportion of variation explained is lower 
than the contemporaneous estimates, it remains with a strong statistical significance. 
In terms of explanatory power, Exhibit 30 and Exhibit 31 show the expanding R-squared 
and regression coefficients of the contemporaneous specification for each asset class. 
While we find a decreasing correlation over time, it is important to note that regression 
coefficients are much more stable. We have in fact a leading indicator that has some 
explanatory power on asset returns, with stable regression coefficients despite some 
decrease in explanatory power over time.

Limitations and risks 
All GLOBALcycle and ECIs values and results shown for periods other than 3Q 2026 are 
hypothetical. They were produced by retroactively applying the indicators' current 
methodology to historical data, and they do not reflect actual or live results. The 
indicators did not exist using this methodology during the period shown above.  
The indicators have inherent limitations. They were prepared with the benefit of 
hindsight. The methodology was designed or adjusted after the historical data was 
reviewed. And the results may not reflect the impact of market, economic, or other 
factors that could have affected outcomes had the indicator actually been in use.  
Historical correlations may not persist and are not indicative of, and do not guarantee, 
future results.

Appendix: Post-Covid China 
In China, we find the accuracy of fit of the ECI to nowcast growth diminished materially 
since the pandemic (Exhibit 69 and Exhibit 70). We believe this is driven by: (i) China’s 
persistent domestic weakness since the post-Covid reopening, which could signal a 
structural shift; and (ii) data on the number of employees has not been published since 
2022. Exhibit 69 shows China growth has been persistently overestimated by the ECI. 
To tackle this issue, we use a post-Covid dummy since 2022 for our growth regressions. 
Specifically, instead of doing a univariate regression of quarterly GDP growth on the 
level of the ECI, we add a variable post-Covid=1 since 2022. In turn, we run a multivariate 
regression of quarterly GDP growth on the level of the ECI, the dummy, and the 
interaction term ECI*post-Covid. Exhibit 70 illustrates how, despite being only three 
years, including the dummy materially improves the fit (which is an expanding measure). 
Because China has such an outsized weight in the EM aggregate, we take the same 
approach for EM. In fact, not using the dummy for emerging markets also leads to the 
framework persistently overestimating post-Covid EM growth (Exhibit 71). And we see a 
similar improvement in fit when including the post-Covid dummy (Exhibit 72).

Appendix: Regression for Global ECIs 
The main drawback to our approach to constructing aggregate ECIs is that we are not 
exploiting the potentially different signal-to-noise ratio of different country indicators, 
or their correlations. We propose an alternative method as a robustness check, and we 
conclude that the overall accuracy of the framework does not change materially.  
The ECI corresponding to a particular country may be better than others in terms of 
signal-to-noise. Additionally, we (unsurprisingly) find that ECIs are generally positively 
correlated across countries, as if in line with a global factor. 
Therefore, we examine a simple regression approach to take advantage of the 
correlation between and signal-to-noise ratio of each country’s ECI to construct global 
aggregate indicators, while preserving tractability in terms of interpretation. For each
