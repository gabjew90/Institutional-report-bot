China tech is breaking
China tech is getting destroyed. Indices are puking, technicals are flashing red, and oversold keeps
getting more oversold. But the real problem runs deeper. Property is collapsing, domestic demand is
broken, and China is exporting deflation to the rest of the world. Beijing needs more than cheap
money to stop the bleeding. Is this capitulation, or just another leg lower?
China tech carnage
Running out of superlatives for the China tech bear. ChiNext prints another ugly candle, closing at its
lowest level since August 2025. The index is massively stretched below its 200-day moving average,
while the 100-day has now crossed below the 200-day, a rare technical development. Oversold is one
thing. A deteriorating trend is another.

The great tech divide
The divergence between US and China tech has become extraordinary. While US tech has been riding
the AI boom, Chinese tech is getting crushed. Same global technology revolution, two completely
different equity outcomes. A brutal reminder that winning the technology race and making money for
shareholders are not necessarily the same thing.

Buying the China crash
Chinese equities have been crushed, but investors are still putting money to work. BofA reports $17bn
of inflows into Chinese equity funds in the week ended October 21, the largest since July, even as the
CSI 300 has fallen 20% since June and its technology sector has plunged 41%. Yet the bigger
positioning picture remains ugly: China equity funds have suffered roughly $177bn in net outflows
year-to-date. Dip buyers are returning, but there is little evidence yet of a sustained reversal in investor
appetite.

China's tech paradox
China is throwing everything at high-tech, but the economic multiplier is surprisingly weak. Goldman
notes that high-tech sectors now account for 38% of industrial production and 18% of investment, but
just 11% of employment. Every RMB1mn of final spending supports only around three jobs, compared
with 4.6 in property. The uncomfortable reality: China can win the technology race without generating
the jobs, household income or domestic demand needed to rescue the broader economy. A booming
tech industry does not necessarily mean a booming economy.

China's property black hole
China's property bust is even worse than expected. Goldman estimates that most major housing
activity indicators have collapsed 60–90% from their 2020–21 peaks. Property is now expected to
subtract 1.8 percentage points from GDP growth this year, with the drag only gradually fading. The
second-order problem is household wealth. With property accounting for more than half of household
assets, falling home prices continue to weigh on confidence and consumption. China's tech ambitions
may be accelerating, but the old economy remains a massive drag.

China's deflation trap
China is flashing a classic deflationary combination. Equities are falling, government bond yields have
reached record lows around 1.6%, and the renminbi has strengthened to a four-year high against the
dollar. Yet lower borrowing costs are doing little to revive the property sector, with investment still
contracting around 20% year-on-year. Hartnett describes a consumer and investor buyer strike. The
uncomfortable conclusion: cheaper money is no longer enough to revive demand. China needs more
than lower yields to break the cycle.
BofA
China is exporting deflation
China's domestic economy is struggling, but its export machine is running at full speed. BofA's Hartnett
highlights Chinese exports surging 36% year-on-year, with high-tech exports up a staggering 63%. The
problem is that this industrial strength is increasingly disconnected from domestic demand. China is
relying on foreign buyers to absorb its production, raising the risk of exporting deflation and squeezing
corporate earnings globally. The next China shock may not stay in China.

Where is the stimulus?
China's economy is still waiting for the fiscal cavalry. Crédit Agricole notes that broad fiscal spending
remained in contraction, while the deficit has continued to undershoot the budget. Meanwhile, October
high-frequency data show new home and auto sales both falling more than 20% year-on-year. The
bank expects a Q4 fiscal acceleration, with government bond issuance potentially reaching RMB4tn

versus RMB2.3tn a year ago. The question for beaten-down Chinese equities is whether the promised
fiscal impulse finally translates into stronger domestic demand, or simply another temporary bounce.
