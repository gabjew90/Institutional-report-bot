US Week In Review 
In its October meeting, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) held its policy rate 
unchanged at an upper bound of 4.0%, following a hike in September. The pause in this meeting 
follows through on the implicit suggestion from some influential voters that conditions do not 
warrant consecutive hikes and that the FOMC has time to further assess developments. The 
highlight of Chair Warsh’s prepared remarks was the plan to retain eight FOMC meetings per 
year while dedicating at least one exclusively to monetary policy strategy, without a vote or 
statement, and the remainder to policy-setting. With inflation still a problem, growth strong, and 
unemployment rate stable, a hike in December is now likely a foregone conclusion, absent a truly 
significant shift in the data or a substantial tightening of financial conditions. Read more on our 
FOMC Recap: HERE. 
Headline PCE inflation rose to 0.5% m/m and 3.6% y/y, from 0.3% and 3.4%, respectively. Core 
PCE inflation was unchanged from the revised 0.3% m/m, while edging up to 3.1% from 3.0% on 
the year, in line with expectations. Core inflation was expected to be hotter than in the prior month 
given CPI and PPI inputs and the 3/6/12month annualized rates moved up, but not dramatically 
so. After very cool numbers over the summer, the last two prints have been noticeably hotter, but 
we are cautiously optimistic that tighter financial conditions and slower economic activity will help 
the disinflationary process to resume in the coming months. 
PCE rose to 3.6% y/y in September 
Source: Natixis, Haver 
Personal income and spending also came in at consensus and continue to paint a dim outlook 
for consumers. While spending continues to hold up, incomes have diminished over time and 
therefore consumers are eating into savings, with the personal savings rate falling to its lowest 
level since Oct 2022 at 3.8%. We think that poor confidence numbers, weaker job prospects, and 
slowing wage growth will weigh on spending in the coming quarters. 
The Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index rebounded by 7.5 points to 91.7 in October, 
as consumers’ assessments of current conditions and their expectations both improved. The 
Present Situation Index rose by 8.6 points to 120.4, while the Expectations Index increased by 
6.9 points to 72.6, recovering from three months of consecutive declines. The Conference Board 
associated the improvement with lower gasoline prices between September and October, 
although gasoline prices rose over 34% on the year and confidence remained on a downward 
trajectory over the longer term. In open-ended responses, references to war and conflict declined 
for a second consecutive month but remained elevated, while concerns about prices, particularly 
food and fuel costs increased. Despite the improvement in confidence, the report indicated 
consumers planned less spending on durable goods and services, suggesting that the rebound 
in confidence may not translate into stronger consumption.

Durable goods orders accelerated in September in the preliminary report, rising 1.8% m/m, a 
touch below expectations but following a 0.1% increase in August. The gain was led by 
transportation equipment, while orders excluding transportation rose 0.5%, up from August’s 
revised 0.3% increase. Core capital goods orders, which are nondefense capital goods excluding 
aircraft, rose 0.5%, following a 1.6% increase in the previous month, suggesting continued but 
slower growth in equipment demand. Core capital goods shipments increased 1.2%, above 
expectations of 0.8% and following a revised 0.6% rise in August. Together, these readings 
suggest capital investment remains resilient, supporting our expectations for continued private 
investment growth.

What to Watch for Next Week 
Next week’s economic calendar includes the ISM Manufacturing survey on Monday, the Job 
Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS) on Tuesday, the ISM Services survey on 
Wednesday, and most importantly, the October Employment Report on Friday. Nonfarm payroll 
growth has been volatile, with a slower underlying trend. Across 2025 and 2026 to date, only one 
reading—March 2026—has exceeded the pre-pandemic average. Following September’s below-
consensus increase of 29k, consensus forecasts a gain of 55k in October. The unemployment 
rate edged up to 4.2% in September and is expected to remain at that level in October.

In addition, our attention next week will turn to the mid-term elections, with all 435 House seats 
and 35 Senate seats being contested. These elections often serve as a referendum on the 
incumbent president, and the performance of the president’s party tends to track presidential 
approval ratings. This will likely put Republicans in a challenging position as President Trump’s 
approval rating has fallen significantly since the start of his second term to around 38%, with 
support also weakening within his own party. The president’s party has lost an average of 25 
House seats in midterm elections looking at data since WW2. 
Latest election data from platforms such as 270towin.com show that the Democrats are well 
positioned to make gains and prediction markets show Democrats will take control over the House 
Securing the Senate for the Democrats, however, will be more challenging, though still possible, 
and according to some, likely. Based on the latest consensus map on 270towin.com, to secure 
majority, Democrats will have to defend 9 seats that are “safe” Democratic wins, three that are 
“likely” and another three that “lean” Democratic, while also capturing at least two of the five true 
swing states (Texas, Kansas, Ohio, Iowa, Maine). Read more on our detailed analysis in 2026

US Rates Strategy 
The first of the main events this week was the FOMC meeting, but it provided little fireworks.  We 
found little information in either the statement or the press conference (to which we suspect Kevin 
Warsh would be greatly pleased).  We do not particularly think that removing one meeting from 
next year’s schedule means one less meeting to hike is particularly relevant for current market 
pricing. Nor did he fully rule out that if necessary, the FOMC would not make a rate decision at 
said meeting, though the subject was not broached either way. Thus while the bull steepening at 
least partly on this was favorable for our views, we certainly will not make it one of our primary 
arguments. 
The second main event of the week was the core PCE deflator, which again brought little new 
information by coming out on consensus. Still, the lack of a bearish surprise here helped clear the 
way for Thursday’s rally which stemmed from AI/tech related weakness, and the ECB focusing 
on tighter financial conditions – something Warsh also alluded to and which is becoming a theme 
amidst the major central bankers.   
Warsh also went down the tighter financial conditions road in July, only to be very hawkish in 
August at Jackson Hole, when the market challenged his inflation fighting credibility.  But the 
current situation is very different, with a Warsh-led hike under the Fed’s belt, a greater amount of 
hikes priced in, expectations solid for a December hike, and market rates significantly higher.  In 
addition, the recent decline/rotation in equities, led by technology, and rising investor concerns 
around AI more broadly, is a building risk to the outlook.

Looking ahead to next week, we see little of note coming out of the refunding announcement, and 
expect unchanged auction sizes with an ongoing message of flexibility.  For the elections, while 
we see little economic shifts coming out of the midterms, at least those relevant to the broader 
fiscal outlook for the United States, we do think there are potential investment implications from 
a Democratic sweep on Congress. On a pure fiscal stance, some may argue a Democratic sweep 
could imply greater spending, and certainly we think that there will be a push to reinstate some of 
the Medicare subsidies that were removed by Trump earlier this presidency (for our full election 
preview, see HERE). 
But our current thinking centers more along the lines of AI investments, which has obviously been 
a major theme this year in a crowding out of USTs story, and the larger rise in real yields as the 
competition for capital increased in 2026.  Indeed, readers know that this was the core argument 
behind our highest 10yr yield forecast on the street from our 2026 year-ahead outlook (ironically 
a now potentially too low 4.60%).  Readers also know we have been bearish the long end for 
more than a year, but turned more neutral earlier this month, admittedly 25bps before the peak.  
Now however, we find ourselves considering if the risks to the long end from the capital/crowding 
out story is to the downside for next year.  Certainly the higher real yield story is well socialized 
at this point, but market expectations are for AI related activities, which have weighed on 
Treasuries all year, to accelerate next year.  If the Democratic Party sweeps into Congress, we 
could see additional fears of regulatory pressure on the AI-buildout – frankly as this is a bipartisan 
issue that state and local governments are already pushing back on, that pressure could come 
whether the sweep or not – but a strong Democratic performance could bring more prominence 
of the discussion to the Federal level as well.   
Critically however, Trump still holds veto power and is supportive of the AI-buildout, so we don’t 
see any major federal legislation on this front – but the tone would surely change.  Also, this is 
not an argument that these activities will particularly slow down, just that even if they do not 
accelerate, issuance, lending, etc. could come in below market expectations, providing some 
additional relief for the long end. 
To be clear, we do not think a Democratic sweep will bring in an immediate UST rally - we will 
need to see the agenda form into 2027.  But we do think this could raise a headwind to one of the 
largest stories of 2026 that helped pushed yields higher all year. And, this line of thinking comes

LatAm Highlights 
  Brazil – Unprecedented election with big consequences 
Brazil’s Lula won the election with 51.79% of the vote, following a strong gain in support between 
rounds; controversies included a court order extending voting hours and pre-election social 
spending. In a pre-election client survey, 76% identified fiscal responsibility as their main concern 
under a Lula presidency. Lula may also face legislative opposition and potential diplomatic and 
trade pressure from the US. The outlook for the BRL in the coming weeks will depend on BCB 
intervention, global conditions, and the new administration’s fiscal plans. Click HERE to read the 
full note.  
  Chile – Hawkish tone from the BCCh does not mean hikes  
Undoubtedly, the tone of this week’s monetary policy press release was hawkish. The BCCh 
wrote that “the risks to inflation have increased markedly.” Yet, in our view, the probability of a 
hike in the short term has increased only marginally. We believe the most likely timing for the start 
of a hiking cycle would be H2 2027, or perhaps 2028. Therefore, we continue to forecast the 
policy rate at 4.50% for the remainder of the year and at least the first half of 2027. 
Growth is weak. Overall GDP growth will likely be close to 0% y/y in 2026. It will rebound in 2027 
but central banks don’t ever hike on expectations of higher growth! Even if we focus exclusively 
on non-mining activity, the three-month moving average, annualized, is running barely above 1%. 
The unemployment rate started the year at 8.5% in January and is now at 9.4%. It has literally 
increased every single month. 
Core inflation is close to the target, at 3.2%, and inflation expectations—particularly two-year 
expectations—are anchored. Of course, one-year expectations have been increasing, but this 
reflects not a deterioration in the central bank’s credibility, but a repricing of oil prices. Even the 
11month expectations remain lowish at 3.4%.  
So why send a hawkish message if they are not serious about actually hiking? Well, perhaps to 
overcorrect for the dovish tone at the Sept meeting, to reflect the material change in the outlook 
for oil prices—with expectations now that prices could remain high for a prolonged period—and 
perhaps to help the beleaguered CLP. 
In short, we give little weight to the hawkish tone from the central bank. We believe the BCCh will 
keep the policy rate at 4.50% for the remainder of the year and in H1 2027.

  Colombia – BanRep to pause… maybe   
After the September 25bps hike to 12.25%, the hawkish presser and press release—which 
included one board member voting for a 50 bps increase and the Finance Minister in the hawkish 
camp, a highly unusual development—we penciled in two additional hikes, taking the terminal 
rate to 12.75%. We then hosted a webinar with board member Bibiana Taboada and fine-tuned 
our call to one more hike, possibly in October. However, after analyzing the most recent data, we 
now expect a pause on the October 30 meeting, while retaining our view that BanRep is probably 
not done tightening. We therefore expect one final hike in December, taking the terminal rate to 
12.50%. 
Forecasting BanRep is very hard, so we have low conviction in our call. That said, we have greater 
conviction that BanRep will probably keep the policy rate high for the coming months and only 
start considering rate cuts in H2 2027, depending on the trajectory of inflation and inflation 
expectations. 
The monthly GDP proxy, ISE, contracted in August on a monthly basis, posting its second 
contraction in the past three months. All three ISE subcomponents contracted in August. The 
index’s three-month moving average is now at -13.2%—ouch! Retail sales growth on a three-
month moving annualized basis is also in negative territory. Unemployment is rising as well. But 
inflation remains above 6% y/y, both headline and core, which is why we are expecting – with low 
conviction- one more hike.
