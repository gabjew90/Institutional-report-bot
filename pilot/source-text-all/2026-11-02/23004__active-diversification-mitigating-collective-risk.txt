Active Diversification: 
Mitigating Collective Risks  
 
Abstract 
The widespread adoption of passive investing has made low-cost, capitalization-
weighted index strategies the default building blocks of modern portfolios. But as 
more capital is committed to the same exposures, in the same weights, through the 
same vehicles, market benchmarks increasingly reflect these investor preferences, 
contributing to greater concentration, shared exposures, and collective risk—the 
fragility that arises when many investors own the same assets for the same 
reasons. These risks have accumulated amid rising uncertainty, precisely when 
diversification is most valuable. This article introduces active diversification: the 
intentional use of active strategies to diversify away from market-embedded 
preferences and collective risks. Drawing on portfolio theory, preference-based 
asset-pricing models, and empirical evidence, we show that active strategies can 
enhance both diversification and returns by reducing reliance on the market’s 
aggregated returns and risks. Even simple equal-weighted departures from market 
weights can significantly reduce concentration risk, while quantitative and 
fundamental strategies can improve outcomes further by incorporating forward-
looking information, market signals, and human judgment. However, collective risk 
is uneven across markets, which requires investors to consider where risk 
mitigation and return enhancement efforts are best implemented. As such, we offer 
three practical principles for implementing active diversification as an intentional 
complement to passive investing. 
 
Key Findings 
1. 
Capitalization-weighted index strategies offer low-cost market access but 
rising concentration, shared exposures, and reduced effective diversification 
increasingly tie risk to collective investor behavior rather than security 
fundamentals.  
2. 
Because market weights embed aggregate investor preferences, active 
diversification can reduce reliance on widely shared exposures, potentially 
improving portfolio resilience and enhancing returns where information 
dispersion remains high. 
3. 
Active diversification complements passive holdings by diversifying information 
sources, preferences, and uncertainty, with equal-weighted, quantitative, and 
fundamental approaches addressing different portfolio risks.

Aggregate preferences and collective risks 
Among the most consequential innovations in finance has been the development of passive market-index strategies, which 
today provide investors with broad, low-cost diversification across hundreds, if not thousands, of securities. By weighting 
securities according to market capitalization, these strategies reflect the collective information and judgments embedded in 
market prices—what is often described as the “wisdom of crowds.” Yet the same dynamics that enable markets to aggregate 
dispersed information can also produce pronounced mispricing. Market dislocations in 1929, 2000, and 2008 illustrate that 
collective behavior is not invariably rational, a point documented thoroughly in Charles Mackay’s Extraordinary Popular 
Delusions and the Madness of Crowds (1841), Charles Kindleberger’s Manias, Panics, and Crashes: A History of Financial 
Crises (1978), and Robert Shiller’s Irrational Exuberance (2000). 
Relying exclusively on capitalization-weighted index investments, therefore, means embracing both the strengths and the 
limitations of the collective preferences and judgments of market participants. This might alternatively be viewed as bearing 
collective risk – risk that arises when a broad set of investors all do the same thing. However, collective risk today goes 
beyond simply accepting the risks of irrationality, something that is already a salient feature of financial markets. With the 
proliferation of passive products, it also means accepting the unintended risks that come from increases in concentration and 
from a growing share of asset flows into passive investments. 
There is an expanding body of research that highlights several structural concerns associated with the continued adoption of 
passive investing. For example, Wurgler (2010) explains how indexing inflates the prices of, and induces excess co-
movement among, index members relative to non-members.1 More recently, Jiang et al. (2025) document that incremental 
flows into passive funds exert a disproportionate influence on the prices and idiosyncratic risks of the market’s largest 
constituents. Complementing this evidence, Brightman and Harvey (2025) find that stocks with high passive ownership exhibit 
increasing sensitivity to market-wide risk, while securities more widely held by active managers display declining market 
betas. Sorensen (2025) also argues that capitalization-weighted index exposures can mask significant embedded 
concentration and diversification risks, underscoring the need for more deliberate portfolio implementations that include 
allocations to active strategies.  
Lochstoer and Muir (2026) argue that passive investing has changed not only who owns stocks, but how markets trade. As 
assets have migrated into index funds, ETFs, and futures, investors increasingly buy and sell the market as a single 
instrument, concentrating activity in systematic exposures rather than individual securities. They find that index-based trading 
has coincided with higher market-wide volatility, greater stock comovement, and a larger share of returns driven by common 
market factors. They estimate that daily market volatility has increased by approximately 127% since the mid-1950s. Green, 
Krishnan, and Sturm (2026) also consider the impact of passive investing and model how rising passive ownership weakens 
the market’s self-correcting pricing mechanism: passive funds buy regardless of valuation, while active managers no longer 
fully offset their flows. The result is greater mispricing of fundamental characteristics, higher concentration, and increased 
price inelasticity. Their model suggests that if passive ownership rises to roughly 65% (currently at approximately 50%), 
volatility could increase sharply — a risk they describe as hiding in plain sight. 
Contrasting these views, Kritzman and Turkington (2026) caution against treating current concentration as inherently 
alarming. Based on a historical analysis using the effective number of constituents (ENC) measure, they argue that today’s 
concentration has historical precedent, little predictive power for future returns, and is less concerning because the largest 
companies are often more diversified entities and less volatile.2 Clarke, de Silva, and Thorley (2026), however, show why the 
current episode may be different. ENC measures concentration in terms of security weights, but not whether those weights 
are attached to unusually volatile stocks. Their volatility-weighted ENC analogue finds that the largest constituents now 
combine high index weights with above-median realized volatility, concentrating risk in fewer names than the capital weights 
imply — the first sustained episode of such a reversal in a century. This directly challenges the reassurance implied by the 
historical ENC analysis: today’s concentration is not merely high by weight but is also concentrated in names with high 
volatility. They further show that this pattern is not limited to U.S. technology stocks but has appeared across major global 
markets on staggered timelines and in different industries. The implication is that cap-weighted index funds may now carry 
materially greater volatility than similar vehicles a decade ago. 
These findings extend the notion of collective risk by showing how passive investing has changed both the ownership and 
behavior of markets. Price-agnostic flows increasingly bind securities to common vehicles, common factors, and common 
sources of volatility, while concentration has become more consequential because the largest weights are now attached to 
more volatile stocks. In this environment, broad market ownership alone may no longer provide the diversification investors 
expect. Addressing this challenge requires a more intentional approach: active diversification, or the use of active strategies to 
diversify portfolios using exposures that are substantively different from the market. 
However, the premise of active diversification rests on the idea that active strategies can be sufficiently different from the 
market to provide diversification benefits.3  Markowitz (1952) explained that a portfolio of railway securities would not be as 
well diversified as a portfolio that included railroad, public utility, mining, and various manufacturing securities. The 
characteristics of the assets held – whether they behave the same or differently at different times – matter for diversification 
purposes. As such, diversifying collective risk requires being different from the market index. A key differentiator for active 
strategies is the information used for security selection and portfolio construction. Understanding the information sources used 
provides important insights into the return characteristics of active strategies and, more importantly, what types of risk they 
can diversify against.

In this article, we make the case that active strategies are an increasingly important source of diversification for investors that 
can provide portfolio benefits beyond benchmark-relative returns. We first define collective risk, provide an overview of its 
relationship with endogenous risk, and discuss why it is a key concern today. Empirical evidence of collective risk in passive 
investments is also provided to further motivate the need for active diversification. We then explore two forms of diversification 
offered by active strategies: uninformed and informed. This includes a review of foundational finance theory, which points 
investors toward active investing more readily than is often assumed, as well as empirical evidence on the persistence of 
active returns in equity markets around the globe. Finally, we provide guidance on implementing active diversification in 
practice. 
Collective risk, uncertainty, and diversification 
We define collective risk broadly as risk that arises when many investors collectively do the same things. It is closely tied to 
the concept of endogenous risk in that it is a fragility that emanates from within the financial system as a function of collective 
investor behavior. Two examples illustrate the implications of endogenous risk. The first is Black Monday in 1987, when the 
failure of portfolio insurance strategies held by a large share of institutional investors induced a feedback loop of selling that 
sent the market tumbling 22.6% in one day. The second is the quantitative crisis of 2007, when many quantitative equity 
market-neutral funds were all running near-identical factor strategies that aggregated into a common risk factor. When one 
fund began deleveraging, it triggered margin calls and forced selling across the other funds. In neither case was the danger 
visible in any single portfolio; it emerged from how widely the same positions were shared. 
Collective risk is distinct from the traditional notion of endogenous risk in both scope and expression. The latter is mechanism-
specific, as in the examples above: a particular strategy or risk-management rule compels many investors to trade in the 
same direction, producing a feedback loop that amplifies price movements. It is episodic and requires a trigger. Collective risk 
requires neither. Rather than being tied to any specific strategy, it is a function of collective ownership and asset flows, and it 
accumulates continuously, eroding effective diversification whether or not a dislocation occurs. Endogenous risk is what 
happens when collective positioning unwinds; collective risk is the condition that makes such an unwind possible. 
Collective risk builds as more investors, and more money, do the same thing. When price-agnostic asset flows, driven by 
investor demand rather than any assessment of value, dominate price discovery, the securities within a strategy become 
increasingly tied to the flows into and out of the strategy itself. Risk becomes less about the characteristics of the individual 
holdings and more about the risk of collective behavior. While capitalization-weighted market index portfolios theoretically 
have the greatest capacity, capacity is not the same as price neutrality, and new passive flows are not necessarily price 
neutral.4 Recent research, cited previously, indicates that collective risks are now extending into the realm of even large 
capitalization passive strategies. 
Compounding the issue, market-capitalization weighted exposures of all varieties, along the size spectrum and across 
regions, have become ubiquitous. They are now widely considered the default investment option for implementing asset-
allocated portfolios and see increasingly regular flows from mechanical, buy-only retirement funding – purchases made 
regardless of valuation. 
A simple illustration provides insight on why this matters. Consider a single small capitalization fund with assets under 
management (AUM) approaching an estimated capacity limit. Liquidity and price impact from trades can be managed based 
on a fund’s holdings and trade impact estimates. Now consider there are 1,000 funds with identical holdings, weights, and 
AUM. The risk from the aggregate holdings of all the funds is not considered by any of the individual fund managers. No 
manager is imprudent, but all are exposed to the collective risk. This is a practical example of what is occurring across the 
passive investing landscape. 
Collective risk is therefore not a property of any individual holding, but of the investor base that owns it. When market 
participants share the same price-agnostic exposures, the same algorithms, the same vehicles, and the same buy-and-hold 
approach, their behavior ceases to be independent, and diversification depends on independence.  
If collective risk is a function of shared positioning, its consequences depend on the environment in which those positions are 
held – and that environment has grown increasingly volatile and markedly less certain. The past three decades have seen the 
proliferation of a broad range of new technologies across societies and economies. There have been massive increases in 
both computing power and the data available to extract answers to increasingly complex questions. A global data network 
infrastructure now allows ideas – both good and bad – to be transmitted virtually instantly to almost any point across the 
globe. And we are currently in the midst of an artificial intelligence revolution that is expected to have a significant impact on 
how we live, work, and continue to innovate. In short, this means that new ideas and innovations can be developed quicker, 
scaled faster, and can have a greater and broader impact in a much shorter time than at any other point in history. All of this 
produces change at a rate we have never before experienced, and change produces uncertainty.

Taken together, these dynamics make the case for diversification – and, more specifically, for diversification that is 
substantively different from the market. If the dominant exposures investors hold are increasingly the same, and if the 
environment in which they hold them is increasingly uncertain, then the value of holding something different increases 
accordingly. The remainder of this article argues that active strategies are an increasingly important source of that difference. 
We begin, in the next section, by examining the clearest empirical evidence of collective risk in practice: the dramatic increase 
in concentration within passive products themselves. 
 Passive investing and collective risk 
Perhaps one of the clearest manifestations of collective risk in passive products is the dramatic and steady increase in 
concentration in the S&P 500 in recent years. The “Magnificent Seven” stocks made headlines for their notable 
outperformance relative to the rest of the index.5 At the end of January 2026, these seven stocks represented over 30% of the 
S&P 500 index. To better understand the implications of this with regard to the diversification offered by the index, we 
translate this concentration into the ENC metric, a commonly used approximation which expresses portfolio concentration in 
terms of the equivalent number of equally-weighted stocks that would produce the same risk characteristics.6 
Exhibit 2 provides a historical perspective on the ENC for the S&P 500 Index. Prior to 2018, the ENC fluctuated from just 
below 80 stocks in early 2000 to slightly above 145 stocks in 2015 – with an overall sample average of 112 stocks. In recent 
years, however, the ENC has contracted sharply, falling to roughly 43 constituents. This level is not only well below the low 
observed during the late‑1990s technology bubble but also represents approximately one‑third of the long‑term historical 
average. For investors, this decline indicates that the S&P 500 no longer offers the breadth of diversification it once provided.  
While it would be convenient to believe that these risks are limited to the S&P 500, that is not the case. As noted previously, 
Clarke, de Silva, and Thorley (2026) show that higher concentration has appeared across major global markets, not just the 
US. This reflects earlier work by Reinganum and Blay (2024), who examined the evolution of active and passive investing 
from 1989 through 2021 and found that a substantial portion of global passive index assets under management was 
concentrated in Morningstar’s US Large Capitalization Blend category, a category commonly proxied by the S&P 500 index. 
But these stocks do not exist in isolation. US large capitalization stocks also make up a large portion of global indexes. 
Consequently, collective risks are being driven by both US and global investors. 
Another notable finding from the study is that passive index investing is generally gaining the greatest ground in core 
exposures like US Large Cap, US Small Cap, US Mid Cap, and Global Equity, which aligns with the use of passive indexes by 
investors as building blocks for portfolio construction. In Europe, there is also evidence that core global equity exposures are 
replacing regional and country-specific exposures that have, up until now, provided investors with useful diversification 
opportunities. In other words, investors’ ability to diversify away from broad market risk is slowly being diminished by collective 
investor preferences toward convenient core exposures.  
As the preceding analysis illustrates, the collective tilt toward capitalization-weighted passive vehicles has amplified 
concentration risk and diminished the effective diversification once characteristic of broad market benchmarks. Recognizing 
these structural shifts raises an important question for practitioners: how can investors reintroduce meaningful diversification 
into portfolios increasingly shaped by shared, index-driven exposures? To begin addressing this challenge, we now turn to the 
role of active approaches – starting with the simplest, information-minimal alternative, uninformed diversification.

Uninformed diversification 
In evaluating the spectrum of diversification opportunities available to investors, a natural starting point is an allocation to an 
equal‑weighted version of the desired market exposure. Equal weighting remains one of the most cost‑effective and 
straightforward approaches for enhancing diversification, as it requires the least amount of information for portfolio formation – 
even less information than what is required for implementing a capitalization-weighted index. Conceptually, this approach can 
be viewed as a form of uninformed diversification: it retains the full constituent set of the underlying market but deliberately 
avoids embedding the collective preferences reflected in market capitalizations. Each security is held in identical proportions, 
making the portfolio structurally indifferent to investor preferences. A further distinguishing feature is that the ENC of an 
equal‑weighted portfolio is equal to its number of holdings; an equal‑weighted portfolio of 100 securities, for example, has an 
ENC of 100. This makes equal weighting an intuitive and transparent complement to capitalization‑weighted benchmarks, 
particularly for investors seeking to mitigate concentration risk without introducing additional complexity. 
Exhibit 3 demonstrates just how effective equal weighting can be at increasing diversification. It begins with a stand-alone 
S&P 500 index holding as of January 31, 2026, when its ENC was estimated at 43 stocks. Progressively larger allocations to 
an equal-weighted S&P 500 portfolio, shown on the x-axis, steadily increases the ENC of the combined exposure. An 
allocation of 43% to the equal-weighted portfolio returns the holding to the historical average ENC of 112 stocks shown in 
Exhibit 2. 
Equal weighting offers a simple diversification alternative, but the approach introduces specific biases. In underweighting the 
largest holdings in the capitalization-weighted index – precisely those most likely to be affected by collective risk – it 
overweights the smaller holdings, producing a size bias relative to the market index.7  While these biases and other 
differences are commonly understood and might even be desirable, they are not necessarily intentional. Investors seeking 
greater control and intentionality in managing their risk exposures and in targeting diversification opportunities will need to 
employ various types of information and, most importantly, human judgment. This leads us to the idea of informed 
diversification. 
Informed diversification8 
The use of information and human judgment in portfolio management is a surprisingly controversial subject. Many academics 
will point to passive capitalization-weighted index funds as the only rational way to invest, while at the same time working 
tirelessly to advance novel portfolio construction methods to produce portfolios that better address some specific risk measure 
or concern or that are more efficient across some dimension or other. Far from being passive, these efforts effectively 
espouse new active approaches to investing. Similarly, many market strategists will readily denounce active management as 
folly, while in the very next breath provide their opinion on how portfolios should be actively re-allocated to capitalize on the 
latest news, changes in interest rates, or some recent economic report. To be fair, passive investing is a remarkably 
convenient and structurally low-cost way to invest. There are trivial information costs for security selection and maintaining the 
capitalization weights of a specific market index requires minimal trading. However, this should not diminish the value of using 
different types of information to support investment decisions.  
Informed diversification relies on the use of different types of information along with human judgment or, more concisely, 
active management. As such, it will be useful to revisit foundational finance theory to understand just how information and 
judgment are considered in the context of portfolio diversification and how different types of information can be used to 
address both measurable and unmeasurable uncertainty, which is highly relevant for mitigating collective risk.

Portfolio theory, the market portfolio, and informed diversification 
Markowitz (1952) introduced portfolio theory, which was based on theoretical notions of how investors should face making 
decisions under uncertainty. It provided a method for converting an investor’s beliefs about the future performance of a set of 
securities, including estimates of expected returns, risks, and correlations, into “efficient” portfolios. Central to this is the notion 
of having the “right kind” of diversification for the “right reasons”. He was essentially proposing informed diversification.     
Arguably, the initial theoretical argument supporting passive indexing stems from the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) by 
Sharpe (1964), which asserts that the capitalization-weighted market portfolio is mean-variance efficient and therefore optimal 
for all investors. This conclusion, however, depends on several simplifying assumptions that are inconsistent with real-world 
investing. Perhaps the most problematic of these assumptions are that (1) all investors have the same (homogeneous) 
beliefs, (2) all investors have access to unlimited borrowing at the risk-free rate, and (3) all investors are rational (mean-
variance) investors or, rather, market risk is the only investor preference that matters.  
First, investors do not share the same (homogeneous) expectations; instead, they form differing (heterogeneous) views about 
expected returns, volatilities, and correlations. As Lintner (1969) showed, while markets can aggregate these diverse beliefs 
into a single set of asset prices, heterogeneous expectations imply that investors will necessarily hold efficient portfolios that 
differ from the market portfolio. 
Second, the assumption of unlimited borrowing at the risk-free rate is unrealistic. In practice, no investor has access to 
unlimited borrowing, much less at the risk-free rate. Theoretical work by Fama (1976) and Markowitz (2005) later confirmed 
that without unlimited borrowing (or shorting), the market portfolio ceases to be efficient. This further weakens the notion that 
the market portfolio is universally appropriate; instead, investors must construct portfolios aligned with their own beliefs, 
constraints, and objectives. 
Finally, CAPM’s argument that market risk is the only risk that matters is no longer supported by the broader asset-pricing 
literature. Notably, Ross’s (1976) Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) introduced a multi-factor framework in which expected 
returns are driven by multiple systematic forces. Although APT is derived from arbitrage considerations rather than investor 
demand, it paved the way for models recognizing that risk and return cannot be captured by a single market factor. 
Together, these theoretical developments underscore that the market portfolio is neither empirically nor conceptually 
adequate as a universal benchmark. Investors with differing expectations, constraints, and preferences rationally arrive at 
different optimal portfolios. Arguably, more than 70 years of academic finance literature has returned us to where Markowitz 
started – informed diversification.  
Markets, preferences, and asset pricing 
Asset pricing theory has evolved since the introduction of the CAPM and is increasingly focused on how investor preferences, 
beyond traditional risk factors, influence market prices. Ibbotson et al. (1984) introduced the New Equilibrium Theory (NET), 
arguing that investors view each asset as a bundle of characteristics that include both risk and non-risk elements such as 
taxes, liquidity, and information costs. Prices therefore reflect collective preferences and aversions for these characteristics. 
Fama and French (2007) later reinforced the need to move beyond classical assumptions, particularly the idea that all 
investors share identical beliefs about expected payoffs. They argued that real-world markets reflect both heterogeneous 
expectations and differing tastes, meaning that investor preferences unrelated to risk can materially affect asset prices. 
Building on these insights, Ibbotson et al. (2018) introduced the Popularity Asset Pricing Model (PAPM), which incorporates 
investor tastes into asset pricing under the simplifying assumption that expectations are homogeneous. The PAPM asserts 
that assets with popular or desirable characteristics tend to trade at a premium, reducing their expected returns, whereas 
unpopular assets – those with characteristics investors generally dislike – must offer higher expected returns to compensate. 
Idzorek et al. (2024) then expanded the model further by introducing the more realistic assumption of heterogeneous 
expectations, making the PAPM one of the most comprehensive frameworks for understanding how both preferences and 
beliefs influence pricing. Under this framework, popularity is not a single factor; it is multidimensional. It can reflect attributes 
such as brand, reputation, competitive advantage, liquidity, or behavioral perceptions, all of which become priced regardless 
of whether they are rational or risk-based. 
Exhibit 4 presents this framework across different investor scenarios considering both investor preferences for an asset 
(popularity) and information – whether the investor is informed or misinformed about the future prospects of a security. When 
assets are popular, they have a lower expected return (−P) because their desirability bids up prices, while unpopular assets 
have higher expected returns (+P). When we consider information, expected returns then depend on both popularity and 
whether the investor is informed or misinformed. The lowest returns occur when an investor is misinformed about a popular 
asset (−P/−I), while the highest returns arise when an informed investor identifies an unpopular asset with strong future 
prospects (+P/+I). This insight is central to diversifying with active strategies. Passive index strategies tend to overweight 
popular stocks and underweight unpopular ones, potentially reducing exposure to higher-return opportunities implied by the 
PAPM framework. This suggests that actively diversifying away from the aggregated preferences of the market can be 
beneficial. It also makes clear that extracting the greatest benefits from active diversification requires the skill to recognize 
undervalued, unpopular assets before the market does.

Preferences in the real world 
There is an old aphorism that says “In theory, there is no difference between theory and practice. In practice, there is.” As we 
saw with the CAPM, theory is often based on a highly simplified representation of the real world. While it is true that PAPM 
theory provides a more intuitive representation of asset pricing, is there any evidence that investor preferences actually affect 
real-world asset returns? To bolster their PAPM argument, Ibbotson et al. (2018) provided empirical evidence that stocks with 
the weakest brands, worst reputations, or with the least competitive advantage often outperform stocks of highly admired, 
strong brands. However, these are simply specific instances of investor preferences. What about the impact of preferences in 
aggregate?  
Disentangling the aggregate effect of investor preferences on asset returns is inherently challenging, but a reasonable proxy 
for a “preference” factor can be constructed. We begin by assuming that the capitalization weights of the market portfolio 
reflect the aggregate preferences of investors. The next step is to construct a “preference-neutral” market portfolio – one that 
retains market exposure while neutralizing the influence of any single security or characteristic embedded in market weights. 
Because capitalization weights embed these preferences, a security’s market beta captures its exposure to them. Portfolio 
weights can therefore be set so that every security contributes equally to total market exposure, where each contribution is 
the product of a security’s weight and its market beta. The resulting portfolio retains market exposure while minimizing 
concentration in the most preferred, or popular, names. Subtracting these preference-neutral weights from market weights 
isolates the residual – a preference portfolio.10 The market is thus separated into two components: a preference portfolio and 
a preference-neutral market portfolio. The former serves as our proxy for a preference factor, allowing us to assess the 
influence of investor preferences on asset returns.

S&P 500 constituent universe. Stocks are ranked on their trailing one-year beta to the preference factor and allocated equally 
across three portfolios – High, Medium, and Low Preference – with holdings equally weighted and rankings updated monthly. 
Results are shown for two samples: the full period, from 1/1/1970 to 12/31/2024, and a more recent period, from 1/1/2000 to

heightened geopolitical uncertainty. These episodes reveal what average correlations often obscure – the value of diversifying 
the information sources used for investment decision making.

Uncertainty, Information, and Active Diversification  
As we have seen, benefiting from active diversification requires investors to be different from the market. In benchmark-
relative terms, this means diversifying away from the influence of aggregate investor preferences and accepting the 
uncertainty that comes from holding securities that lack the implicit affirmation of other investors. Most investors would rather 
bear the market risk that everyone experiences than risk being “wrong and alone,” as Kritzman (1998) explained; if the market 
performs poorly, at least they are not alone. The common thread among less popular securities is that their characteristics 
introduce greater uncertainty about future outcomes. This points to perhaps one of the most important investor preferences of 
all – predictability of returns. Ceteris paribus, investors prefer less uncertainty to more. Addressing that uncertainty requires 
both information and judgment. 
In this context, Frank H. Knight’s foundational distinction between risk and uncertainty remains highly relevant for investment 
practice. Knight (1921) defined risk as measurable uncertainty, where outcomes and their probabilities can be estimated. By 
contrast, Knightian uncertainty, or unmeasurable risk, arises when neither outcomes nor probabilities are knowable. It is within 
this domain of true uncertainty that the potential for economic profits emerges. Under this framework, bearing measurable, 
market‑wide risk earns market beta, while accepting idiosyncratic, unstructured uncertainty creates the conditions for 
differentiated returns and meaningful diversification. 
Quantitative investment processes primarily operate in the realm of measurable risk. They are well suited to identifying and 
exploiting patterns in large, structured datasets and therefore play an important role in active diversification. However, as 
Friedrich Hayek (1945) emphasized, much of the most valuable information in an economy is decentralized, dispersed, and 
inherently difficult to quantify. This information is embedded in individual experiences, expectations, and judgments; it is 
unevenly distributed, costly to acquire, and often absent from formal datasets. As a result, it is not fully reflected in market 
prices, creating opportunities for active managers who can interpret qualitative information and context. 
Hayek (1974) further warned that applying the methods of the physical sciences to social systems risks a “pretence of 
knowledge.” In an investment setting, this cautions against overreliance on purely statistical models when key drivers of future 
outcomes cannot be precisely measured or averaged. Many variables that influence asset prices—such as corporate culture, 
regulatory inflection points, or shifts in social behavior—require judgment-based interpretation. It is precisely in these areas, 
where information is ambiguous and asymmetrically distributed, that fundamental research and human judgment can add the 
greatest value. 
Grinold and Kahn’s (2000) Fundamental Law of Active Management provides a useful framework for linking these ideas. The 
information ratio of an active strategy depends on both the quality of insights (the information coefficient) and the quantity of 
independent decisions (breadth). In practice, managers can pursue breadth by applying modest insights systematically across 
many securities or pursue depth by applying high-conviction insights more selectively. Understanding whether a manager’s 
process relies primarily on scalable quantitative signals, deeper qualitative judgment, or a combination of both is essential for 
assessing the nature and reliability of the active diversification provided. 
Taken together, these perspectives highlight that active returns arise from different types of information operating across 
different types of uncertainty. Quantitative approaches are most effective where risks are measurable and patterns can be 
reliably extracted, while fundamental investing is most valuable where quantifiable signals end – at the intersection of 
ambiguity, judgment, and true uncertainty. These ideas are not merely theoretical; they have clear implications for active 
diversification in the real world. Naturally, this leads to an empirical question: does the use of different types of information – 
quantitative and judgment‑based – actually translate into effective stock selection in practice? 
The value of information and judgment 
The most direct evidence on this question comes from research on quantitative stock-selection models, examined in the 
sections that follow. Before turning to the models themselves, it is worth noting why they matter for collective risk. Each model 
incorporates distinct information sources – different lenses through which securities are valued. To the extent that active 
strategies draw on information not already embedded in the capitalization-weighted consensus, diversity of information is itself 
a form of diversification. The evidence that follows should therefore be read not simply as a test of whether stock selection 
“works,” but as a test of whether differentiated information can deliver returns, and diversification, distinct from the market. 
Recently, Guerard et al. (2024) revisited several long‑standing stock selection models introduced in Guerard and Takano 
(1992), originally developed with Harry Markowitz, Nobel laureate in economics. The authors evaluate four models with 
incremental additions to information content. The first is a regression‑weighted composite based on historical fundamentals – 
earnings, book value, cash flow, and sales – along with their relative measures. The second model replaces historical 
earnings with forecasted earnings, while keeping the remaining variables unchanged. The third model builds on this 
framework by adding an equally weighted composite of analyst earnings forecasts, forecast revisions, and breadth measures. 
The fourth and final model further augments the specification by incorporating momentum as an additional explanatory 
variable.

These findings demonstrate that quantitative and fundamental investing approaches are not substitutes, but complements. 
Quantitative models provide structure, consistency, and scalability, while fundamental insights – particularly those grounded in 
informed expectations about future outcomes – add incremental and differentiated information that can provide diversification 
opportunities. These results not only confirm the effectiveness of systematic stock‑selection models for active stock selection 
but also highlight the enduring value of human judgment for diversifying collective market risk. 
Active diversification in practice 
We have cited recent research and presented empirical evidence of collective risk arising from the broad adoption of passive 
strategies. As a counter to this risk, we have suggested that active strategies, when incorporated intentionally, offer a way to 
diversify not just securities, but information sources, preferences, and different types of uncertainty. We then supported the 
case for active strategies with evidence of attractive active opportunities across global equity markets. 
Active diversification, however, is not achieved simply by deviating from a benchmark. It requires being different from the 
market for the right reasons. Drawing on the theoretical foundations of portfolio theory, insights from asset‑pricing models that 
incorporate investor preferences, and empirical evidence on information dispersion and return predictability, we offer three 
practical principles for implementing active diversification effectively: 
1. 
Allow for Active Diversification Opportunities 
The first and most fundamental principle is that active diversification requires the flexibility to be different from the market. This 
is best framed as diversification away from collective investor preferences rather than as a pursuit of benchmark-relative 
excess returns. Capitalization-weighted indexes embed the market’s aggregated views about popularity, desirability, and 
perceived safety, systematically overweighting the securities investors most prefer and underweighting those that are less 
popular. Mechanical flows into passive index-tracking strategies then compound these overweights. 
Securing that flexibility is more difficult than it may appear. Clarke, de Silva, and Thorley (2026) explain that in an environment 
characterized by high volatility in the most concentrated securities, benchmark-relative mandates face an additional 
constraint. Tracking-error budgets limit how far a manager can deviate from the index. However, underweighting a large, 
volatile constituent consumes far more active risk than an equivalent underweight in a stable one. When the largest weights 
are also the most volatile, the positions a risk-aware manager would most like to reduce are the ones that exhaust the active 
risk budget fastest. They estimate that some managers today may have roughly a third less deployable active risk than in the 
2010s. The opportunity set has not narrowed, but much of it now sits beyond the reach of tightly constrained mandates. This 
suggests that risk budgets may need to widen if benchmark-relative managers are to retain a realistic capacity to add value. It 
also points to a more fundamental remedy: expanding the exposures against which active risk is measured. 
Practical implementation therefore begins with increasing breadth, by moving investment policy away from narrow silos 
defined by size, region, or style. Such silos impose unnecessary constraints and blur the line between policy and strategy. 
Broadening exposures addresses the active constraint on two fronts: a wider opportunity set dilutes the influence of any single 
concentrated position, and it increases the degrees of freedom available for expressing active views. Investment policy should 
therefore focus on broad and distinct market exposures, extending active management opportunities beyond tightly 
constrained benchmark-relative mandates. Exhibit 8 provides an example of how investors might move from commonly used 
policy silos to broad market exposures.

2. 
Diversify Information Sources 
The second principle is that effective active diversification requires diversifying information, not simply holdings. Portfolio 
theory emphasizes holding assets that behave differently. Assets behave differently because they respond to different 
information at different times. The stock-selection results presented earlier reinforce that insight: forecast quality improved 
with each additional layer of information incorporated. 
In practice, this means avoiding reliance on a single type of information or investment approach. Investors are likely best 
served by incorporating both quantitative (or systematic) and fundamental strategies alongside their passive holdings to 
reduce dependence on any single information source or assumption about asset prices. Fundamental data, analyst 
expectations, and price‑based signals each capture different dimensions of risk, return, and uncertainty and can offer 
important diversification benefits.  
Quantitative and fundamental strategies offer more intentional and risk managed approaches to active diversification. 
Investors pursuing these informed approaches should be sure that the strategies used are sufficiently active to provide a 
meaningful benefit. Look for managers that are willing to be “wrong and alone” with the understanding that relative 
performance is likely to have a wider range of outcomes. Benchmark-hugging active strategies will likely only increase costs 
without providing the intended benefits.  
Selecting sufficiently active strategies, however, is only half the task; how much is allocated to them matters just as much. As 
Exhibit 3 showed, restoring a concentrated S&P 500 holding to its historical breadth required roughly a 43% allocation to an 
equal-weighted complement; token allocations are unlikely to alter a portfolio’s collective risk profile. Active allocations should 
therefore be sized against an explicit tracking-error budget, with realistic allowance for implementation frictions – fees, 
capacity constraints, and, perhaps most importantly, the governance discipline needed to hold strategies through inevitable 
periods of underperformance. Strategies abandoned at the point of greatest discomfort incur the costs of active management 
without delivering its diversification benefits. 
Active diversification is basically an argument for informed intentionality. Investors should be explicit about which information 
sources they are allocating to and why. Capitalization-weighted index strategies provide the aggregated information set 
available to all investors. Quantitative, or systematic, strategies focus on measurable risks and statistically reliable patterns, 
making them well suited to diversifying measurable risk and extracting returns in normal markets. Fundamental strategies 
apply human judgment to unmeasurable uncertainty, which is most valuable where expectations diverge and during market 
dislocations, when specialized knowledge allows managers to act as informed, opportunistic buyers. 
This has long-standing support in the literature, and in how that literature has evolved. Waring et al. (2000) argue that 
manager selection is a portfolio construction problem in which active return is optimized against active risk within an explicit 
risk budget, producing a natural mix of index, low-risk active, and traditional active strategies determined by how much active 
risk an investor is willing to bear. Sorensen et al. (2004) extend Grinold and Kahn’s Fundamental Law beyond the skill and 
breadth of a single information source to the combination of multiple alpha sources, showing that blending quantitative and 
fundamental forecasts can improve the information ratio beyond what either achieves alone. Their conclusion is that the 
advantage lies in the diversity of the information used, and that weights across sources should be set with the same discipline 
applied to a mean-variance problem. Together, the two studies suggest that the value of active allocation depends not only on 
how much active risk is taken, but on the range of information that risk draws upon. 
As with many aspects of investing, active diversification is an exercise in both art and science. Investors should inform 
allocations using quantitative methods along with stress-testing to account for turbulent market conditions. However, these 
approaches rely on measurable risks. Diversifying against the unmeasurable uncertainty is less precise. Intuition and 
judgment about the characteristics of different active strategies across market regimes and the roles they are expected to play 
will likely weigh heavily on final allocations.

Allocate for both Risk Mitigation and Return Enhancement 
The third principle is that active diversification is about both return and risk. Because collective risk is uneven across markets, 
active diversification objectives should be aligned with the characteristics of each. The empirical evidence presented earlier 
shows that the greatest active returns are found in emerging markets, developed markets ex-US, and US small- and 
mid-capitalization stocks – segments characterized by greater information dispersion and lower passive ownership. 
Concentrated markets with high passive flows, by contrast, are more exposed to collective risk and make benchmark-relative 
investing more challenging. 
Practical implementation should therefore begin by identifying which markets offer the greatest return enhancement 
opportunities and which are most exposed to collective risk. Investors should be selective in where they deploy active risk and 
for what objective, sizing active allocations in proportion to the return and risk characteristics of each market or segment 
rather than spreading them evenly across all. 
Where return opportunities are greatest, the emphasis should be on return enhancement, with risk mitigation as a secondary 
objective. Segments with the widest information dispersion warrant a larger share of the informed active budget. Fundamental 
approaches that draw on expertise, specialization, and human judgment are well suited to benefit from that dispersion, though 
they generally carry the greatest active risk. Effective active diversification therefore means taking active risk where it is most 
likely to be rewarded and managing it by pairing fundamental strategies with more risk-controlled quantitative approaches and 
with market index strategies. 
Where passive ownership and concentration are greatest, the emphasis should reverse: risk mitigation first (both collective 
and absolute), return enhancement second. Equal-weighted strategies that systematically rebalance away from the most 
popular securities offer an effective starting point. Quantitative strategies that provide exposure to specific and measurable 
risks and to novel datasets are particularly useful as more manageable absolute risk diversification tools, extracting returns 
from statistically reliable relationships. These can also be paired with fundamental approaches that offer opportunistic benefits 
during market dislocations. By tying active exposures to specific objectives in different markets, and by balancing return 
enhancement against risk mitigation across them, investors can align diversification efforts with portfolio objectives – 
improving return potential while increasing diversification across both favorable and adverse market regimes. 
These three principles provide a coherent framework for putting active diversification into practice. Allowing for active 
diversification opportunities creates the necessary flexibility; diversifying information sources ensures that flexibility is used in 
an informed way; and allocating for both risk management and return enhancement directs active risk to where it is most likely 
to provide benefits. None of this requires abandoning passive investing or benchmarks. Rather, it asks investors to be 
intentional about the exposures they hold, the information they rely on, and the objectives they pursue in each market 
segment.  
Conclusion 
The evolution of modern portfolios has been shaped by an extraordinary expansion of passive index investing, which delivers 
low-cost market exposure and broad access across asset classes. Yet diversification implemented solely through 
capitalization-weighted benchmarks comes to reflect collective investor preferences rather than independent sources of risk 
and return. Rising concentration, growing passive ownership, and shared information sets have introduced forms of collective 
risk that traditional portfolio construction frameworks do not consider. These overlapping exposures have built up against a 
backdrop of rising volatility and uncertainty – precisely the environment in which diversification is most needed. 
Active diversification is not about rejecting passive investing; it is about recognizing the risks that its widespread adoption has 
introduced in an environment now shaped by collective behavior. By diversifying away from market-embedded preferences, 
drawing on multiple information sources, and deploying active risk selectively across markets, investors can reintroduce 
meaningful diversification into portfolios dominated by widely held exposures. Equal-weighted strategies demonstrate that 
even simple, uninformed departures from market weights can materially reduce concentration risk, while informed approaches 
show how forward-looking expectations and human judgment can improve diversification outcomes further. 
The theoretical foundations of portfolio theory and modern asset-pricing models reinforce these conclusions. Markets 
aggregate information efficiently, but they also embed aggregate preferences, beliefs, and behavioral biases. The market 
portfolio is therefore neither fully efficient nor universally optimal, and investors with differing expectations, preferences, 
constraints, and objectives must construct portfolios that reflect those differences. Indeed, because market prices – and 
market efficiency – are themselves a function of investor preferences, active opportunities can exist even where prices are 
assumed to be efficient. The empirical evidence presented here bears this out: diversifying away from the market’s most 
popular exposures has historically offered both return and diversification benefits. 
Translating these insights into practice rests on three principles. First, investment policy should allow for active diversification 
opportunities by moving away from narrow silos toward broad market exposures. Second, investors should diversify 
information sources, pairing quantitative strategies suited to measurable risks with fundamental approaches that apply human 
judgment to unmeasurable uncertainty – and size those allocations meaningfully, since positions too small to shift a portfolio’s 
overall exposure will not change the risks it actually bears. Third, active risk should be deployed deliberately, emphasizing 
return enhancement where information dispersion is greatest and risk mitigation where passive ownership and concentration 
are highest. 
Ultimately, the role of active strategies extends beyond benchmark-relative performance. Implemented thoughtfully, active 
diversification helps investors manage a broader set of risks, including those that arise when everyone owns the same assets 
for the same reasons – making active strategies, whether equal-weighted, quantitative, or fundamental, important 
complements to passive investing in modern portfolios. In an investment landscape increasingly defined by common holdings 
and shared preferences, intentional differentiation, grounded in distinct information, can be a source of portfolio resilience.
