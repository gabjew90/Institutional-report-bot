Global markets faced continued volatility in October as investors grappled with 
mounting doubts around the AI trade, French sovereign stress, and geopolitical 
tensions that pushed energy prices higher. Brent crude (+13.6%) extended its rise 
amid concerns over renewed US-Iran escalation, while European natural gas rose 
above €100/MWh for the first time since 2022. Treasury yields reached post-2002 
highs early in the month but ended up posting their best monthly return of 2026 
(+2.3%), as sovereign bonds rebounded amid deteriorating tech sentiment and 
dovish-leaning decisions from the Fed, ECB and BoJ. Equities were mixed, with the 
STOXX 600 (+2.0%) and the equal-weighted S&P 500 (+5.0%) gaining, but the 
Nasdaq (-5.3%) suffering its worst month since March 2025 as concerns around AI 
capex intensified. European credit and European banks also struggled as OAT-Bund 
spreads neared their 2011 peak.
Month in Review - The high-level macro overview
October began with France at the epicentre of renewed European sovereign stress, 
against a backdrop of already elevated global yields. While the pressure stabilised 
for a week or two, renewed political unrest then pushed the 10yr OAT-Bund spread 
to 174bps on October 22, its highest since the peak of the euro sovereign crisis in 
November 2011, while the French 10yr yield reached a post-2002 high of 5.07%. 
The pressure also spilled over into Italian and Spanish spreads, European banks and 
the euro. The euro fell to its lowest levels against the dollar since spring 2025, ending 
the month at 1.1160. And the EURO STOXX Banks index had its worst total return 
month since 2023 (-11.4%). European credit also struggled, with HY spreads seeing 
their biggest monthly widening since 2022 (+65bps to 370bps).
The performance of European assets also wasn't helped by the renewed rise in 
energy prices. Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and increased disruption to grey 
shipping through the Strait of Hormuz kept supply conditions tight, while investors 
moved to price in a rising risk premium amid reports that the US administration may 
resume military strikes against Iran after the midterm elections on November 2. 
That means Brent crude (+13.6%) ended the month at $117.64/bbl, within touching 
distance of its 2026 high, and extending its YTD gain to +93.3%. And in Europe, 
front-month natural gas futures spiked by +43.6% to €103.94/MWh, their highest 
level since the 2022 energy crisis.

AI infrastructure would ultimately generate adequate returns. The pressure

That said, the tech sell-off masked equity resilience and rotation under the surface. 
So while the headline S&P 500 lost -0.9%, the equal-weighted version gained 
+5.0% in October, reversing its September decline. European equities also proved 
relatively resilient outside of France and periphery, with the STOXX 600 returning 
+2.0%, with Germany's DAX (+2.5%) leading among the major DM indices.
Sovereign bonds meanwhile staged a sizeable turnaround over the course of the 
month. Early in October, high energy prices and resilient activity pushed the US 10yr 
yield to a post-2002 high of 5.31%, while the 30yr reached 5.67%. But the 
subsequent deterioration in risk sentiment restored Treasuries’ traditional safe-
haven role, with the 10yr ending the month at 4.93%. This came despite strong US 
inflation data, with September headline CPI rising +0.65% and core CPI +0.33%. 
Central-bank meetings late in the month reinforced the rally, as the Fed, ECB and 
BoJ all remained on hold and left markets less convinced that the full extent of 
tightening previously priced would materialise. Treasuries consequently returned 
+2.3% in October and bunds +1.9%, their strongest monthly performances of 
2026, though both still have negative returns YTD, at -0.5% and -0.4% respectively.
Finally, emerging markets saw some notable volatility in October. EM sovereign 
bonds recovered amid the pullback in global yields but EM equities struggled. This 
included Chinese equities, with the Shanghai Composite (-6.1%) extending its YTD 
loss to -7.4%, as stronger Q3 GDP growth of +4.5% y/y was offset by still soft 
domestic demand and the global technology sell-off. Brazil saw an especially 
dramatic reversal after Lula’s surprise election victory on October 25, with the 
Bovespa falling -9.9% in local-currency terms and -14.3% in USD terms.
Which assets saw the biggest gains in October
Oil: With ongoing disruption to flows out of the Middle East and concerns about 
possible US-Iran re-escalation after the US midterms, oil prices neared their 2026 
highs, with Brent (+13.6%) and WTI (+15.2%) extending their YTD rise to +93.3% 
and 81.3% respectively.
Core sovereign bonds: After a terrible September, most sovereign bonds saw a 
positive month in October (with the notable exception of OATs). Treasuries (+2.3%) 
and bunds (+1.9%) posted their best monthly returns of 2026 so far, with gilts also 
up (+1.7%).
Which assets saw the biggest losses in October?
Tech stocks: Rising doubts about the AI trade pushed the Nasdaq (-5.3%) to its 
worst monthly return since March 2025, while the Philly semiconductor index sunk 
by -15.3%. The chips sell-off also left the KOSPI (-16.2%) as the weakest country 
index globally, while Alphabet (-18.7%) and Meta (-15.2%) were among the notable 
single-name underperformers after disappointing Q3 results.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay 
fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases 
in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the 
maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in 
inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to 
receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets 
holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency 
conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are 
also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by 
indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in 
emerging markets.  The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables 
they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating 
coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. 
Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk. Options on swaps 
(swaptions) the risks typical to options in addition to the risks related to rates movements.
