THINK Ahead: 3 ways the US midterms
could reshape the global economy
From data centres and oil prices to tariffs and strained public finances,

consequential than you think
Next week's US midterms could have significant economic consequences
Whisper it, but are Tuesday’s US midterms a bit of a non-event? Perhaps, but that risks
overlooking some potentially significant economic consequences.
America’s vote has created surprisingly little conversation in recent weeks. No doubt, that is
because the range of plausible outcomes has narrowed. The Senate race still hangs on a knife
edge, but the House looks increasingly likely to fall to the Democrats. Clean sweep or not,
Washington is heading for divided government.
That sounds like a recipe for inaction. But gridlock could increase the importance of areas
where the president still has room to act. Here are three areas where I think the midterms
could have global consequences...

First, the Middle East.
My colleague James Knightley recently argued that a split or Democrat-run Congress could
pull Donald Trump in two directions: emboldening him to double down on the Iran war, while
inviting greater pushback from lawmakers.
Gridlock makes domestic legislation harder, increasing the temptation to focus on areas where
executive power is greater, including foreign policy. At the same time, a Democrat clean sweep
would give the party more confidence to challenge the White House on Iran, potentially
including over war-related funding.
But the new Congress does not take office until January. The more immediate question is what
happens after Tuesday. Is Washington making a final push to lower oil prices? JD Vance’s letter
to Iran this week was the clearest sign of diplomacy in some time. Or is the US preparing for
fresh strikes once the midterms are out of the way and the political cost of higher gasoline
prices becomes less pressing? Reports of additional US military capabilities in the region point
in that direction.
Markets are clearly more worried about the latter. But if escalation does not materialise, our
commodities team thinks some of the sizeable risk premium embedded in energy prices could
unwind.
That matters enormously for Europe. Eurozone inflation could plausibly hit 4% next week, and
that’s biased to go even higher so long as natural gas prices keep rising. If nothing else,
expect elevated inflation until next summer, as I argued last week. The Bank of England is
poised to hike next week for precisely this reason.

Second, AI.
I don’t need to tell you how central this is to the US growth story. This week’s strong GDP data
reminded us of that. AI-related investment is rising by more than 20% year-on-year, while
CapEx away from the build-out is still struggling for momentum.
But it won’t have escaped your attention that AI is becoming a political issue too. Voters are
increasingly vocal about the rapid build-out of infrastructure, particularly data centres. An eye-
catching poll from The Economist showed Democrat support surging this year among people
living within 2km of a data centre, a group it estimates at around 12 million Americans. That is
a serious number of American voters.
The immediate federal consequences may be limited. Permitting battles are often state and
local issues, while attempts by Congress to unwind federal tax incentives or pro-construction
policies could run into a presidential veto. But the midterms look like a marker for just how
politically contentious the AI build-out could become ahead of the next presidential election in
2028. Given how dependent US equity markets have become on the AI story, and how shaky
certain corners of the AI trade are starting to look, this whole issue matters well beyond
Washington.

Third, fiscal policy and tariffs.
America’s fiscal trajectory looks ugly on almost every metric. The federal deficit is around 6%
of GDP, and there is little evidence of appetite in Congress for meaningful consolidation. Last
year’s tax bill was an obvious opportunity to change direction. It was missed. The divided
government makes another serious attempt over the next two years even less likely.
It’s a stark contrast with the UK and Italy, both of which released sensible budgets this week,
and both look markedly better on public finances right now. Italy in particular is often tarred
with the same brush as the US on fiscal policy, despite running a primary surplus.
Back to the US, though, because fiscal gridlock could push attention back towards trade policy.
Remember tariffs? After the Supreme Court struck down Trump’s emergency tariffs, temporary
10% blanket rates ultimately gave way to a more permanent structure under Section 301. The
issue has since slipped remarkably far down the agenda.

But like foreign policy, trade is another power the president can wield unilaterally without
Congress. Could we see the administration go after its trading partners – Europe in particular –
with higher tariffs once again?
In theory, this would matter a lot for inflation and the Fed. Chair Kevin Warsh has repeatedly
noted that roughly half of the inflation basket is running above 3%, and our analysis suggests
tariffs explain a decent chunk of that. Then again, the refunds from the tariffs struck down in
court have started rolling in and have further to run. Over the summer, as the chart above
shows, those rebates exceeded the gross revenue from tariffs.
So yes, Tuesday evening might indeed be a quiet night for financial markets. But what
happens next could make for a much noisier 2027.

THINK Ahead in developed markets
United States (James Knightley)
Midterm elections (Tue): It will be a very busy week in the US, with the midterm
elections on Tuesday and the October jobs report on Friday as the highlights. All 435
House of Representatives seats and 35 of the 100 Senate seats are up for election on 3
November. Worries about the cost of living and the impact of AI on job security are
regularly cited as key factors driving sentiment, while support for the conflict with Iran
has wavered as gasoline prices climbed. Opinion polls currently suggest the Democrats
lead by around eight percentage points nationally, and President Trump’s own approval
rating has hit new lows, leading to Republican Party concerns about a demotivated
electoral base. If the Democrats gain control of Congress, this will hugely constrain what
President Trump can do, leaving him to focus on trade and foreign policy. See our full
preview for more details.
Jobs report (Fri): This is the first of two jobs reports ahead of the next Federal Reserve
policy decision on 9 December. The low-fire, low-hire narrative around the labour market
holds and we expect jobs growth to come in at around 75,000 with the unemployment
rate remaining at 4.2%. Fed officials have made it clear that their focus, for now, is on
getting inflation back down to the 2% target, so it would require a very soft outcome,
potentially even a negative jobs print, to meaningfully move market pricing for that
December meeting. Markets are currently putting an 80% probability of a rate hike,
which looks fair.

Eurozone (Bert Colijn)
Inflation (Wed): It’s the week after the ECB presser where President Christine Lagarde
emphasised a lot of important data points she’ll collect before a December decision. The
October inflation rate was one of them, and it’s up on Wednesday. With energy prices
still rising and a lagged effect of natural gas on consumer prices, we expect inflation to
remain around the 4% mark in coming months. The most important piece of
information is everything outside of energy, though, as the ECB is nervously looking at
second-round effects emerging. So far, the effects have been negligible, and it will be
key to see whether October starts to see some movement in core inflation and food
prices.
