In-Line
Powering AI → powering everything: We raise our power consumption growth 
estimates  by 10% to ~36tn units by 2030. Data centers and semiconductor fabs led 
the last three years of consumption upgrades;  we now see the electrification story 
broadening out to the industrial (allshoring of supply chains) and residential (space 
cooling) sectors – a pivot  underappreciated by investors. The Three A's –  Agentic, 
Allshoring and Aircons – make consumption more versatile, powering the next leg 
of margin upgrades and multiples for equities. But pricing power is shifting, creating 
the next leg of winners (power generators and power grid operators) as power 
spreads expand by ~15% by 2028e, while equipment suppliers see pushback from 
consumers and policymakers on prices post-two years of cost inflation. 
Power markets are changing face again and we see upside surprise not just in 
consumption but also in changing consumption patterns as the need for stable 
demand accelerates beyond data centers to industries. Fossil fuel-led generation is 
becoming a bottleneck in power systems as inflexible nuclear generation rises, 
battery deployment deflates renewable economics in merchant markets,  and access 
to powergrids becomes critical, making distribution/transmission equities re-rate. 
Asia will play a critical role in absorbing 15GW of the US power shortfall;  Europe 
and Japan to see sustained higher consumption after years of decline. 
How to position? We identify 36 winners (See Exhibit 13 ) across power 
infrastructure –  dependable power generators, midstream gas pipeline operators, 
grid companies, and energy storage suppliers. We are selective on equipment 
providers as pricing power shifts to infrastructure owners. Equities are discounting 
power consumption growth at a third below our base case and close to mid-cycle 
consumption levels of the past decade. Changing power consumption patterns  and a 
corresponding  step up in margins will be key to differentiating leaders from 
laggards, with  ~14% upside to 2028e Street earnings for our preferred names.

Executive Summary
• Powering AI has added US$1trn in market cap across the supply chain since 
2024.  Powering everything could add US$0.5trillion in market cap by 2027, 
we estimate, with a new set  of winners emerging - fossil-based power 
generation companies, gas pipeline players, powergrid operators and 
integrated energy players. 
• Outside China, we forecast growth in  power consumption to triple to 23trn 
units of power consumed, while Asia's power consumption could grow 20% 
faster than the US.
• Asia will  require 84GW (~4x increase over 2025) of compute by 2030e as 
it absorbs ~15GW of spillover of data centers and semiconductor 
fabrication needs from the US and the Middle East. Agents to drive a 1.6x 
rise in power consumption globally by 2030. Thailand has seen 
commitments of US$25bn in DC investments. Singapore, India and 
increasingly Japan to surprise on AI infrastructure power needs.
• Demand for dependable power is rising, with 60% of Asia DC capacity 
powered by coal and natural gas. Energy storage will work with renewables 
to power a third of DC capacity.
• Data centers to underwrite 20mntpa of LNG imports into Asia by 2030, 
equivalent to one  fifth of new exports of US shale.
• Global power prices have risen ~20% YoY in 2026 and policymakers are 
tightening the regulations around DCs.  Tiered power pricing is helping to 
drive consumer bills lower.
Power markets are changing face again and we see upside surprises not just in 
consumption, but in changing consumption patterns.  This is  leading to accelerating growth 
in transmission network capex, expanding margins for power generators, increasing 
demand for fossil fuels and a "bring your own power" thematic.  Much is changing in how 
we assess power market pricing, generation mix and quality of earnings for equities. We 
raise our above-Street global power consumption growth estimates by a further 10%, 
implying 15% faster growth than the IEA forecast ( Exhibit 17 ). This is creating the next leg 
of likely winners in the power supply chain (see Exhibit 13 ).  Power generators, power grid 
operators and the gas pipeline producers that power the power plants will see  pricing 
power increase, agents get smarter, weather patterns change and onshoring of 
manufacturing accelerate. We see  merchant power spreads rising by ~15% on average 
amid a backdrop of rising global LNG supply and tighter power markets.  
The world of electricity is transforming at an unprecedented pace. Europe and Japan 
are seeing steady growth in power consumption after nearly two decades of challenges, 
US power consumption is inflecting and Asia (ex China) is seeing 1.5x higher power needs 
than during the last decade. Investment in the global power sector reached a new high of

US$1.6trn in 2025, and consumer power prices rose ~15%. The age of electrification, 
amplified by AI, is reshaping power markets, and, as happened in the 1990s, power prices 
look set to rise further. Agents will consume  a trillion more units of power every year,

Hyper growth in consumption will redefine the power story as spare capacity reduces 
by one fifth globally for the rest of the decade. As data centers make power fungible 
beyond country borders and industrial demand picks up locally due to allshoring, we 
estimate ~36trn units of power will be consumed by 2030e i.e.,15% faster than street 
estimates ( Exhibit 17 ). These power needs will increasingly require more coal, energy 
storage and natural gas, not just renewables. The impact of data centers will be reflected 
in global natural gas markets,  potentially underwriting 20mntpa of LNG imports into Asia 
by 2030, equivalent to a  fifth of new exports of US shale, while also using 0.7TWh of 
battery storage by 2030 and 1bn tons more of  coal ( as large as the current size of the 
global seaborne market for coal). 
Power is fungible for the first time as US data center power needs move to Asia: We 
estimate the US will see a shortfall of 43GW in  DC capacity by 2028 and 80GW by 2029, 
while China hyperscalers plan to grow 2-3GW of capacity outside China annually. Agents 
will be a US$30trn annual market until 2029, according to Morgan Stanley's US Internet 
team, and growth in token consumption is growing faster than agentic revenues, making 
power a  critical bottleneck.  Our bottom-up work suggests ~15GW of US DC shortages 
( Exhibit 29 ) could spill over to the rest of Asia with about 3GW of power capacity being 
used for semi-conductor fabs that are built in ASEAN/India/Korea. Thailand, India and Japan 
are seeing more DC commitments. Thailand is also seeing China hyperscalers underwrite 
capacity. All three countries have ~30GW of spare power capacity as they ramp up natural 
gas, coal and nuclear output. 
Bring your own power: We estimate behind-the-meter power demand will grow to 8GW  
in Asia and 47GW in the US by  2030 while power grids remain constrained. Distributed 
inference pushes compute closer to the end user; distributed power allows those nodes to 
operate with greater resilience and independence from constrained centralized 
infrastructure. Australia, Malaysia and Thailand are increasingly encouraging data centers 
to get their own power, with  wholesale power prices rising 8% in the past year. Power 
generators like Gulf Development, Adani Power and Sembcorp are providing data centers 
behind the meter as they expand outside  the US and China. China, which is a well supplied 
power market, is encouraging data centers to run on renewable power and is seeing rising 
deployment of energy storage. Asia refiners, chemical plants will add ~4GW of behind the 
meter capacity by 2030e, we estimate. 
Solving the grid challenge - batteries and beyond: Asia is seeing a significant shift in 
electricity load curves. Duck curves are reshaping into double-humped camels in India 
( Exhibit 37 ), Thailand is seeing peak load shift to evenings due to EVs ( Exhibit 36 ), the 
Australia load curve keeps changing shape, while rising nuclear supply is enabling  Japan's 
powergrid to absorb variability in its renewables load. In Asia, we see US$1trn in new grid 
investment through 2030e ( Exhibit 39 ), with data center investment helping fund part of 
the upgrade as tiered power pricing with higher prices for DCs cross-subsidize consumers.

Powering AI, Aircon & Allshoring: What's Priced in?
AI Agents will need 2trn units of additional power  to create  the US$30trn in annual 
revenue that Morgan Stanley is forecasting until 2030: the US$11trn Asian industrial 
supercycle that we forecast will require 2trn more power units, while the 0.2bn new air 
conditioners/heat pumps will require 0.6trn more power units by 2030 - about half of this 
power requirement is not yet priced into power generation companies, the  gas midstream 
supply chain or the coal producers that will power these agents. However, the upstream 
power supply chain and renewables are pricing in a lot of the growth opportunity. We see 
14% EPS upgrades across our preferred  OWs in 2028, leading to US$0.5trn in value 
creation.
Asia's incremental power consumption to be 4x US incremental power consumption 
over 2025-2030e.  We see DCs needing 84GW of power by 2030, with a ~70% utilisation 
rate for DC capacity ( Exhibit 26 ). China is a key contributor to the need for DC power. 
India, Southeast Asia and increasingly Japan have been consuming more power this year 
than in the  past three years (on a run rate basis, which adjusts for weather shifts).  The 
increased consumption stems not just from DCs but also from new semiconductor 
manufacturing, which  is raising baseload needs. Air-conditioning and industrial demand 
have shifted the profile of power demand in these markets. In our view, stock multiples 
for power generators are not pricing in ( Exhibit 8 ) this stronger growth (5% CAGR over 
2025-30 in Asia, i.e., 200bps above the five-year averages) from agents & space cooling 
until 2030 as the opportunity from behind-the-meter power supply for AI DCs grows and 
the industrial investment supercycle expands the TAM and  quality of returns for the  
power generators. We estimate Asia's power consumption growth will be 2.5x that of 
Europe+UK and 1.2x that of the  US over 2025-30 ( Exhibit 17 ). 
However, the debate regarding powering AI is shifting to regulatory headwinds and 
how consumers are being impacted by the rising need for dependable power.  The power 
system is seeing demand from not just AI, but aircons and the US$11trn industrial 
investment supercycle (energy security, defence and onshoring outside of AI investments). 
On average, Asian wholesale power prices have risen by 20% in the last year, with power 
spreads expanding for generators in most geographies by 2-3x ( Exhibit 52 ). We see 
slower rates of investment by both grid and power generation companies along with a 
more variable supply of renewables going forward, leading to inflation in power costs for 
consumers. Globally, power utilities have seen a stalling in investment growth after 
significant increases since 2024. 
The need for air-conditioning/heat pumps will add ~40bps to power consumption 
growth ( Exhibit 43 ), we estimate, and could be as large as the incremental power needs 
of  data centers,  with ~50mn air conditioners to be installed annually in India, China, 
Southeast Asia and Japan while Europe sees heat pumps leading to 20% faster growth in 
power needs. For context, we note that  during the summer, cooling accounts for up to 
40% of India's peak power consumption.
 The dispersion in equity performance between equipment and power generation

cost of equipment by refurbishing existing equipment or delaying the deployment of new 
capacity. We do not see this shift in the dynamics of pricing power as priced into fossil

Thirty-Six Trillion Units of  Consumption: It's Electric  
We estimate ~36trn units of power will be consumed by 2030, well in excess of IEA 
forecasts, with consumption of power outside China growing  3.3x faster than during the 
last decade ( Exhibit 16 ). This is placing increased pressure on the power system –  as 
power prices rise, the need for grid deployment accelerates and costs continue to inflate.  
In addition, shifting load patterns are making electron delivery to end consumers even 
more complex. Increasing amounts of nuclear power (inflexible supply) and renewables 
(variable supply) is adding to power prices and the need for behind-the-meter supply, 
especially as the cost to source  stable power from the grid rises. AI will contribute ~26% 
of incremental power consumption over 2026-30e, we estimate ( Exhibit 18 ).
The power-demand surprise is broadening across regions and end-uses: We increase 
our growth forecast for  global electricity consumption by 10% to ~4.2% CAGR over 2025-
30e ( Exhibit 17 ), but see quite a few changes in both fuel and country level consumption 
and generation dynamics over that period – with the largest upward revisions in India, the 
US, Australia and Southeast Asia and we also include behind-the-meter power demand in 
our estimates. China and India continue to add demand from already high bases, while the 
US, Europe, Japan and Australia are seeing a more meaningful re-acceleration versus the 
previous decade as industrial investment, reshoring and electrification raise underlying 
power intensity. Asia remains the center of the cycle, accounting for ~64% of incremental 
global electricity demand through 2030 ( Exhibit 21 ). The fuel mix is also increasingly 
shifting to fossil fuels (~50% share by 2030 slightly above our earlier estimates) in our 
forecasts as dependable power supply and behind-the-meter supply for natural gas-based 
power rises as corporates and economies focus on energy security. ( Exhibit 19 )
Agents are lifting the duck curves higher, cooling is reshaping power consumption 
curves, and industrial demand is increasingly going behind the meter: Although AI is 
emerging as an important source of high-load-factor demand, the broader power cycle is 
underpinned by manufacturing expansion, changing industrial composition and 
electrification. In Asia, we estimate industry will account for ~43% of incremental 
electricity consumption through 2030, or ~2.7x the contribution from data centers, with 
China and India driving the majority of the increase.  ( Exhibit 48 )
Cooling is emerging as another significant demand driver, particularly across lower-
penetration emerging markets. The IEA estimates that cooling can account for 
approximately one-third of India's electricity consumption on hot summer nights, 
compared with just over 10% on an annual basis, illustrating its disproportionate impact 
on load profiles. As AC penetration rises, this growing concentration of demand during 
evening and nighttime hours increases requirements for dependable generation, storage 
and grid flexibility. 
We are more constructive than the IEA on the need for dependable power: Our global 
demand forecast is ~60bps above the IEA through 2030 ( Exhibit 17 ), with the largest 
divergence in the US and Australia, while China and India are broadly aligned. In our view, 
the upside risk is greatest in markets where data centers are being layered on top of 
stronger industrial, commercial and cooling demand, increasing the risk that consumption 
grows faster than generation and grid capacity can respond.

Data Centers: A Quantum Expansion
The need for power and the strain on power systems have been well highlighted by 
chipmakers:
"We're tracking every single gigawatt of land power shell around the world, literally 
everything on the planet." – Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang September 10, 2026
"We want to be sure... It's a judged number based on not just supply of memory 
wafers, but supply and availability and readiness of power sites" – Broadcom CEO 
Hock Tan: September 8, 2026
Power requirements for data centers are accelerating globally as AI adoption broadens 
and newer rack architectures increase compute density. We estimate Asia's data center 
power requirements to more than triple by 2030 to ~120GW, with the region adding 
~80GW of capacity ( Exhibit 24 ). However, the US is seeing an even sharper inflection as 
next-generation AI racks require significantly more absolute power despite substantial 
improvements in compute efficiency. Our estimates now imply US data center electricity 
consumption (excl shortfall) will more than triple to ~1.3trn units by 2030. ( Exhibit 24 )
Asia's power markets are tightening at the same time. Data centers could account for 
~15% of ASEAN electricity consumption by 2030, ~6% in Australia, ~5% in China and ~4% 
in Japan ( Exhibit 25 ). Power-demand growth is accelerating in Malaysia, Japan is reversing 
its multi-decade decline in electricity consumption, and Australia, Taiwan and Singapore 
are seeing tighter power markets. ( Exhibit 17 )
We believe natural-gas-fired generation – one of the fastest sources of dependable 
incremental power ( Exhibit 51 ) – will increasingly complement renewables and 
batteries as data center demand scales. Falling global gas prices should improve the 
economics of gas-fired generation, in our view, while rising renewable penetration 
increases the requirement for dispatchable power and storage to balance the grid. In 
China, data centers should source a greater share of electricity from renewables, but this 
can indirectly increase requirements for flexible generation elsewhere in the system.

AI Needs Power; Grid Needs Time and Batteries  
The power curve is changing shape across most markets, creating higher margins for 
generators that can supply flexible capacity and for batteries that can discharge quickly 
( Exhibit 37 ). However, it also reduces returns for renewables. Data centers, together with 
batteries, energy storage systems and EVs, should make daily power demand more stable, 
albeit at higher levels. This will come at a cost to consumers, as power grids will need to 
become significantly more flexible.
One common trend is the growing spread between daily peak and trough demand. The 
steepness of the demand curve continues to increase across markets, driven not only by 
renewable penetration but also by rising industrial power consumption. The changing 
shape of the power curve is adding to inflationary pressure in electricity prices as grid 
investment requirements increase.
We estimate Asia will require around US$1trn of power-grid investment through 2030 
( Exhibit 39 ) to accommodate rising electricity demand and greater system flexibility. We 
believe data centers will partially fund these investments by absorbing higher power 
tariffs, while also increasing the attractiveness of behind-the-meter power supply 
arrangements for generation companies.

Space Cooling: Growth Is  Hot  
Cooling demand is rising faster than the broader power system: Our bottom-up 
estimates suggest global space-cooling electricity consumption will rise from ~2.9trn units 
in 2025 to ~4.1trn units by 2030 ( Exhibit 43 ), a ~7% CAGR, which implies , ahead of 
global power demand which grows 4.2% a year over the same period. Cooling therefore 
rises from ~10% to ~11% of Asia’s electricity consumption, adding almost ~0.6tn units of 
annual demand by the end of the decade, while Europe will also need similar amount of 
power for heat pumps making up 5%+ of total power consumption share. China remains 
the largest cooling market in absolute terms, but growth is increasingly coming from 
lower-penetration economies. We estimate India’s cooling demand will nearly double to 
~280bn units by 2030, while Vietnam, Malaysia and the Philippines should also grow at 
high-single to low-double-digit rates. 
The pressure on the power system is greater than the annual TWh figures suggest: The 
IEA estimates cooling accounts for only around 10% of global electricity consumption but 
~30% of peak power demand, with ~1,400GW of cooling-related peak load in 2025. In 
India, cooling demand accounted for 40% of peak power demand in 2026 due to El Niño 
conditions. Globally, cooling electricity consumption has already increased by ~50% since 
2015 to ~2.9trn units, and we estimate incremental cooling demand (excluding data 
centers) could be as large as incremental electricity demand from data centers. Asia is 
particularly exposed as rising AC ownership compounds higher utilisation rates. The 
implication is that cooling adds not only energy demand, but also disproportionately 
increases the need for peak generation, storage and grid capacity.
Asia: We forecast Asia's installed AC stock will increase from ~1.1bn units today to ~1.4bn 
by 2030.  We also assume running hours rise ~3.4% a year as temperatures increase. On 
that basis, we estimate cooling electricity demand rises from ~1.5trn units in 2025 to 
~2.1trn units by 2030 (~7% CAGR). This would increase cooling's share of total Asian

With grid capacity increasingly constrained and political scrutiny of data center 
development intensifying, we continue to favor onsite power providers as the most direct 
beneficiaries of near-term infrastructure bottlenecks. We highlight BE, INIO, and SEI (all 
OW) as key beneficiaries, given their ability to accelerate time to power while potentially 
mitigating concerns around grid availability and consumer electricity costs. We remain 
constructive on the broader grid buildout, particularly SRE and AEP (both OW), where 
substantial transmission investment and potential upside to existing capex plans provide 
attractive long-term growth opportunities. We also highlight NEE and VST (both OW), 
whose integrated data center infrastructure platforms offer a differentiated approach to 
capturing incremental load growth.
Onsite power offers a path around near-term bottlenecks. As grid interconnection 
delays persist and data center backlash increases, we believe behind-the-meter (BTM) 
solutions are increasingly well positioned to support the next wave of data center 
development. By reducing reliance on traditional grid infrastructure, onsite generation can 
offer a faster path to power while helping developers navigate mounting political and 
regulatory scrutiny. We therefore reiterate our preference for BE, INIO, and SEI (all OW) as

Reshoring adds a second leg to the BTM power opportunity. While data centers remain

including US Reshoring Flame Has Been Lit For US$10tn Opportunity and CoTD: What

Grid access remains a key bottleneck for AI infrastructure development, but

China
China benefits from the 'powering everything' cycle, but it is not a US-style power 
shortage trade. We expect overall electricity demand to grow at around 5% CAGR 
towards 2035, primarily driven by electrification. The investment question is less whether 
China can produce enough electricity nationally than how it delivers dependable power at 
the right time and price. We prefer defensive power generators with low cost, flexible 
generation capability and resilient cash flows. Yangtze Power is our most preferred stock 
in this thesis. 
Demand - modest industrial growth; data centres and EV charging are bright spots. We 
forecast industrial electricity demand to grow at a 3.7% CAGR over 2025-30, below 
overall power demand growth. However, we see stronger growth coming from EV 
charging and data centres. We expect data centre power demand at ~664TWh by 2030, 
or about 5% of national consumption, compared to 2% in 2025. EV charging is another 
structural driver as EV penetrates - electricity use by charging services grew 55.4% YoY to 
131.5bn kWh in 9M26. This represents 1.6% of the overall power demand, compared to 
0.1% five years ago. 
Supply - structural transition continues; flexibility and interconnection to be key. Wind 
and solar, alongside hydropower and nuclear, should continue taking share from fossil 
generation, in our view. Renewable generation represented approximately 17% of China’s 
total power generation last year, and the government’s target is 20% by 2030. New coal 
capacity need not translate into higher coal-fired output: it can provide backup and 
balancing while utilisation falls. In addition, national adequacy of power capacity does not 
eliminate local grid bottlenecks or weather-related tightness. Therefore, we see priority 
around cross-provincial transmission, distribution upgrades and flexibility, which may 
require accelerated grid investment spending in the coming years.

LatAm  
Power demand growth remains supported by traditional drivers across LatAm, driving 
~3-4% CAGR 2025-30. Unlike some developed markets, where electricity demand has 
historically been relatively flat and data centers are emerging as a major source of 
incremental consumption, LatAm's power demand outlook remains largely driven by 
economic growth, industrial activity, population growth, electrification and the rising 
penetration of electrical appliances. These structural factors should continue to support 
electricity consumption across the region, although growth rates will vary by country 
depending on economic conditions, industrial development and energy policies. As a 
result, we expect power demand to continue expanding even without a meaningful 
contribution from data centers.
Data centers could accelerate demand growth, despite not being the main driver. The 
rapid expansion of AI, cloud computing and digital infrastructure is creating a new source 
of electricity demand globally, with LatAm increasingly attracting interest from 
developers given its renewable resources, relatively competitive energy costs and 
proximity to major markets. However, unlike in the US, where large-scale data center 
investments are becoming a key driver of incremental load, we expect the contribution in 
LatAm to remain relatively modest compared with traditional demand drivers, at least 
through the end of the decade. Under the MS Bull Case scenario, we estimate that DC 
capacity would add ~11GW of new electricity demand by 2030 (~7.9 GW in Brazil), 
equivalent to ~4% of total power demand, from virtually zero today. This would add 
~80bp to the overall power demand CAGR in 2025-30. More details in Latam Data 
Centers - Aiming for a Bull Case (22 Apr 2026).
A steepening duck curve and rising curtailment are increasing the need for flexibility. 
The rapid expansion of intermittent renewable generation across LatAm, particularly in 
Brazil and Chile, has led to increasingly pronounced duck curves, with growing midday 
oversupply followed by steeper evening ramps as solar generation declines. At the same 
time, renewable curtailment has increased significantly, exceeding 20% of potential 
generation in some recent months in Brazil. These trends highlight the growing mismatch 
between when electricity is generated and when it is needed, reinforcing the need for 
flexible resources to shift energy across hours, reduce curtailment and support system 
reliability.
