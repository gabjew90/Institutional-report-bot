Q&A on a Potential GPIF Allocation Shift 
 
 
Q1: What is GPIF and why are investors focused on it? 
Japan’s Government Pension Investment Fund (GPIF) is a quasi-governmental institution 
that manages and invests the reserve funds of Japan’s public pension system. It holds 
nearly $2tn worth of assets, roughly half in foreign assets and invested mostly on an 
unhedged basis. As we have written, a shift in allocation by GPIF, the single biggest 
unhedged foreign asset holder outside of the MoF, would be most meaningful for the 
Yen. A shift by GPIF would also likely lead to similar reallocations by other investors; 
private pensions tend to follow GPIF allocation shifts, and Lifers may raise their hedge 
ratio if USD/JPY continues to move lower. 
Q2: How does GPIF derive its asset management objective? 
The Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare (MHLW) supervises GPIF. Every five years, 
MHLW conducts an extensive examination of the national pension system.1 The MHLW 
then decides on the real return on the portfolio that GPIF should aim for over the next 
five years to deliver a sound, stable and safe public pension system for the next 100 
years. The current real return target is “nominal wage growth plus 1.9%,” and it has 
generally been raised over time. The return target in the 1st medium-term plan 
(FY2006-2009) was nominal wage growth plus 1.1%. The 4th medium-term plan target 
was nominal wage growth plus 1.7%. Once the MHLW decides on the real rate of return, 
it sends an order to GPIF to formulate a basic asset allocation that would realize that 
return. Hence, the basic portfolio is reviewed every five years. GPIF is allowed to review 
and adjust the basic portfolio in the interim period, should market and economic 
environment deviate significantly from the time the basic portfolio was formed. But it 
must follow the rigorous pre-set verification process (Exhibit 1). 
1
In the extensive examination, MHLW estimates future demographics, pension premiums collected from the 
working age population, and the payout to pensioners. It also runs simulations on the pension balance over the 
next 100 years, based on various economic and market environment assumptions.

Q3: What are the data to watch for early signs of an asset allocation shift? 
As we have previously noted, the first signs of any pivot by GPIF should appear in the 
“trust accounts” flows in the monthly International Transactions in Securities (ITS) report 
(as reduced foreign asset purchases or outright sales).2 Comparable data on domestic 
asset flows by domestic investors are generally available on a much less timely basis, 
leaving that side of any rotation more challenging to track in “real-time.” The Flow of 
Funds (FOF) report is the most reliable, but it is released quarterly and with a 5-week lag. 
The Japan Securities Dealers Association (JSDA) releases the timeliest domestic flow 
data (monthly with a roughly 3-week lag), but with narrower scope than the FOF data.3 
Some have also asked about the usefulness of JGB auction results. While it naturally 
offers an aggregate picture of JGB demand from the 19 primary dealers, there is no

Q4: Are there other sources apart from the official data that might signal a shift? 
Yes, mainly the topics of discussion at GPIF’s regular board meetings, as well as any 
comments from GPIF officials or the administration to the media. Board meetings usually 
take place monthly, with special meetings as needed; there were 14 meetings in fiscal 
year 2025 and 13 meetings in fiscal year 2024. The agenda for each meeting gets 
released roughly six business days later, while the minutes of the meeting get released 
3-4 months later, though they do not disclose everything discussed. From those, we 
know that the unscheduled meeting in August was unusual, as well as the “Discussions of 
the Project Team on the Review/Verification of the Basic (Policy) Portfolio” listed on the 
agenda (more in Q6 below). We also know that there was no discussion of the portfolio 
listed on the agenda of the September meeting, but that does not necessarily rule out 
that a review is underway. 
Moreover, the verification process says that PT will conduct regular monitoring, and “as a 
result of regular monitoring, if changes in various indicators are observed and there is a 
possibility that the investment environment has undergone a significant change from 
that assumed at the time of formulation, the PT shall report to the Board of Governors.” 
Most importantly, the government’s reported focus on encouraging investment in 
domestic assets has raised the odds of an asset allocation shift, and recent comments by 
GPIF’s CIO in favor of exploring more active investment in equities, suggest that a broad 
review may indeed be in progress.

The earliest possible timeline for implementation seems to be Spring 2027. In the 
current medium-term plan announced in March 2025, GPIF introduced an “annual 
verification” to affirm or change the current asset allocation by the end of every fiscal 
year. 
The challenge for markets, however, is that there has only been one example of the 
annual review so far, and there was limited evidence in real-time that the review was 
underway. There appeared to be only one report to the Board of Governors on the 
verification process in September 2025, listed as “Procedures for Verification of the 
Policy Asset Mix” on the meeting agenda, followed by simply “Verification of the FY2025 
Policy Asset Mix” on the March 2026 meeting agenda. 
The annual report (released in Japanese in July 2026) subsequently outlined the process 
in more detail: “First, in FY2025, the reviewed verification methods […] were reported to 
the Board of Governors. Furthermore, as a result of Verification 1, GPIF concluded that 
the investment environment had not necessarily undergone a significant change from 
that assumed at the time of formulation. Therefore, it was decided that there was no 
necessity to conduct Verification 2, which is verification of updates to expected returns, 
or to conduct a review of the Policy Asset Mix.” 
Q6: What was the timeline for other recent reviews? 
Ahead of the current medium-term plan, where no changes were made, the working 
group met 20 times (April 2022 – March 2025) and the Board discussed it 6 times (July 
2024 – March 2025). The agenda listed those discussions with the Board as “Regarding 
the next basic portfolio.”  
Perhaps the most comparable review process would be the October 2014 allocation 
adjustment, when GPIF made a significant shift, cutting the domestic bond allocation to 
35% and raising all other allocations, with most of the reallocation going towards both 
domestic and foreign stocks.4 
The October 2014 shift occurred on a faster timeline than planned. MHLW’s once-every 
5-year examination was released in June 2014, and at the same time, the MHLW 
requested a frontloaded review of the basic portfolio. GPIF reviewed and changed the 
basic portfolio in October 2014, six months earlier than the planned transition to the 3rd 
medium term plan in April 2015. While it is possible that the review process was already 
underway, the fact that GPIF accelerated the process by six months suggests that these 
changes are doable ahead of schedule, especially with the government’s involvement.

simply start by shifting its holdings towards the outer bounds. It could also just allow

The less supportive macro backdrop argues against any big immediate shift in allocation. 
But some reallocation can still make sense for two reasons. First and foremost, the 
current allocation was first set in the 4th medium-term plan (FY2020) based on market 
conditions that no longer exist. GPIF notes that the historically low policy rate globally 
and especially the low rate in Japan justified the shift to 25% equal allocation across 
assets, as well as the decision to maintain the same targets in the 5th medium-term plan 
(FY2025). Second, multi-decade highs in Japanese yields means that a 10-year JGB at 
2.83% yields more than nearly half of its bond holdings (as of March 2026)—or nearly 
40% of GPIF’s foreign bond holdings and 98% of its domestic bond holdings. 
Q10: What are the implications of a potential shift for the Yen? 
We have argued since September that the higher probability of a shift in flows relative to 
earlier this year, combined with a faster pace of tightening by the BoJ (which has relieved 
some of the downward pressure on JPY), means that valuation can play a bigger role in 
JPY performance as the policies that have justified the Yen’s undervaluation for years 
appear to be shifting in a more constructive direction. That leaves short USD/JPY a much 
more attractive portfolio hedge now than it has been in recent years, especially with the 
likelihood of greater AI-related volatility that could push both equities and yields lower 
together—a market regime that typically benefits the Yen most.
