Global Equity Views
Themes and implications from the Global Equity Investors Quarterly
In brief
•	 Corporate profits continue to boom around the world. Although the pace  
of revisions is cooling, we see almost 30% earnings growth this year and 
20% in 2027. We have rarely seen such a strong profit cycle. 
•	 The artificial intelligence (AI) investment boom is of course the biggest 
driver, and we see no letup in spending here (although rising funding  
costs bear watching). Profit growth outside the AI boom is robust, but  
rising interest rates are a potential drag here too.
•	 Our investors are more neutral than positive on the outlook for market 
returns. Given strong earnings growth, we recommend staying invested 
and diversifying by style and geography to help manage the concentration 
risks created by AI enthusiasm. After significant underperformance, higher 
quality stocks may well deliver solid returns.

Taking stock
Our research team’s near-term profit forecasts have been rising all year, and that 
dynamic is still in place. Inevitably, though, the pace of gains has cooled a little now 
that investors have come to grips with the full impact of the AI investment boom. 
In the U.S., our team sees 27% growth in operating profits this year, and that’s 
excluding the additional mark-to-market gains reported on investments in the 
likes of Anthropic and OpenAI. At the start of the year, the team forecast just 13% in 
operating profit gain; by June, the target was up to 26% growth (so our forecast rose 
only 1% in the last three months).
The driving force is no mystery: Massive AI investment spending is clearly the 
biggest driver and those investments are still accelerating. We expect robust 
spending well into 2028, but we acknowledge a few key points. First, the market 
has already largely discounted another strong year of investment spending in 2027. 
Second, we’re beginning to see the strains of funding all this AI growth. Hyperscalers 
have increasingly turned to the debt markets (Exhibit 1), as spending now outstrips 
cash flow; with higher interest rates, borrowing costs have become more onerous. 
Recently the bonds and equities of some of the most aggressive AI spenders (for 
example the neocloud companies) have come under pressure, and this trend 
bears watching. In our recent Global Equity Investors Quarterly, we discussed 
the messages from Robert Merton’s classic “distance to default” measure, which 
highlights the risk of a serious financial problem for each company. It’s been a while 
since we’ve had reason to consult that measure, and some of the stocks most under 
pressure in recent days do indeed look vulnerable. We remain constructive on the AI

Beyond AI, the energy sector delivered exceptional 
profit growth this year, with earnings up over 80%. For 
the remainder of the S&P 500 outside AI and energy, 
we estimate annual 10% profit growth this year and 
next. We see a much broader mix of profit growth than 
in the previous three years. In fact, almost 90% of the 
companies in the S&P 500  are growing their earnings, 
a statistic right at the top of the historical range. This 
suggests a wider set of opportunities and plenty of 
chances to diversify portfolio exposure to technology  
and the AI theme.
Valuations have actually declined a little this year in 
most markets as profits have risen faster than stock 
prices. Our research work suggests that markets 
are still slightly expensive but not remarkably so. U.S. 
stocks trade at higher price/earnings multiples than 
most global equities, which we believe is justified by  
U.S. corporations’ superior profit growth.
However, other valuation measures are sending a much 
more negative message. For example, our emerging 
market team underscores that price/book ratios 
have rarely been this high. This shines a light on both 
exceptional profitability (thanks to the AI boom) and 
the downside risks should profit growth fade. We note 
that after yet more underperformance, Chinese equity 
valuations look increasingly interesting. And software 
stocks, generally perceived as “AI losers,” still offer 
good value as well despite a rally from recent lows. 
What happens after a momentum meltdown? 
The lessons of history.  
As equity investors we are always interested in 
insights from both our fundamental and systematic 
research teams. At our Quarterly, the systematic team 
discussed recent swings in performance from the 
momentum factor. As investors rushed to discount 
the impact of AI investment spending, the momentum 
factor proved exceptionally powerful across global 
equity markets over the past year. The shares of AI 
spending beneficiaries soared and the stocks of 
perceived victims were shunned, a trend that reached 
a crescendo in late June. In July momentum collapsed, 
with monthly performance ranked in the bottom 1% 
over the last 100 years; this is especially unusual in  
a rising market. 
So what happens next? In the past, those stocks that led 
the previous momentum wave have tended to recover 
from the drawdown, albeit modestly. But the biggest 
lesson is that the laggards from the momentum phase 
tend to perform very well, as the market realizes that 
their underperformance was exaggerated (Exhibit 2). 
That gives us another reason to take a good look at 
perceived AI losers, the big underperformers during  
the hot streak for momentum,  to see if fundamentals 
are indeed more attractive than the market thinks. 
We can find many opportunities in software and other 
sectors offering attractive valuations and supported  
by businesses that we expect will hold up well as AI 
is rolled out. In many cases the “losers” may actually 
benefit from the new technology. As always, momentum 
will matter for stock pickers, but leadership is changing.

Japan: An equity market with strong earnings 
and a source of diversification 
Japanese stocks have performed well again this year. 
Indeed, over the past five years, we’ve seen more 
positive surprises and reasons to raise our forecasts 
in Japan than anywhere else (Exhibit 3). The economy 
has started growing again after a long hiatus, prices 
have been rising and the low value of the yen has 
boosted exports. But the macro backdrop is just part 
of the picture; encouraged by the government and 
the Tokyo Stock Exchange, Japanese companies have 
sharpened capital allocation, improved governance 
and boosted profitability. This story isn’t new, but our
