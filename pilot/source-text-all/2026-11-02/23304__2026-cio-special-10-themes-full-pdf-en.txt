10 themes for the year ahead 
Welcome to what is now the fourth edition of our 10 
themes for the year ahead. Once again, this is not about 
exotic risk scenarios, but about what we believe will be 
the most important drivers over the medium term. It 
should come as little surprise that geopolitics, artificial 
intelligence (AI) and monetary policy are among them. 
But that does not mean that these themes cannot 
continue to surprise and keep markets on their toes. Let 
us start with geopolitics and the continuing element of 
surprise in America’s reinterpretation of foreign policy. 
Markets have adjusted to a trade policy environment 
where new tariffs may be announced with little lead time. 
But the attack on Iran in February represents a new 
dimension. In addition to the suffering of those directly 
affected, the rest of the world has since been grappling 
with higher energy costs, meaning higher inflation, and 
higher interest rates. As the U.S. administration’s future 
course and possible ways out of the conflict remain 
unclear, markets will have to continue dealing with this 
uncertainty. Just as they will have to deal with the 
ongoing shift in global centers of power and alliances. 
What does this mean for the transatlantic relationship 
and its dependencies? How can resource-dependent 
Europe become more self-sufficient? And which major 
power will emerging markets have to orient themselves 
toward in the future? Asia, at least, holds a trump card in 
being a dominant supplier in the AI value chain. 
And the AI-related spending continues to expand. This is 
reflected in investment in new data centers as well as 
demand for computing power, most recently driven by 
the broader adoption of AI agents. But who ultimately 
benefits from this growth? As we have explained before, 
those companies positioned at the bottlenecks of the AI 
value chain. This can include the “old economy,” such as 
areas involved in the construction and expansion of data 
centers. Or, as reflected in their profits and market 
valuations, semiconductor manufacturers. No fewer than 
three chipmakers are among the ten largest constituents 
of the S&P 500. They now generate far more free cash 
flow than their largest customers, the hyperscalers. The 
daily fluctuation in the market value of a single chip stock 
can exceed the entire market capitalization of the 
European automotive sector. 
For those who consider this concentration in AI 
beneficiaries in the U.S., or in South Korea excessive, it is 
worth noting that structural growth can also be found in 
other markets, including Europe. Not, however, in the 
sectors that long defined Europe, such as autos, but 
rather in what we call the “New Europe:” companies 
benefiting from long-term trends such as electrification, 
defense and infrastructure investment, rather than from 
the economic cycle. Their stock-market performance 
need not shy away from comparison with that of some AI 
pioneers. Indeed, Europe could even be among the 
beneficiaries of the next stage of AI. 
Europe could also benefit from structural reforms, such 
as the pension reform now being advanced in Germany, 
which could ultimately also help deepen domestic 
capital markets. 
For the time being, however, the U.S. continues to be 
home to the most liquid markets and remains the 
pacesetter for the financial world. This is also why the U.S. 
Federal Reserve remains the decisive central bank. That 
makes the policy approach of its Chair, who has been in 
office since May, particularly relevant to investors. 
There is also the potential for unexpected opportunities 
in two areas far removed from the seats of central banks, 
where we see substantial economic potential: space and 
water. We then return firmly back down-to-earth to look 
at the structural changes in the U.S. real estate market. 
That makes for plenty of reading material, which we hope 
provides a better sense of where we see opportunities 
and risks for investors in the coming year. And of 
everything that, from today’s perspective, lies within the 
realm of possibility. But since investors have to learn 
anew every year that even the “impossible” can happen, I 
can only close with the golden rule of prudent investing: 
broad diversification of assets is the most effective way 
to guard against severe losses arising from the 
uncertainties of the future.  
Vincenzo Vedda 
Global Chief Investment Officer

#1 | Europe & America: The tensions that bind
Breaking up is hard to do once the costs of going it alone become clear. “America First” 
might ultimately remind Americans why they built strong alliances in the first place.
Marriages are sometimes appreciated most when one 
spouse walks out and slams the door, leaving the other to 
discover what had long been taken for granted and what 
they now need to provide for themselves. With that in 
mind, could Donald Trump end up strengthening 
transatlantic relations? 
One way to think about the likely longer-term fallout of 
recent transatlantic tiffs is Brexit. That other famous 
politi-cal divorce of the past decade produced two 
unexpected results. Britain left the European family 
household, yet its politics became more like its 
neighbors: more fragmented and volatile than before 
2016. Meanwhile, the rest of Europe proved surprisingly 
resilient. No member followed Britain to the divorce 
court. Rather than inspire copycats, Brexit advertised the 
costs of leaving the European Union to voters 
everywhere. 
Trump's America First policies may ultimately remind 
decision makers why the post-war transatlantic order 
endured. The institutions and alliances created after 
1945 were designed to provide stability beyond the 
political leaders of the day. This helped underpin 
decades of economic integration, investment and 
growth. 
Yet as the decades passed, the benefits of a rules-based 
order, particularly in trade, increasingly came to be taken 
for granted. The costs of protectionism are easy to forget 
after decades of openness, but far harder to ignore once 
barriers begin to rise again. 
President Trump's second term is providing just such a 
reminder with a strong foreign policy agenda. Some 
reflect genuine imbalances and frustrations that have 
accumulated beneath the surface of the transatlantic 
relationship. Yet it would be a mistake to view the post-
war settlement as a one-way transfer of benefits. Europe 
gained security and access to markets. But the United 
States also won, gaining reliable allies, investment 
opportunities and an economic order rooted in broadly 
shared political values that reinforced its global 
influence. Only when those arrangements are questioned 
do the costs of disrupting them become apparent. 
The depth of the transatlantic relationship is measured 
not only in defense commitments or trade flows. It is 
embedded in decades of cross-border investment, 
corporate ownership and integrated capital markets. 
European companies employ millions of American 
workers, while U.S. firms remain among the largest 
investors across Europe. 
This is illustrated by 60% of U.S. foreign direct investment 
heading to Europe1, and a similar proportion going the 
other way. It is these financial linkages that receive far 
less attention than tariff disputes, yet they may prove 
more durable than political disagreements. Unwinding 
the economic ties that bind the two sides of the Atlantic 
would be considerably more difficult and prohibitively 
costly. 
The important question is whether current grievances 
lead to lasting separation or to a rebalancing of 
responsibilities within the alliance. We see today’s 
confrontations ultimately making the transatlantic 
relationship stronger, not merely more transactional. A 
Europe better able to look after itself would be a more 
capable partner, not least in confronting threats from 
both Russia and China. Should Washington eventually 
seek reconciliation, Trump may end up having weakened 
an old alliance of habit, only to provoke a stronger 
alliance of choice.  
The lesson from the Brexit saga is not that separation, or 
the mere threat of it, inevitably makes every union 
stronger. It is that a crisis forces partners to decide what 
a relationship is really for. Political friction can cause 
volatility and lasting damage. But by exposing the costs 
of acting alone, a more likely longer-term consequence 
of all the heated political rhetoric is a more balanced, 
more resilient and ultimately stronger alliance that 
remains the backbone of the Western economic and 
security order.

Already, cooperation in defense, supply chains, energy, 
technology and critical infrastructure appears to be 
deepening. The result may be renewal, rather than an end 
to the transatlantic era. Trump may have set out to put 
America First, but one of his unintended legacies may be 
to remind America why it chose to seek allies in the first 
place.

#2 | EM: Still the engines of global growth
Why emerging markets matter for growth, resources and the energy transition
In the 20th century, North America, Europe and Japan 
primarily shaped the global economy. In the 21st century, 
this balance has gradually shifted towards Asia and the 
wider emerging world. Emerging and developing 
economies already account for 61% of global gross 
domestic product (GDP) on a purchasing-power-parity 
basis. The IMF expects them to grow by 3.8% in 2026, 
compared with 1.7% for advanced economies.1 As many 
developed economies face aging populations, slower 
productivity 
growth 
and 
rising 
debt, 
emerging 
economies are gaining importance in growth, trade and 
investment. Rather than a simple transfer of power, the 
world appears to be moving toward a more multipolar 
landscape, with several regions shaping demand, 
production and capital flows. 
Much of future global growth is likely to originate outside 
traditional 
industrialized 
economies. 
Younger 
populations, 
rising 
incomes, 
urbanization 
and 
infrastructure investment are driving growth across the 
developing world. India is among the fastest-growing 
major economies, while Southeast Asia benefits from 
supply-chain 
diversification 
as 
companies 
seek 
additional manufacturing hubs. 
Growth and technology: drivers of future 
competitiveness 
Sources: Oxford Insights Government AI Readiness Index 2025; 
IMF World Economic Outlook Update, July 2026; DWS 
Investment GmbH as of September 2026. *The score is 
measured on a 0–100 scale with higher scores indicating 
stronger AI development and wider adoption 
The importance of emerging markets extends beyond 
economic growth. Many of them possess the resources 
and industrial capabilities needed for digitalization, 
electrification and renewable energy. They supply a large 
share of the world’s critical minerals, while China leads in 
industries ranging from solar modules to batteries and 
other 
technologies 
essential 
to 
the 
low-carbon 
transition. Much of future energy demand growth is also 
likely to originate in emerging markets. Without their 
participation, neither resilient supply chains nor global 
climate objectives are likely to be achieved. 
 
 
 
“The key question is which economies are producing the 
goods and services the world actually needs,” says Ivy Ng, 
DWS’s Chief Investment Officer for APAC. Demographics 
and natural resources can create opportunities, but do 
not guarantee long-term success. Population growth 
does not automatically generate prosperity, nor does 
rapid economic expansion ensure superior investment 
returns. What matters is whether countries implement 
reforms, 
build 
strong 
institutions 
and 
integrate 
themselves into the most important global value chains 
of their time.  
AI and automation add another dimension. For decades, 
a large and growing labor force was seen as an economic 
advantage. In an increasingly technology-driven world, 
however, productivity may matter more than population 
size. AI readiness varies widely across emerging 
economies. China has scale in AI, patents and advanced 
manufacturing, Taiwan remains critical to advanced-chip 
production and Singapore has strong AI infrastructure 
and public-sector adoption. India benefits from a large 
technology workforce and digital scale, although 
infrastructure and formal job creation remain constraints. 
Economies reliant on routine services and entry-level 
knowledge work may face greater displacement risks.  
Future economic strength may therefore lie not with the 
most populous economies, but with those most 
successful at combining growth, competitiveness and 
reform. 
Countries 
that 
combine 
technological 
capabilities with strong institutions and access to capital 
may be best positioned to move into higher-value 
activities, while others risk losing part of their labor-cost 
advantage. 
For investors, strong economic growth does not 
guarantee strong capital-market performance. Equity 
returns 
also 
depend 
on 
profitability, 
valuations, 
governance, market structure and investor participation. 
The same need for selectivity applies in credit. India’s 
renewable-energy sector, for example, benefits from 
government targets and substantial equity investment, 
supporting long-term credit fundamentals, although 
tight spreads currently favor carry over valuation upside. 
Country and sector selection therefore matter more than 
headline 
GDP 
growth: 
opportunities 
can 
differ 
substantially both between economies and within them. 
Emerging markets are not homogeneous: political 
stability, regulation and capital-market maturity can 
matter as much as growth. Their growing global 
importance may increase their relevance for investors, 
but does not warrant indiscriminate exposure. Long-term 
outcomes are likely to depend on selecting countries 
where reform and competitiveness translate growth into 
stronger companies and capital markets.

#3 | Fed policy: The end of cheap money
For years, interest rates were low and liquidity abundant. Renewed inflation pressures, 
however, could now usher in a period of higher capital costs and a new yield environment
For years, the direction seemed clear: whenever the 
economy stumbled, central banks cut interest rates, 
liquidity flowed into markets and bond yields returned to 
lower levels. As a result, many investors continued to 
expect a return to the playbook of the 2010s, even after 
the recent inflation shock. Yet that assumption is 
increasingly being called into question. “The likely lesson 
from the past 20 years was that the Federal Reserve 
would step in whenever necessary. Under the new Fed 
Chair Kevin Warsh, those days may be numbered, except 
in severe crises, of course. Fiscal policy is stepping out of 
the long shadow of monetary policy and receiving more 
attention 
regarding 
sustainability.” 
says 
Christian 
Scherrmann, Chief U.S. Economist at DWS. 
Budget deficits remain elevated, government debt 
continues to rise, and the financing needs of the public 
sector are becoming increasingly important. The key 
question today is no longer whether the Federal Reserve 
will raise rates one more time. More important is where 
yields ultimately settle over the longer term. 
A new era of higher borrowing costs? 
Sources: Congressional Budget Office, Bloomberg Finance L.P., 
DWS Investment GmbH as of 9/30/26 
Several factors could keep yields above the exceptionally 
low levels of the previous decade. The U.S. economy 
remains resilient, while new technologies, geopolitical 
shifts and expansionary fiscal policy are increasing 
demand for capital. At the longer end of the Treasury 
curve, elevated issuance and a higher term premium may 
continue to exert upward pressure. However, higher real 
and nominal yields also tighten financial conditions and 
can become self-limiting as they weigh on consumers, 
housing, weaker borrowers and economic growth. 
Higher yields need not necessarily be a problem. The key 
question is whether productivity, economic growth and 
corporate earnings can keep pace with rising borrowing 
costs. If not, tighter financial conditions and valuation 
pressure could increasingly become a headwind. 
The impact is also likely to vary across asset classes. 
Investments 
with 
high 
valuations 
and 
earnings 
expectations stretching far into the future appear 
particularly vulnerable to higher yields. By contrast, 
companies with stable cash flows, strong balance sheets 
and significant pricing power may prove more resilient. 
Credit markets could also see greater dispersion. 
Nevertheless, it would be premature to draw an outright 
negative conclusion. Markets have already shown that 
they can absorb higher yields better than many expected. 
Productivity gains driven by artificial intelligence could 
help offset part of the increase in capital costs. However, 
the capital-intensive AI investment cycle is itself 
contributing to inflation and may be vulnerable if tighter 
financial conditions persist – Chair Warsh specifically 
called out record new issuance, demand and tight credit 
spreads. Ultimately, the crucial question is whether 
returns generated by the real economy can grow faster 
than the cost of capital. 
For George Catrambone, Head of Fixed Income Americas 
at DWS, this is the key issue: “It is not the level of yields 
alone that determine whether higher rates become a 
problem, but rather what is driving them. If yields rise 
because of stronger growth and solid fundamentals, 
investors can adapt. If, however, they are driven by larger 
deficits, inflation and higher term premium, the pressure 
on consumers, businesses and valuations increases 
significantly.” This could mark the transition from a 
liquidity-driven market environment to one driven more 
by fundamentals. Corporate quality, capital discipline 
and careful duration selection may once again move to 
the forefront of investment decisions. 
The Fed’s next phase is likely to be shaped primarily by 
inflation, while fiscal stimulus, deficits and Treasury 
issuance may exert separate pressure on longer-term 
yields. Debt levels alone do not prevent the Fed from 
lowering its policy rate. Recent experience shows that 
high rates can expose financial vulnerabilities even when 
fiscal stimulus supports growth. For fixed-income 
investors, greater dispersion may create attractive 
opportunities for selective positioning.

#4 | Can pension reform change how Germans 
invest?
Germany’s pension system relies heavily on current contributions, with relatively little 
capital invested to fund retirement. Pension reform could channel more capital into equity 
markets
Germany’s pension system could be on the verge of 
significant change. A new retirement savings account, 
the “Altersvorsorgedepot,” has already been approved.1 A 
second, more far-reaching reform remains under 
discussion and would add a funded component to the 
statutory system.2 
Germany’s pension debate usually centers on the 
system’s long-term sustainability. An ageing population 
makes reform particularly pressing. Yet the implications 
of the reform currently debated could extend well 
beyond pensions. Could Germany make the transition 
from a nation of savers to a nation of investors? 
Pension assets vs. GDP: Germany starts from a low 
base 
Sources: Thinking Ahead Institute, Global Pension Assets Study 
2025. * 2024 figures were estimates at publication of the study; 
selected countries shown for readability. 
German households hold the bulk of their savings in bank 
deposits and other conservative investment products. 
Equities, by contrast, are still playing a relatively limited 
role. Meanwhile Germany’s pension system relies heavily 
on pay-as-you-go financing, while funded elements 
remain comparatively small by international standards. 
Reform of private retirement savings has already been 
approved. 
Beginning 
in 
2027, 
the 
government-
supported Altersvorsorgedepot will expand the range of 
retirement products available to savers. For the first time, 
investments without a mandatory capital guarantee will 
be permitted, broadening the investment scope to 
include more return-oriented assets such as exchange-
traded funds (ETFs) and mutual funds. 
While the Altersvorsorgedepot gives private retirement 
savers greater investment flexibility, the proposed 
funded pension component could have a much larger 
impact 
on 
capital 
markets. 
Germany’s 
Pension 
Commission has recommended supplementing the 
statutory pension system with a capital-funded pillar.  
Under the proposal, additional contributions of 2 
percent, estimated at 35 billion euros per year, would be 
levied gradually and invested in capital markets over the 
long term.3 If implemented, the proposal could create a 
substantial and steadily growing pool of investment 
capital. 
Germany’s share of global pension assets is small relative 
to its economic size. Although Germany accounts for 
more than 4 percent of global economic output, it 
represents only around one percent of global pension 
assets. Countries such as the Netherlands, Sweden, 
Switzerland and the U.S. have developed significantly 
larger funded pension systems. The proposed reform 
could begin to narrow that gap by gradually building a 
pool of pension capital invested over the long term. 
International comparisons reveal a striking pattern: 
countries with larger equity markets relative to their 
economies often have higher gross-domestic-product 
(GDP) per capita. Germany ranks around the middle of 
the pack on both measures. The relationship does not 
prove that larger equity markets cause greater prosperity. 
But well-developed capital markets can help finance 
investment and innovation, supporting productivity and 
long-term growth.  
The proposed reform’s immediate effect on pension 
benefits may be limited. Over time, however, it could 
strengthen 
Germany’s 
capital 
markets 
and 
help 
households build retirement wealth. “More money will 
flow into capital markets, but the more important 
question is how that money will be invested,” says Rene 
Penzler, Head of Investment Solutions at DWS. While 
German 
retirement 
products 
have 
traditionally 
emphasized guarantees and capital preservation, the 
reforms 
could 
give 
long-term, 
return-oriented 
investment a greater role. 
Ideally, the reform could improve companies’ access to 
equity capital, facilitate the financing of innovative 
businesses and give households a greater opportunity to 
participate in global market returns. Other countries 
demonstrate the potential of such a shift. For example, 
Sweden and the Netherlands have spent decades 
building structures that support both retirement 
provision and the development of their capital markets. 
A similar transformation in Germany may take time. The 
Altersvorsorgedepot is an important first step, but we 
think a broader shift requires a funded component in the 
statutory pension system as well.

#5 | New Europe can hold its own
Europe has little to do with AI, making its stocks easy to ignore. Think so? This 
misconception is wrong in three ways, creating opportunities for investors. The “New 
Europe” has much to offer
Even investors who are positive on the AI boom may be 
concerned about its concentration in equity indexes, 
particularly in the U.S. Despite offering exposure to a 
broad range of sectors, the S&P 500 remains heavily 
tilted toward a relatively small group of technology and 
AI-related companies. Its ten largest constituents 
account for nearly 40% of the index, roughly twice the 
share of the top ten holdings in the Stoxx Europe 600. 
Three of the top ten are cyclical semiconductor 
companies, and most of the remainder are closely tied to 
AI infrastructure, cloud computing and digital platforms. 
Technology companies account for more than 50% of the 
S&P 500 earnings. By comparison, Europe's market 
leadership is distributed across a broader mix of sectors 
and business models. This could be interesting for 
international investors looking for diversification.1 
“New Europe” earnings are growing faster* 
Sources: Bloomberg Finance L.P., DWS Investment GmbH, as 
of September 2026. *Sectors based on MSCI Europe Index 
In our view, however, Europe’s appeal extends well 
beyond diversification. Not only is it also home to a wide 
range of companies that benefit directly or indirectly 
from the AI boom. More importantly, the structure of 
Europe’s equity market has evolved in recent years – for 
the better. Innovative, adaptable and higher-margin 
sectors are gaining weight relative to more challenged 
industries. We call these two sides of the market “New 
Europe” and “Old Europe.” 
Old Europe was tilted towards consumption, exports and 
efficiency gains, with sectors such as autos, chemicals 
and consumer goods at its core. These industries remain 
important pillars of the European economy, but the key 
drivers of growth are changing.  
By contrast, New Europe is increasingly defined by 
investment, resilience and strategic autonomy, reflected 
in 
themes 
such 
as 
electrification, 
industrial 
modernization, consolidation in financial services, 
infrastructure spending, defense and digitalization. This 
shift is most visible in the sectors we like most: 
industrials, banks, semiconductors and utilities. Together, 
these New Europe sectors now make up roughly 40% of 
the Stoxx Europe 600, up from around 25% in 2015. 
These sectors’ earnings have also grown much than 
those of traditional sectors in recent years, as illustrated 
in our chart.  
AI is a strong example. While the U.S. and China continue 
to dominate its development, Europe could benefit from 
the next stage of the cycle: broader adoption of AI across 
manufacturing and services. Europe’s competitive 
advantage may lie less in building globally dominant AI 
platforms and more in applying new technologies across 
its highly developed and diverse industrial base. Around 
one in seven companies in the Stoxx Europe 600 is 
already benefiting from AI-related investment.2 
The changing sector mix is only one reason we remain 
constructive on European equities. Germany’s reform 
agenda could further support an economy already 
showing signs of a modest recovery. Reforms, particularly 
to reduce bureaucracy, could spill over to the rest of 
Europe and speed up the implementation of the Draghi 
report.3 Meanwhile, European companies are already 
benefiting from a rising commitment for public 
investments across the continent, including in defense 
and initiatives to strengthen strategic autonomy. 
Europe’s potential growth rate remains relatively modest 
at 1% to 1.5%, partly because of demographics. This 
makes the international reach of European companies all 
the more important, with 55% of revenues generated by 
Stoxx Europe 600 constituents coming from outside 
Europe. Recent earnings improvements also suggest 
Europe is more than just a cheaper alternative to the U.S. 
Much of that momentum comes from sectors associated 
with New Europe. “Europe has no reason to hide,” says 
Benjardin Gärtner, Head of Equities at DWS. “Over the 
past four years, the Stoxx Europe 600 has kept pace with 
the S&P 500, thanks in part to the index’s changing sector 
composition.4 I also like the fact that companies are 
becoming more confident when it comes to share 
buybacks.” We expect average earnings growth to return 
to double-digit levels in 2026 and 2027, marking a strong 
rebound after three years of stagnation.

#6 | From AI adoption to investment returns
A little less conversation, a little more action: don’t worry about demand. Worry about 
returns
According to Alphabet CEO Sundar Pichai, “2026 is 
officially the year of the agent. AI agents turn our 
consumer and enterprise products into collaborators 
that can take complex actions on your behalf.”1 AI is 
moving from being mainly a chatbot answering questions 
to being an agent carrying out tasks, which may plan a 
sequence of actions, use tools and check its own work. 
These additional steps can require several rounds of 
computation, hence significantly boosting demand for 
token usage and thus compute capacity. 
Why is the rise of AI agents so important? First, they are a 
major driver of token usage. And second, their rise shows 
how fast AI is evolving and shifting the focus from pure 
investment towards monetization. 
Speaking of monetization: among the main beneficiaries 
of the AI boom so far are semiconductor (chip) 
companies. 
Encouragingly 
for 
shareholders, 
chip

Chip companies are not only profiting from the 
enormous 
buildout 
of 
AI 
infrastructure, 
namely 
datacenters. They are also benefiting from their position 
at a bottleneck in the AI supply chain, which can take 
years to alleviate considering the costs and time re-
quired to build new chip factories.  
This brings us to a core AI investment consideration: 
while the entire AI ecosystem can record high top-line 
growth, those sitting at bottlenecks will tend to have 
higher margins, which also help free cash flow. But it 
seems to come at the expense of the financial position of 
hyperscalers, which are driving the investment wave. This 
may concern some investors because hyperscalers are 
shifting from asset-light platforms that generated ample 
cash for investment and shareholder distributions toward 
capital-intensive businesses increasingly dependent on 
external financing.3 This could prove temporary if 
hyperscalers’ cash flows recover as growing AI and cloud 
revenues justify the upfront investment.  
In our view, agents have the biggest potential to drive AI 
revenue in the future. AI labs develop and train the 
models that businesses then use to build agents, while 
the labs themselves buy computing capacity, mostly 
from hyperscalers, to run them. With AI agents 
proliferating rapidly, hyperscalers’ compute capacity 
should remain well utilized for the time being.4 Falling 
costs per token or more efficient token usage should be 
of little concern, provided they stimulate enough 
additional demand to ultimately drive top-line revenue 
growth.5  
Rising usage alone is not enough, however; customers 
must see outcomes worth paying for. In our view, 
efficiency gains alone will no longer suffice. AI will 
increasingly need to deliver better solutions to existing 
problems, 
accelerate 
innovation 
and 
product 
development, or enable new ser-vices at lower cost and 
with superior outcomes – which is exactly what we 
expect AI to deliver in the future.

#7 | Building resilient portfolios in an AI world
Capturing the upside of AI’s transformative investment cycle without relying on it
For much of the period after the global financial crisis 
2008/09, the prevailing concern was not a shortage of 
capital, but a shortage of productive uses for it. Speaking 
at Jackson Hole, Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh 
suggested that AI may be changing this backdrop: “The 
potential for substantially higher growth is on the rise. 
Ever-expanding pools of capital are pouring into AI-
related infrastructure of all sorts1.” AI is therefore more 
than a technology-sector opportunity. It is also built 
upon a broader physical and financial ecosystem of 
enormous dimensions. 
This creates a portfolio challenge: The top decile in 
market capitalization (cap) accounted for 78% of 
aggregate U.S. equity-market value in July 2026, close to 
the upper end of the long-run range2. At the same time, 
the number of companies in the top decile declined to 
165. Elevated market-cap concentration is therefore 
occurring alongside a narrowing company base, 
increasing benchmark dependence on the largest 
businesses. Investors may consequently hold many 
securities while remaining heavily exposed to a narrow 
set of underlying economic drivers. 
U.S. equity-market concentration 
Source: Kenneth R. French Data Library, DWS Investment 
GmbH as of July 2026. 
The risks appear fourfold: First, heavy AI investment may 
not generate enough revenue or productivity gains to 
justify its cost. AI could transform the economy without 
every 
company 
involved 
delivering 
attractive 
shareholder returns3. Second, apparently reasonable 
valuations may depend on demanding profit forecasts. 
Semiconductor companies, for example, may look 
affordable on forward multiples but become expensive if 
earnings disappoint4. Third, building AI requires vast 
amounts of capital, whether raised through debt or 
equity. By competing for a finite pool of funding, the 
buildout could raise financing costs for technology 
companies, utilities and suppliers, favoring those with 
stronger balance sheets5. Finally, AI may succeed without 
today’s perceived winners remaining its principal 
beneficiaries. Investors must assess not only whether AI 
delivers, but also which companies will be the most likely 
to ultimately capture the value. 
Diversification6 would be an appropriate response in our 
view: It should start within the AI ecosystem by 
distinguishing 
between 
the 
companies 
financing 
investment and those whose earnings depend on that 
spending7. 
Companies 
benefiting 
from 
AI 
and 
independent structural trends may offer greater 
resilience. Grid investment for example is also supported 
by electrification and ageing infrastructure; automation 
by labor scarcity and reshoring; and cybersecurity by the 
continuing digitalization. These businesses remain linked 
to AI, but their growth need not depend on AI alone.  
Consider different earnings drivers outside the AI value 
chain: Financials are influenced by interest rates, credit 
growth 
and 
economic activity; 
Health 
Care 
by 
demographics and innovation cycles; Defense by 
security spending; and selected small- & mid-caps by 
domestic economic conditions. Government bonds may 
help hedge against a disinflationary growth shock, 
whereas Energy, Infrastructure and other Real Assets 
may prove more resilient in an inflationary shock. 
Diversification therefore requires a view not only on the 
asset, but also on the macroeconomic risk it is intended 
to hedge8. 
Geography can provide another layer of diversification: 
but only where it introduces genuinely different 
economic drivers. Buying foreign companies exposed to 
the same AI spending cycle likely offers limited benefits9. 
European 
markets 
provide 
greater 
exposure 
to 
industrials, financials and defense, Japan to corporate 
reform and automation, and selected emerging or 
resource-rich economies to domestic growth and 
commodity cycles.  
In conclusion: We remain constructive on AI as a long-
term investment theme. Indeed, one of the greatest risks 
may be underestimating the scale or duration of the 
potential productivity transformation. The most effective 
AI hedge may therefore not be a position against the 
technology, but a diversified portfolio that retains 
exposure to its upside while remaining resilient should 
the identified risks materialize. As Henning Potstada, 
Global Head of Multi Asset at DWS, puts it: “In the AI era, 
true diversification should be measured less by the 
number of holdings than by the diversity of the economic 
engines driving portfolio returns.”

#8 | U.S. real estate in transition
How the shift from employment to productivity growth is reshaping the market
Real-estate markets generally benefit from economic 
growth. For individual property sectors, however, it is not 
only the pace of growth that matters but also the source 
of that growth. This is where a structural shift is emerging 
in the United States. Population growth has slowed to its 
lowest level in more than a century1, while investment in 
Artificial Intelligence (AI), data centers and digital 
infrastructure is accelerating. As a result, future growth 
may rely less on workforce expansion and more on 
productivity gains. For real estate, this distinction 
matters: sectors linked to consumption and goods flows 
may benefit, while property types that depend directly 
on employment growth could face greater challenges. 
Renting has regained a clear cost advantage 
Sources: Census Bureau (rent), National Association of Realtors 
(mortgage), DWS Investment GmbH as of June 2026 
The U.S. economy has continued to expand despite 
historically weak job creation, suggesting productivity is 
playing a growing role. Lower birth rates, an aging 
population and weaker immigration are constraining 
labor-force growth, while AI is improving efficiency and 
supporting 
investment. 
Higher 
productivity 
can 
ultimately translate into stronger profits, incomes and 
spending, helping sustain growth even as workforce 
expansion slows. "The key issue for real estate is no longer 
just how fast the economy grows, but what drives that 
growth,” says Todd Henderson, Head of Real Estate for the 
Americas at DWS. “Demographics and AI may support 
overall activity, yet their impact will vary significantly 
across property sectors." 
For the office sector, this may prove to be a difficult 
combination. Long-term office demand ultimately 
depends on the number of office-based jobs. Slower 
growth in the working-age population therefore limits a 
key source of demand, while AI could further reshape 
employment 
patterns 
across 
traditional 
office 
occupations. Future job growth may increasingly occur in 
healthcare, research and specialized technology fields 
rather than conventional office industries. Employment 
in several major office-using sectors has already 
weakened since late 2022.2 The office market is therefore 
likely to remain highly bifurcated. Prime buildings in 
leading locations may continue to attract tenants and 
command strong rents, while conventional assets 
outside the top tier could face increasing structural 
pressure. 
Residential real estate presents a different picture. 
Demand for housing depends less on population growth 
than on household formation, and the number of 
households has been increasing faster than the 
population for years. Smaller household sizes and more 
single-person households suggest this trend may 
continue. Meanwhile, the cost of buying a home has risen 
much more rapidly than rents. Higher house prices and 
elevated 
mortgage 
rates 
have 
made 
renting 
comparatively attractive. If AI-driven productivity gains 
boost household incomes, demand for larger and higher-
quality homes could increase, particularly within the 
premium segment. 
Industrial real estate could also benefit from the 
changing growth mix. Smarter supply chains may reduce 
inventory 
requirements 
and 
improve 
warehouse 
utilization. However, AI is simultaneously creating new 
sources of demand. Data-center development is 
increasing demand for industrial space, while the 
conversion of industrial land to digital infrastructure can 
constrain supply elsewhere. Continued e-commerce 
growth should provide an additional tailwind. Because 
online retail remains significantly more logistics-
intensive than traditional retail, these trends should 
support demand for modern warehouse and distribution 
facilities. 
The retail sector may also be better positioned than many 
assume. Even a further rise in e-commerce does not 
necessarily imply weaker demand for retail property. 
Many stores now serve as both shopping destinations 
and fulfilment hubs. In addition, a growing share of rental 
income is generated by service-oriented tenants such as 
restaurants, fitness providers, healthcare operators and 
personal-care 
businesses 
rather 
than 
traditional 
merchandise retailers. These activities are likely to 
benefit from both an aging population and rising 
incomes. 
The key takeaway is not that demographics and AI will 
pull the economy in opposite directions. Rather, they are 
changing where growth comes from and how it is 
generated. For real-estate markets, that shift matters. 
Based on current trends, industrial properties, service-
oriented retail assets and higher-quality residential real 
estate 
appear 
relatively 
well 
positioned, 
while 
conventional office buildings outside the prime segment 
may face greater structural challenges.

#9 | Transformation shifts where water matters most
How water runs through the real economy and can create new winners and losers
Across the world, a growing number of countries are 
discovering that reliable access to water can no longer be 
taken for granted. Shipping disruptions in Panama, 
groundwater depletion in India, pressure on the Colorado 
river in the United States and recurring droughts across 
Europe all point to the same conclusion: water is 
emerging as a constraint on trade, food security, 
industrial production and growth. 
Yet water remains one of the most underappreciated 
resource challenges facing investors. While energy 
security has dominated political attention and capital 
allocation 
in 
recent 
years, 
water 
has 
received 
comparatively little focus. This imbalance is becoming 
increasingly difficult to justify. Water is not simply 
another environmental issue, it is a critical input for many 
of the transformations that governments and companies 
are attempting to accelerate, from electrification and 
digitalization to advanced manufacturing and more 
sustainable agriculture. 
The challenge is that these transformations reshape 
where water is needed. The case for cleaner power could 
not be simpler when it comes to water since our chart 
shows that for each unit of electricity, wind and solar 
require far less water than the thermal and nuclear plants 
they might replace. But the transition creates new 
demands elsewhere. For example, the extraction and 
processing of critical minerals needed for batteries, 
transmission networks and renewable technologies are 
highly water-intensive, and much of that mining sits in 
already water-stressed regions. 
Digitalization adds a further layer of complexity. The 
rapid build-out of data centers to support artificial 
intelligence requires water both directly for cooling and 
indirectly through the electricity needed to power 
increasingly sophisticated computing infrastructure. 
Semiconductor manufacturing carries similar exposure. 
Chip fabrication ranks among the world's most water-
intensive industrial processes. Yet many of the regions 
attracting new investment, supported by industrial 
policy and reshoring incentives, are already experiencing 
significant water stress. Arizona in the arid southwestern 
United States is perhaps the most frequently cited 
example, but it is far from unique. 
For investors, the implication is that water is increasingly 
likely to be repriced. “Unlike energy, water has historically 
been 
underpriced, 
rarely 
traded 
and 
chronically 
underinvested,” says Michael Lewis, Head of Research 
ESG. As scarcity becomes more visible, the economic 
value of secure water access is likely to increase, creating 
two distinct opportunity sets. 
The first lies in risk identification. Companies operating in 
water-intensive sectors such as utilities, chemicals and 
food production may face rising input costs and tighter 
regulatory requirements. In some cases, facilities located 
in water-stressed basins could experience curtailments 
or become stranded altogether. Water intensity is 
therefore emerging as an important lens through which 
to assess business resilience, revealing vulnerabilities 
that traditional carbon-focused analysis may overlook. 
The second lies in solutions. Addressing water scarcity 
requires substantial investment in treatment and 
recycling technologies, leakage reduction, desalination, 
precision irrigation, monitoring systems and water-
related infrastructure. Since access to water for 
businesses, municipalities and households is not 
discretionary, this creates the potential for a more 
durable and less cyclical source of demand.

This may change the approach to climate investing which 
has typically been viewed through the lens of reducing

volatile weather and growing resource constraints. Water 
resilience sits at the heart of that challenge. 
As physical climate risks intensify, investment in water 
quality, efficiency, recycling and infrastructure is likely to 
grow. Such solutions can strengthen economic resilience 
while supporting rivers, coastal ecosystems and the 
broader blue economy. In a warming world, water may 
prove as important to economic security as energy.

#10 | Space: The next infrastructure layer
Why communication, data and computing power may increasingly be delivered from 
space
For decades, space was largely the domain of 
governments, the military and a handful of scientific 
missions. Today, that picture is changing rapidly. Falling 
launch costs, reusable rockets and increasingly capable 
satellites are opening up new economic opportunities. 
Space is gradually evolving from a niche for scientific 
exploration into an infrastructure platform on which 
entire industries could increasingly depend. The question 
is becoming less about building cities on Mars and more 
about which services and infrastructure can be delivered 
efficiently from orbit. In that sense, the future of space 
may look less like science fiction and more like a network 
of data centers, communications systems and digital 
services operating above Earth. 
“The key bottleneck for the entire space economy remains 
the ability to launch rockets frequently and reuse them at 
substantially lower cost,” says Sebastian Werner, Head of 
Growth Equities Americas at DWS. Many observers see 
this as the industry's defining inflection point. Like 
railroads, power grids and the internet before it, space 
could 
become 
a 
new 
infrastructure 
layer 
for 
communication, data processing and other services. 
Lower-cost access to orbit could enable entirely new 
business models, just as digital platforms and cloud 
services emerged alongside the internet. 
Launch cost to low Earth orbit set to fall dramatically

illustrative and uncertain), DWS Investment GmbH as of 
September 2026 
Satellites already play a critical role in communications, 
navigation, 
weather 
forecasting 
and 
security 
applications. In the years ahead, they could further 
improve connectivity in remote regions, link aircraft and 
ships 
to 
global 
networks 
and 
provide 
backup 
connectivity 
when 
terrestrial 
systems 
fail. 
Earth 
observation 
is 
becoming 
increasingly 
important. 
Satellites collect information on weather events, 
wildfires, floods, agricultural yields and supply chains. 
Combined with artificial intelligence, these data can 
generate insights that help businesses, governments and 
insurers make better decisions. The economic value is 
still created largely on Earth, even if the technology 
enabling it operates in space. At the same time, 
technological competition between the U.S. and China is 
likely to accelerate investment in space infrastructure, 
communications, defense and computing. 
The next phase of development may involve applications 
that still appear futuristic today. These include orbital 
data centers, manufacturing facilities operating in 
microgravity and, over the longer term, even the use of 
resources beyond Earth. Growing demand for computing 
power could be particularly important. As artificial 
intelligence requires ever larger amounts of energy and 
processing capacity, some observers argue that orbit-
based computing infrastructure could have long-term 
potential. Whether such visions become commercially 
viable will depend largely on further declines in 
transportation costs and continued technological 
progress. As a result, many of these business models are 
more likely to become relevant in the 2030s and 2040s 
than in the immediate future. 
For investors, this creates a much broader opportunity 
set 
than 
simply 
rocket 
manufacturers. 
Potential 
beneficiaries 
include 
satellite 
operators, 
communications providers, Earth-observation and data-
analytics 
companies, 
defense 
contractors, 
semiconductor 
manufacturers 
and 
specialized 
infrastructure and energy-technology firms. Business 
models 
built 
around 
recurring 
revenues 
from 
connectivity, data and services may prove particularly 
attractive. Still, investors may want to remain selective. 
Not every technological vision translates into a 
successful business model, and high levels of investment 
do not guarantee sustainable profits. Commercial 
viability depends on reliable, reusable rockets and timely 
launches, which may require resolving technical 
challenges, securing substantial funding and obtaining 
regulatory approvals. 
By 2050, space could play a role similar to the internet 
today, as an invisible layer of infrastructure supporting 
large parts of the economy. Communication, navigation, 
data processing and Earth observation could become 
routine services delivered from orbit. Much like railroads, 
power grids and mobile networks transformed earlier 
eras, space infrastructure may give rise to entirely new 
business models that are only beginning to emerge 
today. Perhaps the most important change would be the 
least spectacular, as space would no longer be viewed as 
a separate industry, but as an integral part of the 
infrastructure on which much of the global economy 
relies. In that world, the success of the space economy 
would be measured by how seamlessly space-based 
services become embedded in economic activity.

Carry (of an asset) refers to the net return or cost of holding an asset over time, typically derived from income such as interest or 
dividends minus any associated financing or holding costs. A carry trade is an investment strategy that seeks to capture this carry by 
exploiting differences in yields or interest rates between assets, typically by funding at a lower rate and investing in assets with higher 
returns.

a specific time period. 
The International Monetary Fund (IMF), created in 1945 and headquartered in Washington, D.C., is an organization of 191 countries, 
working to foster global monetary cooperation, secure financial stability, facilitate international trade, promote high employment and 
sustainable economic growth, and reduce poverty around the world. 
Productivity measures how much economic output is produced for a given level of inputs (such as capital and labor). 
Purchasing power parity (PPP) is a technique used to determine the relative value of currencies, whereas the purchasing power in both 
currencies is the same. 
The spread is the difference between the quoted rates of return on two different investments, usually of different credit quality. 
Valuation attempts to quantify the attractiveness of an asset, for example through looking at a firm's stock price in relation to its 
earnings. 
Theme 3: The end of cheap money 
A budget deficit is created whenever the spending in a public budget exceeds the income within a given time period. 
Dispersion describes the variation in performance or returns across different investments, sectors or issuers. 
Duration is a measure expressed in years that adds and weights the time periods in which a bond returns cash to its holder. It is used to 
calculate a bond's sensitivity towards interest-rate changes. 
Fiscal policy describes government spending policies that influence macroeconomic conditions. Through fiscal policy, the government 
attempts to improve unemployment rates, control inflation, stabilize business cycles and influence interest rates in an effort to con-
trol the economy. 
Economic stimulus refers to government actions, typically through fiscal and monetary policy, aimed at encouraging private sector 
economic activity to boost growth. 
Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services is rising and, subsequently, purchasing power is falling. 
Liquidity refers to the degree to which an asset or security can be bought or sold in the market without affecting the asset's price and 
to the ability to convert an asset to cash quickly.
