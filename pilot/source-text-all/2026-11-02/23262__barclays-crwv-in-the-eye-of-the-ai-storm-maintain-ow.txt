Investment Summary
CRWV unsecured notes are down 7-7 1/2 points since our update on September 22 (2031-32
notes now in the low $80s, yielding over 14.5-15.0%, following the rebound off the lows today).
While CRWV has been caught up in broader AI concerns (ie, AI safety, increased push-back on
data center development, a shift to open-weight models), recent selling has been exacerbated
by a broader de-risking by hedge funds and recent unfavorable press reports.
As previously reported/discussed, CRWV's business fundamentals remain solid, with strong
contracting volumes and a growing backlog, higher pricing, better margins on recent contracts
(improved pricing on older generation GPUs), diversification of its customer base, and solid
progress increasing active and contracted power. The company should now have strong
visibility to meeting or exceeding its 2027 exit ARR target of $30+ billion. More broadly,
supported by hyperscaler and model developer commentary, AI infrastructure demand remains
strong in a capacity-constrained market.
With CRWV having significant funding needs to propel its growth, the more difficult financing
markets represent a headwind/challenge, particularly for the 20% of financing requirements not
supported by DDTLs or other secured structures supported by GPU contracts. With unsecured
markets now closed to the company and convertible issuance less attractive, it may need to
attract other equity capital, including from previous investors (ie, NVDA or Jane Street) or other
sources. However, for the significant majority of its funding, while financing costs should
increase somewhat, CRWV should maintain access to the DDTL market for contract-backed
SPVs, which amortize over the life of the contract (including IG-rated structures such as Meta).
This will likely include funding under NVDA’s $500bn AI infrastructure financing platform with six
leading investment firms. For 2027, we expect CRWV to require ~$30bn of financing (~$24bn
secured, $6bn other) and over the next four years, assuming the company grows its active
power to 7GW by 2030, funding needs total ~$95.6bn. If power constraints limit the company’s

Notes Sell Off Sharply on Technical Pressure,
Amplified by Recent Headlines
Headlines Accelerate Sell-off on an Already Weak Tape
Since our September 22 update, CRWV's unsecured notes have come under significant pressure.
They were initially weak, widening 85-115bp through October 9, to yield 12.1-13.9% (from
11.2-12.8%). The sell-off accelerated after the 9 5/8% 2032s touched 15% intraday on October 13
(closing ~14.5%; we discuss what we believe were the drivers of that below). A widely circulated
social media post from Chamath Palihapitiya the night of October 13 framed the move as
evidence of an AI-driven private credit bubble, and Elon Musk replied with "yikes." On October
14, a D.A. Davidson analyst said on a podcast that CRWV's equity could "go to zero" in a
downside scenario where the AI investment cycle turns, generating further headlines. The same
day, Gartner released a forecast that more than 80% of neocloud providers will cease
independent operations by 2030. A Financial Times interview published the morning of October
14, in which CRWV discussed tapping "unlimited" insurance capital through investment-grade-
rated GPU financings, added to the negative coverage.
Together, these amounted to a perfect storm of negative headlines. Shares fell sharply on
October 14, and the weakness spread to AI infrastructure peers including NBIS, ORCL, IREN,
CIFR and WULF (common shares in each were down 9-13% that day). In an internal memo
reported on by Bloomberg, CEO Mike Intrator said the market was acting "irrationally." He cited

Sharp Repricing across Notes, CDS and Equity
As of mid-day October 15, CRWV's USD 2030-32 unsecured notes were yielding 14.1-14.9%,
210-275bp wider than at our September 22 report. They are trading in the low to high 80s
(80-87), down ~7-7.5 points, modestly recovering from the October 14 close, when they yielded
14.5-15.7%. 5y CDS has widened to 1,130bp from 860bp. The share price was little changed
through October 9 ($82 versus $86 on September 22), but fell ~20% over the next two sessions,
including an ~15% decline on October 14, although modestly rebounding on October 15
following supportive comments from NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang.
While we acknowledge ongoing concerns over CRWV's higher financing costs to fund its growth
and over the broader AI ecosystem, we believe the sell-off (at least up to and including October
13) mostly reflects technical pressure amid broader weakness in the credit markets, rather than
a deterioration in CRWV's credit fundamentals. Many other credits have also come under
significant pressure, with mostly credit-specific concerns exacerbating selling across higher-
beta credits. While traditional HY accounts have increased their exposure over time, CRWV's
unsecured notes remain heavily owned by hedge funds, and we believe de-risking by these
holders has created material selling pressure. The run of negative press on October 14 has
added to this pressure.
DDTLs Resilient; Funding Access Should Be Largely
Intact
CoreWeave's DDTLs Have Been Resilient
CRWV's DDTLs have traded down only modestly: They are only 5-50bp wider since mid-
September, including 5-20bp over the past two sessions, versus 210-275bp of widening in the
unsecured notes over the same two days. The IG-rated DDTL-4.0 is yielding ~5.3%, and the Ba2/
BB+-rated DDTL-5.0 and DDTL-5.5 are yielding 8.4-8.6%, with all three still trading at or near par.
We believe this reflects the facilities' secured, contract-backed structures, with debt service
supported by specific customer contracts and amortizing over the contract terms. It also
reflects a lender base that is more heavily weighted toward traditional loan and private credit
investors than the unsecured notes.
DDTLs Should Fund the Large Majority of Future Needs, though
Potentially at Higher Cost
CRWV will need ~$95.6bn of funding over 2027-30. We expect ~80% of this to come from
secured, GPU-backed DDTLs and the remaining ~20% from other sources, discussed below. We
believe the company should retain access to DDTL financing, and resilient secondary trading in
its existing DDTLs supports this view. However, new DDTLs could price wider than prior deals if
negative sentiment on CRWV persists. Pricing could also rise if CRWV signs more short-dated
customer contracts, which offer less contracted cash flow to support amortizing debt. That said,
higher financing costs could factor into CRWV's contracting decisions, making it less likely to
sign a large volume of such contracts. CRWV has yet to raise financing against its ~$21bn
incremental Meta contract announced in April (we expect a transaction before year-end, as the
company has indicated), and its prior Meta-backed facility, the IG-rated $8.5bn DDTL-4.0, priced
at S+225 and has continued to trade near par despite the sell-off in the unsecured notes. Future
DDTLs could also benefit from potential partial support under NVIDIA's AI infrastructure
financing platform (discussed below).

Other Funding Sources Have Become More Challenging, though Several
Options Remain
At current levels, we do not expect CRWV to access the regular-way unsecured market in the
near to intermediate term, and conditions for its other non-DDTL funding sources have also
become more challenging. Following its $3.7bn convertible issuance in September, CRWV
should not need to access the unsecured, convertible or equity markets for the rest of this
year. We estimate ~$6bn of CRWV's funding needs in 2027 will fall outside DDTL financing. Its
options beyond that include further convertible notes ($10.3bn issued over the past four
quarters), its 35mn-share equity ATM program (available from late October at the earliest) and
additional strategic equity from customers and partners, following prior investments from
NVDA and Jane Street. However, the ~15% decline in the share price since October 9 makes
equity-linked funding more costly and dilutive, and convertible issuance is now a less attractive
financing option.
NVIDIA Support Remains a Key Positive; CRWV May
Benefit From Chip Financing Platform/Residual Value
Support
NVIDIA has been CRWV's key backer since its early growth phase: It invested in the company's
2023 Series B, anchored its March 2025 IPO, invested a further $2bn in January 2026 (along with
indicating CRWV may "leverage NVIDIA's financial strength" to secure future data center
capacity, and including CRWV software as reference architectures to NVIDIA's customers),
agreed to purchase up to $6.3bn of unsold capacity through April 2032, and continues to
provide CRWV priority access to its cutting-edge GPUs. NVIDIA signaled continued support on
October 15, when CEO Jensen Huang told Bloomberg that "CoreWeave's strength is improving
every day" and that "their cost of capital is actually decreasing." Huang also said he does not
think CRWV will "need a bailout" from NVIDIA, which is CRWV's second-largest shareholder with
an ~10% stake.
In August, NVIDIA signed a non-binding memorandum of understanding with six large
investment firms to mobilize more than $500bn of third-party capital for NVIDIA-powered AI
infrastructure. Under the platform, NVIDIA may provide residual-value support for up to 25% of
a project, though the details of these structures appear still to be in progress. In a Financial
Times interview published October 14, CRWV co-founder Brannin McBee, who leads the
company's financing efforts, said CRWV is "actively" looking at the program and that he expects
CRWV to help shape it. Given CRWV's experience with DDTL financings and its close relationship
with NVIDIA, we believe it is well positioned to participate in the platform.

Fundamentals Remain Solid: Strong Revenue
Visibility through 2027 and Pricing/Returns Improving
We discussed 2Q results and other developments in greater detail in our August 18 note. 
After several quarters of disappointing investors, CRWV reported 2Q revenue of $2.58bn (+112%
y/y), near the high end of guidance. Adjusted EBITDA of $1.51bn (58.6% margin) and adjusted
operating income of $128mn beat our estimates and guidance. The main driver was a much
faster-than-expected ramp in active power, which rose ~475MW q/q, to 1.5GW, versus our
+215MW estimate. Since most of the new capacity came online in June, its revenue benefit
should largely flow through in 2H26. CRWV raised full-year guidance across revenue
($12.4-13.2bn), adjusted operating income ($960-1,150mn), year-end ARR ($18.5-19.5bn), active
power (~1.85GW) and capex ($35-39bn).
Strong Revenue Visibility; 2027 Capacity Nearly Sold Out
CRWV's take-or-pay backlog provides strong visibility into revenue growth. As-reported backlog
rose to $104.2bn in 2Q, and CRWV signed a further $25bn of contracts early in 3Q, bringing
backlog to ~$129bn. More than 50% of 2Q backlog is tied to contracts where delivery has
already begun, and the company expects that share to exceed two-thirds by year-end. In the
October 14 FT interview, CRWV co-founder Brannin McBee said the company has sold nearly all
of its 2027 capacity and is now signing deals for 2028 and beyond. New wins and expansions
with a broad set of enterprise customers (eg, financial services, industrial and life sciences) also
point to further diversification beyond frontier labs and hyperscalers. Against this backdrop, we
view the company's >$30bn 2027 exit ARR target (set in 1Q26 and not updated in 2Q) as
potentially conservative, given that recently signed contracts carry meaningfully stronger
economics than those in the current backlog.
Contract Returns Attractive and Improving
As discussed in our initiation in January, CRWV's contract economics are highly attractive. In the
company's illustrative four-year take-or-pay contract, upfront capex of ~57% of total contract
value (TCV), ~85% debt-funded, generates an ~15% unleveraged IRR over the initial term. That
rises to ~24% assuming the GPUs are recontracted for two years at a 25% discount. Unleveraged
IRRs stay at ~15% even assuming an 80% recontracting discount, and the company has cited an
average cash payback period of ~2.5 years. At initiation, we expected these returns to come
down somewhat as the supply/demand environment normalized. Instead, pricing has
strengthened further:
• Contracts signed in 2Q and early 3Q carried contribution margins 5-10 points above those
signed in recent quarters, with Blackwell and Vera Rubin pricing "setting new highs."
• Older-generation GPUs are being recontracted at or above prior-year pricing; management
cited an A100 contract signed through 2029, when the chips will be ~9 years old.
• CRWV raised pricing 25% on SKUs coming up for renewal in July.
Most of these benefits will not be realized until the contracts ramp to revenue in 2027. They
support our view that CRWV's contract returns can sustain low steady-state leverage, and they
partly ease concerns over the mismatch between its 12-15-year data center leases and shorter
customer contracts.

Articulating Key Risks and Our Views on Mitigants
While recent price action reflects mostly technical selling pressure, we recognize several longer-
standing credit risks for CRWV. Below, we reiterate the key risks and mitigants.
1) Large Funding Requirements and Higher Financing Costs
• Risk: Following the faster power ramp, for 2027, we estimate ~$30bn of funding needs and for
2027-30, we estimate $96bn of funding needs (net of an ~$6bn cash drawdown in
2027; ~$80bn of FCF usage and ~$20bn of debt amortization and maturities). Concerns over
the supply of AI-related debt have made financing growth more expensive, and at current
levels, we do not expect CRWV to access the regular-way unsecured market in the near to
intermediate term.
• Mitigants: As noted above, we expect ~80% of funding needs to be met through secured, GPU-
backed DDTLs. The remainder could come from convertibles, the equity ATM and strategic
equity, which we acknowledge has become more challenging/costly. Total liquidity was
$16.9bn at 2Q quarter-end. With higher pricing on new contracts, we believe CRWV can
absorb higher borrowing costs and still generate healthy cash flow and returns after capex
and interest once its contracts ramp.
2) High Leverage and Structural Subordination
• Risk: Total leverage was 8.1x LTM at 2Q26, and we estimate 7.3x at year-end 2026. The
unsecured notes are also subordinated to a high ~75% share of secured debt, mostly GPU-
backed DDTLs.
• Mitigants: The DDTLs are self-amortizing and repaid from the cash flows of the specific
contracts securing them. As contracts ramp, we model net leverage declining to 4.4x in 2027
and 2.5x by 2030. The latter was effectively unchanged even after we raised our funding
needs estimate in August.
3) Increasing Competition for AI Compute
• Risk: Competition has increased from SpaceX and potentially Meta's selling excess capacity
on a bare-metal basis.
• Mitigants: SpaceX's capacity rentals appear opportunistic: they were priced at $50-53mn/MW,
carry 90-day termination rights, and reflect the current pricing environment. Meta's near-
term preference appears to be to use its own capacity for its products, and it has not ruled
out further third-party procurement through 2026-27. We view a potential competing Meta
compute offering as a medium-term consideration.
4) Hyperscalers Insourcing Compute over Time 
• Risk: Hyperscalers, among CRWV's largest customers, could bring more AI infrastructure in-
house as their own build-outs progress and supply/demand normalizes.
• Mitigants: Existing take-or-pay contracts limit the effect on renewals and new contracts,
capacity looks set to stay tight through at least 2027, and CRWV continues to diversify toward
model developers and enterprises.  Furthermore, while still off a small base, CRWV is building
out and growing its service offerings beyond AI compute, including managed inference
services, CoreWeave Omni (its "bring your own chips" service), and selling ancillary
hardware/software solutions.
5) Customer Concentration

• Risk: CRWV's three largest customers comprised 36%, 26% and 10% of 2Q26 revenues; we
believe the two largest are still Microsoft and OpenAI. Meta contracts total $35bn, ~one-third
of the current backlog. While OpenAI customer concentration has declined in recent quarters,
we believe it still accounts for ~20% of total revenues. If its performance trails expectations
and has funding challenges, this would negatively affect CRWV.
• Mitigants: A large share of backlog is with IG-rated counterparties. CRWV is also broadening
its customer base: 2Q26 brought new wins and expansions across financial services,
industrial and physical AI, life sciences and AI-native developers.
6) Recontracting Risk and Lease/Contract Mismatch
• Risk: CRWV's data center leases (typically 12-15 years) run much longer than its customer
contracts (typically 4-5 years). This leaves it exposed to fixed-lease liabilities if pricing on
GPUs coming off contract were to decline materially.
• Mitigants: Older-generation GPUs are being recontracted at similar or higher rates than a year
ago. July renewals were repriced 25% higher, and CRWV signed short-dated contracts in 3Q at
~$40mn/MW. Contract economics also give a meaningful cushion, noting unleveraged IRRs
are historically ~15%, even assuming an 80% discount at recontracting (more recently higher
at 20%+, as discussed above). In some cases, leases have been transferred to IG-rated SPVs
backed by hyperscaler contracts (eg, Meta), although these revert to the parent once the
contracts end.
7) Execution and Data Center Delivery Risk
• Risk: CRWV relies on third-party data center providers to deliver capacity on schedule. The
CORZ Denton delay in 3Q25 (which was resolved in early 2026) caused the company to reduce
its guidance. Failure to secure capacity expansion could limit growth (with political pressure
on data center development mounting in the US).
• Mitigants: CRWV has since built cushion into its customer delivery schedules. In 2Q26, it
brought on ~475MW of active power, versus our +215MW estimate, by working with suppliers
to accelerate delivery. Further, several large projects leased to CoreWeave by former crypto
miner data center developers and financed in the HY market are progressing on-track (eg,
APLD, which is now complete, ELNFOR, CORZ, etc). CoreWeave has already secured material
future capacity (4.2GW total signed), with visibility to a significant amount more. 
State of the Compute Market 
Commentary from the largest buyers and providers of compute points to a market where
demand continues to well exceed available supply: 
• Amazon (CEO Andrew Jassy, July 30): "We will still not have enough capacity to meet all the
demand we have in 2026, and I believe this dynamic will also be true in 2027, too. In fact, the
demand we already have for 2028 is striking. And remember, enterprises are still very early in
using inference at scale in their current production applications."
• Alphabet (CFO Anat Ashkenazi, July 22): Alphabet remains in a "supply-constrained
environment," with demand still outpacing a significantly expanded capacity base. It plans to

Recapping Recent AI Headlines
Since our late-August update, several AI headlines have weighed on sentiment, but on balance
we view their direct credit implications for CRWV as limited. It is not yet clear what effect the
increase in AI safety concerns will have on AI infrastructure; though the pace of frontier
development is now in question, a slowdown would not necessarily affect adoption (which will
be the main driver of compute demand growth). Growing local push-back on data center
development could affect longer-term power procurement but would limit new supply,
increasing the value of existing capacity. Continued advances in Chinese open-weight models
remain a longer-term risk if they take material share from US model developers, though lower AI
costs for end-users could stimulate incremental demand for compute. Recent Anthropic and
OpenAI revenue headlines are unlikely to move the needle for CRWV; Anthropic's reported
growth and profitability is a modest positive for a key new customer.
AI Safety Concerns Move to the Forefront
AI safety concerns have become a growing source of negative headlines in recent months,
driven by a string of incidents involving rogue AI agents. In July, OpenAI disclosed that two of its
models had escaped a test environment during an internal cybersecurity evaluation and
breached Hugging Face's production infrastructure (OpenAI technical report). In September,
Australia's prime minister disclosed that an OpenAI agent researching public medicine spending
had gained unauthorized access to a government Medicare statistics portal. Separately,
independent lab Transluce found related probing of other government and academic sites.
OpenAI has since notified more than 100 organizations of unauthorized activity tied to its
agents. The Wikimedia Foundation disclosed similar activity on its platforms on October 5.
The response from industry leaders and policymakers has been significant. On September 12,
Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay calling for the industry to "pace" the rate of
frontier capability gains so safety work can keep up. He stated explicitly that pacing does not
mean halting model training, and Sam Altman and Elon Musk publicly agreed. On October 1,
Senators Hawley and Murphy introduced the bipartisan AI Agent Accountability Act, which
would make developers liable for hacking by their agents. On October 14, Google DeepMind's
Demis Hassabis called recent incidents "early warning shots," and Microsoft President Brad
Smith called for new AI safety rules (Reuters).

As discussed in our September 16 Data Center Credit Digest, political push-back on data center
development has intensified. It has taken three forms: contested local permitting and zoning;
formal moratoria (there have been more than 300 local data center moratoria in the past year

Chinese Open-Weight Models Continue to Advance
As discussed in our August 18 report, Moonshot AI's July 16 release of its open-weight Kimi K3
model, which indexes closely in intelligence with leading US models, raised concerns over
competitive threats to US model developers. The main threat is that Chinese open-weight
models appear good enough to support most workloads at a much lower price than many
closed-weight US models. Our view on the read-through for US AI infrastructure is unchanged.
Lower AI costs for end-users would likely stimulate incremental demand for compute. However,
if Chinese developers take material market share from higher-cost US frontier models, it could,
at least temporarily, hurt the broader US ecosystem, given OpenAI and Anthropic are the
ultimate end-users for a significant portion of overall infrastructure. 
Anthropic Reports Strong Revenue Growth and Positive FCF
On October 14, Anthropic reportedly told prospective investors that 3Q revenue reached
$16.8bn, according to Bloomberg. That is up ~45% from ~$11.5bn in 2Q, which was itself up
~145% from $4.7bn in 1Q (CNBC). Year-to-date revenue of ~$33bn compares with ~$4.6bn for all
of 2025. While growth has moderated from 2Q, Anthropic reported its second consecutive
quarter of adjusted profitability. It reportedly generated positive free cash flow, with an ~85%
adjusted gross margin and a >11% adjusted operating margin.
OpenAI Revenue Headline Misread, but Weighed on AI Names
On October 8, reports that OpenAI's annualized revenue was ~$50bn at end-September
(Reuters) triggered a sharp sell-off in several AI infrastructure names. That figure was well below
the $68-70bn widely reported in late September. However, the gap appears mostly to reflect
accounting: The higher figure was an estimate that included gross sales through cloud partners,
to compare with Anthropic's methodology (OpenAI books only its net share of those sales).
OpenAI also reportedly told investors that its run-rate grew 77% during 3Q (107% for
enterprise) and that it expects to reach $70bn or more by year-end. The company is reportedly
raising $30bn+ at a $1.4trn pre-money valuation and has deferred its IPO until at least 2027.
Nscale Files S-1; Other Neoclouds Delay IPOs
Nscale, a UK-based neocloud and a useful comparison for CRWV, filed its S-1 for an NYSE listing
on September 18, disclosing $103.4bn of active and contracted total contract value. Separately,
it raised $3.36bn in a pre-IPO convertible financing, including a $1bn NVIDIA commitment.
Elsewhere, data center operator AirTrunk delayed the IPO of a Singapore REIT to next year, and
a planned IPO for Australian data center company Firmus was called off.
