The Fed delivers a 
hawkish hold 
The U.S. Federal Reserve held rates steady 
today, pausing after its September hike to weigh 
incoming data. Resilient growth and persistent 
price pressures kept the Fed on hawkish footing, 
though it opted to wait before moving again. We 
expect one more hike by year-end, followed by a 
prolonged pause as inflation moderates. 
Key takeaways 
• The Fed voted to keep its target policy rate unchanged at 3.75% 
to 4.00% in a split 10-2 decision, following its September 
increase. 
• The brief policy statement acknowledged the policy pause, 
assessing that “the accumulation of more information will help 
the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure 
a timely return to… 2% inflation goal.” 
• Eight FOMC meetings per year were assessed to be the “right 
number,” though one meeting will focus on broad monetary 
policy strategy in 2027 with no vote or statement anticipated. 
Two such meetings in 2028 will allow for strategic thinking about 
“big questions.” 
• Chair Warsh struck an optimistic tone on the economy, 
acknowledging that more work is needed to deliver price 
stability. But the tightening in financial conditions bought 
policymakers time to assess incoming data before making 
another move, likely in December.  
What happened? 
The FOMC paused in October after its September rate increase, with 
a divided 10-2 vote. The dissents contrast with September's 
unanimous decision to raise rates for the first time since 2023 and 
reflect uncertainty over the inflation outlook.

The brief statement described the economy as strong and domestic activity as resilient amid elevated 
uncertainty. It again offered no forward guidance, saying only that the current stance will ensure a 
"timely" return to the 2% target. 
Investors expected a clear message after September's surprisingly hawkish, decisive hike. The Fed instead 
cited recent tightening in financial conditions and said more information will guide its policy stance. This 
signals another increase by year-end, but beyond that, the outlook is less clear given the Fed's data-
dependent approach. 
In the press conference, Chair Warsh struck a constructive tone, noting the "overwhelming judgement at 
the FOMC is that monetary medicine is starting to work." He indicated the coming period should bring 
more clarity that inflation is returning to target in a timely manner. 
Core inflation has surprised to the upside in recent months, and near-term price pressures could persist. 
Distortions from last year's government shutdown complicate the trend, likely lifting year-over-year 
prices in coming months and keeping a year-end hike on the table. We still expect inflation to moderate 
through 2027 and labor markets to stay stable or loosen slightly, easing pressure to raise rates next year. 
Our year-end forecast for the 10-year Treasury yield is 4.95%. 
Risk assets remain a key driver of easy financial conditions, offsetting the rise in real yields, with the 
wealth effect supporting activity. Third quarter growth likely accelerated, though recent equity market 
volatility is a potential headwind. That gives the Fed time to assess before lifting rates beyond what we see 
as neutral. 
The meeting also brought a surprise: the Fed will pursue strategic deliberations in some of its annual 
meetings. Eight meetings per year were judged the "right number," but one meeting in 2027 will focus on 
broad monetary policy strategy, with no vote or statement expected. The count rises to two strategic 
meetings in 2028 to address "big questions." Investors are likely left with plenty to consider after today's 
decision. 
The Fed catches its breath 
The Fed has shifted from its hawkish September stance to a wait-and-see approach, holding rates steady 
despite persistent price pressures. Tighter financial conditions and a softer-than-expected September jobs 
report contributed to the pause. The Fed will assess incoming data before its next move. Having hiked in 
September for the first time since 2023, it has removed the “dose of accommodation,” and we expect 
another increase by year-end. 
Elevated energy prices, renewed geopolitical tensions and higher yields from intensifying competition for 
capital have complicated an otherwise encouraging inflation picture. We expect the Fed to follow through 
on its projected hike by year-end, but additional increases are unlikely given the risk to growth. We expect 
stable growth and moderating price pressures to dominate through 2027, keeping the Fed on hold, though 
oil, tariffs, tech spending and fiscal policy remain swing factors. 
Another hike likely precedes a prolonged pause. September's hotter-than-expected core CPI and 
firm PPI led markets to price in a renewed hiking cycle, but there is limited evidence of a reflating 
economy. While commodity prices warrant watching, recent policymaker commentary signals growing 
uncertainty, supporting a prudent approach. 
That uncertainty was reflected in today's dissents, with 2 FOMC members voting for another increase. 
Governor Hammack has warned of inflation risks from repeated supply shocks, and Governor Cook 
shares that concern. The 10-year Treasury yield's rise of only ~5 basis points (bps) since the September 
meeting masks sharp intra-period swings driving tighter financial conditions, and several policymakers 
noted the central bank has flexibility and can take time to assess the economy before acting.

We don't think the data justifies the additional hikes markets are pricing in through year-end 2027. 
Further out, we still see risks skewed toward a prolonged pause, though oil, tariffs, tech spending and 
fiscal policy remain swing factors. 
Escalating U.S./Iran tensions have pushed oil and gas prices sharply higher and could decide 
whether another hike comes this year. Crude has risen from $101 to $115 since the last Fed meeting, while 
diesel has remained close to $6.30/gallon. Despite the near-term upside pressures, we believe inflation 
will resume a moderating path through 2027 as tariff pass-through fades, shelter costs cool, and earlier 
AI-led price pressures ease. We forecast core PCE at 3.0% by year-end and 2.5% in 2027. 
Today's pause is largely about taking stock. Chair Warsh has signaled the Fed will defend price 
stability, and even with financial conditions assessed to still be accommodative, policymakers are taking a 
prudent approach in pausing. Incoming data will determine the direction of the policy path. The hawkish 
tone keeps optionality to raise rates again, but not so hawkish as to justify the hiking cycle markets 
anticipate. 
A stabilizing labor market gives the Fed more room to focus on inflation. September payrolls 
slowed to 29,000 from a revised 133,000 in August, but still trend above the 45,000 average pace of the 
past year. Unemployment edged up to 4.2% as participation rose, while wage growth slowed to 0.1% 
month-over-month and 3.0% year-over-year, consistent with the Fed's 2% target. We read this as 
stabilization rather than reacceleration and expect a low-hiring, low-firing dynamic with steady 
unemployment next year. 
The Fed's September projections called for 2.3% GDP growth and 3.4% core PCE in 2026, reflecting solid 
growth alongside a temporary inflation shock. Core PCE is expected to fall to 2.5% in 2027 as that shock 
fades, and the updated projections reinforce the picture outlined in June. 
We see the fed funds rate near neutral, limiting further moves. Energy risks warrant watching, 
and upward pressure on yields may persist from fiscal concerns and heavy AI-related issuance. Absent a 
negative growth shock, we remain cautious on extending duration and favor income opportunities in 
shorter-to-medium maturities. We now forecast the 10-year Treasury yield at 4.95% by year-end and 
4.75% by end-2027. 
What does this mean for investors? 
Economic growth has remained resilient despite elevated uncertainty, rising yields and geopolitical 
tensions. In this environment, we see room to add credit exposure, though selectively, given still-stretched 
valuations. Strong earnings support a modest risk-on posture, but the S&P 500's forward P/E sits above 
its long-run average, and analysts forecast double-digit EPS growth, more than triple the 7.5% long-run 
average. Investment grade spreads near 90 bps have been tighter than current levels only 4% of the time 
over the past 25 years, per Bloomberg. 
Rather than abandon risk, we see an opportunity to reconcile a positive economic outlook with rich 
valuations by looking deeper into the fixed income universe. Expanding that opportunity set does not 
require moving lower in credit quality. Instead, we believe the best ideas lie in lesser-known credit 
segments where investors are rewarded for navigating complexity. 
Securitized credit offers a compelling yield profile with lower rate sensitivity than traditional bonds. 
Commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS), asset-backed securities (ABS) and collateralized loan 
obligations (CLOs) can yield more than traditional investment grade corporate bonds, while carrying a 
shorter duration, a valuable trait as rates remain volatile.  
Esoteric ABS, backed by non-traditional collateral such as data centers and aircraft and railcar leases, has 
offered consistently higher yields than equivalently rated corporates. CLOs, a floating-rate asset class with 
near-zero duration of about 0.25 years, have low correlation to most traditional fixed income sectors and 
have historically posted far lower default rates than corporate bonds.

Within the broader market, we also see selective opportunities in preferred securities, leveraged loans and 
certain emerging markets economies with strong fundamentals or exposure to higher oil prices.  
Listed infrastructure looks attractive. The AI boom needs power, and power needs regulated 
infrastructure to generate and transmit it. Data centers can be built in 12 to 36 months, while 
transmission upgrades and new generation capacity take far longer. That mismatch may create durable 
scarcity value and earnings potential for utilities able to deliver capacity ahead of demand. 
As equity portfolios become increasingly concentrated in the AI trade, listed infrastructure may offer a 
compelling complement. It provides exposure to the digital economy through physical assets that are 
largely insulated from the technology they support. Recently, the correlation between infrastructure and 
technology has turned negative, which has strengthened the defensive diversification benefit. 
Preferred securities stand out amid the rate volatility weighing on broader fixed income. Spreads have 
tightened in both $1,000-par and contingent capital (CoCo) segments, helping drive outperformance 
versus most U.S. investment grade sectors this year. 
Preferred issuers look fundamentally strong. About 80% of preferred securities come from banks, insurers 
and utilities — heavily regulated sectors showing sound fundamentals. Technicals also support the asset 
class. European Additional Tier 1 (AT1) CoCo issuers have front-loaded supply, with roughly 80% of full-
year volume already issued and well absorbed, and supply should moderate. Demand remains strong for 
the tax-advantaged qualified dividend income (QDI) that certain preferred structures offer, while hybrid 
preferreds remain eligible for corporate bond index inclusion. 
Opportunities also exist beyond traditional credit. We favor an integrated approach combining 
convertible arbitrage, long-short credit and opportunistic CLO investing, strategically allocated within a 
single framework. 
Convertible arbitrage anchors this approach. Convertible bonds’ embedded optionality means they 
can benefit from volatility, potentially improving returns when traditional strategies struggle. The global 
convertible market has grown to $600 billion, with liquidity at record levels, offering a deep and diverse 
opportunity set. 
Long-short credit adds flexibility. By taking both long and short positions across high-yield bonds and 
senior loans, including distressed situations and tactical hedges, this component seeks alpha while 
actively managing downside, a dynamic tool for navigating cyclical turns rather than a passive bet on 
spreads. 
Opportunistic CLO debt and equity investing completes the framework. High-conviction positions 
in structured credit, deployed selectively when valuations compel, can offer a differentiated return stream 
with low correlation to the other two strategies, which may enhance diversification. 
Together, we believe these three strategies aim to navigate different market environments, with the 
potential for attractive risk-adjusted returns, enhanced resilience during market downturns, and 
additional diversification relative to both equities and traditional credit. Investors who incorporate this 
multi-strategy approach may be better prepared to respond to evolving market conditions.

Important information on risk  
All investments carry a certain degree of risk and there is no assurance that an investment will provide positive performance over any period of time. Equity 
investing involves risk. Investments are also subject to political, currency and regulatory risks. These risks may be magnified in emerging markets. Diversification 
is a technique to help reduce risk. There is no guarantee that diversification will protect against a loss of income. 
Debt or fixed income securities are subject to market risk, credit risk, interest rate risk, call risk, derivatives risk, dollar roll transaction risk and income risk. As 
interest rates rise, bond prices fall. Investing in municipal bonds involves risks such as interest rate risk, credit risk and market risk, including the possible loss of 
principal. The value of the portfolio will fluctuate based on the value of the underlying securities. There are special risks associated with investments in high yield 
bonds, hedging activities and the potential use of leverage. Portfolios that include lower rated municipal bonds, commonly referred to as “high yield” or “junk”

Preferred securities are subordinate to bonds and other debt instruments in a company’s capital structure and therefore are subject to greater credit risk. Certain 
types of preferred, hybrid or debt securities with special loss absorption provisions, such as contingent capital securities (CoCos), may be or become so 
subordinated that they present risks equivalent to, or in some cases even greater than, the same company’s common stock.  
Concentration in infrastructure-related securities involves sector risk and concentration risk, particularly greater exposure to adverse economic, regulatory, 
political, legal, liquidity, and tax risks associated with MLPs and REITs. 
Convertible arbitrage involves significant risks, including the potential for loss on both long convertible security positions and short equity positions if the 
anticipated pricing relationship between the two does not perform as expected. These strategies may employ leverage, which can magnify losses, and are 
subject to liquidity, credit, and market volatility risks that may be heightened during periods of market stress. Below investment grade or high yield debt securities 
are subject to heightened credit risk, liquidity risk and potential for default. These securities, while generally offering higher yields than investment grade debt with 
similar maturities, involve greater risks, including the possibility of interest deferral, default or bankruptcy, and are regarded as predominantly speculative with 
respect to the issuer’s capacity to pay dividends or interest and repay principal. Issuers of high yield securities may be highly leveraged and may have fewer 
methods of financing available. The prices of these lower-grade securities are typically more sensitive to negative developments, such as a decline in the issuer’s 
revenues or a general economic downturn, than are the prices of higher-grade securities. The secondary market for high yield securities may not be as liquid as 
the secondary market for more highly rated securities, a factor which may have an adverse effect on a portfolio’s ability to dispose of a particular security. There 
are fewer dealers in the market for high yield securities than for investment grade obligations. Any investment in collateralized loan obligations or other structured 
vehicles involves significant risks not associated with more conventional investment alternatives.
