The engine driving markets is getting more expensive to run, putting more 
pressure on AI-driven earnings to deliver.
Market Outlook  |  4Q26
Artificial intelligence (AI) has become 
one of the main engines behind  
market returns, driving demand for 
chips, data centers, power, software, 
infrastructure, and capital. Every engine 
requires fuel, and financing costs have 
increased. Higher rates, energy demand, 
and competition for  labor are making it 
more expensive for  AI companies to 
borrow.
Markets are already giving AI a lot 
of credit. The next test is whether 
companies can turn all that spending  
into enough revenue, profit, and cash 
flow to justify the cost. 
With expectations high and capital 
more expensive, investors need proof 
that AI spending can turn into revenue, 
profit, and cash flow. Self-funded growth, 
more than one source of earnings, and 
high-quality income matter more when 
borrowing costs are higher and markets 
have already priced in a lot of good news.

Macro backdrop: The fuel bill is rising
Long-term U.S. Treasury yields aren’t moving only 
because of the Fed. The economy is still growing, 
the government is financing deficits, energy costs 
are higher, and companies are raising capital for big 
projects. Together, these forces are keeping yields 
elevated. The AI buildout adds another demand for 
capital as companies spend heavily on chips, data 
centers, power plants, and grid upgrades.
Inflation feels sticky. But our leading indicators of 
inflation show a easier path ahead. The number 
of categories over 3% in personal consumption 
expenditures (PCE) on a 6-month basis are gradually 
rolling over and wages are not perky. These factors 
will make inflation data look better in 2027.

U.S. equities: Still in the driver’s seat
U.S. equities remain the main source of market 
strength. Large companies tied to technology, 
semiconductors, software, and AI infrastructure still 
have sales growth, earnings power, and cash flow. 
Those advantages matter more when money costs 
more, because companies that can fund their own 
growth don’t have to rely as much on outside financing.
The headline index still hides how narrow the 
advance has been. In late September, only about 
half of S&P 500 companies were trading above their 
200-day moving averages, and roughly three-quarters 
fell during the month, even as the largest technology 
stocks lifted the index. Concentrated leadership can 
sustain markets for a while, but broader profit growth 
would make the advance steadier.

The AI cycle still has room to run. Demand is moving 
beyond training models into day-to-day use, where 
AI tools answer questions, write code, and help 
automate work. Chip and hardware companies are 
still signaling steady investment. Even so, spending 
growth could slow from here, and investors are likely 
to separate companies that can earn real returns from 
those that can’t. 
Our view: Higher yields raise the bar for risk 
assets. We want investments with visible 
return drivers, stronger cash flow, and less 
dependence on easier monetary policy.

The market will need more proof from the AI cycle. 
We’d become more cautious if earnings estimates fell 
for a sustained period, margins weakened, or large 
technology companies showed weaker returns on 
each new dollar of AI investment. If credit conditions 
tightened at the same time, the market would have 
less room for disappointment.
Our view: We remain constructive on U.S. large 
caps, especially companies that can turn AI 
demand into revenue, margins, and free  
cash flow.
Small caps: Feeling the drag
Small-cap stocks look cheaper, and their earnings 
could recover. But many smaller companies carry 
more debt, rely more on floating-rate loans, and have 
less room to absorb higher financing costs than their 
larger counterparts.
Higher energy and borrowing costs can also 
squeeze profits. Analysts expect earnings to improve, 
but the proof has not shown up yet. Lower prices 
alone are not enough; small caps also need easier 
financing and clearer earnings momentum.

stocks, including Taiwan semiconductors, Korean

That overlap affects how much exposure investors 
are really getting. Developed markets remain 
uneven: Japan has the stronger company story, while 
parts of Europe depend more on fiscal spending and 
a clearer earnings pickup. Emerging markets offer 
a broader set of drivers, including India’s domestic

Europe: Running against resistance
Europe has some real areas of momentum. 
Germany’s spending plans, higher defense budgets, 
grid upgrades, and energy-security investment are 
creating openings in defense, capital goods, industrial 
automation, utilities, and banks.
The challenge is that Europe is still running against 
resistance. Productivity is weak, populations are 
aging, energy costs remain high, and Chinese 
competition is tougher. Government spending can 
help certain industries, but it doesn’t solve those 
broader challenges. Government-bond risk also 
matters. If French or Italian bond yields rise too far 
relative to German bonds, European stocks could 
come under pressure.

Business confidence remains solid, and wage growth

Rates and the yen now matter more to the outcome. 
Higher Japanese bond yields can make future 
earnings worth less today and raise funding costs. 
A stronger yen can hurt exporters when overseas 
earnings are translated back into yen, while helping 
an unhedged U.S. investor.
Stocks and currency should be evaluated separately. 
The equity case depends on better company behavior 
and stronger profits. Currency can either add to or 
subtract from those returns for U.S. investors.
Our view: Japan remains our preferred 
developed market outside the U.S., with currency 
exposure treated as a separate decision.
Emerging markets: Multiple engines under one hood
Emerging markets are no longer just a China story. 
The region now has several engines: AI hardware 
in Taiwan and Korea, domestic growth in India, 
manufacturing strength in China, and demand for 
commodities and infrastructure.
Taiwan and Korea are closely tied to the AI buildout. 
Semiconductor supply remains tight, which is helping 
earnings. That link creates opportunity, but it also 
means these markets can move with the same AI 
spending cycle that is driving parts of the U.S. market. 
India offers a different engine through domestic 
investment, consumer demand, and services, 
although high valuations and oil prices remain risks.
China still needs a split view. Exports and selected 
technology platforms are holding up better than the 
domestic economy. Consumer confidence, property 
activity, private investment, and credit demand remain 
weak. Policy changes may help margins in some 
industries, but a broader recovery still needs stronger 
consumer demand and better credit flow.

Commodity-producing countries can benefit from 
the same demand for power, infrastructure, and 
defense spending. Selection still matters because 
policy quality, external balances, and political risk vary 
widely across countries. China’s domestic-demand 
recovery also remains a key swing factor for the

Our view: Our emerging-market overweight is 
selective. We favor AI hardware in Taiwan and 
Korea, domestic demand in India, and commodity 
or infrastructure beneficiaries, while staying 
cautious on China’s domestic recovery.
Fixed income: The pressure gauge
Higher yields give investors more income, but they 
also show how much borrowing costs have risen. 
Bonds still play an important role, but they may not 
always cushion stock losses. If inflation, energy prices, 
or heavy government borrowing push rates higher, 
stocks and bonds can both come under pressure.
When inflation stays elevated, bond yields often 
do, too—and that can put pressure on bond prices. 
Intermediate U.S. Treasuries, agency securities, and 
high-quality securitized and corporate credit can 
help generate income while leaving room to adjust if 
growth weakens. Long-term bonds offer income, but 
they are more vulnerable if rates stay high or rise.

Currencies: Power flows through the system
Currency moves can change what investors actually 
earn. A strong foreign stock or bond return can 
look weaker once converted back into U.S. dollars. 
A stronger dollar can also make borrowing harder 
outside the U.S. Currency moves are one way that 
higher funding costs travel through the global system. 
Countries and companies that rely on dollar funding 
may face higher costs when the dollar rises. Higher 
yields in other developed markets have also made 
some currency trades less attractive. Together, these 
pressures can weigh on borrowers that depend on 
foreign capital.
Dollar demand remains supported by resilient U.S. 
growth, high long-term U.S. Treasury yields, and global 
demand for dollar funding. When funding gets tighter, 
stronger balance sheets matter more. Countries with 
credible central banks, manageable outside borrowing 
needs, healthy reserves, and disciplined policy look 
better positioned if dollar funding remains expensive.

Portfolio construction: Look under the hood
AI exposure can show up in more places than 
investors may expect. It can appear in the bonds 
of large technology companies, data-center real 
estate, utilities, power infrastructure, semiconductor 
suppliers, commodities, and North Asian equities. If 
many of those holdings rely on the same AI spending 
cycle, a portfolio may be less diversified than it looks.

Our goal is to build portfolios that don’t depend on 
everything going right at the same time. That means 
pairing select stock exposure with high-quality 
income, some protection against inflation surprises, 
and enough liquidity to adjust when markets shift.
From here, we’re watching whether the market has 
enough power to keep running. Cooler inflation and 
softer labor demand could help by easing pressure 
on rates. Weaker AI cash-flow results, wider credit 
spreads, slower Chinese demand, or renewed U.S. 
dollar strength would be signs the engine is starting 
to strain.
Conclusion: Follow the power source
We’re staying selective because capital still has a 
cost. We favor holdings with steady earnings, healthy 
cash flow, and funding that can hold up if rates stay 
higher for longer.
For investors, the same discipline applies: know 
what’s powering returns, where risks may be 
clustered, and whether too much depends on the 
same source of fuel.

Glossary of terms
Active management is an investment approach in which 
portfolio managers choose investments rather than simply 
tracking an index.
Agency securities are bonds issued or guaranteed by U.S. 
government-related agencies or government-sponsored 
entities.
Asset class is a group of investments with similar traits, such 
as stocks, bonds, real estate, or commodities.
A basis point is a small unit of measure. One basis point 
equals 0.01%.
Bond yield refers to the income an investor earns from a 
bond, shown as a percentage of the bond’s price.
Borrowing costs are the interest rate or cost a borrower 
pays to raise money.
Capital is money used to run, grow, or invest in a business, 
project, or economy.
Capital discipline refers to careful use of money so that 
spending, borrowing, or investing is expected to create 
enough return.
Capital spending refers to money a company spends on 
long-term assets, such as equipment, buildings, technology, 
or infrastructure.
Cash flow is the money moving into and out of a business.
A central bank is an institution that helps manage a 
country’s money supply and interest-rate policy, such as the 
Federal Reserve in the United States.
Core bonds are higher-quality bonds that can provide 
income and help balance a portfolio.
Corporate bonds are bonds issued by companies to borrow 
money from investors.
Cost of capital refers to the cost a company, government, or 
borrower pays to raise money through debt or equity.
Credit refers to lending money to a borrower, such as a 
company or government, with the expectation of being 
repaid with interest.
Credit conditions refer to how easy or hard it is for 
borrowers to get financing.
Credit risk is the risk that a borrower’s finances weaken or 
that the borrower may not repay as expected.
Credit spreads are the extra yield investors receive for 
owning a bond with more credit risk than a U.S. Treasury 
bond.
Currency exposure refers to the effect that exchange-rate 
changes can have on investment returns.
Data centers are facilities that hold computer servers and 
networking equipment used to store, process, and move 
data.
Deficit is the result of a government spending more than it 
collects in revenue.
Duration measures the sensitivity of a bond’s price to 
changes in interest rates.
Earnings refer to a company’s profit after costs and 
expenses.
Earnings estimates refer to analysts’ forecasts of a 
company’s future profits.
Emerging markets are countries with developing financial 
markets and economies, which may offer growth potential 
but can carry higher risk.
Equities refer to stocks, or ownership shares in a company.
External balances refer to a country’s financial position 
with the rest of the world, including trade, borrowing, and 
reserves.
The Federal Reserve is the U.S. central bank, often called 
the Fed, which helps set interest-rate policy.
Fixed income is a category of investments such as bonds 
that typically pay income to investors.
Free cash flow refers to cash a company has left after 
paying expenses and investing in the business.
Funding costs refer to the cost a company, government, or 
borrower pays to get financing.
Government-bond spreads are the difference between the 
yields of two government bonds, often used to compare risk 
between countries.
Hedging is an investment approach used to reduce a 
specific risk, such as currency risk.
High-quality bonds are bonds issued by borrowers 
considered more likely to repay their debt.
High-yield bonds are bonds issued by borrowers with 
weaker credit profiles that usually pay higher income to 
compensate investors for added risk.

Income refers to money an investment pays to an investor, 
such as bond interest or stock dividends.
Inflation is a general rise in prices that reduces what a dollar 
can buy over time.
Interest-rate risk is the risk that an investment’s value may 
fall when interest rates rise.
Investment-grade bonds are bonds issued by borrowers 
with stronger credit profiles and a lower expected risk of 
default.
Liquidity refers to how easily an investment can be bought 
or sold without a large effect on its price.
Long-term maturities are bonds or loans that come due 
many years in the future.
Market breadth is a measure of how many stocks are 
participating in a market move.
Margins are a measure of how much profit a company keeps 
from its sales after costs.
Moving average is a measure of an investment’s average 
price over a set period, often used to judge its trend.
200-day moving average represents a stock’s average 
price over the past 200 trading days, often used to judge 
whether the longer-term trend is improving or weakening.
Non-investment-grade bonds are bonds issued by 
borrowers with weaker credit profiles; also called high-yield 
bonds.
Overweight, neutral, and underweight are portfolio views 
that indicate whether an investment is held above, near, or 
below its normal target weight.
Portfolio diversification refers to spreading investments 
across different areas to reduce reliance on any single 
source of return or risk.
Real estate investment trusts (REITs) are companies that own 
or finance income-producing real estate and trade like stocks.
Real yields refer to bond yields after adjusting for  
expected inflation.
Refinancing refers to replacing existing debt with new debt, 
often at a different interest rate.
Risk assets refer to investments such as stocks, high-yield 
bonds, or real estate that can offer higher returns but may 
also have larger price swings.
Securitized credit refers to bonds backed by pools of loans 
or other assets, such as mortgages, auto loans, or credit-
card receivables.
Sovereign risk is the risk that a government’s finances, 
policies, or political conditions could hurt its bonds, currency, 
or markets.
Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) are U.S. 
government bonds whose principal adjusts with inflation.
U.S. Treasury yields refer to the interest rates the U.S. 
government pays to borrow money for different lengths of time.
Valuation is a way to judge whether an investment looks 
expensive or inexpensive compared with its earnings, cash 
flow, assets, or history.
Volatility measures how much and how quickly an 
investment’s price moves up or down.
Yield represents the income an investment pays, usually 
shown as a percentage of its price.

The STOXX Europe 600 Index measures large-, mid-, and 
small-cap stocks across developed European markets.
