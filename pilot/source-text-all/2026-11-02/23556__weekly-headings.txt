• Is A Shift in Power Likely? | If there is one constant in Washington, it is change. Since 2000, only one election has left the balance of power
unchanged. Our base case is no different: Democrats flip the House (our policy team assigns a 90% probability), while the Senate remains a
toss-up. With Democrats needing four seats to take control, key races to watch include Texas, Michigan, Maine, and Alaska. Beyond
Washington, however, the 36 gubernatorial races may prove just as consequential. With data center policy largely set at the state level,
these races could shape the direction of AI policy, with important implications for investment, economic growth, and equity markets.
• Is a Blue Wave in the Cards? | Midterms have often produced wave elections, with the party out of power picking up a significant number
of seats. In 2010, Republicans flipped 63 House seats; in 2018, Democrats flipped 44. Could we see a similar tsunami-like shift this year? We
don’t think so. While Democrats have built a sizable lead in the Generic House Ballot (+9), the number of truly competitive districts has
dwindled. As a result, we expect Democrats to pick up closer to ~20 seats, roughly in line with the historical average. Still, if a bigger wave
begins to build on election night, we’ll be watching races such as VA-02, VA-07, and key Iowa and Kentucky districts for early signs.
• What Are the Policy Implications? | Coming off one of the least productive Congresses in modern history, the outlook for major legislation
remains limited. Still, several must-address issues loom. Congress will need to pass appropriations bills or a continuing resolution to fund
the government beyond December 12 and avoid a government shutdown, while also raising the debt ceiling, expected to become binding
sometime next year. Spending priorities, particularly healthcare and defense, will likely be key areas of negotiation. But even if legislative
activity remains subdued, policy risk will not disappear. The president retains broad authority over trade through executive action, making
tariffs a potential source of economic and market volatility over the next 24 months. Geopolitical and military decisions, particularly in the
near term, could also remain an important source of uncertainty, with much of that decision-making resting with the president.
• What Is the Impact on the Economy and Markets? | When we rank the fundamental drivers of the markets, politics comes in eighth out of
10. Why? Because fundamentals such as GDP growth, earnings, valuations, and monetary policy ultimately matter more. But this cycle
could be slightly different. The growing number of data center moratoriums could have broader implications given AI’s increasing
contribution to investment, economic growth, and earnings. With that in mind, here is how the election could shape our market outlook:
• Economy—Historically, economic growth has been a bit slower under divided government. Annualized GDP growth has averaged 2.8%
under divided government vs 3.7% under one party control. Notably, 10 of the 13 recessions since 1945 have occurred during periods of
divided government. One reason may be that unified governments have an easier time passing large fiscal packages, leaving less policy
flexibility under divided government if the economy weakens. For now, we are maintaining our 2027 GDP forecast of 2.3%. However, we
will be watching whether data center delays or moratoriums slow the momentum in capex spending and create downside risk to growth.
• Equities—Similar to the economy, S&P 500 returns have historically been slightly stronger under unified government, averaging ~9.5%
annualized versus 8.2% under divided government. But the more compelling trend is what happens after midterms. Regardless of which
party wins, the S&P 500 has gained an average ~15% in the 12 months following a midterm election and, remarkably, has been positive
after every midterm since 1942, a streak spanning 21 consecutive elections. Combined with a constructive earnings backdrop, that
history leaves us comfortable with our 12-month S&P 500 target of 8,450. However, beyond 2028, rising populism fueled by widening
income inequality could bring higher capital gains, dividend, and corporate tax rates, creating a longer-term headwind for equities.
• Fixed Income—The election outcome does not change our outlook for the Fed, where we expect just one hike in December over the next
12 months, or for interest rates, with a 12-month 10-year Treasury yield target of 4.5% to 4.75%. With the deficit running near $2 trillion,
the bid-to-cover ratio remains a key gauge of investor appetite for US Treasuries, and we expect demand to remain healthy at ~2.5x.

Economy
•
3Q GDP expanded a robust 3.4% QoQ annualized, driven by strong consumer spending (+3.8%) and a surge in private investments
(+19.4%), particularly in equipment. Resilient domestic demand and continued AI-related investment supported economic activity.
•
September headline and core PCE inflation rose 0.5% and 0.3% MoM, respectively, with their corresponding yearly rates accelerating
to 3.6% and 3.1%. With August core PCE also revised higher, the report points to persistent underlying price pressures beyond energy.
•
Consumer confidence rebounded to 91.7 in October, driven by easing gasoline prices and improved business conditions. However,
confidence remains on a long-term downward trend, with elevated prices weighing on purchasing power and future spending plans.
•
Focus of the Week: Market attention turns to the labor market, starting with the release of the Job Openings report on Tuesday which
is expected to show openings firmly above 7 million for the sixth month in a row. On Friday, the all-important BLS employment report
will be released, and we expect the economy to have added 70,000 jobs in October, up from an unrevised 29,000 jobs in September.
Equity
•
With 61% of the S&P 500's market cap having reported 3Q26 results, corporate fundamentals remain a key area of strength, as
consensus estimates now call for 29% year-over-year EPS growth. If realized, this would mark the third consecutive quarter of 25%+
earnings growth for the index. However, expectations have proven to be a high bar to beat, with S&P 500 companies beating EPS
estimates by just 2% in aggregate, the weakest earnings surprise rate since 4Q22. As firms navigate a mixed demand environment and
rising input costs, the muted earnings beat rate helps explain the S&P 500's 1% decline since the start of earnings season (10/13).
•
Fortunately for investors, positive seasonality is approaching as we move into November. Dating back to 1950, November and
December have been the strongest two-month stretch of the year for the S&P 500, gaining more than 3% on average and delivering
positive returns 77% of the time. While history is never a guarantee of future results, seasonal tailwinds, particularly as political
uncertainty fades following the midterms, could provide an added boost. In fact, the S&P 500 has gained ~4% on average during
November and December in midterm election years. Combined with resilient corporate earnings and continued AI-driven capital
spending, these tailwinds support our constructive view on US equities into year-end and beyond.
•
Focus of the Week: Given slightly softer than expected capex reported by US hyperscalers this week, markets will be focused on
earnings results from AMD on Tuesday evening to get additional insights into AI infrastructure spending.
Fixed Income
•
With Governor Cook and Cleveland Fed President Hammack dissenting in favor of a second 25 bp rate hike, the Fed held its policy rate
steady at 3.75%–4.00%. Despite upgrading its assessment of economic growth from “solid” to “strong,” Chair Warsh noted that
September’s rate hike is beginning to tighten financial conditions, pointing to wider credit spreads and greater differentiation among
tech stocks, though he stopped short of describing conditions as restrictive. He also announced that, beginning next fall, one of the
Fed’s eight annual meetings will be dedicated solely to monetary policy strategy, increasing to two meetings in 2028. Markets
interpreted the meeting as less hawkish than feared, fueling a rally in intermediate Treasuries as expectations for additional hikes
further out eased. Still, a December hike remains ~70% priced in.
•
Credit markets calmed this week, with spreads tightening across the board. Still, CCC-rated spreads have nearly doubled since
February (to 1,096 bps). With B and BB spreads near historically tight levels, the ratio between CCC and B spreads is near its widest
since 2000, pointing to stress among the weakest borrowers rather than broad deterioration. CCC widening alone hasn't reliably
signaled significant trouble for the S&P 500, so the bigger concern would be stress spreading to stronger high-yield credits (B and BB).
•
Focus of the Week: Alongside key economic data, the Treasury’s Quarterly Refunding (QRA) is in focus. Borrowing estimates come
Monday afternoon, followed by financing details and Treasury issuance guidance before Wednesday’s open.
Washington Policy
•
The Trump administration warned Gulf partners to prepare for a potential short, intense air strike targeting Iranian leadership, nuclear
facilities, and energy infrastructure, though President Trump has not made a final decision. Following Israel’s October 27 elections,
neither Prime Minister Netanyahu’s bloc nor the opposition secured the 61 seats needed to form a government. Diplomatic efforts have
intensified, with Vice President JD Vance opening a communication channel with senior Iranian security officials. However, DC still
wants meaningful nuclear concessions before offering economic relief, while Tehran is demanding relief from the US naval blockade
and sanctions before advancing negotiations.
International
•
New data on German consumer sentiment shows 60 consecutive months in the red. The last positive print was in 2021, shortly before
Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. While the Russia/Ukraine war has weighed on consumer confidence across much of Europe, the outsized
impact in Germany reflects its proximity to the frontline as well as Cold War history. Despite ongoing fiscal stimulus, sluggish
confidence in Europe’s largest economy remains a headwind to sustained economic acceleration for the euro zone as a whole.
•
With Lula da Silva unexpectedly reelected as president, Brazil’s Bovespa Index posted its steepest one-day drop since 2020, and the
currency had its sharpest drop since 1999. This profit-taking came after hefty gains following Oct. 4. Just as the gains felt overdone, we
view the selloff as excessive. Brazil’s economic fundamentals tend to be shaped more by commodity prices than policy decisions.

LEADING ECONOMIC INDEX | The Leading Economic Index (LEI) is a monthly composite statistic published by The Conference Board that predicts future shifts in the business cycle, typically looking six to

they become evident in the overall economy.
MICHIGAN CONSUMER SENTIMENT INDEX | The Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index (MCSI) is a monthly survey-based economic indicator measuring US consumer confidence regarding their personal
finances, business conditions, and buying power. It is a leading indicator for predicting consumer spending, which drives the majority of the US economy.
