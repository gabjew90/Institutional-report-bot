Given the amount of interest from last year’s datacenter contract webinar and the amount
of scrutiny on these contracts recently, we hosted another discussion with the same legal
expert one year later to see what has changed and what remains the same (replay link ). Our
key takeaways include:
Regulatory change is primarily at local rather than national level. While it’s emerging as a
bottleneck power remains the biggest near-term constraint for development.
Power availability and timing remains the key driver of winning a contract: Having
accessible power within 12mo is a platinum asset whereas >48 months is commercially
unattractive. A 400MW site can still be highly valuable if 100MW arrives within 12–24
months with a credible path to the remainder.
Large deals are highly bespoke. While termination-for-convenience is unacceptable,
acceptable cancellation rights generally relate to loss of the power pathway or failure to
meet defined development milestones. Leases may also impose delay remedies such as
free-rent credits or liquidated damages. Optionalities such as expansion rights, right-of-
first-refusals, purchase options, and extensions options are all key negotiating points.
Credit support is critical for neoclouds. Neocloud deals rarely close without credit
enhancement, which includes upfront cash, letters of credit, guarantees, or support from
AI/chip companies. However, the market has shifted from 100% guarantees toward limited
support e.g. covering ~80% of construction debt rather than the full obligation. Since
guarantees amortize linearly, thus reducing the protection over time, while guarantors
increasingly seek step-in rights to take over space and power.
Key contractual “gotchas”: Watch for buried termination-for-convenience clauses,
inadequate or rapidly amortizing credit support, lease transfers that eliminate credit
protection, and excessive tenant control over future expansion, contraction, or campus
development.
What happens if a neocloud fails? Which could happen in the next 1–3 years due
to mismatch between a longer data center lease vs. much shorter customer contracts.
However, strong upfront credit support can protect owners if the tenant fails and the facility
is recovered promptly, and the capacity can be offered at a very competitive rate.
Demand remains strong: There is no visible slowdown in deal activity, although
underwriting is becoming more disciplined. Longer term, demand should broaden from
remote training campuses toward smaller urban and suburban inference facilities over the
next 3–5 years.

DETAILS
KEY TAKEAWAYS
We recently held a conversation with Jeffrey Moerdler1,a real estate lawyer specializing in data centers and digital infrastructure,
to discuss the in’s and out’s in a datacenter contract, and what has changed over the past year. Our key takeaways include:
• Regulatory change in the US is primarily local rather than national. Power availability remains the most constraining factor
near term, but community acceptance and changing rules have also become major concerns. Developers must assess
not only whether a site works under current rules but also whether local requirements may change before the project is
completed.
• Power: The value of powered land depends on the timing of power availability and a reliable path to obtaining it. Power
available <12mo = platinum asset; 12-24mo = gold asset; 24-48 mo = silver; and >48 mo is generally not commercially
attractive. A site planned for 400MW can still be highly valuable if 100MW will be available within the first 12 to 24 months
and the remaining capacity has a credible pathway. Responsibility for obtaining power varies by transaction.
• Lease structure: every large deal (e.g. >100MW) is bespoke, but we discussed several key aspects of these contracts:
• Hyperscalers typically insist on their own lease forms that are customized and favorable to their own benefit. These
forms often contain termination-for-convenience rights to start with that are generally used as negotiating leverage for
other optionality, but termination for convenience should never be accepted and if overlooked can be very risky (even
though it still occurs). Historically there hasn’t been much termination for convenience included as it should have been
taken out of the final contract.
• Optionality could include expansion rights for space and power, rights of first refusal, purchase options, and extension
options etc. Less sophisticated or financially pressured developers may accept many of these terms because they value
the tenant's investment-grade credit and recognizable name.
• Acceptable cancellation rights generally relate to loss of the power pathway or failure to meet defined development
milestones. Some agreements provide extension and force majeure rights, and some allow termination only if the outside
completion date is missed rather than for every intermediate milestone. Leases may also impose delay remedies such
as free-rent credits or liquidated damages. The precise remedies depend on deal size, customer type, and negotiating
leverage.
• Neocloud credit enhancement: Credit support is especially important in new neocloud datacenter developments,
and it’s extremely rare to complete a neocloud deal today without a credit wrapper, which includes an upfront cash
contribution, a letter of credit, a limited guarantee or support from an AI or chip company.
• Credit support shifting to <100% guarantees: The market has shifted over the last year from full lease guarantees
toward limited guarantees, and this is happening across different guarantors including hyperscaler, AI companies and
chip companies. Nowadays, guarantor may cover 80 percent of a construction loan but not all rent under the lease vs.
previously 100%. The developer must fund the remaining equity above the construction loan. Guarantees also commonly
amortize on a straight-line basis over the initial lease term, meaning a guarantee supporting a 10-year lease may have
little value left by year 7 or 8. This is happening. Guarantors are also seeking stronger step-in rights, allowing them to take
over the space and power if they must fund obligations under the guarantee. Previously, such rights were often handled
privately between the guarantor and the customer rather than expressly included in the lease.
• Potential pitfalls in datacenter contracts: the legal expert identified several potential “gotchas” in the contracts that, if
not examined closely before signing, could cause serious issues later:

• A buried termination-for-convenience right
• Credit enhancement that is too limited or amortizes too quickly
• A tenant's ability to transfer the lease while eliminating the relevant credit support (A transfer may be acceptable to a
developer if the original credit enhancement remains in place, because the developer is relying primarily on that support
to make the transaction bankable)
• Excessive tenant control over expansion, contraction, adjacent development, and other future campus decisions
• What’s the downside to a neocloud contract? Our expert expects several neoclouds (probably not the largest public
ones) to fail in the next 1–3 years, due to a mismatch between its 15-year data center lease obligation and its customer
contracts, which may last only days, weeks, months, or, in the strongest cases 1–3 years. However, with substantial upfront
credit support, this could turn into an attractive immediately available contract at a lower cost either for the developer or the
guarantor.
• As an example, suppose it takes $10M/MW to build capacity and the contract has 30% upfront guarantee, if the neocloud
later fails, the owner could regain a completed facility with an effective basis of $7M/MW (assuming possession can be
recovered promptly) which can make the pricing very competitive in the market.
• No demand slowdown: the expert reports no current slowdown in deal activity. He sees more thoughtful underwriting
and greater concern about becoming a counterparty to a speculator, but the overall market continues to move upward on a
“hockey stick.”
• Future opportunities: the expert expects demand for smaller facilities in urban, suburban, localized areas to grow over the
next 3-5 years as the market moves beyond the training on remote campuses and develops more local inference nodes.
