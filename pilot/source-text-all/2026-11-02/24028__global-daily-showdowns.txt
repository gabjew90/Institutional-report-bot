Global Daily 
Market comment 
This week is shaping up as a series of showdowns. In the United States we have the midterm 
elections where prediction markets favour the Democrats overwhelmingly to take control of the 
House and, less assuredly, to also net control of the Senate. 
Flowing on from the midterms we see heightened risk that President Trump will make good on 
his hints to resume the war against Iran. Belligerents on both sides have commented in recent 
days that this time the gloves will be off and the list of targets expanded. Active Brent crude 
futures settled 2.4% higher on Friday but have opened a little softer this morning. US equities 
were dumped mightily with the NASDAQ composite index seeing particularly heavy declines on 
Friday, but Asian stocks are mixed this morning. Treasury yields were steady despite a strong lead 
from Europe where most sovereign yields (with the exception of France, Italy and Greece) finished 
lower. France’s credit rating was placed on negative outlook by Fitch. 
Democratic Senator for Connecticut and vocal Trump critic Chris Murphy was just hospitalised 
after suffering an apparent stroke. Over the weekend he told media that he had been briefed on 
the administration’s new attack plans, calling them “insane” and suggesting that they would lead 
to “devastating escalation and the likely destruction of our commitment to the rule of law”. 
Iranian figures have said that “there are no geographical limitations” to retaliation if the war 
restarts. That is seen as a warning that not only Gulf countries would be targeted, but possibly 
also European countries involved in assisting the US war effort. Cyberattacks are also considered 
likely to be part of any response. 
Israel, the US’s partner in war against Iran, is mired in political horse trading following last week’s 
indecisive election result that saw incumbent PM Netanyahu’s bloc lose its majority in the Knesset. 
Netanyahu was reportedly champing at the bit to launch strikes on Iran before the poll but is 
rumoured to have been reigned in by President Trump and resistance from the chief of the IDF.  
Axios reports that Netanyahu is now urging Trump to strike immediately after the midterm 
elections, but negotiations are underway behind the scenes in a renewed attempt to reach a deal 
that avoids war. Trump will want concessions on Iran’s nuclear program; Iran won’t want to give 
them. Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi says that a plan to re-open Hormuz within two days (the 
offered timeline seems to be getting shorter and shorter) is being explored, but without progress 
on the nuclear issue it seems unlikely that the US would give up its leverage over Iranian oil flows. 
The Israeli opposition bloc, led by former IDF chief Gadi Eisenkot, failed to secure sufficient seats 
to govern in its own right and must now contemplate a deal with Arab parties or with ultra-
orthodox Jewish lawmakers to secure minority government. Both options are fraught with 
difficulty. In the case of the former, even if an agreement with Arab parties could be struck, it 
would require opposition leaders to renege on campaign promises not to form a government 
with those parties. In the case of the latter, Eisenkot would likely need to drop his requirement 
that members of his government accept mandatory military or national service – which he won’t 
want to do.

the hopes of forming a national unity government. That seems unlikely to occur as Netanyahu –

Meanwhile, Poland is facing something of a constitutional crisis following moves by Prime 
Minister and former President of the European Council Donald Tusk’s government to deploy 
troops to secure the country’s constitutional court building. Polish President Karol Nawrocki was 
en route to Senegal for a trade mission at the time and dramatically instructed his jet to turn 
around and return to Warsaw to address the unfolding crisis. 
Tusk and his allies have been critical of the makeup of the constitutional court, arguing that some 
members were appointed illegitimately by the previous Law and Justice Party government in 
2015. Four of six new appointments to the court made by Tusk’s government have not been 
recognised by sitting members, who argue that the constitutional requirement to take oaths of 
office before the President have not been fulfilled. Right-wing figures have accused Tusk of 
launching a judicial coup, while Tusk and his allies have similarly accused Nawrocki and 
opposition figures of being too close to Russia. 
In China last week’s plenary session of the Communist Party’s Central Committee saw its lowest 
attendance since the Mao Zedong era as President Xi Jinping cracks down on figures accused of 
corruption. The Wall Street Journal reports that up to a quarter of attendees that were on the 
Central Committee when the current term began four years ago were absent from the meeting. 
Many of those have been ejected in a series of purges that have seen Xi consolidate his grip on 
power.  
China’s seven-seat Central Military Commission – the supreme leadership body of the Chinese 
armed forces – currently only has two serving members: Chairman Xi himself and Vice Chairman 
General Zhang Shengmin. General Zhang was this week appointed to China’s twenty-member 
Politburo. The scope of the purges at the top of China’s military hierarchy have raised questions 
among some analysts regarding China’s preparedness to fight a war over Taiwan in the near term. 
Meanwhile, in New Zealand voters are preparing to head to the polls this weekend for a general 
election. The governing right-wing coalition of the National, ACT, and New Zealand First parties is 
favoured by prediction markets to secure a second term, with most recent polling pointing 
towards the right-wing bloc extending its advantage over a presumptive left-wing bloc led by 
Labour’s Chris Hipkins. 
A shock decision by the Reserve Bank of New Zealand last week to leave the Official Cash Rate 
unchanged at 2.75% may prove a handy fillip for the incumbents. The opposition Labour Party has 
pledged to reinstate the RBNZ’s ‘maximum sustainable employment’ mandate which was 
abolished by the present government when it came to power to narrow the central bank’s focus 
to fighting inflation.  
If the right-wing bloc is to succeed, tensions will remain over trade, immigration, climate policy 
and the degree of government intervention in markets. The Libertarian-oriented ACT party 
favours broad-based deregulation and market-oriented approaches, the more conservative New 
Zealand First sees a much greater role for the state in directing economic activity and social 
issues. Here, just as elsewhere, reconciliations are never easy.

Tuesday: The Reserve Bank of Australia meets to set interest rates and is widely tipped to deliver 
another 25bp rate rise (including by yours truly). That will mark the fifth rate rise Down Under this 
year and take the cash rate target up to 4.85%. RBA Deputy Governor Hauser recently remarked 
that the Aussie inflation outlook has become “materially worse” and pending events in the Middle 
East this week, it might get even worser. 
In the US on Tuesday we will see the September JOLTS report, but the main event will be the 
midterm elections where prediction markets favor the Democrats to capture majorities in the 
Senate and, especially, in the House. BOC Governor Macklem is schedule to speak to Canadian 
lawmakers. 
Wednesday: New Zealand Q2 employment figures are out, with the unemployment rate expected 
to fall marginally to 5.5%. Following that we will see more PMIs (services, this time), preliminary 
Eurozone CPI for October (headline inflation expected to rise to 4% while core remains at 2.5%) 
and the US Treasury’s quarterly refunding announcement. ECB’s Lane will be speaking. 
Thursday: The headliner for the day is the Bank of England’s Bank Rate decision, where a 25bp 
increase to 4% is favored by the Bloomberg survey and by RaboResearch’s Stefan Koopman. RBA’s 
Hauser is scheduled to speak during the Asian session. Following that we get French industrial 
and manufacturing production for September, Italian retail sales for the same and Eurozone PPI 
for September. In the US we get the latest Challenger jobs cuts.  
The Bank of Canada’s Macklem will be speaking again, as will the ECB’s Christine Lagarde and 
Philip Lane. 
Friday: Non-farm payrolls takes central billing with 65,000 jobs expected to have been added in 
October and the unemployment rate to remain at 4.2%. Also on the day we will see industrial 
production figures for Germany and Spain, retail sales figures for the Eurozone, and French 
current account figures for the month of September. The latest University of Michigan consumer 
sentiment report will also be released, and the Bank of England’s Huw Pill will be speaking.
