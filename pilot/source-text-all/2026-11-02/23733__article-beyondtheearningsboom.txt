The third quarter proved a challenging one for global markets. 
Re-escalating U.S.-Iran tensions and tightening supply drove 
extreme volatility in energy markets, with Brent crude oil futures 
surging 40% and the resulting energy shock keeping inflation 
fears firmly in focus. Monetary conditions tightened further as 
a number of central banks raised policy rates, while mounting 
fiscal pressures and sovereign debt concerns compounded the 
pressure on longer-duration bonds, pushing the 10-year U.S. 
Treasury yield up 50 bps over the month, closing September at 
5.29%1—its highest level in more than two decades.
It was also a choppy quarter for equities. July saw a reversal among some of the 
strongest year-to-date beneficiaries of the artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure 
build-out, with semiconductors falling sharply and other areas of physical AI 
infrastructure coming under pressure. These losses were largely recovered as 
the quarter progressed, with the release of several next-generation autonomous 
agents and frontier models contributing to a notable improvement in AI sentiment 
during September. At the same time, market participation narrowed considerably 
over the quarter, with less than one-third of MSCI World constituents 
outperforming the index in September, down from around two-thirds in July.2 The 
relative performance of our quality portfolios broadly followed this round trip, 
improving as participation broadened before weakening again as it narrowed.
The MSCI World Index’s modest 2% gain in the third quarter takes its year-
to-date return to a respectable 12%. Underpinning this performance has been 
an extremely buoyant earnings backdrop, with the index’s 12-month forward 
earnings per share (EPS) rising 23% year-to-date, even as its multiple has 
fallen 10% to 18x next-12-month (NTM) price-to-earnings (P/E)—below its 
COVID-era peak of 21x and only slightly above its 10-year historic average of 
17x. The earnings momentum has been even stronger for the S&P 500 Index, 
where forward earnings are up 31% year-to-date.

While the earnings strength has been a nearly market-wide 
phenomenon (with only four of the index’s 11 sectors—utilities, 
health care, consumer staples and real estate—delivering EPS 
growth of less than 10%), the bulk of earnings upgrades for 
the index come from two distinct pockets: energy and AI.
In energy, persistently high oil prices have driven EPS growth 
of 60% year-to-date, while the AI deployment boom has 
propelled earnings growth in semiconductors (+111% EPS), 
hardware (+38%) and the hyperscalers (+32%). Aggregating 
energy and these three “AI-enablers” areas together explains 
over 70% of the increase in index earnings growth. Taking into 
account that AI spend has also benefited pockets of industrials 
supporting the physical data centre build-out, the true 
earnings growth concentration is likely to be even greater.
Beyond the obvious heavy reliance on AI-related earnings 
growth and volatile energy prices, we would point to two 
factors which make us question the quality of the market’s 
earnings growth. First, the earnings boom has not been 
matched by cash generation: while the MSCI World 12-month 
forward EPS has risen by 23% year-to-date, its free cash flow 
growth has been 12 percentage points lower. In other words, 
the improvement in underlying corporate economics is less 
pronounced than headline earnings growth would imply, as the 
semiconductor companies and other infrastructure providers 
take 100% of the AI buildout revenue and profit today, while 
those spending the money capitalise most of their costs.
Second, this exceptional earnings growth comes from a 
starting point at which corporate margins are already close 
to historical highs. While today’s extraordinary AI earnings 
are accruing to the companies supplying the build-out, we 
have yet to see a commensurate benefit to margins among 
corporate customers buying and deploying the technology. 
There is evidence of productivity gains in areas such as 
advertising and coding, but these have yet to translate into a 
material earnings benefit across the wider corporate sector, 
which implies that the margin improvement has largely been 
driven by the AI-enablers, rather than adopters.
In our view, two areas in particular will test how well this 
earnings backdrop holds up from here.
Can corporates sustain earnings in a 
higher-inflation world?
Higher inflation may support nominal revenues and earnings, 
but periods of high inflation have not historically translated 
into higher returns on equity for corporates. As Warren Buffett 
observed in his 1977 Fortune article, How Inflation Swindles the 
Equity Investor, as prices rise, so too does the capital required 
to sustain a business, with inventories and productive assets 
ultimately needing to be replenished at higher costs.
Keeping quality investors on their toes, inflation can also 
temporarily gift even relatively low quality businesses the 
appearance of pricing power, as they may initially be able 
to pass higher costs on to customers. As inflation persists, 
however, and higher interest rates compound rising input 
and labour costs, genuine pricing power should become 
easier to distinguish, with those businesses better placed to 
protect their economics.
Can the AI earnings boom endure?
The sustainability of earnings in parts of the AI complex 
will largely depend on whether the AI build-out has created 
newfound and durable pricing power across the supply chain. 
Putting to one side the rare bottlenecks, the usual rule of 
capital cycles is that exceptionally attractive economics 
incentivise additional investment and bring new capacity 
online. Some of today’s supply constraints may persist for 
some time, not least because the relatively short replacement 
cycle for leading-edge AI chips could create a recurring source 
of demand. Current earnings expectations appear to assume 
that demand will remain strong enough to absorb much of 
that additional supply and sustain today’s unusually favourable 
economics. The question is whether AI can indeed reverse the 
multi-decade trend of memory, power and other components 
becoming cheaper per relevant unit of output, or whether 
we are experiencing a more traditional—albeit exceptionally 
strong—capital cycle in which increased demand ultimately 
brings forth increased supply. The distinction will be 
enormously consequential in areas such as memory, where 
operating margins have moved from a historical through-cycle 
range of roughly 0%-30% towards 70%-80%.
The opportunity 
Today’s market is underpinned by earnings growth that is 
exceptional by any historical standards. More than 20% 
earnings accretion in just the first nine months of the year 
has rarely been seen outside of one-off recoveries from 
earlier sharp cyclical slowdowns, like COVID or the Global 
Financial Crisis, never mind from a very high margin base. 
The sustainability of this growth will have a consequential 
impact on the direction of broader market returns.
Against this backdrop, we find the opportunity in quality 
compounders particularly compelling. This group, in our 
view, offers resilient growth and proven pricing power, which 
should translate in reliable earnings compounding, with 
much lower sensitivity to the AI build-out, commodity prices 
and inflation cycles than the broader market.
Importantly, investors are currently paying relatively little 
for that optionality, as the premium attached to the more 
dependable earnings growth we seek has compressed. Our
