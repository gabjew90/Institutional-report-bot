An unlikely election issue
 
 
It may be apocryphal, but recently I was told of a survey done in the US where a majority of 
respondents answered that they would rather live next to a nuclear power plant than a hyperscale 
data center.
 While unverified, this answer pretty much sums up public sentiment around the buildout 
of new large data 
centers and
 underlines the remarkable fact that they have become a major issue in 
the 2026 US mid
-term elections.
 
 
So what’s going on here, and why the hostility?
 There are a whole cacophony of reasons, ranging from 
fear of the impact of Artificial Intelligence, the identification of the data center boom with President 
Trump, the vast scale of the buildings and fears over the usage of water, a precious resource in m
any 
areas of the country.
 However, the major reason that is cited again and again
 is power; the fact that 
hyperscale data centers consume huge amounts of electricity at a time when energy prices are a 
significant pressure on household budgets.
  
 
The power problem
 
 
The very clear answer to the power problem is “user pays”; the principle that the hyperscaler must pay 
for the new electricity generation that they require.
 However, we believe that, in order to get a “social 
license” to develop and build, the hyperscalers now have to do more.
 This license 
– not a piece of 
paper, but a majority community support, or at least a sufficient dampening of hostility 
– will require 
them to fund electricity generation and grid buildout over and above their own needs, and to offer 
subsidies to existing b
ill payers as a quid pro quo to operate in their communities.
 
 
 
 
A roadmap for growth
 
 
A number of hyperscalers
, in conjunction with local utilities, have developed plans to do exactly this, 
and we believe that as infrastructure investors these utilities represent the optimal way to benefit from 
the hyperscale data center buildout.
 Here are some specific examples:
 
Building the social contract for data cent
er growth 
In this article Jim Wright, Fund Manager of the Premier Miton Global 
Infrastructure Income Fund, explores how utilities are developing innovative 
structures that allow data cente
r expansion to support both shareholders and 
local communities through investment in generation, grid infrastructure and 
customer savings.

• 
In Arkansas the local utility, Entergy (a stock we own in the Premier Miton Global 
Infrastructure Income Fund) recently announced the second phase of its plan to support 
Google’s hyperscale data center buildout in the town of West Memphis.
 As part of this plan 
Google has committed to pay the full cost for the new power resources it needs, and to fund 
grid investments which will result in $2.1 billion of net savings to other customers starting in 
2028 and extending over 20 years.
 
• 
NiSource, the local utility in Indiana (another stock we own), has created a similar structure 
around a new data center campus developed by Microsoft in its service territory.
 They will 
ensure similar tangible benefits offered to local billpayers in conjunction with Microsoft fully 
funding the generation and grid buildout required to serve its own needs.
 In October 2026 
NiSource announced that an expanded agreement would result in approximately $1.75 
billion of savings for existing customers, which the company quantified as an annual saving of 
$140 per household.
 
• 
American Electric Power (yes, we own this stock as well) has identified contracted load 
growth of 69 Gigawatts by 2030 across its seven state utility companies, in Texas, Ohio, 
Oklahoma, Indiana, Kentucky, Louisiana and Virginia, with 90% of this growth co
ming from 
data center customers.
 As a result of this growth, the company is forecasting up to $16 billion 
of cost offsets for existing residential customers, as the large load customers contribute to 
fixed expenses over the life of their agreements.
 
 
The frameworks that utilities such as Entergy, NiSource and American Electric Power have started to 
put in place around hyperscale data center construction can be seen as a roadmap for other utilities 
in other service areas to, in effect, gain a social lic
ense to power the data center growth while 
providing tangible benefits to local communities.
 In some states and regions where it is tougher to 
build new electricity generation, where political hostility is elevated or where there are other regional 
nuances, this roadmap may not lead to the same destination. Overall, however, we see huge 
opportunit
ies for those utilities who can build a consensus, and this is where we are focused as 
infrastructure investors.
