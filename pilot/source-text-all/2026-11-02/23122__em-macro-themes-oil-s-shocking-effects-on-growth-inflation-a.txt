Eight months into the Iran conflict, we provide updated estimates of the effects 
n
of oil price shocks on growth, inflation and external balances in EM economies, 
drawing lessons from recent experience and the analysis of our regional teams. 
The effects of this year’s oil shock on growth and inflation in EM economies have 
n
so far been surprisingly muted. High-frequency activity data suggest that EM 
growth has remained broadly stable at an above-trend rate. And, while inflation 
has risen, it has increased by less than expected, with initial concerns about a 
2021/22-style inflation shock now appearing well wide of the mark. 
The muted response partly reflects one-off factors, including government 
n
intervention, strong EM FX and the offsetting effects of the AI boom and weak 
food inflation. But fundamental features of the shock have also played a role. 
First, measured in real terms, the current oil shock is moderate by historical 
standards, even after accounting for wider diesel and gasoline spreads. Second, 
the steady decline in the oil intensity of production has reduced oil’s capacity to 
shock. Even so, we should not be complacent: oil prices could rise further and, as 
our estimates show, still generate significant economic shocks.  
We argue that empirical estimates of the effects of oil price shocks on inflation, 
n
BoPs and (especially) growth need to be interpreted with care, as they are highly 
sensitive to whether the shock is demand- or supply-driven, the period 
considered and the specification used. We therefore combine our empirical 
estimates with calibrated estimates based on each country’s net oil exports as a 
share of GDP (for GDP and BoPs) and country-specific estimates (for CPI).

o

Oil’s Shocking Effects on Growth, Inflation and BoPs 
 
 
In this EM Macro Themes, we assess the effects of higher oil prices on real GDP growth, 
current accounts and inflation in the countries within our coverage. A key aim is to bring 
these results together within a comprehensive and consistent framework. We combine 
empirical evidence with calibrated measures based on each country’s net oil exports as 
a share of GDP, allowing for differences across shock types and economies. Although a 
common methodology facilitates cross-country comparisons, it may not fully capture 
country-specific features. We therefore report our “blended” results alongside those 
produced for the LatAm1 and Asia2 regions by our country economists, which are 
complemented, rather than substituted, by our estimates. 
Muted Impact of Oil Prices on the Economy (So Far) 
Oil prices have risen sharply since the start of the Iran war. Yet economic activity has 
remained surprisingly strong: EM growth has held broadly stable at an above-trend 
pace, while DM growth has accelerated to its fastest rate since the post-pandemic 
recovery and now stands well above our estimate of potential (Exhibit 1, LHS). The 
inflation response has also been much more muted than initially feared across both EMs 
and DMs, and early concerns that the conflict would trigger a repeat of the 2021/22 
inflation shock now appear less likely (Exhibit 1, RHS).

factors. First, governments have been especially pro-active in limiting the pass-through 
to retail fuel prices via the implementation of subsidies, taxes and broader fuel-market

Empirical Estimates of Oil Price Effects: Highly Sensitive for GDP, More 
Reliable for External Balances and CPI  
Estimating the effects of oil price shocks on inflation, current account balances and, 
especially, growth is challenging. Oil’s importance to economies changes over time, 
and the impact of a shock depends on what initially caused prices to rise. If prices rise 
because stronger growth boosts oil demand, the effects are likely to be small—akin to 
wind resistance on a fast-moving car. But if prices rise because supply has been 
curtailed, the effects are likely to be larger—more analogous to running out of petrol. 
Our estimates therefore attempt to distinguish between demand-driven and 
supply-driven oil shocks (see Box 1 for details). 
Exhibit 6 shows our empirical estimates of the cumulative real GDP response to a 10% oil 
price shock over six quarters. The ranking is generally intuitive: the largest losses are 
concentrated among net importers—notably Hong Kong, Pakistan, Spain, Hungary and 
the UK—while net exporters such as Nigeria, Bahrain, Brazil and Saudi Arabia record the 
clearest gains. However, there are some notable outliers (e.g., the positive estimate for 
Philippines, a notable oil importer).  
The magnitude of these responses varies sharply by shock type. Supply-driven price 
increases produce much larger GDP losses than demand-driven increases because the 
latter coincide with stronger global activity, which partly offsets the terms-of-trade loss. 
That said, the distinction between demand and supply shocks should be treated with 
caution, as the empirical strategies used to identify them can bias the estimates of their 
impact.5  
5
Most identification strategies focus on whether global growth is strong or weak at the time of the shock.

Relative to GDP, the estimated effects of oil price changes on current account balances 
are considerably more reliable/robust, in the sense that they are far less sensitive to the 
distinction between demand- and supply-driven oil shocks, and they are closely 
correlated with their main structural driver (net oil exports as a share of GDP (Exhibit 7).6 
Most of the adjustment comes within two quarters, consistent with the effect operating 
mechanically through the oil import bill rather than through slower-moving volume or 
income channels. The majority of the sample sits within ±0.5pp of GDP, with the large 
Gulf exporters, Norway, and Kazakhstan standing well apart on the gains side, and 
Ukraine, Thailand, Chile, Lebanon and Pakistan recording the largest deterioration. 
There are few outliers but still some (including Vietnam’s estimated positive response 
despite being an oil importer).  
However, when shocks are classified in this way, it is unsurprising that growth is found to perform better in the 
former category than in the latter. The current oil price shock illustrates how misidentification can bias empirical 
results. Although this year’s shock is clearly supply-driven—in that it reflects reduced supply from the Gulf—
most empirical identification strategies would classify it as demand-driven, because global growth was 
accelerating both before and after its onset.

‘Blended’ Empirical and Calibrated Estimates: Combining the Best of 
Both  
To reduce our reliance on imprecise point estimates, we supplement the econometric 
results with data-guided calibrations. Current account responses correlate strongly with 
net fuel exports as a share of GDP (R² = 0.84), as does the impact on GDP growth—
albeit to a weaker extent (R² = 0.14; Exhibit 10). Accordingly, we calibrate GDP and 
current account responses to each country’s net fuel export position, with coefficients 
linearly interpolated between -0.8 and +1.1 for GDP and between -0.9 and +2.5 for the 
current account. 
CPI weights provide little guidance—partly because the sample ranges from high-excise 
DMs to low-tax, highly subsidized EMs. We instead use mean DM and EM responses from 
the pass-through literature, distinguishing between oil importers and exporters (see Box 
1 for further details).

inflation. Conversely, the CEEMEA region has the most polarized effects, with the Gulf 
exporters and Kazakhstan standing out as the clearest beneficiaries, while Lebanon, 
Ukraine and Kenya are among the hardest hit. DMs and LatAm sit closer to the middle on 
all three metrics, with Chile the notable exception given the largest CPI response in our 
sample. Taken together, these blended estimates provide a consistent framework for 
mapping oil price moves into the growth, inflation, and external outlook across our 
coverage.

Box 1: Oil Shock Impulses 
Estimation 
To estimate the effect of oil shocks on the domestic and external outlook, we employ a set of quarterly 
structural vector autoregressive (SVAR) models, each consisting of five variables: an external bloc 
comprising (i) real world GDP and (ii) the real oil price, and three domestic variables—(iii) real GDP, (iv) CPI, 
and (v) the current account as a percentage share of GDP. The models are estimated country by country, 
with the external bloc treated as exogenous to domestic conditions: no domestic variable is allowed to 
affect world GDP or the oil price in any period. We use a VAR to separate the shock from its propagation 
and trace the full dynamic response rather than a contemporaneous correlation. 
We implement two identification schemes. The first is a Cholesky decomposition, with the identified shocks 
taken from the external bloc. The second is a sign-restricted approach: a global demand shock happens 
when world activity and the oil price co-move, and an oil supply shock when they move in opposite 
directions. The two approaches give similar results for the current account and inflation. Conversely, 
effects on real GDP depend on the nature of the shock: the response is positive if the shock is 
demand-driven—which is accompanied by a global expansion—but negative to a supply shock, effectively 
a pure terms-of-trade loss. 
Calibration 
Deriving empirical estimates that yield reliable results across a broad cross section of countries and distinct 
shock types is difficult. For individual countries, various adjustments—from the choice of estimation 
methodology to the length of the sample period—can help ensure that the results are “sensible” (i.e., 
consistent with the country’s structural oil production and usage characteristics). However, this is no longer 
feasible for a large sample of countries, as an adjustment made for one country is likely to distort the 
results for another. To avoid relying on potentially imprecise point estimates, we therefore supplement our 
empirical estimates with calibrated estimates based on each country’s structural oil characteristics. 
Calibration differs across the three variables. For GDP, estimated responses correlate reasonably well with 
net fuel export status. Guided by our empirical estimates, we set bounds of -0.8 for oil importers and +1.1 
for oil exporters, and calibrate each country’s response linearly between them, in proportion to its net fuel 
exports as a share of GDP. For the current account, we apply the same technique, with bounds of -0.9 for 
importers and +2.5 for exporters. For CPI responses, estimates are stable across specifications, with 
demand shocks giving somewhat larger responses than supply shocks. However, the fuel CPI weights have 
surprisingly little explanatory power, because the sample spans very diverse pricing regimes, from the Euro 
area—a high-excise subsample—to EMs with low-tax, high-subsidy markets (see Appendix I). We review 
the strand of the literature that estimates pass-through across large country panels and takes these 
differences into account. We thus recover mean responses for DM and EM, importers and exporters. 
Blended Estimates 
Finally, we combine our empirical and calibrated estimates into a set of “blended” estimates. For GDP—
where the empirical results are highly sensitive to the sample period and type of shock—we place relatively 
greater weight on the calibrated estimates, assigning them a 70% weight. For current account balances—
where the empirical estimates appear more robust—we assign a smaller weight to the calibrated estimates 
and average the two. For CPI, we rely on estimates derived from the country-specific literature when the 
estimate is below 0.1 or above 0.75 (Exhibit 14).
