Which way forward? 
 
Global Daily 
Market comment 
Disappointing earnings reports weighed on equity markets, led by a fall in tech shares. Korea’s 
Kospi index fell 3.5% and the Nasdaq 100 slid 2% lower.  
But all eyes are increasingly turning to the US midterm elections next week, and the risk of 
renewed escalation of the US-Iran war after the polls have closed. Oil prices rose to the highest 
level since April as evidence builds of possible new US strikes on Iran. 
There are still many paths forward from here. This week, we’ve –again– seen several attempts at 
restarting diplomacy. However, none have yielded progress. At the same time, an increasing 
number of recent statements from President Trump, and evidence of US and Israeli logistical 
preparations suggest that military strikes may restart after November 3. The Wall Street Journal 
reports that President Trump was recently briefed on the attack options. 
As our global and energy strategists write, we assume the US, Israel, and a broader Arab and 
European coalition are likely to attack Iran and the Houthis and to defend Saudi Arabia. If such an 
escalation happens, Iran and the Houthis would probably attack the Gulf countries and potentially 
Egypt. This fighting could last until the end of 2027Q1 at varying intensity.  
That will contribute to elevated oil prices. Our main concerns remain diesel and marine fuel oils. 
Between the wars in Iran and Ukraine, nearly 12% of the world's refining capacity for making 
gasoline, diesel and other fuels is offline. Inventory declines will be even steeper if we see double 
escalation in Russia-Ukraine and the Middle East, and it looks like the only path forward to lower 
prices and rising inventories is double de-escalation in both Russia-Ukraine and the U.S.-Iran wars. 
This is the basis for revising our refined products forecasts higher. 
So, energy inflation is bound to stay high for some time. President Lagarde admitted that inflation 
will stay well above target in the coming quarters.  
She said that the ECB debated whether a rate hike was appropriate already, but policymakers 
decided to leave policy unchanged yesterday. Lagarde hinted that the ECB is preparing a rate hike 
in December, but the ECB’s messaging wasn’t strictly hawkish. 
Even though energy prices now exceed the ECB’s baseline forecasts, President Lagarde noted that 
the Governing Council does not see greater risk of second-round effects than in September. This 
suggests that policymakers expect that the latest increase in energy prices will have a stronger 
negative impact on growth than the earlier energy, limiting passthrough to wages and prices.  
Indeed, Lagarde referred to the deteriorating sentiment amongst consumers. The ECB also 
expects that tighter financial conditions will weigh on the growth outlook. Shortly after the 
meeting, anonymous sources tactically leaked internal estimates that the recent rise in bond yields 
subtract around 1 percentage point from 2027-2028 growth and 90 basis points from inflation, 
compared to the September baseline.  
This mixed messaging from the ECB suggests that policymakers hope that they can take a pause 
after a December hike. That’s all, of course, subject to a highly uncertain outlook. But that’s not 
really Lagarde’s concern anymore: she will be leaving the ECB after the February policy meeting.

Lagarde’s resignation kicks of the process of finding her replacement, against the backdrop of 
recent French bond market turmoil. Markets may still be on edge by the time her successor starts, 
given the timing of French elections. So, the next ECB president may have to walk a fine line 
between keeping pressure on politicians to solve their fiscal challenges and limiting the risk of 
contagion.  
Besides, Lagarde isn’t the only Executive Board leaving in early 2027. Schnabel will move to the 
IMF at the start of the year, and Lane’s term ends in May. The departure of three experienced 
policymakers and the challenging environment make experience in central banking a key 
requirement for the next president. Arguably, this favours the two frontrunners: former Governing 
Council members Knot and De Cos. 
 
Day ahead 
Eurozone Q3 GDP is the data highlight of the day. Bloomberg consensus forecasts a growth of 
0.2% q/q. Ireland is bound to distort these figures, though. The Irish economy grew at a rate of 
5.5% last quarter, according to the statistics office. That alone should add some 17bp to the bloc’s 
aggregate growth figure. 
France offsets that somewhat. French GDP contracted by 0.1% q/q in Q3. Household consumption 
was unexpectedly strong at 0.4% q/q, but that partly reflects electricity demand – driven by hot 
weather. Meanwhile, both private and public investment, as well as exports, dragged growth 
lower. Investment fell across a broad range of sectors. IT equipment was the main exception, 
indicating that AI, automation and digitalization continue to support Eurozone growth too – albeit 
to a lesser extent than the US. 
Estimates of the October inflation rate in Italy and Germany complete today’s data for the 
Eurozone. That will be followed by August GDP figures for Canada.
