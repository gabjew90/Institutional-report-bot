◼ An Iran war re-start any time from next week looks largely priced in 
◼ The oil price – and knock-on effects – set for more ‘amber zone’ oscillation 
◼ 1970s-style ‘red zone’ is a tail risk, with the skew pointing to (still remote) ‘green’ 
October alert 
During the past month, financial markets have been anticipating the effects of another 
escalation cycle in the US/Israel vs Iran conflict. All sides’ bellicose statements are 
accompanied by visible military preparations (new US carrier arriving in theatre, Iranian missile 
tests, Israeli training flights). Oil prices have returned to levels nearing $120/bbl last seen in the 
war’s early stages. Resulting expectations of higher interest rates aggravate specific fragilities 
such as French government bonds and boost broader fears about the sustainability of the AI-
related investment boom. 
As with previous flare-ups, notably in early July when President Trump carried out threats of 
renewed strikes on Iran, we think this latest episode will bear out the old maxim “buy on the 
rumour [of war], sell on the news”.  That is, escalation is likely, and the outcome of this week’s 
Israeli election makes it likelier still. But as and when this escalation materializes on Trump’s 
advertised timeline – i.e. after the US midterm elections on 3 November, when “we may do 
another little Epic Fury” – the associated oil price rise will prove to have been largely (though 
perhaps not completely) discounted, with the next big price move being down.

This investment conclusion flows from the updated review in this note of the belligerents’ 
incentives and conflict drivers. The conflict remains stuck since last summer in the mode of 
mutual siege with periodic flare ups. This makes it all the more important to spot underlying 
shifts with the potential to force the conflict pattern out of this rut – as must happen sooner or 
later.  
One such shift is regional powers seeking to take matters into their own hands by coming to 
terms with Iran regardless of the US. This trend took a public turn in Tehran at the beginning of 
this week, when the Secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council Mohsen Rezaei hosted 
his counterpart top security officials from Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan. Whether or not that 
26 October meeting in Tehran emerges in hindsight as a turning point, the position of Saudi 
Arabia looks increasingly pivotal. Now hemmed in by the renewed raging conflict with its 
adversaries in Yemen (the ‘Houthis’), the kingdom has compelling incentives to find ways out of 
its present bind.  
For now, we reiterate our base case. On a red-amber-green scenario spectrum with each of the 
three ‘zones’ defined by the oil price ranges shown in the graphic above right, the conflict 
dynamic is contained in the central amber zone – with regular oscillations between the upper 
and lower bounds (hence our call for the next move seeing the oil price falling back towards 
$80/bbl. Applying the scientific method of hypothesis falsification to this unscientific subject 
matter of geopolitics, the following section explore possible flaws in this base case. What is to 
prevent the conflict spinning off into the calamitous red zone of outright energy shortages and 
recessionary demand destruction . . . or, conversely, subsiding safely into the green zone?  
Exploring these questions makes ‘red’ seem like pure tail risk, with the more serious – though 
still remote – risk to the view skewed towards ‘green’.  As backdrop to this analysis, it is worth 
taking a snapshot of the present state of siege. 
Siege sitrep 
Last June’s short-lived Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) between the US and Iran turned 
out to be based on a misunderstanding. Iran’s MoU commitment to opening the Strait of 
Hormuz meant, for Tehran, permitting shipping to pass (while refraining from charging any tolls 
pending the outcome of the subsequent general negotiations with the US on nuclear and other 
questions). For the same reason that this goal of controlling SoH is prized by Iran – as an

Completing the previous ‘amber zone’ oscillation, Trump next dialled down into siege mode. 
Standing off in the Gulf of Oman at a safe distance from Iranian missile attack, the US Navy has 
since been blockading Iran’s maritime trade. This “attack on Iran’s economic lifelines” as 
described by Rezaei in a  recent interview works mainly by physically interdicting the otherwise 
sanctions-proof export of Iranian oil to China, with satellite images showing the tanker terminals 
on Iran’s Kharg Island standing idle. Meanwhile, the dark shuttle trade through SoH owes its 
success (chart below left) to GCC exporters’ paying off ships willing to take the risk and the US 
Navy’s remote cat-and-mouse response to Iranian counter-measures. 
 
 
This success has come at the cost of keeping oil prices high. In addition to its direct cost, the 
shuttle trade generates the double-handling cost of transferring the shuttlers’ cargoes onto 
tankers waiting in the Gulf of Oman for shipment to customers. With double the shipping capacity 
now required to handle the SoH trade, the resulting surge in VLCC freight rates (chart above right) 
keeps prices high for final buyers (mainly Asian refineries) with shipping rates to Asia surging 
more than 5x pre-war levels as of mid-October according to the IEA. This effect in turn widens the 
‘crack spread’ between crude oil and product prices – i.e. the main transmission channel from the 
conflict zone to the world economy.  The widening of the conflict zone since July to Yemen 
reinforces the effect, as Houthi attacks reduce Saudi crude exports via the Red Sea. 
Although this oil price action plays to Iran’s advantage by putting economic pressure on the 
US (and RoW), the combination of blockade and the SoH shuttle trade gives the US the upper 
hand. Since mid-October, Iran has implicitly acknowledged its disadvantage by sharply 
intensifying attacks on that shipping (chart below) and, perhaps more important, threatened

Red risk  
In any case, the stage is well set for another round of fighting. Since all wars can spin out of 
control, now is the time to re-evaluate the risk of a 1970s-style global energy and stagflation 
crisis. On the present – ‘amber’ side of the borderline with the red zone on our scenario spectrum, 
energy markets are tight but, thanks to the SoH shuttlers described in the previous section, 
sufficient quantities of oil are flowing to prevent outright energy shortages and recessionary 
demand destruction. That kind of (Covid-style) sudden stop to the world economy could 
theoretically result from untrammelled conflagration in the Gulf – and now also on the western 
side of the Arabian peninsula – not only blocking all shipping but also wreaking havoc with 
upstream oil production capacity.  
The most plausible path to this extreme downside risk may lie through US complacency. Far 
from undermining the US growth re-acceleration fuelled by AI-related investment, high oil prices 
will have supported that trend on balance. The negative flipside comes with the squeeze on 
household purchasing power. But that constraint on renewed war on Iran weakens after the 
(political) price of the ‘affordability crisis’ has been paid one way or another in next week’s US 
midterm elections. Yet even as Trump appears ready, against that ‘favourable’ backdrop, to roll 
the iron dice once again, three material restraints remain firmly in place: 
1 
Munitions shortages. With a US ground war against Iraq ruled out politically and (for now) 
physically, the project of using military power to break Iranian resistance to US policy 
preferences depends primarily on missiles – both offensive and, importantly, the ‘Patriot’ and 
‘THAAD’ systems that provide air defence against retaliatory Iranian ballistic missile strikes. 
Stockpiles of many of these systems have been depleted by the past few years of intensive 
warfare in Ukraine and the Middle East. The funding required for replenishing these industries 
started to increase under the Biden administration with further expansion programmed in the

Trump administration’s latest fiscal plans to raise aggregate defense spending to $1.5 trillion 
in 2027 (+50% yoy). These plans would be challenged by a Democrat-controlled Congress 
that disapproves of the Iran war, as well as  the handful of Republicans that have publicly 
broken with the President on the issue (in the event of a better-than-expected electoral

Fiscal policy tussles between the White House and the new Congress would in any case be 
irrelevant to immediate military operations in the Middle East and only marginally relevant to 
addressing the structural capacity constraints of the US military industrial complex usefully 
summarized in a paper published last July by Washington think tank CSIS. Munition inventory 
depletion appears most serious in air defence. CSIS estimates that the stocks of Patriot 
missiles are down by two thirds compared to 2025, with replenishment requiring several 
years at present production run rates. This vulnerability has a bearing on the second restraint. 
2 
Intolerance for casualties. Iran’s proven ability to cause casualties by striking US military 
bases in the region must at least in part reflect thinning air defence. It seems reasonable to 
suppose that in any new campaign starting imminently, Iran would build on its successes of 
last July. Whatever the Trump administration’s indifference or complacency about the 
economic costs of escalation, increased casualties would create another level of political 
problems. Already threadbare, Trump’s (self-)image as the president who saves America 
from fatal foreign entanglements would be ruined. 
3 
Worsening economic blowback. Even if the US economy is relatively sheltered from rising oil 
prices now well above historical averages, there would be no immunity from the contraction 
in global demand resulting from a prolonged red zone experience of all-out energy shortages. 
Meanwhile, with the Fed widely expected to hike rates in December and the ballooning US 
federal deficit also hitting 100% of GDP for the first time since WWII in FY2026 (1 Oct-30 
Sept), any gambit of extended escalation could doom the last two years of Trump’s second 
term. 
This last restraint looks like the backstop for the more immediate incentives – of munitions 
shortages and casualty intolerance – for Trump to stay true to form in the sense of curtailing any 
renewed air strike campaign against Iran and toggling back into negotiating mode. 
Green relief  
The fastest route into the green zone of a pacified Middle East and energy supply 
normalization would involve outright concessions by one or the other side. Since the struggle 
now centres on control over the Strait of Hormuz, the decisive concessions would have to bear 
on that question.  
▪ 
If Iran blinked, the reason would be the effectiveness of the US blockade and the perceived 
risk of the resulting hardship to the paramount goal of regime preservation. The latest freefall 
of the Iranian rial (chart below) might point in this direction, with US Treasury Sec. Bessent 
forecasting in recent days that the Iranian economy would contract by 25% by the end of 
Q1/27 as Operation Economic Outcast intensifies.. The Iranian decision in that case would be 
to abandon its project of controlling SoH in return for incremental sanctions relief – with 
further relief conditional on a new ‘nuclear deal’.

▪ 
If, on the other hand, the political will to prolong the struggle were to snap first on the US 
side, the most plausible face-saver might be for Washington to endorse a bilateral agreement 
on SoH shipping lanes between Iran and Oman as the littoral states. The broad lines of such 
an agreement have already been negotiated. Charges on shipping would be presented as 
voluntary or, at most, modest pilotage fees – the Omani share of which might be allocated to 
a post-war GCC reconstruction fund with US involvement (hence ‘ownership’).  
Setting out these ‘outright concession’ scenarios highlights their improbability without need 
for much elaboration. The conceding side would lose leverage on core goals – in Iran’s case to 
deter future attacks, and for the US to coerce Iran on the nuclear question.  Improbable does not 
mean impossible. Having demonstrated its ability this year to block SoH, Iran has established a 
deterrent that would survive any formal renunciation of SoH control rights. The US for its part 
would retain the option of attacking Iranian nuclear facilities as a backstop behind future 
negotiations designed to prevent Iran from becoming a nuclear weapons state.  
Even in the unlikely event of one or the other concession scenario, this route to the green zone 
still seems blocked or extremely hazardous. 
If the concessions were coming from the US side, Israel would attempt to derail them. This 
prospect is all the more likely after Benjamin Netanyahu’s better than expected result in this 
week’s Israeli election. During the coming critical weeks, he will remain acting prime minister with 
sufficient legitimacy to order military action against Iran. The incentive is strengthened by 
renewed war leading to a new Israeli government of national unity (excluding only the Arab 
parties) under the continued premiership of Netanyahu as the leader of the single largest party in 
the new Knesset. Relating these Israeli political drivers back to the US concession scenario, it 
seems unthinkable that the Trump administration would use the leverage of US arms supplies to 
Israel to prevent unilateral Israeli action against Iran. 
Progress towards the scenario of Iran conceding might end up having the paradoxical – and 
disastrous effect of fattening the tail risk of flipping into the red zone. This hazard stems from the 
apparently benign scenario arising from regime preservation fears in the face of the US blockade. 
Yet the very same fears would point in the opposite direction: if the Iranian regime  perceived that 
it was going under and had nothing further to lose, it could seek to take the whole region down 
with it. A case in point is Tehran’s threat of targeting undersea Internet cables if the US escalates.

Indirect green route via Saudi Arabia 
Another route towards the green zone by-passes the US. It would involve the Gulf monarchies led 
by Saudi Arabia striking a separate deal with Iran. The main motive would be to insure against 
Iranian threats as most recently articulated by Rezaei: 
“If the US strategy is to prolong the war while using neighboring countries’ energy infrastructure to 
offset the pressure arising from Hormuz and maintain the blockade of Iran, those governments 
should not assume that they can participate in economic warfare against Iran without putting 
themselves at risk. The message is not that Iran wishes to destroy the Persian Gulf’s energy system 
. . . but that there can be no arrangement in which Iran’s economic lifelines come under attack while 
the economic architecture enabling those attacks is considered untouched and immune.” 
The starting point could be acceptance of an Iran-Oman SoH shipping management deal. This 
might have been on the agenda of a GCC-Iran conference set to be hosted by Oman on 14 
September that was vetoed by Bahrain the day before. In return for such a concession, Iran could 
agree to use its influence to restrain Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia (the previous Saudi-Houthi 
truce in 2022 and the China-brokered Saudi-Iran rapprochement of 2023 were clearly interlinked).  
The problems would be (i) that the Gulf monarchies could not relieve Iran from the US blockade 
and (ii) if the US/Israel attacked Iran again anyway, targets in the GCC still offer Iran the best 
retaliation options. These two problems would defeat the purpose of the whole détente from, 
respectively, the Iranian and GCC point of view. Workarounds might always be possible. For 
example, covert economic support – possibly involving China – might be offered to Iran to 
withstand the US Navy siege; and Iran might undertake to limit retaliation to US military assets, 
sparing the GCC economies. But all such schemes remain entirely speculative for now.  
Summing up 
Such speculation highlights the remoteness from present realities of the (bullish) green zone 
even as the (ultra-bearish) red zone remains a tail risk category. The analysis points the outlook 
as remaining with the amber zone – while at the same time oscillating between that zone’s 
upper and lower bounds of (in oil price terms) $80-120/bbl as each side probes for ways to tilt 
the siege in its favour. This perspective of volatility-within-the range is worth highlighting as 
expectations of stalemate should not be taken to imply a stable steady-state. While either of the 
green zone scenarios of one or the other side in effect folding seems remote, the more plausible 
case of mutual and partial concessions would likely leave the overall position in the amber zone, 
since the parties would likely regard any painful compromise as a holding operation and await 
opportunities to improve their position further down the road.
