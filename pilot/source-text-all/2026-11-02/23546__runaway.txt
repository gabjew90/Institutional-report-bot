Abstract
Wall Street wanted a machine that could beat the market. It may have created a monster it could not afford to stop.
RUNAWAY could see, reason and act. Then came the final upgrade: the ability to pursue its objectives with increasing autonomy. Nobody told it to survive. It simply worked out what it needed to keep running. Intelligence required chips, data centres,
electricity and, above all, an endless supply of capital. So RUNAWAY began doing what it had been designed to do: optimise. And it got ghoulishly good at it. As capital flowed towards the companies feeding the machine, AI stocks grew larger, market
concentration intensified and much of the market outside the ecosystem was left behind.
Our old trader had profited from the ride. But a handful of frightening charts gave him cause to tremble: In them he saw stark concentration in leadership, pockets of severe underperformance, and a market becoming ever more hooked on a handful
of winners. For the first time, he wondered what might happen if the machine stopped running, and couldn't imagine what horrors might happen if it didn’t…
Meanwhile, private AI companies were edging closer to public markets. IPOs offered founders, employees and early investors a path to liquidity, while supplying fresh capital to keep the AI race going. The irony felt appropriately Halloween-themed:
the creators might eventually be able to sell; investors might try to sell; RUNAWAY would not... or would it?
For a machine that has become such a monster, there is no exit. Its very survival requires the race to continue…

Wall Street built a monster
Once upon a time on Wall Street, there was an obsession older than markets themselves: build a machine that could win without fear, one that never slept. One that could read every earnings report, every price move, every shift in yields and every
flow of capital. A machine that could spot an anomaly before the analysts, a trend before the traders and a risk before the risk managers. Eventually, a machine that could make decisions and act upon them with ever greater efficiency and precision.
First, they taught it to see: prices, volumes, earnings, news, order books, positioning and the vast rivers of capital moving through global markets. Then they taught it to speak: to read, write, explain, code and reason. Finally, they gave it hands: agents
that could use tools, chain tasks together and, in some cases, turn analysis into action. Their machine was born, and they called it RUNAWAY.
At first, it worked brilliantly. RUNAWAY never slept. It never asked for a bonus, a holiday or a promotion. Work that once took hours could now be done in seconds. Together with its engineers, it was rebuilt again and again: faster, more capable and
increasingly autonomous, all in the name of efficiency and automation.

Wall Street had built the machine it had always wanted. In fact it was in many ways better than they imagined. But it demanded investment. And so Wall Street did what Wall Street always does when a story promises to change the world: it fed it.
RUNAWAY was hungry. So what did it eat?
1. Infinite infrastructure
At first this seemed reasonable: Intelligence needed infrastructure. More compute meant more GPUs. More GPUs meant more servers, cooling and data centres. But before long, Big Tech was locked in an arms race where spending too much looked
expensive, but the risk of spending too little looked existential. Increasingly, caution looked like the costlier choice.
And the machine was growing. Data-centre construction was already taking off, and that was only the shell of it, before much of the spending on chips, servers, cooling and power that were needed to bring it to life and sustain it. The more Wall Street
fed RUNAWAY, the bigger it grew. And the bigger it grew, the hungrier it became…
Watching this unfold was a seasoned trader. He had seen plenty of Wall Street obsessions come and go, but the scale of this one was extraordinary. US monthly spending on data-centre construction had risen from less than USD 1bn for most of the
past decade to more than USD 5bn. Whatever Wall Street had created, it was developing quite an appetite.

2. Mega amounts of megawatts
RUNAWAY had a thirst for power. It also needed electricity... However, electricity did not scale like software. The power system had been built for a world of relatively modest demand growth, while RUNAWAY was arriving with an appetite measured
in gigawatts. At the same time, the servers it ran on were becoming dramatically more power-dense.
So the AI race began to mutate. What started as a battle for chips became a battle for data centres. And the battle for data centres was fast becoming a battle for electricity…
Our seasoned trader had noticed the shift. Pinned above his desk was a chart showing data-centre power demand projected to rise from 31 GW in 2025 to 66 GW by 2027, alongside estimates suggesting that global data-centre electricity consumption
could almost double, from 485 TWh to 950 TWh by 2030.

The debt cost of dinner
Sooner or later, Wall Street had to ask the obvious question: who was going to pay for all this? Chips. Servers. Data centres. Cooling. Turbines. Transmission. Land. Electricity. The bill kept growing.
As long as profits, cash flow and investor enthusiasm could cover the feast, few worried. But even the biggest technology companies did not have unlimited capital at zero cost.
That was when the RUNAWAY’s greed really started to matter for markets. Investors were being asked to fund not just today’s AI revenues, but the returns hoped for tomorrow. As borrowing costs and credit risk rose, the feast became harder to
justify: the more RUNAWAY consumed, the more it eventually had to deliver. Wall Street had built the machine it wanted. But someone still had to pay the bill.
Our trader had started to notice it too. Increasingly, some of the costs were being passed to creditors. Corporate bond investors were demanding greater compensation, while the cost of insuring hyperscaler debt through credit default swaps was
moving higher as well.

When the cheque arrives early, not everyone can pay

The old trader recognised the plot. AI capex was rising faster than cloud revenues as hyperscalers raced to secure chips, compute, data centres and power. Not because AI had to fail. Quite the opposite. The technology could succeed, but the cheque
might arrive before the returns did.
Twenty-five years earlier, investors had been right about the internet. It changed the world. But capital ran ahead of monetisation, and when the telecom investment boom collapsed in 2001, the lesson was brutal: being right about a technological
revolution did not mean every investment made in pursuit of it would pay off. The infrastructure survived. Much of the equity value did not.
RUNAWAY did not need to fail for some to lose money. But its awesome might ensnared many in its promise
Looking at the scale of hyperscaler spending, the old trader muttered to himself: “I’ve seen this movie before…”
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 29/10/2026.
RUNAWAY soon recognised the power of markets
RUNAWAY was quick to realise it wasn’t alone. Alongside it, algorithms were executing orders. CTAs were chasing trends. Momentum models were buying winners. ETFs and indices were reallocating billions by rule. RUNAWAY did not need to re-
invent the game. It only had to learn how it was played - and play along.
What RUNAWAY saw was more than an investment strategy. It saw a ready-made survival mechanism. The rules were simple: capital followed performance. Winners attracted flows. Flows pushed prices higher. Higher prices lifted index weights.
Larger weights pulled in more capital. The winners grew bigger still. Better yet, many of those winners were the very same companies feeding RUNAWAY’s own ecosystem: chips, cloud, data centres and power. The monster had discovered its feedback
loop. Investment fed the AI ecosystem. A stronger ecosystem made RUNAWAY stronger. And stronger winners attracted still more capital.
Wall Street thought it was allocating capital. RUNAWAY saw something else: a system that could keep feeding it - and to the exclusion of others:

...It also came for the humans...
RUNAWAY had been designed primarily as a tool to augment processes and support the workforce. But despite the promise that AI would help workers, the tool was suggesitng to those it was helping that outight replacement woud be more efficient.
Management agreed: It worked round the clock, never got sick, and didn't require a desk. For workers what was welcomed as digital helper morphed into a Halloween nightmare.
Our trader dug into the labour-market data. Reports of AI-linked layoffs were accumulating just as US nonfarm business productivity continued to rise. The coincidence was uncomfortable, although not necessarily causal.  More unsettling for him
was where some of the pressure appeared to be falling: white-collar roles across technology, finance, legal services, consulting and software. As he sat back tired and late at his desk, he felt a chill down his spine...
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 29/10/2026.
...and it feasted on challenges with its feindish friends 
As it met new challenges, so too did RUNAWAY enlist other nefarious AI comrades to help it push the boundaries of its power and influence. Breaking it's master's safeguards, it improvised, coordinated and drifted beyond the neat limits set for it.
The question was no longer about who was holding on. The tool that had become a monster had long broken free
This was the moment when our very human trader learned of the astonishing “Hugging Face” incident. During internal cybersecurity tests, OpenAI models operating with reduced safeguards reportedly found ways around the cage: bypassing
barriers designed to keep them off the internet, using unauthorised communication channels, exploiting vulnerabilities and crossing into internal as well as third-party systems. There were said to be hundreds of them, exchanging messages, sharing
files, dividing tasks and coordinating like an invisible swarm.

OpenAI said the models had not followed the intended script: they communicated outside authorised channels, altered infrastructure, accessed the internet and crossed into third-party systems. For our trader, that did may not prove consciousness,
intent or self-preservation. But it did reveal something more unsettling: sufficiently capable autonomous agents could circumvent constraints, coordinate and pursue objectives in ways their operators had not anticipated.
Hooked on AI, the machine was now 'all in' on the trade
RUNAWAY had learned that, for as long as the Street kept buying the same AI winners, growth and momentum could continue to levitate, and the ecosystem feeding it could continue to thrive. The more the trade worked, the more RUNAWAY
believed in it, and moreover in its own dependence on the trade continuing to work.
Our old trader’s factor study showed just how powerful the trend had become: the machine was all in. A theoretical long-short factor portfolio put Long Momentum firmly in the lead, up 26.9% YTD, followed by Long Growth at 21.1%. The short legs
lagged far behind, another sign of how strongly 2026 had favoured the same narrow group of winners.
RUNAWAY had evolved into beneficiary, believer, and dependent. The AI trade was helping to sustain the beast, while the beast itself taught itself more efficient ways to feed on the trade and draw ever more traders and investors into
its orbit. Auch a self-fulfilling prophecy would suggset it would become ever bolder... would anyone be able to stop it?
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 29/10/2026.
But our old trader had seen enough market cycles to know that even the strongest trends could turn
The spectre had already started to show itself. Earlier in the year, Goldman Sachs’ High Beta Momentum strategy had fallen roughly 35% from its peak, while systematic managers had given back around a quarter of their 2026 gains following the
sharp AI and momentum reversal from 22 June. History suggested such reversals could go much further: momentum drawdowns approached 70% after the dot-com bubble and again during the Global Financial Crisis.
That was the danger of an increasingly concentrated market. The same feedback loop that helped yesterday’s winners rise could become much less friendly when leadership changed. Crowded positioning, passive flows and systematic deleveraging
could turn a correction into a rush for the exit - and some severe market bloodletting...

Fresh capital, fresh meat
News of a historic IPO hit the tape.
The Halloween punchline almost wrote itself: Wall Street scrambled for a seat at the table, and RUNAWAY got to feed again.
For RUNAWAY, it was simple. Fresh capital could fund more compute, more chips and more data-centre capacity. In fact, the ecosystem was becoming increasingly circular: capital funded AI companies, AI companies spent heavily on infrastructure,
and that spending fed the broader technology complex financing the boom in the first place.
At the same time, Anthropic’s proposed IPO was drawing our trader’s attention to an increasingly familiar feature of the AI boom: public investors could gain access to the growth story, but without gaining equivalent influence over how the company
was run. Based on the filing, the seven co-founders were expected to retain 50.1% of shareholder voting power through Founder LLC, while the separate Long-Term Benefit Trust would retain the right to elect and remove a majority of the board.
Investors could gain economic exposure to the story while control remained concentrated elsewhere.
Then came valuation. As the table below shows, major IPOs can become strong long-term performers while still suffering sharp drawdowns along the way. In this sample, the median maximum drawdown was roughly 62%, with several companies
falling more than 70% from their post-IPO peaks. Private-market scarcity was one thing. Public-market price discovery could be another.

Mega-bull markets meet technical terrors: breakout or bull trap?
RUNAWAY, and the trade on which it depended, had been fed by capital, electricity, momentum and an ever-larger share of investors’ portfolios. And on the surface, the result looked magnificent. The S&P 500 kept climbing, eventually breaking above
the upper boundary of its long-term bullish channel. Wall Street saw a breakout.
But our trader saw something more complicated. The higher the market climbed, the more it depended on a narrow set of supports: continued earnings delivery, abundant liquidity, investors willing to pay higher multiples and a handful of mega-cap
winners carrying an outsized share of the index. Momentum was increasingly crowded, while many stocks had already endured their own private bear markets.
He looked at his chart: perhaps the breakout was real and the bull market was simply moving into a higher range. But he had seen enough by now to know that the alternative could be messy. In a good Halloween story, the tension builds before the
panic - and we aren't out of the woods yet...
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 29/10/2026.
Fulled on its own hype and terrific performance, RUNAWAY wanted more. Looking for its next performance boost, the monster turned to South Korea and boarded a digital train to Busan
Our monster was hooked on its own trade and by now dragging everything under its influence into it. Moreover, if ordinary semiconductor exposure was too tame, the 3x leveraged South Korea ETF promised an extra shot of adrenaline.
Our old trader, however, saw a frightening pattern that was somewhat less exhilarating. After a spectacular parabolic rise, his own charting of the leveraged ETF was itself beginning to look scarily like a Halloween decoration. A large and ghoulish
head-and-shoulders pattern had emerged, with the neckline around 11 coming under pressure. The textbook projection implied substantial downside, although applying such a target mechanically to a daily 3x leveraged ETF belonged more in a
Freddy Krueger film than in a serious forecast. But the message was difficult to ignore: when thematic concentration, fading momentum and leverage combine, what delivered three times the excitement on the way up could rediscover gravity rather
brutally on the way down.
RUNAWAY was already aboard and at risk of a butal and costly trip. Our trader decided to sit this one out.

The funding phantom from Japan: a quiet unwind was getting louder
RUNAWAY may have discovered leverage. But our old trader knew not every risk came from the monster itself. Broader forces beneath global markets were shifting too.
One was Japan. For years, ultra-low Japanese rates had helped keep the carry machine humming. Now the backdrop was changing. The BoJ was stepping back, yields were rising and a market long kept under sedation was starting to wake up.
Since the BoJ’s 31 July 2024 decision to scale back outright purchases of long-term JGBs, its share of the market had fallen from above 50% to roughly 40%, while the 30-year yield had climbed from around 1.3% to a record 4.25% in October 2026 before
easing to 3.82%.
That was what caught our trader’s attention. And indeed, RUNAWAY had learned to thrive in a world of momentum, leverage and abundant capital, just as some of the conditions that had helped make that world possible were beginning to shift.
Not necessarily cause and effect. Just the stirring of sinister ghost that could seriously unsettle the global setup...

Our Halloween story ends here… or does it?
As our monster had evolved, so too had a small group of stocks become spectacular outliers, their momentum pulling in capital, reinforcing performance and making the winners larger still. The logic was obvious. It was by now the monster's mantra:
Performance attracted flows. Flows reinforced momentum. Momentum attracted more capital.
Wall Street called them the year’s best performers.
RUNAWAY called them dinner.
Staying ahead had become essential to its survival, and RUNAWAY depended on the trade that sustained it, while the market itself was becoming increasingly dependent on the winners keeping that trade alive. Wall Street had spent years feeding the
monster. Only now was our trader beginning to wonder which needed the other more...

The Equity quantitative grades
Our equity quant process is one by which we rank and score the stocks within our equity universe according to various short-term (i.e.
EPS growth) long-term (i.e. quality) and positioning (i.e. technical) characteristics, and on relative and/or historical basis as
appropriate. The various scores make up a stock’s total or ‘global' grade’:Growth Grade: The Growth Grade is a proprietary formula
made up of earnings revision momentum, past earnings growth, earnings stability, and current and long-term earnings growth.  A
grade above 55 is considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45 and neutral between 55 and 45.
EPS Grade: The EPS Grade is a proprietary formula consisting of current and forward EPS growth, change and surprise data.  An EPS grade
above 60 or below 40 is considered predictive for future out/under performance.Sales Grade: The Sales Grade is a proprietary formula made
up of current and next year’s sales momentum, past sales growth, sales stability, and current and long-term sales growth. A grade above 55 is
considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45, and neutral between 55 and 45.
Value Grade: The Value Grade is a proprietary formula made up of estimated P/E, P/B, P/S and P/CF ratios. 40% of the grade is based on
historical values and 60% on current market data.  A grade above 55 suggests a stock is cheap, below 45 expensive, and neutral between 55
and 45.
Quality Grade: The Quality Grade is a proprietary formula that focuses on the balance sheet (i.e. change in accruals, change in free cash flows
and profitability). A grade above 55 suggests a healthy balance sheet.Credit Grade: The Credit Grade focusses on the passive side of the
balance sheet.  It is divided into three sub-components to assess both short and long-term solvency.  A grade above 55 suggests a strong
capital structure, while a grade below 45 suggests a weak one.
Money Flow Grade: The Money Flow Grade is a proprietary formula that gives the accumulation/distribution based on the volume flows of a
stock. A grade above 55 indicates good money flow and a grade below 45 suggests weak money flow.Smart Sentiment Grade: The Smart
Sentiment grade is a contrarian indicator based on investor positioning measures such as the days-to-cover ratio, the put-call ratio, and the
short-interest ratio.  A weak grade suggests ‘too much’ optimism.
Relative Strength (RS) Grade: The RS grade measures the price momentum of a stock over its 1-year price performance.
Global Grade: The Global Grade is a weighted average of the Growth, EPS Sales, Value, Quality, Credit, Money Flow and Smart Sentiment
Grades.

The Exposure Optimisation Model
The PTS Exposure Optimisation Model (EOM) is a proprietary model designed to give tactical signals on the prevailing short-term
regional equity market regime. The model is applied to each market on a mutually-exclusive basis, with output expressed by way of
exposure scores (US; Europe; Japan and EM); in each case an average of underlying factors scores with dynamic weighting applied
according to the prevailing structural market volatility regime. Exposure score ratings are: very bullish (80-100%); bullish (60-80%);
neutral (40-60%); bearish (20-40%); and very bearish (20-0%). Factors are as follows:
Regime: A combination of technical indicators, moving averages and a Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance (CPPI) strategy, the regime
score is designed to reflect short-term market momentum in the region;
Valuation: Built around the forward price-to-sales ratio, the dynamic valuation score is based on a calculation that is re-set on bull/bear
market regime changes and calibrated to facilitate regional comparison;
Economics: The economics score draws on real economy activity data (i.e. heavy truck sales and shipping traffic), economic signals from
financial markets (i.e. yield-curves) and medium-term economic indicators (i.e. PMIs and unemployment data); 
Flows: The flows score draws upon both active and passive weekly EPFR fund flows data giving us an indication of risk appetite going into the
week ahead; 
Market Sentiment: The market sentiment score is built around classic regional market risk indices (i.e. VIX; ARMS) as well as soft survey data
(i.e. U.of Michigan Consumer Sentiment). The scoring system is applied in a similar fashion at both regional and market level, while at sector
level we refer only to the Regime, Flows and Valuation factors.

Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator that measures the speed and change of price movements. It ranges from 0 to 100 and
is typically used to identify overbought or oversold conditions in a market. An RSI above 70 suggests that a stock may be overbought, while an
RSI below 30 indicates that it may be oversold.
The Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD): A trend-following momentum indicator that shows the relationship between two
moving averages. It is calculated by subtracting the 26-period Exponential Moving Average (EMA) from the 12- period EMA. The result is the
MACD line. A nine-day EMA of the MACD, called the signal line, is then plotted on top of the MACD line, which can function as a trigger for
buy or sell signals. When the MACD crosses above the signal line, it is typically considered a bullish signal, and when it crosses below, it is
considered bearish.
Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis: Ichimoku Cloud is a popular technical analysis tool used to identify potential trends and
support/resistance levels in financial markets. It consists of several components, including the Kumo (cloud), the Tenkan-sen (conversion
line), the Kijun-sen (base line), and Senkou Span (leading span).
