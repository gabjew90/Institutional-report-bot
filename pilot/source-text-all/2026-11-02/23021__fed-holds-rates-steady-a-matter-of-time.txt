Fed Holds Rates Steady: A Matter of Time
The Federal Reserve (Fed) held rates steady at 3.75–4.00%, even as markets price in more
tightening amid persistent in lation. Sharp commodity moves and mixed growth signals are
making the Fed’s objectives for in lation and employment increasingly di icult.

Key takeaways
The Fed’s decision held interest rates steady at 3.75–4.00%.
In lation remains stubborn, but base eﬀects could provide some relief in the  irst half of

Growth is slowing globally, even as markets increasingly price in further monetary policy

Federal funds rate remains at 3.75–4.00%
Today, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) announced it has held the federal funds
target range steady at 3.75–4.00%. Markets, however, are pricing in a hike in December
followed by three further increases in 2027 as in lation concerns build.
In lation is likely to remain the dominant consideration within the Fed’s dual mandate, but risks
in the labor market are beginning to emerge. Headline Consumer Price Index measures
reaccelerated to 3.74% year over year in September, from 3.40% in August and 3.36% in July,
reinforcing concerns over persistent in lation. Sharp moves in commodity markets add to that
pressure, with West Texas Intermediate crude oil trading well above $100 per barrel. Despite the
limited ability of monetary policy to directly address supply-driven in lation, sustained increases
in input costs could complicate the Fed’s assessment. The core Personal Consumption
Expenditures (PCE) Price Index may oﬀer little relief in the near term, as unfavorable base eﬀects
are keeping in lation elevated. However, we believe those eﬀects should reverse meaningfully
during the  irst half of 2027 and could begin to shift the balance of risks.
Growth, meanwhile, is sending mixed signals. The Atlanta Fed gross domestic product (GDP)
tracker points to 3.3% annualized growth for the third quarter, while October’s manufacturing
Purchasing Manager’s Index rose to 54.5 and September retail sales increased 6.3% year over
year, although the latter is not adjusted for in lation. That resilience in growth is contrasted by a
softer labor market: September nonfarm payrolls increased by just 29,000, down from 133,000
in August, alongside signs of wage disin lation.
The arti icial intelligence (AI) investment boom adds another dimension to the policy challenge.
Capital expenditure across major technology companies has continued to accelerate,
supporting demand in the near term while potentially laying the foundation for stronger

productivity over the long term. For the Fed, the challenge is determining which of those forces
dominates, and when.
Policy and real economy crosscurrents
Rates markets are pricing an increasingly demanding combination of strong growth and sticky
in lation. That is not our base case. The overnight index swap pricing implies three further rate
hikes next year, yet the probability of rates falling over the next 12 months has also increased. In
our view, that re lects an unusually wide distribution of outcomes and leaves the current
expected path for rates susceptible to repricing if either growth disappoints or in lation begins
to ease.
The key question is therefore how long the current combination of resilient activity and elevated
in lation can persist. For now, activity indicators remain robust, but softer payroll growth and
wage disin lation could increasingly challenge that picture. At the same time, the recent sharp
moves in oil add another near-term complication for in lation.
AI further widens the range of potential outcomes. The scale of capital expenditure provides an
additional source of demand today, while potential productivity bene its could ultimately have
the opposite eﬀect. Supply constraints could delay that productivity dividend even as the
investment impulse remains strong.
Core PCE and GDP estimates are due the day after the Fed meeting, creating a concentrated set
of macroeconomic catalysts ahead of the U.S. midterm elections. Markets will also remain
focused on the Fed’s assessment of the AI buildout, its balance sheet, and moves in global
sovereign bond markets.
Opportunities amid a shifting interest rate outlook
We believe the growing dispersion in economic outcomes and monetary policy expectations is
creating opportunities across asset classes. In  ixed income, real yields are becoming
increasingly attractive, although we see little immediate catalyst for nominal yields to fall absent
a growth shock or risk-oﬀ event. We therefore continue to favor the intermediate part of the U.S.
curve while remaining cautious on longer-duration government bonds. Outside the U.S., the
recent repricing in developed market rates is beginning to create more attractive entry points,
particularly where we believe monetary policy expectations have become overly hawkish.
Despite higher real yields, we remain constructive on equities, favoring broad-based U.S.
exposure. However, we believe an environment of slowing growth, persistent in lation, and
geopolitical uncertainty argues for selectivity, with quality and sentiment remaining important
considerations.
Currency markets provide another way to capture growing global divergence. We continue to
view the U.S. dollar as a strong portfolio diversi ier and attractive relative to select developed
market currencies. We remain cautious on the euro, however, as weaker growth oﬀsets the
repricing toward higher rates. We also remain selectively positive on emerging market
currencies where stronger growth, lower in lation, and orthodox monetary policy may provide
support.
The Atlanta Fed GDP tracker (formally called GDPNow) is a real-time estimate of U.S. economic
growth produced by the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta. It is a model-based, continuously
updated forecast of real U.S. GDP growth for the current quarter, expressed at an annualized
rate.

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is a measure of the average change over time in the prices paid
by urban consumers for a market basket of consumer goods and services. You cannot invest
directly in an index.
The Core Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) Price Index is the Fed's preferred measure
of underlying in lation. It tracks the prices consumers pay for goods and services while
excluding food and energy prices, which tend to be more volatile and can obscure longer-term
in lation trends.
Duration is a measurement of the sensitivity of a bond’s price to changes in Treasury yields. A
fund’s duration is the weighted average of duration of the bonds in the portfolio. Duration
should be interpreted as the approximate change in a bond’s (or fund’s) price for a 100-basis-
point change in Treasury yields. Duration is based on historical performance and does not
represent future results.
Overnight index swap pricing is a market-based measure of investors’ expectations for the
future path of central bank interest rates.
The Purchasing Manager’s Index (PMI) is an economic indicator that gauges sentiment in
manufacturing, based on monthly surveys of purchasing managers. You cannot invest directly
in an index.
The Producer Price Index (PPI) is an in lation gauge that measures changes in the prices
businesses receive for their output. Rising PPI readings can signal increasing cost pressures that
may eventually aﬀect consumer prices, corporate margins, and monetary policy. You cannot
invest directly in an index.
A yield curve graphical representation of  ixed-income security yields (usually U.S. Treasuries) at
their respective maturities, starting with the shortest time to maturity and sequentially plotting
in a line chart to the longest maturity. A yield curve is based on historical performance and does
not represent future results.
