Some relief for tech stocks and long-term rates
As the Nasdaq recovered somewhat from last week’s sell-o, long-term rates also rebounded
a bit this week. The correlation between tech stocks and long-term rates illustrates the
dynamics currently shaping markets. Improving prospects for the tech sector imply greater
demand for capital and higher interest rates—and vice versa.
The Fed oered few surprises on Wednesday. Its upbeat assessment of the economy, inflation remaining too
high and two dissenters favouring a hike—combined with a decision to leave rates unchanged—echoed its
stance in July. The yield curve steepened and the USD weakened again, although the moves were much smaller
than in July. Having already seen one hike, it seems reasonable to continue expecting further increases. We
maintain our forecast of two more Fed hikes.

US public debt is a bigger concern than you think
For now, the US debt situation is not a major concern: with growth and deﬁcits at their current levels, debt
ratios are not rising sharply. The Congressional Budget Oce (CBO) forecasts weaker growth ahead but largely
unchanged deﬁcits. These forecasts may prove too optimistic. Higher term premiums and a weaker USD could
lie ahead over the longer term.
The recent rise in long-term rates largely reflects higher real yields, driven by strong demand for capital to
ﬁnance both public deﬁcits and data centre investments. Strong growth and high expected returns can also
justify high interest rates. So far, we have seen very few signs of stress related to US debt sustainability, with term
premiums largely unchanged. This makes sense as long as both deﬁcits and growth remain at current levels.
Annual growth at 6% is not likely to last, if deﬁcits were to increase from here, problems could soon escalate.
With the US midterm elections approaching, Democrats may gain greater influence over the budget. They have
argued for reversing some Medicaid cuts and raising taxes on the wealthy. We cannot rule out such changes,
but a presidential veto could readily block major initiatives. Historical precedent oers little support for relying
on politicians to resolve the ﬁscal challenges.
Assuming no signiﬁcant legislative changes, at least two aspects of the CBO’s forecasts warrant attention.
First, its interest rate assumptions appear low relative to current market-implied rates. If market-implied rates
materialise, deﬁcits would be 1 percentage point higher than the CBO forecasts. More concerning are its
projections for healthcare and social security spending (Medicaid, Medicare and social security). Over recent
decades, these expenses have risen broadly in line with the shares of the population aged over 65 and over 80.
A model based on these demographic trends and the outlook suggests that the forecasts could underestimate
spending by several percentage points.
Adding these two potential forecast errors to the deﬁcit projections suggests that the trend of widening deﬁcits
over recent decades would continue, rather than stabilise as the CBO expects. If this were combined with a
recession, which typically increases deﬁcits by at least a few percentage points, the consequences for debt
sustainability would be catastrophic. But even in our baseline scenario, where we don’t see a recession, higher
term premiums would seem highly likely. It would also support our forecast of a weaker USD over the longer
term—a situation that could emerge once Europe’s issues have been addressed.
