Networking allows other devices to communicate and build larger device
ecosystems. Networking refers to devices that don’t process or store data on their own,
but instead help other devices communicate. Networking devices in particular solve 4 main
issues: forwarding, performance, reliability, and security.
Networking companies are primarily product companies. The key product categories
include switching, enterprise networking, optical transport, and transceivers. Companies
in our coverage also supply components, notably optical fiber and optical modules.
Networking primarily serves three main product categories: enterprises, service providers,
and hyperscalers.
Networking has historically been one of the worst performing subsectors of tech.
Networking stocks have historically underperformed, driven by low growth. Low growth in
turn was in large part due to high exposure to service providers; the end customers did not
grow revenue or capex, pressuring growth of suppliers.
Networking has re-rated on the AI opportunity. As compute performance has
outscaled network performance, this has created a “bandwidth wall” in which networking
is increasingly the bottleneck to overall AI performance. This has in turn lead to the
emergence of new, AI-focused network architectures. These include: Scale-up, which
refers to densely connecting accelerators in the same rack so that they behave as a single
node. Scale-out, which refers to connecting different racks and different nodes. Scale-
across, which refers to direct connections enabling separate data centers to behave a
single cluster. Scale-in, which connects end users to accelerated compute.
Networking can seem technical, but not all technology matters. We believe investors
should be focusing more on the space, and in particular on technologies that can actually
drive the stocks.

WHAT IS NETWORKING, REALLY, AND WHY DO WE NEED IT?
Networking products exist to connect devices to other devices.
• For context: a recap on how IT works. An individual end-user (like yourself) accesses an end-user device like a laptop,
desktop, smartphone, or tablet. However, many of the applications that you use are not actually running on your device, but
on a server, which refers to a computer that serves groups of users by interacting with the end-user’s devices instead of
with the end-user directly. For instance, when you send an email to a coworker, your laptop is probably sending the data to a
server, which is running Microsoft Outlook Exchange on the back end, which in turn handles your email. For a more thorough
entry-level explanation of how enterprise IT works, see the introduction section of: Technology Primer: An Introduction to
the Public / Private Cloud, Hybrid Cloud, Multi-Cloud, Containers, and Kubernetes. Networking is an $89B sector within the
broader IT universe (Exhibit 1).
• Networking allows all of these other devices to connect to each other and build larger device ecosystems. Most
hardware devices exist to perform a function on their own, like running a workload or storing data. By contrast, networking
devices serve to help other devices communicate.
• Networking devices solve 4 main issues: forwarding, performance, reliability, and security.
• Forwarding deals with getting messages where they need to go. Networking devices package data and find the right
location for it so that data is transmitted efficiently.
• Performance means this data needs to be handled quickly and reliably. Networking devices handle huge quantities of
data, so this data needs to be transmitted at low latency (low delays) and high scale (lots of data).
• Reliability refers to the stability and consistency of transmission.
• Security is about protecting the data and making sure it only goes to the proper users.
• The core networking industry can be broadly split into optical transport, data center switching, and enterprise. We
also cover optical modules and optical fiber. Exhibit 2 explores each area in detail.
• Switching refers to products that solve the forwarding problem in a straightforward way by matching data to where it is
meant to go. Routers operate similarly. However, while switches follow more simplistic rules than routers1, switches can
handle higher volumes at faster speeds and lower prices compared to routers, and accordingly form the backbone for
much of networking architecture. This is especially true for AI back-end switching - the high volume of data sent between
AI accelerators puts enormous performance requirements on the network, and has been a key growth area in the industry
as a result.
• Optical transport refers to systems that are meant to send data over long distances, typically for service provider
(think telco) networks, although many data center operators have their own data center interconnect as well. Over long
distances, this often becomes a performance and reliability issue, and so in addition to typical optical fiber systems and
switching and routing, optical transport often involves both hardware and software designed for error checking and signal
amplification.
1  A networking switch consists of a number of ports connected to a switch ASIC, and works by maintaining a “switching table” which
is essentially a simple lookup table consisting of the address of each device connected to one of its ports. When it receives a data
packet, it first checks the sender address, and if it is not a match for the switching table, it updates the entry for that port to match the
address of the sender. It then checks the recipient address. If there is a match on the switching table, the switch sends the packet to
the corresponding port. If there is no match, it sends the packet to all ports except the sender (in hopes that the recipient is connected
somehow). This sounds unsophisticated (and in many ways, it is), and a router performs largely the same function but is able to handle
more complex algorithms.

• Enterprise networking is a bit of a miscellaneous category. It can broadly be defined into enterprise local-area networks
(LAN), referring to networking equipment used within a single campus (think the office WiFi router in your office building),
and enterprise wide-area networks (WAN), which refers to systems designed to connect different campuses. Because
enterprises networks need to manage a variety of different users who may have different levels of trust, enterprise
networking often involves sophisticated cybersecurity hardware and software.

signals), with the other being copper. Optical fiber can transport more data over higher speeds compared to copper,

HOW DO NETWORKING COMPANIES MAKE MONEY?
Networking and optical companies are primarily products companies. This means, for the most part, analyzing company
revenues means analyzing the sales of a tangible device. The diversity of products and focuses means each company in our
coverage is unique, as highlighted by Exhibit 4.
The three key customer segments that buy networking products are enterprise, service provider, and hyperscalers.
Each segment has slightly different architectural needs within the product categories.
• Enterprise customers are large companies or organizations that use networking to connect their members. As
explained above, while enterprise is a somewhat miscellaneous category, it basically refers to an organization that needs
a closed network for its members to work on. This can look like the WiFi network in an office, or a VPN spanning multiple
different campuses.
• Service providers run large-scale networks that provide connectivity for customers across a wide geographic
range. Examples of service providers are Internet providers and telecom carriers. While service provider used to be more
significant, growth has stagnated as the market has become more saturated.
• Hyperscalers are public cloud service providers with millions (or billions of users). Hyperscalers maintain data centers
with massive storage, compute, and infrastructure capacities. Hyperscalers hold huge quantities of compute capacity,
but they are not constantly using all of it. This lets them rent out compute capacity to AI labs for model inferencing and
training. The need to meet the ever-growing demands of AI labs means hyperscalers need to improve connectivity within and
between data centers, creating a clear room for growth in networking.

ZERO TO HERO: NETWORKING USED TO BE ONE OF THE WORST PERFORMING TECH SUBSECTORS.
Networking stocks were historically one of the worst performing tech subsectors. Over the past 25 years on average,
networking stocks have experienced negative growth (Exhibit 6). This comes from their exposure to the service provider
segment and the rise of cloud.
• The service provider segment has struggled to grow. Service provider customers create connectivity across wide
geographic regions. As teleco and Internet penetration have scaled into almost all regions, very few areas of the world are
left without connectivity. This caps the amount of spend for networking to replacement networking equipment, rather than
expansion into new regions. Demand for better connectivity is also relatively limited, so service provider exposure has slowed
growth.
• Cloud has generally been a headwind for enterprising networking, with Arista being a notable exception. As with
compute servers and storage, much of the value proposition of working with OEMs like Cisco, Arista, and HPE Juniper,

THE HYPE(RSCALER): DATA CENTER NEEDS ARE DRIVING NETWORKING GROWTH
So, is the hype real? We believe it is: the bandwidth wall explains why networking stocks have become structural
AI winners. While AI accelerators and chip performance have followed Moore’s law, with compute capacity and performance
steadily increasing, networking has not scaled at the same rate. Exhibit 7 shows that server racks have increased in FLOPS,
bandwidth, and processing speed. Networking devices have to manage these higher performing devices with similarly high
speed and processing capacity. Networking is increasingly important for breaking through the bottleneck and delivering higher
performance.
Hyperscaler demand has driven a re-rating in networking growth. Networking equipment is critical for the functioning
of hyperscaler data centers, and demand for AI is enormous. The shipments of AI accelerators has doubled since 2023, and
volumes are projected to continue increasing well into the future, seen in Exhibit 8. Networking is well-positioned to keep up
with this demand, as AI front-end and back-end switching estimates project continued growth (Exhibit 9).
• How does an AI data center network actually work? Data centers contain many servers. The servers are stacked in racks
almost like bookshelves, shown in Exhibit 10. These servers need to communicate with other servers to transfer, compute,
and store data. Each rack has a switch on top of it. The switch is connected to cables that connect to servers within the same
rack and other racks.
• There have generally been two ways to expand the data center network: scale up and scale out. Data center capacity
is constrained by the number of switches, since only a limited number of ports fit on each switch. Data centers have to make
connections as efficiently as possible to maximize the utility of each port. They scale their connections to do so. Increasing
scale for data centers allows for better GPU utilization, redundancy to maximize path efficiency, and improved power
management.
• Scale up means increasing connections and capacity within the same rack, so servers that are right by
each other can communicate. Scale up is the most low-latency way to increase system capacity, but the amount of
connections that can be made within one rack is limited.
• Scale out involves connecting more racks to one another. Scale out helps expand compute without requiring long
links between data centers. While scale out effectively increases processing capacity more than scale up, some projects
require computing power that exceeds the size of one data center. Individual data centers are constrained by power
allocations and real estate, which can make scale across necessary.
• Spine and leaf-switching: One key switch-based network structure, commonly used in AI data centers for scale-out,
is the spine and leaf switch. Spine and leaf switching is a multilayer switching network, where every “spine” switch is
connected to every “leaf” switch, and then the leaf switches have half their ports connected to spines and the other half
connected to either individual servers or (in the case of AI data centers) to scale-up nodes (Exhibit 11).
• Vendors are increasingly also talking about scale across, which means connecting racks in completely different
data centers to each other. This allows separate data centers to act like one by forming a cluster (Exhibit 12). An example
of scale across is Colossus. xAI was unable to get enough power for their project in one state, so they had to divide the
Colossus project into two data centers with one on each side of the Tennessee state line.
• Scale across is a model training story. We believe latency matters less for inferencing and for agentic workloads
than people think. Inferencing and agentic workloads are part of the process of actually applying a trained model to real
requests. While end users like you and I care about latency, the time it takes an AI to respond to a request is much longer

• Continuing with the theme naming, Nvidia has announced the roll-out of scale in, which connects end users to
accelerated compute. Scale-in is often dismissed as a rebranded version of the front-end network, which in turn is not
too different from enterprise networks. However, Nvidia is arguing that the emergence of agentic inference will put more
demand on the front end, and has branded the new front-end network as scale-in. It remains to be seen how differentiated
Nvidia’s scale-in is compared to existing front-end solution.
• Other technologies supporting the ramp up in hyperscaler demand are InfiniBand and Ethernet. These products
both scale compute power. Ethernet is the prevailing network architecture, which is an open-source standard that
has dominated networking ever since Xerox PARC researchers published the Ethernet Blue Book in 1980. However,
conventional Ethernet based solutions have struggled with resending data that gets lost, and these lost packets are a
more acute issue in AI scale-out networks, which have seen spikier traffic. As a result, Nvidia initially developed InfiniBand,
which is a combined hardware and software technology that ensures lossless transmission of data through Virtual Lanes.
A consortium of industry participants including most incumbent networking players (e.g. Arista, HPE, and Cisco) as well as
the hyperscalers (Microsoft, Oracle, and Meta are all steering members) have developed Ultra Ethernet in response, which
is an open standard that optimizes ethernet for AI workloads, in turn offering an open, multi-vendor alternative to Nvidia’s
proprietary Infiniband. For more information, see our colleagues’ note U.S. Semiconductors - Infiniwho? Etherwhat? A short
primer on AI networking.
• CPOs, or co-packaged optics, are a new technology to keep on your radar as hyperscaler solutions roll out. CPOs
are advanced transceivers that integrate the optical engine into the switching ASIC. Usually, changing from the optical engine
to the switching ASIC causes a delay because the data has to transfer from copper to fiberoptics. This new design allows for
lower latency since the components can communicate with each other faster. The CPO also has higher bandwidth. CPOs let
transceivers send and receive signals even faster, making switching more efficient.
• Since hyperscalers care about speed, the rollout of this product will be important. However, CPOs have been
delayed in rolling out so far, causing speculation as to when they will come onto the market again. CPOs are also
expensive; the Asia tech hardware team estimates that CPOs cost 10% more than pluggable solutions, in Future of Tech:
Mapping the CPO Value Chain. As demand increases for optical equipment, this remains a technology to watch. For more
information, see Co-Packaged Optics (CPO) Primer: Inside the War for Datacenter Connectivity.
• Near-packaged optics, or NPOs, have grown as a solution to the CPO delay. While CPO has been delayed, NPOs
have helped fill this demand. While not fully integrating the optical engine with the ASIC, near-packaged optics bring the

Glossary
• Packet: a unit of data sent across a network
• AI accelerator: hardware component that speeds up AI workloads by performing massive computations in parallel
• Chiplet: chiplets are mini semi conductor chips that break the functions of a full chip into smaller pieces. This allows many
chips to be combined into one system that is more scalable and flexible than a standard chip.
• Latency: the time delay between sending data in one place and receiving it in another
• Bandwidth: the transmission capacity of a network
• FLOP: floating point operations per second, this is a measure of how many calculations a GPU can make
• Leaf-spine: two-tier data center design where every server-facing leaf switch connects to every spine switch.
• Radix: number of ports on a switch chip or system; higher radix means fewer network tiers.
• NOS: network operating system (EOS, IOS XE, NX-OS, open-source SONiC).
• Merchant silicon: switch chips bought from a chip vendor rather than designed in-house.
• White box / blue box: commodity hardware running customer or third-party software; a blue box is branded hardware sold
for open software.
• Scale-up / scale-out / scale-across: accelerator links within a rack or pod / across racks in one site / between sites.
• ARR / RPO: annualised recurring revenue / contracted revenue not yet recognised.
• Deferred revenue: billed or shipped but not yet recognised, often pending customer acceptance.
