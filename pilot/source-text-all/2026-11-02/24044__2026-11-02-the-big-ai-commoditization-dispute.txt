THE BIG AI COMMODITIZATION DISPUTE
Dario Perkins
02 Nov 2026
Even if AI is as groundbreaking as people say, there is a dispute about whether it will be profitable
for the companies leading the buildout. Bears believe the technology will end up commoditized,
going the same way as the railways, fiber-optics and Dotcom. Those technologies changed the
world, but they also brought a wave of financial distress and bankruptcies. At its root, this is an
argument about “tokenomics”, or the unit economics of producing AI tokens. 
Here is a brief explainer:
1/8
Let’s start with some definitions. “Tokens” are chunks of text that AI
models read and produce. The debate is about whether producing these
tokens will be a profitable endeavor for the AI industry – particularly the
companies that will own the data centers and the model developers
(esp. the frontier US labs - OpenAI and Anthropic) that are creating new
AI services. For easier analysis, it helps to keep those two groups
separate, although in reality there is a lot of overlap in what they do.

2/8
First the datacenter owners. These have large fixed costs. They have
acquired buildings, purchased gazillions of pricey GPUs, taken on debt,
and written multi-billion-dollar take-or-pay contracts to fund SPVs
(where they owe money even if they don’t use the compute). Their
revenue comes from GPU rentals. Right now, the GPU rental price is
elevated because compute demand is rising rapidly and GPUs (the ones
in service…) are scarce. To remain profitable, GPU rentals must cover
the fixed costs.

3/8
Now to the model developers. The good news - their costs are falling
rapidly due to strong efficiency gains. Better software and more
powerful hardware are driving TOKEN COSTS lower. Token costs =
rental price of GPUs per hour, divided by the number of tokens produced
by those GPUs per hour. (The denominator in that ratio is exploding
higher..) But there is bad news, too – intense competition is also driving
TOKEN PRICES lower. Chinese open-weight AI is v popular and only 4-7
months behind. 

4/8
The bear thesis is that this is a “race to the bottom” for both groups. For
datacenters, more supply is coming online, with an est. $500bn worth of
GPUs sitting in stockpiles. Perhaps the resilience in GPU rentals is just
a bottleneck, and rates will soon plummet. But because the datacenter
operators have fixed costs, they will be forced to keep operating –
putting further pressure on prices. Like previous commodity cycles, the
resultant supply glut will only clear with a wave of bankruptcies. 

5/8
But the problems don’t stop there – because the model developers are
also facing serious financial strains. As open-source AI supply takes
over, the frontier US labs will inevitably lose their “moats”. But training
costs will keep rising…. Worse, financial problems at the labs and the
data centers will interact in nasty ways, thanks to all that circular
financing and cross-equity investment. The virtuous cycle of the last
three years could very quickly become a doom loop instead.

6/8
The bull thesis, in contrast, is usually a version of Jevons paradox – the
idea that falling prices trigger a large increase demand, such that total
spending rises. If demand is extremely elastic, the increase in output
swamps the decline in prices – raising revenues. Gavin Baker, from
Atreides Management, sets out one of the more thoughtful versions of
this, an optimistic scenario where “everyone makes money” in the AI
ecosystem – even if open-weight Chinese models eventually dominate.

7/8
What does the bull scenario need? Two ingredients. First, the plunge in
token prices causes demand to explode. This keeps GPU rentals high
and datacenters profitable. Second, even as the competition from open-
weight AI intensifies, frontier US labs retain their edge. In the Baker
scenario this is because NVIDIA acts as trainer of last resort. NVIDIA
wants to keep the boom going, and has the resources to backstop the
system – by investing in the frontier, subsidizing datacenter funding etc.

8/8
Which scenario is likely? We lean towards the bears, and the
commoditization scenario. Semiconductor demand is notoriously
cyclical, and as more supply comes online, the current bottlenecks
could eventually turn into a sizeable glut. If that happens, NVIDIA won’t
be able to provide that backstop for the ecosystem anymore. In the end,
most of the benefits of this new technology would flow to end users, not
the companies leading the buildout.
