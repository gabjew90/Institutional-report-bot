Thought of the day
Germany’s trade deficit with 29 core Asia-Pacific economies is forecast 
to reach nearly EUR 146bn in 2026, according to a Reuters report citing 
Germany Trade & Invest analysis. Exports are expected to decline 3.2% to EUR 
185.3bn, while imports are anticipated to rise 6.4% to EUR 330.9bn. China 
accounts for around half of Germany’s goods trade with the region and is 
driving much of the deterioration. German machinery exports to China fell 
18% in the first seven months of 2026 from a year earlier, while automotive 
exports dropped 30%.
But investors should be careful not to extrapolate changing German trade 
patterns into a uniformly negative outlook for European companies or 
equities.
The trade data primarily reflect intensifying competition and weaker 
demand in China, rather than broad weakness across Asia-Pacific.
Chinese companies have expanded domestic production in advanced 
technology, machinery, electric vehicles, and batteries, reducing demand for 
products that Germany previously supplied. German car sales in China have 
nearly halved from a peak above 5 million vehicles as local manufacturers 
have gained market share. Yet, excluding China, German exports to Asia-
Pacific are forecast to grow 2.8% in 2026, and the regional deficit would 
narrow to just under EUR 35bn. German exports to India have almost doubled 
over the past decade, while exports to the 11 ASEAN economies are expected 
to grow faster than imports this year.

Corporate earnings and structural investment provide a more 
constructive signal than Germany’s trade headline. Europe is in the early 
stages of a cyclical earnings recovery following several years of stagnation, 
supported by investment linked to AI, electrification, defense spending, 
and reshoring. We forecast 15% Eurozone earnings growth in both 2026 
and 2027. At the same time, European companies have been defending 
profitability with established cost discipline strategies. Recent weakness has 
also reduced the Stoxx Europe 600’s price-to-earnings valuation by 15% since 
the start of the Middle East conflict, creating an attractive entry point even as 
higher energy costs, interest rates, and soft consumer demand remain risks.
So, we retain an Attractive rating on Eurozone equities and favor a selective 
approach that combines cyclical recovery with structural growth. Within the 
region, we like IT, industrials, banks, Germany, and health care. We also 
continue to recommend our “European leaders“ and “Swiss mid-caps in 
focus” themes.
In addition, we remain positive on consumer discretionary. The sector has sold 
off due to higher interest rates and energy prices, but it could recover strongly 
should the outlook for energy flows improve. Rather than turning negative 
after the recent sell-off with price-to-book valuations close to decade lows, 
we recommend a more selective approach through our “Luxury & Lifestyles” 
theme, which favors higher-end consumption that we believe should prove 
more resilient.
Caught our attention
Fed on hold, ECB up next. The Federal Reserve kept interest rates 
unchanged at 3.75–4% on Wednesday, as widely expected. The decision 
carried a mildly hawkish message: Officials upgraded their assessment 
of economic growth to “strong” from "solid," while two policymakers 
dissented in favor of a 25-basis-point hike. The press conference was 
more dovish than the statement. Chair Kevin Warsh emphasized signs 
that the Fed’s “medicine” is working, pointing to improvement in inflation 
compensation and inflation expectations despite the recent firm inflation 
data. He also highlighted tighter financial and credit conditions since 
September. Still, his comment that “overall financial conditions are tighter” 
but that he was “unwilling and unable to call them tight” suggests the 
tightening cycle is not over. Market reaction was relatively muted, with focus 
now shifting to the European Central Bank’s rate decision today.
Our view: We continue to expect the Fed to hike rates by 25 basis points 
in both December and March, compared with the three increases currently 
priced by markets over the next 12 months. Warsh’s comments reinforced 
a gradual rather than accelerated tightening path, in our view, keeping the 
backdrop constructive for equities, fixed income, and gold. While Warsh 
announced that the Fed will hold fewer rate-setting FOMC meetings in 2027 
and 2028, we see no clear directional policy implication. As for the ECB, we 
expect it to also be on hold at today’s meeting, but market focus will likely

Healey offers targeted relief. UK Chancellor John Healey unveiled a 
budget focused on near-term cost-of-living support while maintaining the 
government’s fiscal rules. Measures include a GBP 100 payment for up to 
20 million households, an extension of the temporary VAT cut on electricity 
bills, the removal of gas standing charges, and another fuel-duty freeze. The 
package will be partly funded by raising the Bank Corporation Tax Surcharge 
from 3% to 6% for 15 months. Other revenue measures include a further two 
percentage point increase in the tax rates on savings and property income. 
The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) forecasts GDP growth of 1.2% 
this year and 1% next year, before growth strengthens to 1.6% in 2028. The 
OBR expects borrowing to decline from 4.2% of GDP this year to 1.8% by 
the 2030-2031 period, with GBP 15bn of fiscal headroom.
Our view: The budget announced was broadly as expected and should have 
only a limited effect on the UK economic growth and inflation outlook. Its 
targeted support may provide modest relief from the energy shock, but the 
policy package is small relative to the effect of higher debt-interest costs 
and other forecast revisions. Markets had already anticipated lower fiscal 
headroom, helping explain the muted reaction in gilts. The more persistent 
issue is fiscal credibility, as much of the planned tightening is forecast to 
occur close to and following the next general election and could be delayed. 
This is likely to keep a UK-specific risk premium embedded in longer-dated 
yields. We remain constructive on sterling, with higher relative interest rates 
supporting the currency against the Swiss franc. For UK banks, the temporary 
surcharge proposed surpassed expectations of a more negative outcome, and 
any resulting earnings headwinds should be manageable. More broadly, we 
continue to like European bank stocks, where the earnings story is expanding 
beyond rates to accelerating loan growth, Europe’s emerging capex cycle, fee 
income and wealth management, and AI-driven efficiencies.
Tech earnings are mixed. Alphabet shares fell nearly 10% in after-hours 
trading on Wednesday as its September-quarter sales from most of its core 
businesses came in below market expectations. Also, Samsung Electronics fell 
nearly 5% on Thursday morning in Seoul after profit missed estimates. But 
Alphabet’s Google Cloud posted a year-over-year revenue jump of 103%, 
and Microsoft’s Azure Cloud generated revenues that are 48% higher from 
the same period last year. Oracle also beat estimates on both top and bottom 
lines, forecasting USD 30bn in free cash flow in FY2029, when investors had 
expected it to remain negative. Nasdaq futures are down 1.3% at the time of 
writing. Amazon is due to report earnings later today while Apple’s earnings 
are expected next week.
Our view: Without commenting on individual companies, market reaction to 
the latest set of results underscores the elevated expectations going into the 
reporting season and investors’ sharpening focus on monetization and return 
on investment. We think the results overall are in line with our constructive 
view on the AI growth story. We continue to see opportunities in hyperscalers 
as well as high-quality semiconductor and hardware beneficiaries, but 
investors should also consider exposure to defensive areas of tech.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
