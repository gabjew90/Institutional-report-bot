Fed and ECB poised for December rate hikes 
Global central banks are set to raise policy rates further even though the Fed and the ECB 
stood pat at their latest meetings. In the US, economic growth remains strong and nominal 
spending is too robust for inflation to return sustainably to 2%. Every Taylor rule variant 
tracked by the Fed points to higher rates, even excluding energy effects. Officials have 
opted to gather more information, but the case for tightening remains compelling. We 
maintain our forecast for a December hike followed by three further increases in 2027, 
taking the policy rate to 4.75–5.0%. In the euro area, the ECB needs to balance rising 
energy prices that pose upside risks to inflation and tighter financing conditions with 
downside risks to growth. As the risk of indirect and second round effects increase with 
the duration and magnitude of the energy price shock, a further increase in December is 
quite likely as President Lagarde indicated at the ECB press conference. 
Over in the UK, Chancellor Healey presented his first budget. It was a low-key budget, de-
signed to restore fiscal credibility in a challenging environment. For now, the new Prime 
Minister and the Chancellor have the benefit of the doubt from Gilt investors, but more 
concrete measures on how to boost long-term growth, in particular, are needed to sus-
tainably lower risk premiums on UK Gilts. 
 
Finally, inflationary pressures continue to keep the Fed in tightening mode, just as an in-
creasing share of the AI buildout needs to be financed by debt. These pressures have 
started to be reflected in rising credit spreads and put equity markets in a materially more 
difficult position. We reiterate our preference for Swiss equities and consumer staples, 
and would favour high-quality exposure over growth and leverage in a portfolio.

Fed meeting review 
A hike is coming, but not yet 
The Fed held rates steady, as widely expected. Yet the case for tighter policy remains 
overwhelming: economic growth is strong, as indicated in the statement, and inflation 
is simply too high. Every Taylor rule variant that the Fed tracks suggests rates ought 
to be higher, even when excluding the impact from energy prices on inflation. We 
maintain our forecast for one additional hike in December and three more in 2027.  
 
The Fed left interest rates unchanged at 3.75-4% on Wednesday, as expected. But two 
officials – Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack – dissented in favour of a quarter-point increase. 
The statement was also tweaked to reflect the economy’s strength. This left the obvious 
question, put to Kevin Warsh at the start of his press conference: why not raise rates now? 
The chairman said there had been a “heck of a family fight”, but that officials believed the 
“accumulation of more information” they will receive over the next six weeks, as the state-
ment put it, would help them make a better decision. 
 
The explanation was not entirely convincing. Mr Warsh acknowledged that financial con-
ditions were tighter but not tight. Officials may hope that an improvement in the geopolit-
ical outlook will eventually bring down oil prices, though we doubt this can be the base 
case for any of them. They may also expect financial conditions to tighten further if equity 
markets remain under pressure. Finally, the majority may have judged that postponing 
action until after the midterms carried little immediate cost but averted a possible political 
conflict, despite Warsh's denial that such considerations influenced the decision.  
 
For us, nominal spending is simply too strong for inflation to return to target in a timely 
manner. Most Taylor rules reach a similar conclusion: the fed funds rate is at the lower 
end of their plausible ranges, given the current state of the economy, with the median rule 
suggesting a rate of 5–5.5% would be more appropriate (Exhibit 1 and Box 1 for more 
details). Feeding the FOMC officials’ latest projections for inflation and unemployment, 
published in September, into those rules suggests that the Fed could then begin cutting 
rates gradually over the next few years. But the result depends on their forecast that in-
flation will ease towards target. There is a clear tension: FOMC members expect inflation 
to fall with only very modest rate increases, as the dark-blue line in the chart shows. We 
think this is unlikely. We expect, instead, the Fed to raise rates in December and several 
more times next year, taking them to a peak of 4.75–5.0%.

Box 1: Taylor rules – a guide to policy calibration 
So-called “Taylor rules” follow an empirical observation of John Taylor (1993) about past 
Fed policy decisions. Since then, they provide a framework to think about monetary policy 
rather than a strict rule that central bank should follow blindly. 
 
Taylor’s rule states that the fed funds rate should move more strongly and in the same 
direction as deviations of inflation from its target, and of economic activity from its true 
underlying “potential”.  
 
Over time, research in monetary policy has developed alternative formulations of the clas-
sical rule, building on Taylor’s work. Most notably, modern specifications make explicit the 
notion of inertia in setting the policy rate. Exhibit 2 shows the projected path for the fed 
funds rate according to a selected sample of Taylor rules used by the Cleveland Fed, which 
incorporate such improvements. These projections use the latest Fed’s Summary of Eco-
nomic Projections’ (SEP) for the “natural” interest rate (the one which keeps activity at its 
potential) and the equilibrium unemployment rate. When combined with the Fed’s fore-
casts for inflation and unemployment, rules with no inertia indicate a need for more ag-
gressive rate hikes, potentially reaching 6%. 
 
While Taylor rules call for discipline, the Fed has admittedly stayed more often than not 
on the lower end of the calculated range (Exhibit 1). Inertia may be one explanation for 
this tendency (though only when hiking is called for). On the other hand, the only periods 
when rates significantly deviated from the described range were during the aftermath of 
the dotcom bubble in the early 2000s and the pandemic period, during both of which un-
certainty in the environment warranted caution. That being said, history shows that the 
fed funds rate converges, sooner or later, towards the median of the various Taylor rule 
specifications.

UK fixed income 
Chancellor Healey’s first budget is a first step 
The Chancellor delivered a low-key and ‘no-nonsense’ budget, aimed at restoring fiscal 
credibility. It is based on conservative assumptions with regard to economic growth 
and interest rates. For now, the new Prime Minister and the Chancellor have the ben-
efit of the doubt from Gilt investors. But the backloaded nature of the planned fiscal 
consolidation will leave questions about the credibility of projected fiscal tightening. 
More concrete measures on how to boost long-term growth, in particular, are needed 
to sustainably lower risk premiums on GiIts. 
 
With his first budget, Chancellor Healey presented a low-key budget that was close to mar-
ket expectations. It provides relief from high energy costs for households and gives tar-
geted support to businesses. In particular, eligible households receive £100 for energy 
costs. Electricity VAT relief and fuel-duty support measures are extended. These measures 
are paid for by a package of targeted tax raises mainly from a temporary increase in the 
bank surcharge and a rise in savings and property income-taxes. The budgetary impact on 
inflation and growth is small and hence it will not have an impact on monetary policy.  
 
The Autumn Budget also came with a set of new OBR (Office for Budget Responsibility) 
economic and fiscal forecasts. Apart from higher inflation due to energy prices and weaker 
2027 growth, the October projections indicate a higher trajectory of public sector net bor-
rowing, mostly because of higher interest rates. That said, fiscal consolidation remains on 
track (Exhibit 1). As in the US, issuance amounts for long-term bonds have been reduced 
while those for short- and intermediate maturities have been increased (Exhibit 2).  
 
 
The Chancellor delivered a ‘no-nonsense budget’, without major policy changes, aimed at 
restoring market confidence. It is based on conservative assumptions with regard to eco-
nomic growth and interest rates. Therefore, the market has accepted that the fiscal head-
room of GBP 15bn is at the lower end of expectations. That said, tougher decisions on 
welfare spending or defence were left for next year’s Spending Review, with difficult dis-
cussions about departmental spending cuts. Therefore, the backloaded nature of the 
planned fiscal consolidation will raise questions about the credibility of projected fiscal 
tightening. For now, the new Prime Minister and the Chancellor have the benefit of the 
doubt from Gilt investors, but more concrete measures on how to boost the long-term 
growth profile in particular are needed to sustainably lower risk premiums on GiIts.

US equities 
A regime change in markets 
After a tremendous four years in equity markets, the Goldilocks environment of falling 
inflation and unlimited capex resources is fading. Inflationary pressures have started 
to resurface and will likely keep the Fed in tightening mode, just as an increasing 
share of the AI buildout needs to be financed by debt issuance. These pressures have 
started to be reflected in rising credit spreads and put equity markets in a materially 
more difficult position than at any point over the past four years. Upside over the com-
ing 12 months is likely limited to single digits after four consecutive years of double-
digit returns. We reiterate our preference for Swiss equities and consumer staples, 
and would favour high-quality exposure over growth and leverage in a portfolio. 
 
Equity markets have entered a significantly more complicated environment than in recent 
quarters. Even though earnings continue to deliver decent growth rates of around 30% 
year-on-year in third-quarter reporting, performance has been lacklustre (Exhibit 1). We 
see several factors curbing support for equities. First, after a surge in cyclical indicators 
in the first half of 2026, they now stand at levels typically associated with the top of the 
cycle. Some momentum indicators have already begun to turn, pointing to a moderation 
of the remarkable earnings-upgrading cycle that has been underway since early 2025 (Ex-
hibit 2).  
 
 
Second, the credit market has started to wake up to the surge in capex spending, with 
spreads rising predominantly for Neocloud borrowers and in lower-rated market segments 
(Exhibit 3). This trend is likely to continue, as the ramp-up in AI debt financing has only 
just begun. Combined with the largest Neoclouds and SpaceX, the five Hyperscalers have 
issued a combined USD 300bn in debt this year. That is sizeable, given that total non-
financial US corporate bond issuance through July stood at USD 800bn. With the com-
bined free cash flow of all five Hyperscalers turning negative in Q3, every incremental US 
dollar invested in data centres from now on must come from either credit or equity issu-
ance. Hyperscalers in aggregate plan to raise capex by a further USD 200–300bn in 2027, 
which would need to be reflected in additional debt issuance over the coming quarters. As 
a result, Hyperscaler debt issuance could easily exceed USD 500bn in 2027 (Exhibit 4).

Alphabet experienced a more negative free-cash-flow outcome than expected, against the 
backdrop of a slight shortfall in quarterly capex, and the shares suffered a violent 10% 
drop. This suggests that the ramp-up in capex in 2027 is likely to prove more difficult and 
will be more closely scrutinised by credit investors than the spending increase in 2026,

Third, rising credit spreads usually have repercussions for the cycle itself, as bank lending 
conditions tend to tighten in response. An increase in CCC spreads is typically followed by 
a tightening of US banks’ lending standards (Exhibit 5). Given that the Senior Loan Officer 
Opinion Survey (SLOOS) is itself one of the best leading indicators for the US ISM, higher 
credit spreads can eventually weigh on the cycle via the bank-lending channel (Exhibit 6). 
 
 
Finally, these early signs of a peaking cycle are occurring against a backdrop of continued 
upward pressure on inflation. The 10-month lag of core CPI means that it does not yet 
reflect the strengthening of growth at the beginning of the year (Exhibit 7). At the same 
time, oil and gas prices are adding to upside pressure on headline inflation (Exhibit 8). 
This will likely keep the Fed on its tightening path for several more months, leaving little 
leeway to provide support to markets.

Chart of the Week 
US consumer: Mind the gap 
• The chart shows that US real labour income growth has historically correlated 
strongly with real consumption 
• Over the past 12 months, wage growth has driven almost all nominal labour income 
gains (4.15%), while employment and hours contributed only 0.8 percentage points 
• The pick-up in inflation, with the PCE deflator rising from 2.8% yoy at the end of last 
year to 3.6% in September, has been the primary drag on real labour incomes 
• Contrary to income trends, real consumption growth has strengthened 
• This divergence stems from tax refunds, the wealth effect from rising equities, and 
a declining savings rate 
• The gap between the two lines will have to close at some point. Real consumption 
was flat month-on-month in September, and consumption is unlikely to be as strong 
in Q4 as it was last quarter  
• Still, consumer spending may remain supported by further savings depletion and 
possibly stronger wage growth over coming months amid a tightening labour market
