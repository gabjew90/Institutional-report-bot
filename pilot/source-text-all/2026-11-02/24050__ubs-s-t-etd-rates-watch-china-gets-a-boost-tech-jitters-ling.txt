ETD Rates Watch: China Gets A Boost,
Tech Jitters Linger As Rates Stay Quiet
Daniel Noorian
02-Nov-2026, 03:37 ET (EST)
Chinese equity markets received a boost in the Asia session after reports that the financial regulator met with
banks and securities firms to discuss measures to support capital markets. Chinese media Cailian reported
that the meeting will study optimising lending programmes to support stock buybacks, while there were also
reports that a major state-owned bank plans to increase lending quotas to asset managers to support stock
purchases. The Shenzhen Composite is up 1.92% as at 0710 GMT, while China H shares are up 0.67%.
However, Japanese markets struggled, with the Nikkei down 0.33%. The main news was shares in Kioxia
falling just under 16%. The firm manufactures semiconductor memory products and provided a cautious
outlook, adding to worries around parts of the AI sector. Moves of 10% and more seem to be becoming more
commonplace in the last week, which I don’t think is an especially healthy sign. The news has had little bearing
on Nasdaq, which is up 0.8% in early trade on Monday after Friday’s 2.86% fall.
Rates markets were fairly quiet, with volumes reverting back to recent averages and just 130k TY trading in
the overnight session. There was no real pattern to flows, with the larger call buying seen on Friday absent.
Just as a reminder, on Friday paper paid from 9.5 up to 11 for 50k FVF7 107 calls, while the Week 1 TY
107.75 calls were bought for 7 in 50k. The main news so far on Monday has been that Alphabet is conducting
a seven-part Euro bond offering, including 4y, 6y, 9y, 13y and 19y tenors. Its previous Euro offering raised
EUR 9 bn.
Central banks are in focus again this week, with the RBA meeting on Tuesday and the BoE on Thursday.
Before that, we get the quarterly borrowing estimates on Monday and the quarterly refunding announcement
on Wednesday. UBS expects the US Treasury to leave auction sizes unchanged for the coming quarter and
expects the Treasury to prepare markets for adjustments to supply from May 2027.
Markets are pricing in 21bp of hikes for the RBA meeting. The RBA has said that one of the channels through
which higher rates dampen transactions and prices is the housing market. The latest data from Cotality
showed that Australian house prices have fallen for the seventh straight month and are on track for the largest
drop in 40 years. The Home Value Index for major cities fell 1.7% in October and is now down 8.1% over the
last seven months.
More criticism of last week’s UK Budget, this time from unions, warning Chancellor Healey against pursuing “a
trade-off between jobs and wages”. This is in regard to him refusing to recommit to fulfilling a 2024 manifesto
pledge to end “discriminatory age bands”. Unison said: “Labour ministers shouldn’t be propagating the idea of
a trade-off between jobs and wages as millions of workers struggle to afford food and petrol.”
Ryanair warned on Monday that it will need to cut costs across the group in order to offset a $2 bn increase in
fuel charges next year. The group said it would review airport deals, consider cutting crew on flights and also
increase the use of AI to bring down expenses. The firm reported a 15% drop in net profits for the first fiscal
half of the year. The firm also said that fares dropped in the first half of the year, with the US-Iran war leading
to hesitancy among consumers.
The final NYT/Siena poll on the US midterms showed that the Democrats are ahead in the seven key states
most likely to decide control of the Senate. Polls have previously underestimated President Trump voters, but

the Democrats’ lead is said to be such that they could win the Senate even if the polls underestimated
Republicans by as much as they did in 2016 or 2020. The polls show the largest gains for Democrats in Maine
and Michigan.
The Financial Times [paywall] has a good stat on equity market performance and the midterms. It found that
US stocks have rallied in the six months after the midterms for more than 70 years. The last time they declined
was in 1946. The most striking difference this year is that US stocks have never been this expensive heading
into the midterms. You also need to consider politics, with Carson Investment noting that since 1951 the S&P
500 has delivered an average annual return of 13.7% under a Republican president and a split Congress.
However, that falls to just 4.9% when the Democrats control both the House and Senate.
