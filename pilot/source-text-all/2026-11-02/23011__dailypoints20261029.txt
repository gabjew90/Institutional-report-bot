JUST HOW STRONG IS THE US ECONOMY AND AT WHAT COST TO INFLATION? 
We'll find out just how strong the US economy is and at what cost to inflation when this morning's Q3 GDP and PCE inflation figures arrive 
(8:30amET).  
For GDP, I've gone with 3% q/q SAAR. There is a wide range of consensus views with the median estimate at 3.2% q/q SAAR and the range 
mostly clustered with about 2½% to about 4%. Some of the reason for this is that  
The consumer is a big part of the strength. Pending this morning's updates for spending and incomes in September, Q3 is tracking a gain of 
3.3% q/q SAAR and possibly higher if expectations for September come true and without negative revisions. September should be solid 
based on retail sales, albeit mostly fed by higher prices versus volumes. Further, it’s the saving rate that is likely to continue taking it on the 
chin as spending growth exceeds income growth. 
The inflation figures may nevertheless steal the show. Core PCE inflation is estimated to rise by 0.4% m/m (consensus 0.3%). This is based 
upon core CPI that was up by 0.3%, conversion to PCE weights, contributions from pertinent producer price categories that are included in 
PCE, and a small estimate for other methodological differences between CPI and PCE.

REASONS WHY HOUSING WON'T STOP BOC HIKES 
One of the pushbacks on Bank of Canada rate increases is that they shouldn’t be happening for the sake of the housing market. I believe 
this narrative is incorrect. 
• 
Their job is managing inflation to a 2% target.  
• 
Shelter inflation is being overtaken by other inflation drivers like services ex-shelter, but clients who tell me that shelter inflation is 
a drag on overall inflation are not respecting the data (charts 5, 6). 
• 
Housing is holding up better than some narratives. Look at starts, for example (chart 7).  
• 
The worst damage that you can do to housing is 
to allow inflation to rip and then slam the brakes 
on later with steeper and higher rate increases. 
Hiking earlier than later could mitigate the risk of 
even higher rates later on and with it more 
damage to housing. Homebuilders need to 
understand that the worst risk to their market is 
a repeat of runaway inflation. 
• 
Condo markets in Toronto and Vancouver 
remain troubled but that's not a rates issue. 
Supply overshot and investors often behaved 
irresponsibly.  
• 
It's also an immigration issue as Canada 
tightened policy after a wild overshoot on temps. 
I've long argued Canada will get back training 
immigration targets likely by next year and in a 
smarter way this time. We don't have enough 
of the skilled labour to meet the investment 
needs.  
• 
Other growth drivers are taking over. 
Consumer spending. Investment. Exports.  
• 
Canada has allocated most activity toward 
short-term spending for too long. The country 
is rotating toward an investment-led agenda. 
Chart 8 shows the share of GDP into 
consumption, housing and current spending 
by governments. About 86% of GDP is in here-
today-gone-tomorrow spending. That’s at the 
highest since the 1980s but not quite as high 
as the 1970s. The country has had a very short-term mindset for too long. Absent longer-term investment, productivity and 
prosperity suffer. The country is in the early stages of having a very different macro framework. 
• 
Mortgage rates remain cheap. Yes, cheap. In a longer run sense, the 5-year mortgage rate's key driver—the five-year GoC bond 
yield—remains low in real terms by taking the current 3.6% level and deducting your favourite measure of inflation or expected 
inflation. Housing models use real rates. With the information at hand, I find the 5-year bond yield to be around fair value, having 
incorporated reasonable estimates of where the policy rate is going alongside a tacked on term premium. 
Fundamentally, housing's challenges are an income problem. Rates can't fix that. Affordability is strained by years of sub-par income 
growth. A productivity and investment agenda would generate greater housing rewards than anything rates could do.

ONTARIO’S FISCAL UPDATE 
Mitch Villeneuve is a provincial economist on our team and here’s what he has to say about Ontario’s fiscal update today. 
Ontario releases its mid-year fiscal update around 1pm EDT today. Its Spring budget pushed back its planned return to budget balance by a 
year to FY29, and developments since then point to further deterioration in the deficit path. The final deficit number for 2025–26 (FY26) 
came in at $13 bn, up a bit from the $12.3 bn forecast in March, due in part to weaker personal income tax assessments—which will carry 
forward into future years. The real GDP forecast has also slipped since the Spring, though higher GDP inflation is mitigating the impact on 
projected nominal GDP. 
The biggest impacts will come from the cost of new measures. Minister Bethlenfalvy confirmed yesterday what he hinted at last week, 
which is that Ontario will match the federal Productivity Mega Deduction, at a fiscal cost of $8 bn over five years. He had announced in the 
Spring that Ontario would adopt the feds’ Super Deduction, though didn’t include the associated cost in the fiscal numbers at that time, as 
the federal legislation was still pending. Combined, these two measures will drop tax revenues by around $12 billion in total over the 
forecast horizon. Ontario has also made a number of new spending announcements, most notably a $2.7 bn contribution to the joint 
Canada-Ontario support package for the steel and auto sectors announced last week—though the impact of this announcement on the 
deficit is likely to be minimal given that it appears to be loans rather than grants. 
The independent Financial Accountability Office of Ontario forecast in the Summer a deficit of $17 bn in FY27, dropping to $8-10 bn in 
future years. I agree that the deficit path is likely to be revised higher compared to the Budget 2026 numbers, but expect the government to 
set out a clearer path toward an eventual return to balance.
