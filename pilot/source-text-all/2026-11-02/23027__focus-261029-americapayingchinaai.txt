In July 2026, Chinese start-up Moonshot AI released Kimi 
K3, a model that Axios reported outperformed Anthrop-
ic’s latest system on a popular coding test while costing 
40% less. A few months earlier, Anthropic had accused 
Moonshot and two other Chinese labs of secretly using its 
Claude model to train their own, highlighting an argument 
that has already been circulating among investors. Micro-
soft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta plan to spend roughly 
$700bn on infrastructure in 2026, about 75% more than 
in 2025, according to the Financial Times. If Chinese labs 
can reproduce the same results for less expensive just 
a few months later, are American shareholders basically 
funding China’s AI industry? The evidence suggests the 
argument is partly right but overstated. US spending does 
not flow to China, but it buys a lead that pays off for a 
shorter time than investors might hope.
How AI models are built and copied
To understand the debate, it helps to know what “the fron-
tier” means. In AI, the frontier refers to the most capable 
models available at any given time, today produced 
by companies such as Anthropic, OpenAI and Google. 
Reaching it is extremely expensive. Labs need vast data 
centres filled with specialised chips, large amounts of 
data, and many failed experiments before a model works 
as intended.
Chinese labs such as DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax 
follow a different commercial model. They mostly release 
“open-weight” models, meaning anyone can download 
the model and run it on their own servers, often for free. 
Companies then pay only for the computing power they 
use, which makes these models much cheaper than their 
American counterparts.
The controversy is based on a technique called distilla-
tion. Instead of learning everything from scratch, a new 
model is trained on the answers produced by a more 
advanced one, much like a student copying an expert’s 
worked solutions. Distillation is common and legitimate 
within a company. It becomes a problem when one lab 
uses a competitor’s model without permission, since it 
captures the result of costly research without paying for it.
The case for the “subsidy” argument
The most direct evidence came in February 2026. 
Anthropic reported, in findings covered by The Decoder, 
that DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax had generated 
more than 16 million exchanges with Claude through 
around 24,000 fraudulent accounts. Computing’s break-
down shows that MiniMax accounted for over 13 million 
of these exchanges, Moonshot for more than 3.4 million 
and DeepSeek for about 150,000.. The requests focused 
on coding, reasoning and the ability to carry out complex 
tasks autonomously, precisely the skills on which US labs 
have spent most and for which businesses pay the highest 
prices. In a memo to the US congressional comitte, 
OpenAI made similar accusations against DeepSeek the 
same month, and Google disclosed an attempt to copy its 
Gemini model using more than 100,000 prompts. These 
remain company allegations, however, and nobody has 
measured how much the Chinese labs actually gained.
Source: Anthropic
The spending gap adds weight to the argument. US 
hyperscalers are expected to pass $1tn of annual invest-
ment in 2027, based on estimates from Evercore and 
Bank of America. By comparison, ByteDance, China’s 
largest spender, plans to invest about $28 billion in 2026, 
EqualOcean reports. Labs such as DeepSeek or Moon-
shot have no comparable infrastructure at all. Supporters 
of the subsidy argument point out that most of the cost 
of building an AI model comes before the final version. 
Labs spend heavily on experiments that fail, on collecting 
data and on testing. A lab that learns from a rival’s answers 
skips these costly steps and goes straight to the result, 
while the rival has paid for all of them.
Source: Company guidance
Yet the results are close. Research institute, Epoch AI, 
notes that Chinese models have trailed the best Ameri-
can ones by an average of seven months since 2023. 
However, open-weight models in general, including 
those developed in the United States, trail the leading 
closed models by a comparable margin, suggesting that 
the delay is not specific to China. Their adoption is also 
growing rapidly. On OpenRouter, a platform that lets devel-
opers switch between AI models, Chinese models went 
from about 1% of usage in late 2024 to around 46% by 
mid-2026, according to Forkast, citing CNBC. The reason

is price. OpenRouter’s own analysis finds these models 
60% to 90% cheaper than leading US alternatives. Even 
American companies have adopted them. In March 2026, 
the coding tool Cursor launched a model built on Moon-
shot’s Kimi, without disclosing it at launch.

Why the argument goes too far
The idea that Chinese companies do not invest in AI 
does not survive a closer look. Alibaba and Tencent 
together spent more than RMB 120bn, around $17bn, in 
the second quarter of 2026 alone, according to TMTPost. 
The famous claim that DeepSeek trained a leading model 
for just $5.6mn covered only the final training run, as 
DeepSeek itself stated. The research firm SemiAnalysis 
estimated that the company’s servers alone were worth 
around $1.6bn, nearly 300 times that figure. Chinese labs 
spend far less than American ones, but they are far from 
working on a shoestring budget.
Ideas also travel in both directions. Several of the most 
important techniques for making AI cheaper to train and 
run were developed by DeepSeek and published openly, 
and US labs have since adopted them. CSET data cited by 
Al Jazeera show that China also produces more AI research 
than the United States, with around 27% of global publi-
cations in 2024 against 12% for the US. Limited access 
to advanced chips played a role too. Because US export 
controls restrict China’s access to the most powerful 
hardware, Chinese engineers were pushed to get more 
out of less, making cheap models partly a genuine engi-
neering achievement.
Source: Epoch AI
In addition, most of this American spending is safe from 
copying. The majority of hyperscaler investment does 
not go into creating new AI models. It funds data centres 
used for cloud services rented to other companies, for 
advertising systems and for running AI tools for paying 
customers. Google Cloud alone has more than $460bn 
of contracted future revenue, and Meta attributed part 
of its 33% revenue growth in the first quarter of 2026 
to AI-driven ad targetingas reported by Yahoo Finance. 
A Chinese lab copying a model’s answers takes nothing 
away from these businesses. Only the money spent on 
developing new models is truly exposed, and it repre-
sents a fraction of the headline figures.
It is also worth asking who captures the benefit of cheap 
Chinese models. Of 178 large open-weight models 
released by Chinese labs in 2026, 59% carry the Apache 
2.0 licence and 22% the MIT licence, both of which allow 
free commercial use. The main winners are therefore 
developers and businesses around the world, including 
American start-ups. In 2025, Andreessen Horowitz, one of 
Silicon Valley’s largest venture capital firms, reported that 
up to 80% of the start-ups pitching it with open models 
were building on Chinese ones. Seen this way, Chinese 
labs subsidise the global software industry as much as US 
companies subsidise Chinese labs.
Copying is also becoming harder. US labs are deploying 
tools that detect suspicious usage patterns and can delib-
erately degrade the answers sent to suspected copiers. 
The scale of the alleged campaigns also varies widely. 
The 150,000 exchanges Anthropic attributes to DeepS-
eek are small compared with the data needed to train a 
modern model, which suggests that most of its progress 
came from its own work..
Finally, American labs continue to capture the vast major-
ity of AI revenue. Anthropic’s annualised revenue passed 
$65bn at the end of July 2026 and OpenAI’s reached 
about $40bn. By comparison, Epoch AI estimates that no 
other lab, Chinese ones included, earns more than $1bn.

The gap is narrowing faster than 
expected
Two recent developments weaken the case against the 
subsidy argument. The first concerns revenue. Until 
recently, open-weight models won a lot of usage but very 
little money. In September 2026, open-weight models 
handled 71.5% of the traffic on Vercel’s AI gateway, a 
service developer use to access different models. They 
also captured 23% of spending on the platform, up from 
under 4% in June. Between August and September alone, 
Anthropic’s share of spending on the platform fell from 
63.7% to 43.9%. Although these figures cover a single 
platform, the trend they reveal is significant.
The second concerns performance. A model trained 
on another model’s answers usually comes close to its 
teacher without surpassing it, which would keep Chinese 
labs one step behind. Kimi K3 challenges that assump-
tion, having beaten Anthropic’s Fable 5 on Arena’s coding 
ranking and placed above Opus 4.8 in its general ranking. 
These rankings are based on user votes for specific tasks 
rather than independent tests, so it would be premature 
to call it a Chinese lead. But the idea that Chinese models 
will always remain a few months behind looks less certain 
than it did at the start of the year.
Investment implications
For the large US technology groups, the impact remains 
limited. Their profits depend primarily on cloud services 
and advertising, with less reliance on exclusive access to 
the most advanced models. Cheaper AI has also stimu-
lated demand, benefiting chipmakers in the process. The 
pressure is more direct for frontier AI labs, as they can still 
charge a premium for the most complex tasks, but pricing 
power is weakening across everyday applications. As 
Anthropic and OpenAI consider potential IPOs, their finan-
cial disclosures should provide a better indication of how 
this pressure is affecting margins.
Conclusion
The argument that America bears the costs while China 
reaps the benefits has some merit. Chinese labs build 
on US breakthroughs, sometimes through practices that 
American companies allege are illicit, and their low-cost 
open models are gaining market share. Chinese com-
panies are also investing heavily, developing their own 
innovations and generating far less revenue than their US 
counterparts. A more accurate assessment is that Amer-
ica’s technological lead generates returns for an increas-
ingly limited period. Recent developments suggest this 
period is narrowing rapidly, and investors should monitor 
whether growth in demand is sufficient to offset the 
decline in pricing power.
