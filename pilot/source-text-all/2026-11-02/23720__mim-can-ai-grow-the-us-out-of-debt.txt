Key takeaways
	
▪Productivity gains from AI depend on a favorable policy and 
research environment. 
	
▪To alleviate deficits, AI productivity-led growth will have to 
offset the drag from a shrinking labor force and a structurally 
worsening fiscal environment.
	
▪The timing and extent of AI productivity gain remains 
uncertain, making it a difficult “strategy” for deficit reduction.

Consistent high growth is hard  
to achieve
The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget 
estimates that the U.S. would need broad-based growth 
of 3.3% per year on average just to hold debt at 100% 
of GDP. To reduce deficits to 3% of GDP, the real 
growth rate would need to average 4.4% over ten years.
The Congressional Budget Office (CBO), in a 2025 
analysis, estimates that the average annual growth 
from 2023-2035 would be 2.4% or higher in just 5% of 
simulated scenarios. The median estimate of average 
annual growth is 1.9%.
According to the CBO, achieving and sustaining real 
growth is a low-probability event.
What about AI boosting 
productivity?
AI productivity improvements could raise expected 
potential GDP for some time, but, as the forecast from 
the Congressional Budget Office shows, likely not 
indefinitely. 
Productivity growth (and therefore potential GDP 
growth) has depended on and will continue to depend 
on a favorable policy environment. To date, AI 
development in the U.S. has benefited from access to 
global supply chains that eventually produced cutting-
edge chips, a large cluster of highly skilled researchers 
and technicians, and institutions that protect and 
encourage intellectual property. 
The magnitude and duration of the productivity boost 
would have to be unprecedented. As it stands, any 
boost to potential GDP growth from AI will have to offset 
(and eventually surpass) the drag from a shrinking 
labor force. Fiscal policy will also be an uphill battle 
and a moving target, given growing non-discretionary 
obligations and interest payments, as well as the lack of 
political will to tackle structural fiscal issues.

AI will likely have at least some positive effect on measured productivity growth, but we do not believe that 
the U.S. (or other countries) will be able to grow their way out of debt using an AI-driven productivity boost. 
AI growth will have to overcome the drag from demographics and a shrinking labor force, and structural 
fiscal challenges will continue to worsen. The chances of achieving the consistent high real growth required 
are low.

What about other economies?
In the event it is possible to grow out of the debt (likely 
with the help of fiscal reform), we believe the only other 
large country that could approximately follow the path of 
the U.S. is China. Most other countries cannot grow as 
fast as the U.S. Productivity growth in Japan and Europe is 
much lower, and they also face their unique demographic, 
fiscal and investment challenges. 
Interestingly, if there is any fiscal policy improvement 
in the U.S., it may help other countries’ indebtedness. 
While we do not believe other countries could grow their 
way out of debt, a fiscal improvement in the U.S. would 
likely lower yields globally, as most sovereign debt is still 
implicitly linked to U.S. Treasuries.
In any case, basing a deficit-reduction strategy on lofty 
and speculative expectations of a new technology is an 
uncertain strategy. The timing and level of productivity 
improvement are still uncertain.
Outlook
We have no change to our forecast this month. The Iran 
conflict continues, and we expect high headline inflation 
for 2026. Falling oil prices in 2027 should bring headline 
inflation down significantly, although the Iran conflict has 
gone on longer than expected. 
In line with market expectations, we expect a hike in 
December of 2026 followed by another in Q1 2027. Yields 
will likely remain high as markets pay more attention to the 
fiscal situation of the U.S., combined with higher inflation 
and still relatively strong economic growth. 
Risks
There are two main risks to our current outlook. 
The Iran conflict continues to be a risk. September’s inflation data (CPI, PPI and import prices) all showed 
significant effects from higher energy prices. Retail sales data also showed consumers spending a lot more 
(nominally) on energy. Broadening of this inflation effect means the Fed might raise rates more than currently 
expected, pushing up both the policy rate and 10-year outlooks. 
The AI boom is also seeing some threats to its growth over the last few quarters. Some of the growth may 
come back depending on the election outcomes in November, but popular pushback against AI continues, and 
there are rising concerns about the business models that support valuations as well as the level of borrowing.
