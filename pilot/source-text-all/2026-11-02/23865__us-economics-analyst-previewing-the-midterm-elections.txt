While the Senate outcome looks less certain than the House, it likely also matters less to 
the policy outlook if it comes in addition to a Democratic win in the House. Senate rules 
require 60 votes to pass most legislation—the exception is “reconciliation” bills that 
need only 51 votes—so measures would need bipartisan support to reach President 
Trump’s desk in any scenario.1 More importantly, President Trump could veto any 
legislation Congress passes. 
That said, the Senate alone is responsible for confirming President Trump’s nominees, 
which a Democratic Senate could block. The president could in theory make recess 
appointments through 2028, but a Democratic House and Senate would likely deny him 
the opportunity by declining to recess. President Trump could designate “acting” 
officials to fill vacancies at cabinet agencies (e.g., the Treasury or State Dept.) if 
necessary, but not at independent agencies like the Federal Reserve or in the courts. A 
Democratic Senate win next week would incentivize President Trump to make personnel 
changes quickly, so that the Republican-majority Senate could confirm them before 
year’s end.2 
Winning the Senate majority would also give Democrats slightly more leverage in policy 
disputes with congressional Republicans and the Trump administration, as the majority 
sets the agenda and writes the legislation. This would come into play primarily around 
key fiscal deadlines and other must-pass legislation, when Senate Republicans would be 
under pressure to avoid whatever disruption might ensue from blocking bills. In theory,

Fiscal Deadlines Would Grow More Important 
As bipartisan support would be needed to enact any legislation, control of one or both 
chambers would mainly allow Democrats to block Republican initiatives rather than 
enact their own. Most of the Trump administration’s key initiatives have relied on 
executive action rather than legislation, so it could be argued that the midterm election 
outcome matters less than usual. Still, a Democratic majority could use forcing events 
like the expiration of spending authority (risking a shutdown) or debt limit deadlines 
(risking a lapse in borrowing capacity) to try to win concessions.  
The next relevant deadline is Dec. 11, when spending authority expires.  It is not 
uncommon for Congress to reach a full-year spending deal in a post-election lame-duck 
session, as the outgoing party sees one last chance to exert greater influence while the 
incoming party sees an opportunity to clear items from the agenda before taking power. 
This looks unlikely this year given policy disputes and the distance between the two 
sides’ proposed spending levels. Instead, Congress seems likely to push the deadline to 
February or March 2027. 
The other deadline is the debt limit, which is likely to be reached by Q1 2027. The 
Treasury will likely manage this by running down its balance at the Fed over the summer 
and through the use of other “extraordinary measures” it typically employs around debt 
limit deadlines, but Congress will likely need to raise the debt limit no later than October 
2027 (Exhibit 6).  Congressional Republicans are likely to attempt to raise the debt limit 
before year’s end, but thin margins in the House and pushback from fiscal conservatives 
make this unlikely to succeed.

Spending 
After a fiscal boost in 2026, the fiscal impulse next year looks roughly neutral (Exhibit 8). 
While a divided government typically leads to a less expansionary fiscal policy, spending 
might rise slightly under Democratic control.   
 
A “win-win” deal between the parties on spending would boost defense and 
non-defense funding. “Discretionary” funding that Congress must approve each year has 
been roughly flat in nominal terms since the start of the second Trump administration, as 
Congress has simply extended prior spending bills. Republicans accepted this because 
their priorities—defense and immigration funding—got a boost using the party-line 
reconciliation process instead. Democrats accepted this because it avoided the cuts to 
non-defense spending that Republicans proposed. Republicans launched an attempt to 
pass another boost to defense spending via reconciliation earlier this year, but that 
effort looks unlikely to succeed and, if it does, it would provide only a fraction of the 
more than $500bn increase the White House is seeking. The upshot is that if the White 
House wants more defense funding, it would need to agree to a proportional boost to 
non-defense funding, which Democrats have consistently demanded. It isn’t clear how 
such a deal would come out, but it would be unlikely to boost spending by more than a 
few tenths of a percent of GDP. 
Reversing spending cuts from last year’s fiscal package and reinstating expanded ACA 
health insurance subsidies would also add to the deficit. The spending cuts enacted in 
last year’s legislation totaled around $1.5 trillion through 2034, but amounted to only 
$85bn in 2027 (0.25% of GDP). The bulk of the cuts fall on Medicaid (-$55bn in 2027) 
and nutrition programs (-$18bn).  Reinstating ACA health insurance subsidies is also a 
clear Democratic priority and would add around $50bn in annual spending if they 
followed their proposal from last year. Democrats might condition an extension of 
spending authority or a debt limit increase on the reversal of some of these cuts.

Likely to Raise Tariff Rates Broadly 
Democratic leaders have indicated they will try to reverse some of the tariffs the Trump 
administration has imposed if they win the majority.  Provisions rolling back tariffs on 
specific countries or products could make their way into negotiations around fiscal 
deadlines and could potentially even pass as standalone measures given opposition to 
tariffs among some Senate Republicans. By far the most likely focus would be reducing 
tariffs on imports from Canada.   
However, after the election we also expect President Trump to release findings on the 
Section 301 investigation into industrial overcapacity. This was launched alongside the 
Section 301 investigation into forced labor, which was used to impose a 10-12.5% tariff 
after the Supreme Court ruled the prior IEEPA-based tariff regime unconstitutional. We 
expect the combined measures to restore tariff rates for individual trading partners to 
roughly the rate in place prior to the Supreme Court ruling, though additional 
exemptions granted since the ruling would result in a slightly lower effective tariff rate 
than at the beginning of the year (Exhibit 9). 
 
AI and Data Centers 
There is a good chance that the next Congress passes legislation regulating AI, given the 
economic importance of the sector and the risks that frontier labs have themselves 
highlighted. That said, it is far from clear that the midterm election outcome will 
materially shift the outlook on the issue.  
Data center regulation is a natural focus ahead of the election because it is primarily a 
state and local issue and 36 states elect their governor Nov. 3. However, we expect the 
issue to fade somewhat after Election Day. While voters have very clear directional views 
on data centers, they are a very low political priority. For example, while 64% of voters 
say they would be less likely to vote for an official who supports building a data center in 
their community, only 5% of voters list it as one of their top two most important issues.
