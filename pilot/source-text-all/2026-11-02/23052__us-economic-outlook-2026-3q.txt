Key Takeaways
•
What’s behind the global bond selloff? Much ink has been spilled chalking it up to 
rising worries about the US government’s fiscal health. But we believe that shifting 
perceptions of the strength of the US economy and labor market have played a 
bigger role (Page 30). Partly that’s because nearly all of the yield increase has come 
from higher real (inflation-adjusted) yields. By contrast, inflation expectations 
embedded in bond yields (“breakeven” yields) have hardly risen since 2025, despite 
a 60% rise in gold prices ostensibly driven by fears of currency debasement.
•
The artificial intelligence boom is a key part of that economic strength. Its boost to 
aggregate demand pushes up interest rates, given that gross domestic product is 
already about at potential. But the AI boost is likely to subside. Either AI’s 
productivity boost leads to steep job cuts, which hasn’t happened yet, or AI 
disappoints and investment slows drastically. Either case will call for the Fed to cut 
rates deeply to support continued healthy economic growth. We still expect a net 
100 basis points in rate cuts through the end of 2028, driving the 10-year Treasury 
yield to 3.5% by 2029, down from 5.0% currently.
•
Workers are already being squeezed by real wage growth dipping into negative 
territory (Page 12). That’s helping fuel the corporate earnings boom, but it is 
causing household savings to dip ever lower, which we expect to lead to slowing 
consumption growth.

Growth Boost From AI to Fade by 2028
GDP growth has downshifted a gear after averaging 2.8% over 2022-2 . That’s been 
driven by lower population growth, the expiration of some temporary factors boosting 
growth, and the lingering effects of high interest rates. 
We expect the boost to GDP growth from AI will diminish as spending grows at a more 
reasonable pace. That causes GDP growth to slow a bit further through 2028. Slower 
GDP growth reduces the demand for workers, so we expect the unemployment rate to 
tick up to an average of 4.7% in 2028 from 4.3% in 2025. Easing monetary policy and 
other factors should drive a rebound in GDP growth in the later years of our forecast.
Inflation to Resume Falling After 2026
The oil shock from the Iran war contributes around 0.8 percentage points to inflation in 
2026. Tariffs are also still exerting an upward influence. As these factors fade, inflation 
should resume its fall in coming years. Housing inflation continues trending down. And 
wage growth has already returned to levels consistent with inflation at or below the 
Fed’s 2  target.

After Second-Half 2026 Upswing, We Expect GDP Growth to Resume Slowing
 
Real GDP by Expenditure, % Quarter-Over-Quarter Growth (Annualized)
Second-Quarter GDP Growth Depressed by Net Exports and Inventories
Economic growth in the second quarter was considerably stronger than headline GDP 
growth of 1.5% quarter over quarter. The statistically noisy categories of exports and 
inventories subtracted 1.9 percentage points from growth. Accordingly, final domestic 
demand (which removes net exports and inventories) was up 3.4% quarter over quarter. 
That solid result was driven by a rebound in consumption growth from a weak first 
quarter as well as very strong nonresidential fixed investment growth at 8.5% 
annualized.
As is the usual pattern, exports and inventories will likely swing in the other direction to 
make a large positive contribution in the second half of 2026, contributing an average 
0.9 percentage points to the average 3% growth we expect in the third and fourth 
quarters. After that temporary boost, however, we expect GDP growth to start slowing 
again.

Productivity Growth Allowing Near-Jobless Expansion
Job Growth to Turn Negative in 2028
On the supply side, productivity growth—running well above its recent historical 
norm—is enabling the seeming paradox of an economic expansion nearly bereft of job 
growth. Labor productivity growth averaged 1.0% in the decade before the pandemic, 
but over 2020-25, it averaged 1.9%. The impact of AI is likely to sustain continued high 
productivity growth in coming years. In order to realize an adequate return on 
investment on AI, companies will have to start trimming their headcount. That will push 
total hours worked and job growth into negative territory in 2028, despite GDP growth 
remaining well above zero. This would be similar to the dot-com recession (which 
arguably wasn’t really a recession)—GDP growth averaged 1.3% over 2001-02, while job 
growth averaged negative 0.7%.

Weak Real Wage  rowth  elping to Fuel Stock Market’s Rise
Wages and Inflation, % Growth Year Over Year
Corporate Profits and Labor Income, % Share Gross Domestic Income
Real Wage Growth Turns Negative in Q2
Real wage growth dropped to negative 0.4% year over year in the second quarter of 
2026, based on our composite wage growth index and PCE inflation. The slowdown has 
been exacerbated by the energy price shock, but it began well before it, as real wage 
growth had averaged 1.9% in 2024 before slowing to 0.9% by the fourth quarter of 2025.
An important accounting formula is: The percent change in the labor share 
approximately equals real wage growth minus productivity growth. With real wage 
growth running well below productivity growth at 1.8% year over year, the labor share of 
income has slipped. In turn, that’s helped boost domestic corporate profits to over 13  
of GDP, up from just 9% in 2019 before the pandemic. This has been a major driver of 
the brisk corporate earnings growth that’s fueled the ongoing stock market e pansion. It 
seems unlikely, however, that households will be able to shoulder such meager real 
wage growth for long before they pull back consumption.

Need to Boost Savings Will Probably Drive Lower Consumption
Personal Saving Rate (%)
Household Net Worth, % GDP
Saving Rate 4 Percentage Points Below Prepandemic Level
The personal saving rate was a mere 2.8% in the second quarter, over 4 percentage 
points below the 2018-19 average of 6.9%. While household consumption growth has 
slowed, real wage growth has been slowing even faster (and job growth has slowed as 
well), causing spending growth to outstrip household income. 
Part of the story is the wealth effect from the runup in asset prices. Household net worth 
is on track to stand around 600% of GDP at the end of 2026, up from 520% on average 
over 2018-19. Based on a historical regression, that explains around 2 percentage points 
of the 4-percentage-point shortfall in the personal saving rate. The rest of the savings 
shortfall could partly represent a delayed reaction to the recent deceleration in real 
wage growth. To the e tent that’s true, we’d e pect it to correct eventually. Also, asset 
price returns are likely to be lower than in the recent past, leading to some reversion in 
household net worth as a share of GDP and a diminishing of the wealth effect.

We expect private fixed investment growth to accelerate to 4.1% in 2026 from 2.7% in

Labor Market No Longer Weakening, but Some Slack Remains
Unemployment at 4.1%, Still Slightly Above Its Natural Rate
We think the labor market is neither clearly strengthening nor weakening but 
maintaining a state slightly below full employment. 
Growth in nonfarm payroll employment edged back up to 0.4% as of August 2026 after a 
low of 0.1% as of February 2026. The current growth rate is close to our estimate of 
breakeven job growth at 0.35 . “Breakeven” denotes the growth rate required to keep 
unemployment flat.  ence, it’s unsurprising that unemployment, averaging  .1  in the 
last three months, is about in line with where it was a year ago (4.2% as of August 2025). 
In between, there was an uptick in unemployment toward the end of 2025 (to 4.5%), 
which has since subsided. The rise and fall may have been exaggerated by limited data 
collection during the shutdown. But with the current job growth rate in line with our 
breakeven estimate, we don’t e pect unemployment to continue falling.
As it stands, we think the labor market still has some slack, existing in a state slightly 
below full employment. The current unemployment rate of 4.1% is slightly above our 
estimate of the natural rate of unemployment at 3.7% (which was the average rate in 
2019, on the eve of the pandemic). But perhaps the clearest sign is the tepid pace of 
wage growth, evincing diminished bargaining power of workers.

So Far, AI-Driven Job Loss Has Probably Been Small
White-Collar Industries, % Share of Total Employment
But Young College Grads May Be Canaries in the Coal Mine
AI has probably only caused a modest amount of job loss so far. Most of the decline in 
white-collar employment since the 2023 peak1 is attributable to a correction of the 
overstaffing that built up over 2020-22. To isolate AI’s impact, we might compare the 
white-collar share of total employment with its 2017-19 trend, before the hiring binge. 
The shortfall currently stands at about 0.4 percentage points, or around 0.6 million jobs. 
But even this is probably an overestimate of AI’s impact. Even without AI, the upward 
trend in the white-collar share of employment probably would have slackened 
compared with the 2010s’ pace.

The Short End  as Led This Year’s Yield Increase
Why Have Yields Risen? It Depends on the Time Frame
For all the talk about government-debt worries driving up long-dated yields, the yield 
increase in 2026 has been led by the short end, in tandem with shifting expectations for 
monetary policy. The federal-funds rate expected by first-quarter 2027 has shifted by 
1.00-1.25 percentage points. That’s driven the 5-year yield up by 1.1 points, while the 30-
year is up by a lesser 0.5 points. The yield curve after five years has moved in a roughly 
parallel manner, with the 5-year, 5-year forward and 20-year, 10-year forward both 
increasing by 0.5 and 0.4 percentage points, respectively.1 That points to an upward 
revision in the e pected natural (or “terminal”) federal-funds rate. 
Federal-funds rate expectations have increased, owing greatly to dissipating labor 
market fears as the unemployment rate has ceased rising. The inflationary shock from 
Iran is playing a role, but more important for the Fed is sticky core inflation (which 
e cludes energy prices). The latter indicates an economy still running slightly hot. AI’s 
impact on investment and stock prices is playing a key role here  insofar as that’s 
expected to persist, it boosts the natural rate component of longer yields.
On the other hand, if we slightly lengthen the time frame, the chief development has 
been the steepening of the yield curve. Since the big peak in October 2023, the 30-
year/5-year spread has widened by over 0.5 percentage points. The US fiscal situation is 
a key contributor to this, although it’s less about crisis risk and more about debt supply.

Isn’t the upward trend in gold prices in the past few years a product of the

Term Premium Is Trending Up, but That  oesn’t Reflect  ebt Crisis Risks  
Term Premium Is Merely Converging Back to Historical Average
Another vantage point is to decompose yields into the expected short rate over the life 
of the bond and the term premium. The latter compensates investors for risk. According 
to the model of Kim and Wright, the increase in the 10-year yield in 2026 has been 
driven about equally by increases in the expected federal-funds rate and the term 
premium.
To the extent the model is correct (which is not guaranteed, as the term premium is a 
latent variable that is never measured directly), the term premium’s rise probably 
doesn’t reflect rising risk of a US fiscal crisis. In the event of a crisis, the main risk is a 
surge in inflation to wipe out the real value of the debt. But as we’ve seen, that risk isn’t 
being priced into markets currently. Without inflation, the venue for wiping out debt in a 
crisis is outright default. But that’s e tremely unlikely for an advanced economy with 
control of its currency like the US, and we doubt any default risk is being incorporated 
into the term premium.
Instead, we interpret the rise in the term premium over the past few years largely as a 
reversion to its historical average. This follows more than a decade in which the term 
premium was compressed by central bank purchases, along with macro risks being 
concentrated on the deflationary side for which long-term bonds are an attractive 
hedge. The term premium is now closing in on its 1990-2019 average of 1.0%, in line 
with our long-term assumption for the spread between the 10-year yield and the 
federal-funds rate.

Debt Outlook, While Dire, Has Held About Steady in Recent Years
The outlook for the federal debt is concerning, with the Congressional Budget Office 
projecting federal debt to rise from 98% of GDP at the end of 2025 to 175% by 2056. 
 owever, this outlook hasn’t actually changed much in recent years on net. The CB ’s

against the notion that bad fiscal news has induced investors to begin heavily pricing in 
crisis risk.
The growing US debt load has played a role in rising yields via the term premium. But 
it’s best to think about it as one factor among several (including AI’s appetite for 
capital), which have created an excess of supply over demand for long-term debt. We 
can think of investors as existing along a spectrum, and some require a higher term 
premium to hold long-term debt than others. Hence, a higher term premium is needed 
to bring demand in line with increased supply. This is a more mundane mechanism 
than increased pricing of crisis risks.
