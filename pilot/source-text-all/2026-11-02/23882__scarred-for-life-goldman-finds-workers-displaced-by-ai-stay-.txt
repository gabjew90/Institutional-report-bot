 
Scarred For Life: Goldman Finds Workers Displaced 
By AI Stay Jobless Longer, Then Land In Jobs AI 
Will Take Next 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SATURDAY, OCT 31, 2026 - 10:30 PM 
Three and a half years ago, we said AI would come for the white-collar workforce. 
Back in March 2023, when ChatGPT was still a novelty and Nvidia was a $650 billion company, 
we wrote up a Goldman report which found that "roughly two-thirds of current jobs are exposed 
to some degree of AI automation," and that generative AI "could expose the equivalent of 300 
million full-time jobs to automation." Our translation at the time, in "AI Will Lead To 300 Million 
Layoffs In The US And Europe" (Mar 29, 2023), was blunt: "Think of it as the robotization of the 
service sector." 
Then, for about three years, not much happened. The unemployment rate barely moved, the 
AI-doomers were mocked, and the Goldman CEO himself took to the pages of the NYT to 
declare that "The A.I. Job Apocalypse Is Overblown" (which we covered in May). More recently, 
Apollo's Torsten Slok has done some of the most careful work on the topic, finding that AI is 
hitting paychecks, not payrolls... at least not yet. 
Well, "yet" may have just arrived. In a new report (available to pro subs), Goldman economist 
Pierfrancesco Mei writes that "across nonmanufacturing industries, higher AI adoption is now 
associated with weaker employment growth and higher unemployment." Worse, the workers 
getting pushed out are having a much harder time getting back in: 
We find that workers displaced from AI-exposed jobs face more difficult transitions back into 
employment than other displaced workers. They take about a month longer to find new jobs and 
are roughly 10pp more likely to remain unemployed a year after displacement—effects 
comparable in magnitude to those seen in earlier waves of technological change. 
In other words, AI's labor bill is starting to come due, and the workers it displaces are the ones 
paying it. 
 

 
From Paychecks To Payrolls 
First, a quick recap of how we got here, because until very recently the best evidence said the 
opposite. 
In a July 30 white paper titled "The Impact of AI on the U.S. Labor Market," Slok and Apollo's 
Sania Edlich matched AI usage data to 321 occupations and found that pay in the most 
AI-exposed jobs rose 6.7% more slowly after 2023 than in low-exposure work, with no 
statistically significant change in employment. Their read: firms were capturing AI's productivity 
gains through wage compression rather than headcount cuts. As recently as August 22, Slok 
told Bloomberg that AI's employment fallout was "insignificant." 
(Slok has been skeptical of the AI payoff in general: back in June he found zero margin boost 
outside of tech, which raises the uncomfortable question of what exactly companies will do 
when the wage squeeze alone stops being enough.) 
Fast forward to this week, when Goldman chief economist Jan Hatzius wrote in his "Global 
Views: Downside Growth Risks" note that "we are starting to see clearer signs that the AI impact 
is spreading beyond the technology sector." As we detailed in "Peak AI Impulse" on Tuesday, 
the share of firms using AI in an industry now has a statistically significant negative correlation 
with that industry's employment growth and a statistically significant positive one with its 
unemployment rate. Napkin math off Goldman's regressions: every 10pp of extra AI adoption is 
associated with roughly 0.9pp weaker employment growth and a 0.9pp higher unemployment 
rate. And the list of victims now includes finance, insurance, real estate and professional 
services, not just web hosting and computing: 
 
None of this came as a surprise to anyone watching the headlines this month. Our reaction to 
the October 23 batch of pink slips was short: 
 

 
We then tallied the October cuts: roughly 14,000 disclosed job cuts in just over three weeks, at a 
card issuer, a bank, a credit scorer and a streamer as much as at software companies. 
So that's the "jobs" part. The question Goldman tackles now is what happens to the people 
behind those numbers. 
A Month Longer, And 10 Points More Likely To Still Be Jobless 
To answer it, Mei uses newly released data from the Census Bureau's Displaced Worker 
Supplement, a biennial survey of workers who lost their jobs involuntarily, covering over 15,000 
displaced workers from 2016 through 2025. The newest cohort (job losses in 2023-2025) "likely 
captures the first wave of AI-displaced workers." 
Since no survey asks "did a robot take your job?", Goldman builds a composite score from the 
occupation's AI exposure, the industry's AI adoption rate and whether the job loss came from a 
position being eliminated. Then it compares the top 30% (the workers most likely displaced by 
AI) with everyone else who got laid off. 
The result: nothing much through 2024, and then a clear break. Workers most likely displaced 
by AI in 2025 took about a month longer to find a new job and were roughly 10pp more likely to 
still be unemployed a year later: 
 
Note the red dashed lines. Goldman says these short-run effects "are comparable in magnitude 
to our earlier estimates of the impact of past waves of technological change on displaced 
workers." In other words, at least for the people who lose their jobs to it, AI is already doing 
about as much damage as the earlier waves of automation did. That should end the "AI is 
different, it only creates jobs" argument for now. 
 

 
The Scar That Doesn't Heal 
This is the bad part, because Goldman's own history of past tech waves says the damage lasts. 
Using four decades of individual-level data, the bank previously found that: 
...the effects of technological displacement are long-lasting. In the years following a job loss, 
real earnings for technology-displaced workers grew more slowly than those of other displaced 
workers, and their probability of experiencing another unemployment spell remained persistently 
higher. 
How much more slowly? The chart below shows the cumulative real wage gap a decade after 
displacement, 1979-2022. Older, non-college, rural and long-tenured workers fell roughly 8-10 
points behind other laid-off workers, about twice as much as their younger, college-educated, 
urban and short-tenured peers: 
 
Goldman does find one silver lining in the new data: younger workers seem to be handling the 
AI transition better. In 2025, AI-displaced workers under 35 took about three weeks longer than 
other displaced workers to get back into full-time work, versus about five weeks for those 35 and 
older, and they cycled through fewer temporary and side jobs on the way: 
 

 
 
Goldman also notes that college students "appear to be responding to these shifts by 
increasingly choosing majors that feed into occupations with lower AI exposure." That's 
encouraging, although it's probably cold comfort to the 22-25 year olds in the most AI-exposed 
occupations, whose employment the Stanford Digital Economy Lab estimates is about 19% 
below where it would otherwise be, largely because nobody is hiring them in the first place. The 
young adapt faster when they get laid off. Many of them never get hired at all. 
Out Of The Frying Pan... 
Here is the most troubling finding in the note. In earlier tech waves, Goldman says, 
"occupational downgrading was a key driver of the worse labor market outcomes for displaced 
workers": people pushed out by technology tended to land in more routine, less complex jobs, 
"likely because the same technological changes that eliminated their positions also eroded the 
value of their existing skills." 
Something similar, and maybe worse, is happening now. AI-displaced workers are much more 
likely to leave their old occupation and industry altogether. But when they find new work: 
...these workers—especially older ones—have so far increasingly moved into roles with similar 
or even higher AI exposure (Exhibit 4). This is probably because they tend to seek jobs that 
draw on their existing skills, which leaves them more vulnerable to further automation and 
displacement. 
 

 
The chart says it all: the probability that a worker displaced from an AI-risk job lands in a role 
with the same or higher AI exposure hovered in the mid-50s from 2018 through 2023, then 
jumped to about 65% in 2024 and to nearly 75% in 2025: 
 
Translation: three out of four workers knocked out by AI are walking straight back into its path. 
Think of the customer service rep who retrains as a claims processor: the next round of 
automation finds them all over again. 
The BLS data behind Goldman's study tells a similar story at the aggregate level. According to 
the August Worker Displacement release, 7.4 million workers were displaced from their jobs in 
2023-2025, up from 6.3 million in the prior survey. And among long-tenured displaced workers 
who found new full-time jobs, only about 49% earned as much or more than at their lost job, 
down from about 62% two years earlier. Reemployment rates also fall off a cliff with age: 72.9% 
for workers 25-54, 57.3% for those 55-64 and just 38.6% for those 65 and older. 
 

 
 
Slok found that AI was squeezing wages for workers who kept their jobs. It turns out the workers 
who lose them get squeezed too, only harder. 
Waiting For The Recession 
So why isn't the unemployment rate (4.2% in September) screaming yet? Goldman's Joseph 
Briggs had the answer in last Thursday's "Revisiting the Extent and Timing of AI Labor 
Displacement", and it is hardly comforting. 
First, the good news, such as it is. Briggs concedes that "concerns of imminent AI-related labor 
displacement have cooled in recent months after hitting a fever pitch this spring." Layoffs 
attributed to AI in Challenger's job cut report peaked near 40K in May and dropped below 5K in 
both August and September, while news stories on AI and job losses are back to where they 
were at the start of the year: 
 
 

 
(Then again, the October pink slips at Barclays, Amex and FICO came after this chart was 
drawn, so we'd hold off on declaring victory.) 
Now the bad news. Using three separate approaches to history, Briggs finds that each 1% boost 
to labor productivity from technology lifts the unemployment rate by 0.3-0.4pp at the peak, 
driven mostly by more job losses rather than less hiring. Apply that to Goldman's forecast that AI 
eventually raises productivity by 15%, and: 
...these estimates imply that up to 9% of workers could be displaced during the AI transition, 
with an additional headwind from slower hiring weighing on overall employment by 3%. 
That's roughly 15 million American workers, and it's up from the 6-7% Goldman was talking 
about at the start of the year: 
Risks skewed toward greater displacement, indeed. (We noted the escalation to "15 million job 
body bags" back in June, only weeks after Goldman's CEO told everyone not to panic.) And 
Briggs says the risks are still to the upside: displacement was bigger in the late-1990s boom, 
"the period most widely viewed as comparable to today," and after larger productivity shocks, 
which is what Goldman expects AI to be. 
What does "very large" look like? Goldman looked at 25 occupations that technology made 
redundant in the past, from photo processors and proofreaders to file clerks and bank tellers. 
Employment in those jobs fell by around 25% on average in the five years after the decline 
began, and by more than 50% for photo processing: 
 
 

 
Briggs calls these "likely upper bounds" for AI, since the sample was chosen for maximum 
disruption. But several of them (data entry, payroll and order clerks, insurance underwriters) are 
exactly the kind of work AI is going after now. 
Which brings us to timing, and the key point of the note: automation is cyclical. "Historically, 
most of the labor headwinds from routine job automation are concentrated in economic 
recessions," Briggs writes, and the chart shows it clearly. The share of Americans working 
routine jobs held flat through every expansion and then dropped in steps in the early 1980s, 
2001, 2008 and 2020, and never recovered: 
 
The occupation data say the same thing. In the tech-disrupted jobs, each 1pp rise in the overall 
unemployment rate added about 4% more job losses on top of the roughly 25% baseline, as 
separations rose and hiring dried up: 
 

 
 
In other words, companies keep the extra workers while times are good, and the moment the 
economy turns they use the slowdown as cover to automate. Or, as Fortune summed up the 
note, AI could displace one in 11 workers, "but the layoffs are likely waiting for a recession." 
Briggs' conclusion: 
...a more concerning takeaway from our analysis is that a cyclical slowdown would likely 
accelerate AI-related automation and job losses, thereby amplifying the broader business cycle. 
The Fed and other central banks should therefore remain attuned to any emerging downside 
growth or labor market risks once the near-term inflation constraint is lifted, since a quick pivot 
to easing could help ensure that automation-related job losses do not accelerate at a time when 
the economy is ill-equipped to create the new jobs necessary to support a smooth AI transition. 
A "quick pivot to easing" is a tall order from a Fed that Goldman itself still expects to hike again 
in December. Good luck with that. 
Translation: the mass AI layoffs have merely been postponed. All those jobs that AI can already 
do but that companies haven't bothered cutting while profits are booming are sitting in a queue, 
and the first real recession will clear it all at once. Put differently, whenever the next downturn 
hits, it will be the regular cyclical layoffs plus several years' worth of pent-up AI layoffs, on top of 
each other. And with the AI capex boom itself now the main thing propping up growth (as 
Hatzius warned, that impulse is peaking), the trigger for that recession may well be AI too. 
To be fair, not everyone on the Street is there yet. BofA's US economists wrote in Friday's US 
Economic Weekly (also available to pro subs) that "AI's labor market impact is likely to remain 
limited through '26, with broader reallocation more likely in '27," and they expect payrolls to rise 
an above-consensus 75K when the October jobs report comes out next Friday. Even the bull 
case, then, puts the reckoning about two months away. And the AI CEOs who spent the past 
three years predicting a white-collar bloodbath have suddenly turned optimistic, as we noted in 
 

 
"Tech CEOs: Just Kidding About That Jobpocalypse" (Jul 6), probably because nothing builds 
support for new data centers like a promise to keep everyone employed. 
The More AI Spreads, The Worse It Gets 
Put it all together and the conclusion is hard to escape. According to Briggs, only 23% of US 
establishments have built AI into some of their regular workflows, and even that "overstates the 
amount of automation that is taking place today." That's the adoption level that has already 
produced statistically significant job losses outside tech, and AI-displaced workers who take a 
month longer to find work, are 10pp more likely to still be unemployed a year later, and mostly 
land in jobs that are next in line for automation. 
So what happens if (and it's still an "if," given how much of the AI capex is financed with debt) 
adoption doubles or triples from here? Then every one of these effects should get bigger: more 
displaced workers chasing fewer jobs that AI can't do, longer stretches out of work, and a 
second round of layoffs for anyone who moved into the next exposed role. And per Briggs, the 
bulk of it is likely to land all at once, as soon as the next recession strikes, exactly when the 
economy has the least room to absorb it. 
The layoffs keep coming in the meantime. On Friday, Comcast filed WARN notices for 634 jobs 
at its Philadelphia headquarters (plus 278 more in New Jersey and Washington state) as it 
restructures in part by "deploying AI agents to connect with customers," days after the Dutch 
government said it will cut 25,000 civil servant jobs in an "AI-led efficiency drive." 
Goldman ends on a cautiously optimistic note, betting that the young will adapt and that 
students are already choosing more AI-resistant majors. We hope they're right. But the bank's 
own data says the first wave of AI-displaced workers is following the same path as every 
previous wave of technological change: longer unemployment, lower pay and a higher chance 
of losing the next job too. Three years after we first warned about AI and the labor market, the 
evidence is finally catching up.
