Looking out the Private Credit window 
     
   
 
 
2027 to usher in a recovery cycle 
PC has had a tough 2026 amid high software concentration, smaller revenue footprint, 
and rising interest costs. While these portfolio challenges remain and are expected to 
weigh on default rates and returns again next year, we think mitigants such as higher 
quality deals, better spreads, and improved fundraising/distributions could feed a PC 
recovery cycle in 2027. Looking out the window, we see emerging green shoots. 
Better vintages, but not a clean slate 
Private credit’s recovery is likely to start in new deals, not legacy portfolios: better deal-
flow, compensation, and terms should improve the setup for 2027. We expect new-issue 
spreads to widen ~20bp to 540bp, a gradual normalization rather than a sharp repricing. 
Yet we also project increasing non-accruals and a 7.0% default rate, with refinancing 
pressure and software/services exposure keeping older vintages vulnerable.  
More inflows, but modest primary comeback 
Fundraising has recovered with distributions supporting capital recycling: vehicles raised 
$105bn through 3Q26, up 21% vs ‘25, driven by a record $72bn in Q2’26. However, the 
durability of the rebound is questionable given concentrated inflows, and a significant 
sequential fall in Q3 inflows. That said, fundraising’s historical 2-3 qtr lead on 
deployment should feed new underwriting, and drive a marginal 6% increase in 2027, 
primarily in new money, as some refinancings migrate to BSL.  
RV favors Debt over Equity  
We favor PC over PE as higher debt-service costs and underwater equity stakes in 
challenged sectors drive PE underperformance absent a significant earnings rebound. 
Similarly, unlisted BDC redemption pressures, which are likely to persist, should remain 
primarily an equity problem, with credit implications limited to selective downgrades 
rather than collective distress. We think that PC and BSL space could continue to 
converge as larger borrowers refi upwards, and a developing PC secondary market 
broadens portfolio-management flexibility for GPs and LPs.

 Private Credit in 2027 
Private Credit has had its fair share of scrutiny this year, with the odds stacked up 
against it. Amid high software & service concentrations, smaller revenue footprints, and 
now rising interest expense, the asset class has faced a series of headwinds, 
suppressing return prospects and investor interest for the asset class as a diversification 
and yield-enhancing tool. We see those challenges persist into 2027. 
At the same time, Private Credit remains an all-important cog in the credit wheel given 
its formidable capital coffers, and access to deal-flow. We think both these dimensions 
will help improve PC appeal next year - through higher quality new entrants that provide 
more compensation at better deal-terms, and improving fundraising prospects that 
chases this exposure. In addition, should negative sentiment afflicting the AI complex 
extend into the new year, this is likely to shrink the private over public credit premium, 
incentivizing issuers to fund their capex needs in PC. All things equal, this improves the 
availability of high-quality deals at attractive spreads for the new PC vintages. 
All told, while portfolio challenges from historical vintages remain and will likely weigh 
on blended returns again next year, we think that emerging mitigating factors will feed a 
recovery cycle in PC in 2027.  
Record Q2 Drives Fundraising Rebound 
Private credit fundraising has reaccelerated in 2026 following a comparatively softer 
fundraising environment in 2025. North American private credit vehicles raised $105 
billion through 3Q26, up 21% from $87 billion over the comparable period in 2025. The 
improvement has been driven almost entirely by a record 2Q26, when $72 billion was 
raised across private credit strategies (including $46 billion in direct lending) versus just 
$15 billion of total private credit fundraising in 2Q25. 
Record 2Q26 fundraising coincided with a sharp reversal in Fed rate expectations and 
stronger demand for floating-rate credit. However, the YTD rebound remains heavily 
concentrated in a single quarter: fundraising fell to $15 billion in 3Q26 from $72 billion 
in 2Q26, raising questions around the durability of the recovery.

Issuance should increase marginally on new money  
Higher fundraising to drive issuance rebound 
Heading into 2026, we expected private credit issuance to decline roughly 15%, 
reflecting slower fundraising and greater scrutiny of existing portfolio exposures. 
Issuance has slowed more than expected, with LTM gross volumes at $111 billion as of 
3Q26, down 25% YoY and well below the $148 billion recorded a year earlier. From here, 
our forward indicators point to a gradual inflection in issuance, with $118 billion 
expected over the NTM, representing a marginal 6% increase from the current $111bn 
level (Exhibit 6). 
The expected recovery is supported by improving capital availability following the recent 
pickup in fundraising. Historically, fundraising leads deployment by roughly 2–3 quarters, 
suggesting that the capital raised during the record 2nd quarter haul should increasingly 
support underwriting in 2027. Our issuance framework, which incorporates fundraising 
alongside middle-market CLO formation, LBO leverage, rates and market volatility, 
points to higher issuance NTM, albeit with volumes remaining well below the 2024 peak.

refinancings. While the elevated near-term maturity wall would ordinarily imply greater

increasingly migrating into the BSL market as the bar for PC primary market activity 
remains high given general cautiousness (Exhibit 8). As a result, higher fundraising 
should provide incremental capacity for new deployment, while refinancing activity 
remains comparatively subdued despite the maturity calendar.

Defaults and non-accruals to remain elevated 
We estimate the next-twelve-month (NTM) non-accrual rate using the relationship 
between starting prices and subsequent non-accrual entries. For each price bucket, we 
calculate the proportion of performing exposure that enters non-accrual (“NA”) status 
over the following twelve months. Like loan and high-yield default rates, this measure 
captures the flow of new credit stress, rather than the stock of non-accrual exposure at 
a given point in time. 
Exhibit 10 shows that forward NA rates are consistently highest in loans with lower fair 
value marks: loans marked below 80pts tend to have a 20-50% rate of entering NA 
status over subsequent NTM period, compared to a <1% rate in Loans marked above 
95pts. Within each price bucket, rates also vary with prevailing macroeconomic 
conditions.

holdings implies an NTM non-accrual rate of 3.4%, up from 2.9% as of Q2. This scenario

BDC grief is largely an equity issue 
Regular readers of our pages know that we have been cautious around BDC performance 
(see Five stages of BDC grief and BDC stress tests – when does the clock run out?). In 
2027, we continue to expect redemption pressure, and our estimates factor in the 
highest allowable rates (5% AUM).  
That said, we have also maintained that cracks in unlisted BDC land are unlikely to spill 
over into broader credit markets. First, BDCs have a range of levers to pull and 
dispersion across them is high. Second, low prevailing leverage across PC largely makes 
this an equity problem, not a credit one. Our analysis suggests that aggregate leverage 
across PC sits <1x (Exhibit 13). In addition, the weakest link - the unlisted BDCs - 
account for only $350bn of the asset class, and their equity portion constitutes an even 
smaller piece of the pie - $190bn (~15% of PC). As such, continuing redemptions are 
likely to afflict a contained cross section, at the rate of 5%/qtr.  
All told, BDC credit problems amount to selective downgrades, not collective distress.

We see scope for the private credit premium to compress as the BSL market 
increasingly provides refinancing off-take for borrowers migrating out of private credit. 
Because PC has historically represented a lower-quality borrower universe than BSL, 
these flows should gradually narrow the credit-quality gap between the two markets. All

PC vs PE 
We came into 2026 expecting a PE recovery which didn’t pan out.  
First, we were penciling in a friendlier rate environment and open primary markets 
enabling exit opportunities to rise from their paltry post-2022 levels. Instead, we got 
geopolitical shocks and rate-hikes - reversing both those expectations.  
Second, the AI revolution has dealt a blow to PE portfolios that tend to have a 
disproportionate exposure to software and services. Our analysis shows that many of the 
’22 vintage deals in these sectors are now likely to have their equities under water in the 
face of AI disintermediation (see report: The next default cycle). To be clear, existing 
business moats have extended the timeline of disruption, allowing managers time to 
remediate their portfolios, but this will also require capital infusions- which we are not 
seeing broad-based evidence of.  
Third, a higher rate environment induces natural headwinds for equity part of floating 
rate capital structures, as precious cashflows go towards servicing costlier debt instead. 
This is one of the reasons why while PC distributions have recovered from lows, PE 
distributions have not (Exhibit 4, Exhibit 5). 
To us, this means 1) capital structures of older vintages will continue to remain 
challenged- certainly equities if not also selective debt portions. 2) blended PE returns 
will remain muted as older vintages offset better prospects of newer ones 3) absent a 
significant jump in earnings, PE is poised to underperform PC as capital gets rerouted to 
senior debt

Private Credit Secondaries 
Private credit secondaries remain a relatively small but rapidly developing segment of 
the broader private capital secondary market, with total deal volume of $20bn in 1H26, 
up 122% from $9bn in 1H25 and already matching FY2025 volume. GP-led transactions 
accounted for $17bn vs $3bn of LP-led activity, making GP-leds the primary driver of 
growth.  The development mirrors the more mature PE secondary market, where GP-led 
structures, and particularly continuation vehicles (CVs), are increasingly used as 
portfolio-management tools, allowing sponsors to provide liquidity to existing LPs while 
retaining exposure to assets beyond the original fund life. For PC, the growing use of 
GP-led solutions similarly expands the toolkit available to managers and investors for 
managing liquidity and existing portfolio exposure, supported by the build-out of 
dedicated secondary capital. 
Increasing turnover to drive another step-up in ‘27 
We expect private credit secondary volume to reach $40bn in 2027, as turnover rate 
continues to rise toward PE-like levels as GP-led structures became more varied and 
secondary markets more pervasive. 
Market development has been considerably faster than we expected. Based on the 
updated data, we estimate turnover increased to 1.5% in 1H26 alone, from 1% in 2025. 
For 2027, we assume turnover reaches 2%, reflecting continued growth in GP-led 
transactions, greater use of secondaries for liquidity and portfolio management, and a 
deeper dedicated buyer base. This implies $40bn of secondary volume in 2027, 
effectively pulling forward part of the maturation we previously expected to occur over a 
five-year horizon.  
The framework remains consistent with our prior methodology: growth comes from both 
expansion of the underlying private credit market and, increasingly, higher secondary 
turnover as the market develops. If PC AUM reaches $3tn by 2030, this could push 
secondary volumes to $120bn annually, longer-term.
