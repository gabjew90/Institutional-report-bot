Oil: the calm before another storm? 
• 
We are raising our 31 December Brent crude oil forecast from USD95/bbl to 
USD105/bbl. Importantly, the revision does not assume another escalation in Middle 
East hostilities.  
• 
Instead, it reflects our view that the physical constraints keeping the oil market tight are 
unlikely to dissipate before year-end. The US midterm elections on 3 November 
nevertheless present an important catalyst.  
• 
Another escalation would hit a market with fewer shock absorbers than at the start of the 
conflict, leaving risks to our revised forecast firmly skewed to the upside. 
Event risk: US elections 
The US midterm elections on 3 November represent the next major geopolitical catalyst for 
oil markets. The possibility of renewed hostilities after the vote has increased uncertainty 
around an already fragile physical market. Escalation is far from inevitable, but investors 
must consider not only the probability of another supply shock, but also the condition of the 
market should one occur. 
On that measure, the picture is uncomfortable. Eight months of adapting to disrupted Middle 
Eastern supply have materially weakened the buffers that absorbed the initial shock. 
Strategic inventories have been drawn down, tanker availability has tightened, alternative 
export routes are stretched and refined-product inventories are depleted. Chinese crude 
buying is recovering, and underlying demand may be stronger than headline consumption 
suggests. 
Our Oil Market Resilience Dashboard characterises current conditions as ‘vulnerable’. 
Persian Gulf exports stood at 14.3mb/d as of 26 October and were weakening, effective 
throughput through the Strait of Hormuz was around 60% of the pre-conflict normal, global 
distillate stocks remained 4.9% below their five-year average and diesel cracks had reached 
USD103.8/bbl. Importantly, we estimate effective surplus OPEC capacity at zero under 
prevailing logistical constraints. 
These conditions are unlikely to normalise before the end of the year, which is why we are 
raising our year-end Brent forecast from USD95/bbl to USD105/bbl. 
The forecast does not require another geopolitical shock. Rather, it assumes that the 
constraints already confronting Middle Eastern exports, shipping and refined-product 
markets persist through year-end. Further escalation therefore represents upside risk to our 
new forecast rather than being embedded within it. 
At USD95/bbl, our previous forecast allowed for greater normalisation than we now expect. 
USD105/bbl reflects a market where scarcity persists even if geopolitical conditions do not 
deteriorate. 
The next barrel lost matters more 
The oil market has proved remarkably adaptable since the escalation in Middle East 
hostilities in February. Strategic-stock releases, alternative export routes, ship-to-ship 
transfers, longer shipping journeys and demand adjustments prevented the initial disruption 
to Persian Gulf supply from developing into a more severe shortage. 
But that resilience has come at a cost. Persian Gulf exports remain constrained and effective 
throughput through Hormuz is running at only around 60% of normal. Importantly, the 
distinction between productive and deliverable capacity has widened. Persian Gulf 
producers may retain the ability to increase production, but that provides limited immediate 
protection if additional barrels cannot readily transit the strait, alternative pipelines are 
constrained or sufficient tanker capacity is unavailable.

This changes the response to another disruption. Earlier in the conflict, lost barrels could be 
offset through inventory drawdowns, releases from strategic reserves, utilisation of spare 
capacity and rerouting. Those buffers have since heavily diminished. The system therefore 
has less capacity to absorb another shock. 
The marginal cost of disruption is rising. Losing the next barrel could have a materially larger 
price impact than losing the first. That asymmetry sits at the heart of our forecast revision. 
We do not need additional supply losses to justify USD105/bbl. But if another disruption 
occurs, the weaker starting point has the potential to produce a disproportionate price 
response. 
Shipping is the new spare capacity 
The loss of flexibility is clearest in shipping. Middle Eastern producers have developed 
alternative ways of reaching consumers, including greater use of pipelines, loading outside 
the Persian Gulf and ship-to-ship transfers. These adaptations have helped maintain 
exports, but they have also increased the time, distance and complexity of delivering each 
barrel. 
By mid-October, attacks on tankers transiting the Strait of Hormuz had increased 
substantially. Shuttling crude out of the Persian Gulf was adding roughly a week to some 
voyages, while longer diversions towards Asia also increased journey times. Persian Gulf-to-
China very large crude carrier (VLCC) hire costs had approached USD1m/day. Long 
voyages remove capacity from the market because vessels remain occupied for longer. 
Shipping capacity has consequently become another form of global spare capacity. This 
creates a potentially self-reinforcing squeeze if conditions worsen. A deterioration in shipping 
could therefore be as consequential as an outright loss of production. More importantly for 
our base case, these logistical pressures do not need to worsen to support higher prices. 
Simply persisting near current levels would constrain the market's ability to rebuild 
inventories before year-end. 
The market has already drawn on its insurance 
Strategic reserves provided an important buffer when Middle Eastern supply initially fell. But 
emergency releases redistribute supply but do not replace lost production, refining or 
shipping capacity. 
Planned G7 releases provided welcome relief, particularly to stressed product markets. Yet 
repeated withdrawals inevitably leave less flexibility to confront the next disruption. Global 
spare crude inventory cover has subsequently fallen to around 41 days, after approaching 
45 days earlier this year (Figure 4). 
Strategic reserves are not yet exhausted, but their marginal effectiveness is diminishing. 
Another supply shock would begin with less inventory insurance than the first.

Product markets remain the pressure point 
Crude inventories alone understate the tightness in the physical market. Global distillate 
inventories remain are sitting around 4.9% below their five-year average, while diesel cracks 
are close to USD104/bbl. 
This matters because supplying additional crude does not automatically produce additional 
diesel, jet fuel or gasoline. Refinery outages, maintenance and disrupted trade flows 
constrain the ability of the downstream system to respond. 
Product tightness should therefore help sustain crude prices even without another 
deterioration in geopolitical conditions. It could also amplify the impact of any renewed 
supply disruption. Another surge in diesel cracks accompanied by falling inventories would 
indicate geopolitical stress was transmitting through the refining system to consumers, 
potentially requiring an even greater price response to ration demand. 
Demand could rebound as constraints ease 
The apparent weakness of global oil demand also warrants caution. Oil consumption has 
performed unusually poorly relative to economic activity in 2026. Structural changes 
including electric vehicle adoption, vehicle-efficiency gains, biofuels and declining Chinese 
oil intensity explain part of the divergence. But they are unlikely to explain all of it. 
Our analysis suggests some of the apparent demand weakness reflects supply-constrained 
demand. Product shortages, high transport costs, trade restrictions and refining bottlenecks 
have restricted consumption despite otherwise resilient economic activity. 
This has an important consequence for the price outlook. Improving supply conditions would 
not necessarily translate one-for-one into inventory accumulation. Lower prices, falling 
transport costs and better product availability could unlock some of the demand previously 
suppressed by physical constraints. 
Better supply would therefore be likely to generate its own demand response, slowing the 
return to surplus. That reduces the downside to oil prices even under a relatively benign 
geopolitical scenario.

China reinforces the price floor 
Chinese demand reinforces this dynamic. The earlier collapse in Chinese crude purchases 
played an important role in helping the global market absorb the disruptions to Middle 
Eastern supply. That process is now reversing. 
By September, crude imports had recovered to just over 9mb/d, while October tracking 
showed weekly volumes above 9.5mb/d. Refinery throughput had reached a six-month high, 
and domestic crude inventories were being drawn down more aggressively. 
This provides an important floor beneath the price’s downside potential. A material fall in 
crude prices would increase the incentive for Chinese refiners to accelerate purchases and 
rebuild inventories. Incremental increases in Middle Eastern supply could therefore be 
absorbed before a persistent global surplus develops. 
This is another reason our previous USD95/bbl forecast looks more consistent with a 
meaningful de-escalation of hostilities than with the current physical market. 
Three paths beyond the US elections 
The midterms are a significant near-term event risk. We see three broad forward paths: 
1. De-escalation: downside towards USD95/bbl 
The clearest downside risk to our new forecast would be a credible diplomatic breakthrough 
accompanied by a material improvement in shipping conditions. Restoring confidence in 
transits through the Strait of Hormuz would allow some of the geopolitical premium to unwind 
quickly. Freight rates, physical differentials and backwardation would probably weaken 
alongside Brent. 
Under such a scenario, Brent could move back towards USD90–95/bbl. 
But de-escalation should not be confused with normalisation. Inventories would still need to 
be rebuilt. Tanker availability would take time to recover. Strategic stocks would remain 
depleted. And lower prices could trigger stronger purchasing by China and revive previously 
suppressed consumption. 
De-escalation can remove the geopolitical premium but would not immediately remove the 
scarcity premium. 
2. Status quo: USD105/bbl, base case 
Our revised USD105/bbl forecast is essentially a status-quo scenario. 
It does not require any escalation in the Middle East conflict. Rather, it assumes the 
constraints already confronting the market persist broadly through year-end: Hormuz 
continues to operate below normal capacity, tanker availability remains tight, freight stays 
elevated, product inventories remain depleted and incremental increases in supply struggles 
to rebuild global buffers.

That backdrop is sufficient, in our view, to keep Brent around USD105/bbl at year-end. 
The market can continue functioning under these conditions, but it would struggle to heal. 
Restricted flows and expensive logistics prevent inventories from rebuilding quickly, leaving 
the oil market in a persistent scarcity equilibrium.

3. Renewed escalation: the nonlinear outcome 
The third scenario presents the greatest risk to prices. A renewed deterioration in Hormuz 
traffic, damage to export infrastructure, disruption of alternative routes or attacks severe 
enough to discourage tanker traffic would confront a market already short of flexibility. 
Unlike earlier in the crisis, there are fewer readily available mechanisms for absorbing 
another major disruption. Tanker capacity is constrained, alternative routes are stretched, 
product inventories are tight and immediately deliverable OPEC spare capacity is limited 
under current logistical conditions. 
This creates the potential for a convex price response. The market would no longer simply 
be pricing the barrels directly removed by another escalation. It would also have to price the 
diminishing ability of the physical system to replace them. 
We would therefore view USD120/bbl and above as a scenario marker rather than a formal 
forecast should escalation materially impair regional exports. The scale and persistence of 
any move would ultimately depend on the size of the disruption to physical flows. 
The payoff has become asymmetric 
This leaves us with an increasingly asymmetric distribution of price risks around our new 
forecast. A credible de-escalation could trigger a sharp correction. Geopolitical premiums 
and positioning can adjust considerably faster than physical fundamentals. 
But the downside contains powerful stabilisers. Lower prices would encourage Chinese 
buying, facilitate commercial inventory rebuilding, support the recovery of supply-constrained 
demand and ultimately increase the attractiveness of replenishing strategic stocks. 
The upside has fewer natural constraints. Another material deterioration in Hormuz 
throughput would occur when tanker capacity, alternative routes, product inventories and 
effective spare supply are already stretched. 
Put simply, our previous USD95/bbl forecast now looks increasingly like a de-escalation 
outcome. USD105/bbl reflects continuation of today's constrained physical market. A 
material escalation in the conflict would create the risk of USD120/bbl-plus oil. 
That is the asymmetry.

Watch barrels, not rhetoric 
The market appears to be resisting reacting to every geopolitical headline around US 
elections. The experience of the past eight months shows that the oil-price implications 
depend more on what actually happens to physical flows. 
Seven indicators will be critical: 
• 
Hormuz throughput: the clearest measure of deliverable supply. 
• 
Persian Gulf exports: falling volumes would confirm an escalation is affecting physical 
balances. 
• 
Tanker rates and availability: a further squeeze would signal deteriorating logistical 
spare capacity. 
• 
War-risk insurance: an early indicator of shipowners' willingness to enter the Gulf. 
• 
Diesel cracks: another surge would signal intensifying downstream shortages. 
• 
Chinese crude imports: stronger purchases could quickly absorb recovering supply. 
• 
Inventory cover: further draws would show that the system remains unable to rebuild 
its buffers. 
The distinction is important. Brent rising on geopolitical headlines represents a risk premium. 
Brent rising alongside falling exports, higher freight rates, widening product cracks and 
stronger backwardation represents physical tightening. 
The latter would pose the greater threat to our revised forecast.

Risks remain skewed higher 
The US midterm elections should be viewed as a catalyst, not the basis for our forecast 
change. Our USD105/bbl year-end forecast assumes no further deterioration in geopolitical 
conditions. It instead reflects our expectation that the physical constraints already affecting 
the market will persist through year-end. 
The market has absorbed eight months of disruption by drawing down inventories, stretching 
alternative export routes, using scarce tanker capacity more intensively and suppressing 
consumption. Those adjustments have prevented a more severe shortage, but they have 
also reduced the system's ability to absorb another shock. 
The market has adapted. It has not recovered. This creates an increasingly asymmetric 
payoff. A meaningful de-escalation could push Brent back towards USD90–95/bbl as 
geopolitical premiums unwind. But lower prices would encourage inventory rebuilding, 
stronger Chinese buying and a recovery in previously suppressed demand, helping limit the 
downside.
