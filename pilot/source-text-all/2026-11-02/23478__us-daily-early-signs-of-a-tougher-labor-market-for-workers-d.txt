Early Signs of a Tougher Labor Market for Workers Displaced from AI-Exposed Jobs 
 
 
We recently noted that the labor market impact of AI appears to be broadening beyond 
the technology sector. Across nonmanufacturing industries, higher AI adoption is now 
associated with weaker employment growth and a higher unemployment rate. Amid 
accelerating AI adoption, how have workers displaced from AI-exposed jobs fared so 
far? In today’s Daily, we use newly released data from the Current Population Survey’s 
Displaced Worker Supplement (DWS) to assess how workers displaced from AI-exposed 
jobs navigated job loss and reemployment, and what their experience suggests about 
future AI-related displacement. 
Workers Displaced from AI-Exposed Jobs Face More Difficult Transitions Back Into 
Employment… 
The DWS is a biennial survey that collects employment histories from workers who 
experienced involuntary job loss over the prior three years, whether through layoffs, 
plant closings, or the elimination of their position. We analyze data on over 15,000 
displaced workers who lost their jobs over 2016-2025. This includes newly released data 
covering job losses in 2023-2025, a cohort that likely captures the first wave of 
AI-displaced workers. 
Because no single survey question identifies whether AI caused a given job loss, we 
construct a composite score capturing the likelihood that it did, drawing on the 
occupation and industry in which the job was lost and the stated reason for separation. 
The score rises with the occupation’s exposure to AI1 and the industry’s AI adoption rate 
and is higher when the job loss reflected the elimination of a position. We find that, since 
2025, workers most likely displaced by AI—those in the top 30% of our score—have 
faced more difficult transitions back into employment than other displaced workers. As 
Exhibit 1 shows, they took about a month longer to find a new job and were roughly 
10pp more likely to remain unemployed a year after displacement.

These short-run effects are comparable in magnitude to our earlier estimates of the 
impact of past waves of technological change on displaced workers. This suggests that 
lessons from those episodes can help gauge the labor market effects of further 
AI-driven displacement. Using four decades of individual-level data, we previously found 
that the effects of technological displacement are long-lasting. In the years following a 
job loss, real earnings for technology-displaced workers grew more slowly than those of 
other displaced workers, and their probability of experiencing another unemployment 
spell remained persistently higher. 
But the impact varied considerably across workers: younger, college-educated, and 
urban workers experienced cumulative real earnings losses only half as large as those of 
other technology-displaced workers. Workers with shorter tenure at the time of 
displacement also fared better, likely because their skills were less occupation-specific 
and therefore more transferable (Exhibit 2).

While it is still too early to assess the impact along all of these dimensions, our analysis 
suggests that younger workers have so far navigated AI-driven displacement better than 
older workers. Exhibit 3 shows that younger AI-displaced workers took about three 
weeks longer than other displaced workers to return to full-time employment, compared 
with about five weeks longer for older AI-displaced workers. They also experienced a 
smaller increase in the probability of cycling through multiple jobs during the transition 
or holding a second job alongside their new full-time position. 
Taken together, these findings suggest that younger workers may be able to adapt more 
flexibly to AI disruption. That is an encouraging sign given concerns that AI-driven job 
losses could disproportionately affect recent graduates. Another reason for optimism is 
that college students appear to be responding to these shifts by increasingly choosing 
majors that feed into occupations with lower AI exposure and stronger job growth. This 
suggests that they are already reorienting their skills toward areas of growing labor 
demand.

…And Are Moving Into Jobs at Risk of Further Displacement 
In past waves of technological change, occupational downgrading was a key driver of the 
worse labor market outcomes for displaced workers. We previously found that 
technology-displaced workers were more likely upon reemployment to move into 
occupations with higher routine content and lower complexity, likely because the same 
technological changes that eliminated their positions also eroded the value of their 
existing skills. 
We see early signs of a similar pattern today. AI-displaced workers are significantly more 
likely to leave their prior occupation and industry altogether, likely reflecting reduced 
demand for the jobs from which they were displaced. But upon reemployment, these 
workers—especially older ones—have so far increasingly moved into roles with similar or 
even higher AI exposure (Exhibit 4). This is probably because they tend to seek jobs that 
draw on their existing skills, which leaves them more vulnerable to further automation 
and displacement.
