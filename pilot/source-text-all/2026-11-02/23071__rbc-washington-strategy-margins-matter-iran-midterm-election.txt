Iran, Midterm Elections, and the Energy Trade-Offs of Escalation 
While Democrats are favored to reclaim the House, the size of their victory, and whether they 
secure the Senate, will determine how effectively they can constrain the administration's 
agenda. A narrow House majority would primarily unlock congressional oversight and leverage 
over federal spending, while a larger Democratic wave could embolden Republican defections 
and complicate the administration's priorities. From Russia sanctions waivers to Iran war 
funding, year-end defense legislation looks set to be a key battleground regardless of the margin. 
Ultimately, President Trump still holds the veto card, making wholesale policy reversals unlikely. 
However, mounting congressional opposition could push the administration toward greater 
reliance on unilateral executive action, intensifying legal challenges and policy uncertainty 
heading into 2027.

Iran: Escalate to Exit or End the Regime? 
The US midterms have been viewed as constraining President Trump's Iran war choices, given 
the risks of mass voter rejection in a $4+ per gallon retail gasoline environment. And yet, the 
war has proven impervious to pre-election off-ramps, with both Washington and Tehran 
sticking to their entrenched negotiating positions. In fact, the scope of the conflict has 
expanded with the Houthi entry into the conflict, undercutting the price impact of higher oil 
exports through the Strait of Hormuz. Moreover, the direction of travel still seemingly points 
toward near-term US military escalation, regional talks on Monday in Tehran that included 
Saudi Arabia, and reports that Vice President Vance has reached out to Supreme National 
Security Council’s hardline secretary Mohsen Rezaei.  
In the event the US opts to target Iran again, the key question in our view is whether the US 
action will constitute a final salvo before the US picks up and leaves, or if it will be a more 
extensive military operation to "finish the job" and help orchestrate regime change. We have 
heard concerns from some regional actors that a brief US escalation followed by a hasty exit 
would essentially amount to an Iranian victory, even if the country faces an extensive 
reconstruction process. While some market participants might see such an outcome as bearish 
for oil — believing it would lead to a full Hormuz reopening — the exact nature of Iranian 
operational controls will be critical, and any direct US military strikes on Iran still raise the risk 
that Gulf energy facilities could be targeted in retaliation. 
Certainly, the risks of a more devastating Iranian retaliatory response would increase if the US 
opts for a more sweeping military campaign. Faced with an existential survival threat, the IRGC 
leadership could push for strikes on critical civilian infrastructure, including desalination 
facilities, and encourage its remaining proxy network to go all-in on targeting US allies in the 
region. Some of the leading "finish the job" advocates have insisted that with the Democrats 
likely to take at least the House, President Trump should focus on making foreign policy his 
legacy issue, since Congress has less power to curtail his actions in this sphere. From our 
vantage point, even if Congress cannot force an end to the war, Democratic control over one 
or both chambers will likely result in a more rigorous effort to control the purse strings. 
Moreover, a substantial win for the Democrats next Tuesday could embolden some “America 
First” Republicans to break ranks and join this financial oversight effort as early as the lame-
duck session of Congress when the 2027 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) comes up 
for a vote.

Raise Your Right Hand 
A blue wave on Tuesday is unlikely to result in major changes to the President's energy 
dominance agenda, as Democrats are unlikely to secure a 60+ seat Senate majority, and the 
President remaining able to rely extensively on executive orders to govern. However, even if 
the party only captures the House of Representatives, we anticipate a wave of hearings and 
investigations on energy deals involving administration insiders, as well as military 
actions/foreign policy decisions involving key global producers. We expect Venezuela to be the 
subject of a number of inquiries, as both House and Senate Democrats are already seeking to 
ascertain if oil companies knew in advance of the US military operation in January to seize 
Nicolás Maduro. The scope of a post-election inquiry could expand to whether any companies 
were influenced by or benefited from privileged information about the operation, as well as 
the Pentagon's legal authority to take a 35% stake in North American Blue Energy Partners and 
the terms of that agreement.  
Democrat-led committees will also likely turn their attention to Russia, with the recent diesel 
deal and waiving of sanctions eliciting criticism not only from Democrats but also from leading 
GOP Russia critics (including Senators Susan Collins, Mitch McConnell, and Lisa Murkowski, 
and Representative Brian Fitzpatrick). Lawmakers have already asked several banks and port 
authorities to provide contracts, shipping and payment records, and communications related 
to the diesel deal. We also envision a renewed effort to challenge the administration's 
suspension of the sanctions measures mandated by the recently passed Graham Act—which 
authorizes 100% secondary tariffs on imports from key purchasers of Russia oil, as well as 
imposes further restrictions on Russian banks, energy investment, and sovereign debt. Earlier 
this month, 17 House Democrats and 5 House Republicans sent a letter to the White House 
calling for the President to apply the Graham Act "to the full extent and without delay." The 
scale of a Democrat victory could influence how likely Republicans are to break with the White 
House on Russia and curtail the President's sanction waiver authority. Like the case with the 
Iran war funding, a large Democratic wave could embolden Republicans to challenge the 
administration’s Russia sanctions waivers through year-end defense legislation.   
Beyond Venezuela and Russia, based on letters already sent by Democratic legislators, we also 
expect congressional reviews of the government's equity stakes in several critical mineral 
firms, as well as the CFTC investigation into large energy trades around Iran war news.

Domestic Energy: Power Politics 
The administration has prioritized accelerating federal approvals for data centers and 
associated generation, transmission, and natural gas infrastructure, an initiative that may 
endure even under Democratic congressional control. However, growing concerns over 
electricity affordability could shift the emphasis from accelerating development to 
determining who bears the associated costs. The House's overwhelming bipartisan passage of 
the Ratepayer Protection Act signals growing support for requiring data centers to shoulder 
their own costs, and a Democratic majority could pursue even stricter oversight. The House 
Judiciary ranking member Jamie Raskin (MD-8) has already opened an inquiry into major 
hyperscalers over nondisclosure agreements with local governments concerning data center 
projects, one avenue through which Democrats could further pursue investigations and 
subpoenas if the party wins the House. 
Beyond data centers, Democratic control of Congress could complicate the administration's 
broader efforts to expand conventional energy production and roll back environmental 
regulations. New congressional majorities could investigate federal leasing decisions, 
environmental deregulation, and DOE funding allocations, while using appropriations to 
constrain certain initiatives. Clean energy developers could benefit from heightened oversight 
of the administration's restrictions on offshore wind and solar approvals, alongside renewed 
pressure to restore canceled federal funding. However, we continue to note that presidential 
veto authority and existing executive powers would limit Democrats' ability to reverse the 
administration's policies or reinstate previously repealed clean energy incentives. 
Bipartisan permitting reform could offer the clearest avenue for legislative cooperation, yet 
we remain skeptical that this historical nonstarter could endure after the elections. The 
recently introduced American Affordability and Jobs Act seeks to accelerate energy 
infrastructure development across conventional and renewable technologies, although 
Democrats are increasingly demanding assurances against discriminatory treatment of clean 
energy projects. A sizable Democratic victory could strengthen their negotiating leverage, 
potentially complicating passage in the lame-duck session.
