 
AI Debt Contagion: Credit Correlations Hit Highest 
Since The Iran War As Goldman Names The New 
"Defensives" 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 30, 2026 - 04:25 PM 
There is a threshold. 
For months, the AI credit binge was something the rest of the bond market could watch from a 
safe distance: hyperscalers and neoclouds issued, datacenter project bonds piled up, and 
everyone else carried on. Then, starting in mid-October, the distance collapsed. In her latest 
Global Credit Trader, "Taking Stock of Performance and Where to Invest From Here"(available 
to pro subs), Goldman's chief credit strategist Amanda Lynam puts it bluntly: 
Price action since mid-October has shown that there is a threshold beyond which AI-related 
cost-of-capital concerns can threaten the broader growth narrative. Over these past few weeks, 
this overhang has affected the non-AI landscape, with spreads decompressing (i.e., lower rated 
cohorts underperforming) and correlations moving notably higher. 
In other words, the AI debt problem is now everybody's debt problem, which is precisely why we 
have been warning since last October that AI is now a debt bubble too. 
The note is also a useful marker of how fast the mood swings. Just last weekend, Lynam 
conceded that Goldman's credit bear case wasn't bearish enough, and that spreads would 
"moderately overshoot" the March peaks of 93bp in IG and 335bp in HY. Five days later, with IG 
at 86bp and HY at 312bp, the answer to whether the macro backdrop can support non-AI credit 
is "a qualified yes." 
Highest Correlation Since The Iran War 
Start with the plumbing. According to Goldman, the combination of AI funding concerns, 
elevated yields and escalating geopolitical tensions pushed USD credit spreads to break out 
wider from their local range, and in the process pairwise correlations of spread changes in the 
 

 
USD market hit their highest level since the 1Q2026 Iran escalation. Across both IG and HY, the 
AI and non-AI lines have converged at the top: 
 
Translation: when everything trades together, picking "good" credits stops working right when 
you need it most. We saw the spillover in real time last week, when the stress jumped from the 
neoclouds to the very top of the ratings stack: 
One silver lining: as Goldman's rates strategists noted, the past few weeks have also catalyzed 
a rally in duration, with Treasuries acting as a hedge amid the risk-off tone, something we 
discussed on Tuesday in "Growth Shock": When The AI Trade Sneezes, Bonds Now Rally. 
Buy The BBBs, Fear The BBs, Avoid The CCCs 
So where does Goldman want to be? In IG, Lynam is sticking with the call she made two weeks 
ago, when Goldman quietly downgraded the AA cohort in favor of BBBs (in "AI, Credit, and a 
Higher Cost of Capital", available here for pro subs). The recent decompression in non-AI IG, 
she argues, is a chance to position for the spread ratio to compress again: 
 

 
 
The logic: the AA end of IG is where the hyperscalers live, and that's where the supply is 
coming from. Case in point: this morning, Microsoft, which had not sold a dollar bond since 2017 
and whose CFO said on Wednesday night's call that the company hadn't "yet" tapped the debt 
market, kicked off a 10-part dollar bond offering looking to raise at least $25 billion. As we asked 
in "If The Cleanest Hyperscaler Needs $25 Billion In Bonds, Who Doesn't?", apparently "yet" 
lasted until breakfast. 
Junk is more complicated. Goldman says the HY market now trades in three parts: AI-related, 
regular-way performing HY, and distressed. On each: 
●​ AI-related HY: neutral, "particularly project finance structures, which are 
underwriting-dependent (due to structural nuances) but retain an attractive yield profile." 
Underwriting-dependent is doing a lot of work there, as anyone who read this morning's 
post on the banks stuck holding the $15 billion bridge loan for Anthropic's Texas data 
center (after a planned $5-7 billion junk bond takeout was shelved) can attest. 
●​ BBs: neutral, because Goldman expects them "to continue to absorb the brunt of 
AI-related financing (and potential duration headwinds if rates drift higher, once more)." 
In other words, the AI bill lands squarely on the top of junk. 
 

 
●​ Single-Bs: overweight, to own the carry, though Lynam warns that "dispersion within 
single-Bs" will rise as "the bar for avoiding fundamental deterioration moves higher." 
●​ CCCs: underweight, as higher rates, elevated input costs and idiosyncratic pressures 
create "macro and refinancing stresses which are unlikely to abate in the near term." 
That last one is an easy call, because the bottom of the market is already a disaster. Per the 
performance dashboard at the back of the note, USD CCC bonds are down 5.3% YTD, with 
spreads 422bp wider at 1,307bp and an all-in yield of 18%. CCC leveraged loans are worse: 
down 9% YTD, spreads 845bp wider at 2,409bp, yielding 30%. Meanwhile BBs and single-Bs 
are still in the green for the year. Which is what we flagged ten days ago: 
And the forecast dashboard says the fallen-angel pipeline is far from done: $38 billion of USD 
downgrades to junk so far this year versus a full-year forecast of $60 billion, roughly the $22 
billion still to come that Goldman penciled in two weeks ago. All of it lands in, you guessed it, 
the BB bucket. 
For crossover investors, Goldman's preferred way to pick up spread is to move down the capital 
structure rather than down in credit quality: corporate hybrids "offer compelling value" with the 
recent widening an "attractive entry point," while the subordinated debt of money center banks 
"also screens attractive": 
 
Put differently, Goldman would rather own a junior claim on a strong balance sheet than a senior 
claim on a weak one. 
The New "Defensives" 
The most useful idea in the note is a new definition of defensive. With AI and non-AI risk now 
the main fault line, Lynam argues correlation to AI spreads has become "an increasingly 
important consideration for asset allocators": 
 

 
In IG, Utilities, Financials and Healthcare/Pharma have shown lower correlation to AI-related 
spreads, while Energy also stands out in HY. We think adding these new 'defensive' sectors to 
portfolios is a prudent risk management tool. 
The chart shows Banks (purple) mostly at the bottom of the IG pack, and Energy near the 
bottom in HY, with non-AI TMT, unsurprisingly, glued to the AI trade: 
 
Two caveats. First, Goldman itself notes these longs "remain exposed to credit risk". Second, 
look at the far right of both panels: every sector's correlation to AI spreads has jumped again in 
the past two weeks, with the HY index at its highest since July. So the new defensives are 
defensive... until they aren't. 
JPMorgan's view, for what it's worth, is less alarmed. Relaying color from a FICC call on AI 
financing, JPM's Market Intelligence desk (available to pro subs) wrote on Tuesday that "Credit 
markets are becoming more discriminating on AI Infra, but there's nothing to suggest a broader 
funding crisis," while estimating that AI infrastructure has absorbed more than $500 billion of 
debt YTD. JPM credit analyst Erica Spear added that growing debt supply "may pressure credit 
spreads into 2027." Reassuring, sort of. 
Even The Pros Got Run Over 
Next, Goldman checked how the money managers actually did. Screening the 20 largest USD 
IG and HY mutual funds and ETFs: 
●​ IG: $345 billion of assets, only five of them actively managed. Returns are -0.5% YTD; 
the past month was +1.5%, "driven in large part by the rally in rates since October 5th." 
●​ HY: $202 billion of assets, eleven actively managed. Returns are +0.6% over one month, 
-0.9% over three months and just +0.2% YTD. HY "did not benefit as much from the 
duration exposure." 
 

 
 
Translation: strip out the Treasury rally and IG funds would have had a lousy month too. Then 
Goldman looked at the 20 largest active funds against their benchmarks, and the verdict is 
unambiguous: "the past month has been uniformly challenging across the IG and HY universe." 
Active IG funds lagged their benchmark by 0.7% in the past month, dragging their YTD excess 
return to -0.2%. Active HY managers lagged by 0.6% in the month, and are only ahead for the 
year (+0.9%) thanks to the carry from higher all-in yields: 
 

 
 
Which, maybe, explains a lot about the panic selling of the past few weeks: when your 
year-to-date alpha evaporates in a month, you don't wait around to find out whether the "growth 
narrative" survives. 
Europe: No AI Supply, Plenty Of Everything Else 
Across the Atlantic, the story flips. EUR credit has, so far, benefited from a lack of AI-related 
issuance, but Goldman says elevated energy prices and, more recently, sovereign and fiscal 
risks have more than offset it, pushing spreads wider. Hence Goldman's preference for USD 
over EUR credit. 
Banks, Lynam notes, are "the main conduit for sovereign risk in corporate credit", and EUR 
senior bank spreads have widened relative to non-financials, even excluding France: 
 

 
 
Goldman remains underweight French banks (no surprise, with France bracing for a Moody's 
downgrade), but thinks better-anchored fundamentals should keep senior bank spreads 
elsewhere from revisiting the 2011-12 wides. Spoiler alert: France won't do a damn thing unless 
a bond crisis forces it to. 
Keep The Hedges, Even Though They Cost More 
Do macro index hedges (CDX, iTraxx) still work in a bifurcated market? Goldman's answer: yes, 
because when the supply of capital to fund the buildout is put in jeopardy, "the impact on growth 
is felt more broadly and spreads across the credit market move together." That's the correlation 
chart again. 
The bad news is that protection isn't cheap anymore, with 3M implied vol near post-COVID 
medians in USD credit and at the 65th percentile in EUR. The good news: skew has flattened as 
spreads moved off their tights, improving the economics of outright payers, and a flatter term 
structure makes longer-dated hedges more attractive: 
 

 
 
Tactical tip: use local rallies to add protection. 
"Good Enough"... For How Long? 
To sum up, Goldman's baseline is that growth remains "good enough" for higher-quality credit, 
and its forecasts have IG tightening to 82bp and HY to 295bp by year-end. But Lynam is careful 
to hedge her own optimism: 
Acute pressures have eased somewhat, but the risks have not been resolved. Our macro 
baseline remains 'good enough' for higher-quality credit, particularly in the U.S. However, 
valuations are still tight by historical standards, and the left tail is thicker. As a result, we do not 
view broad-based 'dip buying' as prudent. 
We'd go a step further. A week ago the bear case wasn't bearish enough; today it's a qualified 
yes. Meanwhile, the "recent firmness" was already being tested as the note went out: after 
Alphabet shed half a trillion in market cap on Wednesday, credit spreads widened again today; 
Microsoft is out borrowing $25 billion-plus; banks are stuck holding Anthropic's bridge; and the 
next leg of fallen angels is all heading into the BB bucket that Goldman expects to absorb the AI 
bill. When the strategist telling you to own BBBs also tells you the left tail is getting fatter and to 
keep your hedges on, we'd listen to the second half of the sentence. 
Then again, CCC loans yielding 30% have never been a sign of a "good enough" economy... 
they're usually the sign that the threshold has already been crossed, at least for someone.
