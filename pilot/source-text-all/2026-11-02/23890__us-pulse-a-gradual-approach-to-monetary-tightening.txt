US Pulse: a gradual approach to monetary tightening 
What we’re watching 
• 
Following the decision to hold rates at its October meeting, we expect the Federal Open 
Market Committee (FOMC) to increase rates by 25bp at the December meeting and 
another 25bp increase in Q1 next year.  
• 
Advance Q3 GDP estimates showed that the economy rose 3.4% saar, with both private 
consumption and business investment driving growth. In our view, the strength in 
demand argues against the FOMC leaving policy rates on hold for much longer. 
• 
At the midterm election this week, the Republicans are widely expected to lose control of 
Congress. A Democratic clean sweep or a divided Congress would indicate policy 
gridlock may lie ahead, with protracted cross-party budget negotiations. 
• 
The October nonfarm payroll report is expected to affirm that the labour market remains 
stable. The preliminary University of Michigan consumer survey is expected to show that 
sentiment remains downbeat.  
• 
Financial markets are currently being driven by concerns over big tech valuations and 
plans for continued rapid investment in AI infrastructure and capacity. The Fed is closely 
watching how financial market developments may affect financial conditions.

ANZ US Pulse | 2 November 2026 
2 
Strong growth led by consumption and investment 
The US economy gathered pace in Q3, with real GDP expanding at a 3.4% annualised rate, 
up from 2.2% in Q2. The underlying momentum was even stronger, with real final sales to 
private domestic purchasers rising 4.9%, compared with 4.6% in Q2. Investment, 
consumption and government spending all contributed to growth, partly offset by weaker 
exports and surging imports. 
Consumption remains the primary growth engine, contributing 2.5ppt to headline GDP 
growth in Q3. Real consumption rose 3.8% annualised, matching the strong pace of Q2, with 
gains across goods and services and strengthening in healthcare, food services and 
accommodation. But this resilience masks growing divergence across households.  
Recent Federal Reserve Bank of New York research suggests that spending growth since 
2023 has been disproportionately driven by higher-income households, reflecting stronger 
wealth gains at the top of the distribution alongside greater inflation pressures on lower-
income households. The latest Consumer Expenditure Survey provides some further 
evidence of uneven spending dynamics. Average annual expenditure among households in 
the highest income quintile rose 6.1% y/y to USD159.5k in 2025, while growth across the 
second, middle and fourth quintiles was considerably softer at 2.5–3.1%. The exception was 
the lowest quintile, where expenditure rose 8.2%. A detailed breakdown of expenditure by 
income quintile is not yet available, although some of the increase at the lower end may 
reflect higher spending on essentials such as food, shelter and healthcare rather than 
stronger discretionary demand. More broadly, the data reinforce the broader point that the 
strength in aggregate consumption is not evenly distributed across households.  
In Q3, gross private domestic investment surged 19.4% annualised, contributing 3.3ppt to 
growth. The AI buildout is increasingly visible: equipment investment jumped 20.9%, marking 
a third consecutive quarter of double-digit growth, while structures investment recovered on 
stronger data-centre construction. Housing remains the clear counterpoint, with residential 
investment constrained by high mortgage rates and stretched affordability. Two very different 
investment cycles are increasingly evident within the same economy (Figure 1). 
Net trade subtracted 3.3ppt from headline growth, with surging goods imports accounting for 
much of the drag. Yet the composition is revealing capital goods imports as a share of total 
goods imports are at record highs, while consumer-goods imports are at record lows (Figure 
2). Part of the widening trade deficit therefore reflects the investment boom itself, as 
companies import computers, semiconductors and other equipment to support the AI and 
data-centre buildout. 
The bigger picture is one of strong growth, but with an increasingly distinctive composition. 
Consumption remains robust but uneven across households, while investment is booming 
on the back of the AI capex cycle. At the same time, the accompanying surge in capital-
goods imports is weighing on headline GDP. For the Fed, the unambiguous signal is that the 
economy remains robust in aggregate despite growing divergence beneath the surface. 
While the growth outlook remains firm, concentrated drivers leave the economy more 
exposed to any reversal in wealth-driven consumption or the AI investment cycle.

Data Pulse: the week ahead 
The October nonfarm payrolls report will provide an important signal on labour market 
conditions. The Bloomberg median expects nonfarm payroll growth of 65k, up from 29k in 
September. Labour demand and job openings remain soft. The unemployment rate is 
expected to remain unchanged at 4.2%. Nevertheless, jobless claims remain low, with the 
four-week average of initial claims at its lowest level since 1969. 
The ISM manufacturing index is expected to remain steady to 54.5 in October, while the 
services index is expected to ease slightly to 54.8 from 54.9. Regional surveys are pointing 
to resilient services activity. Persistent cost pressures across both the manufacturing and 
service sector indicate that inflation risks remain elevated.   
The preliminary University of Michigan consumer sentiment index is expected to remain at a 
five-month low of 48.0. Persistent inflation, subdued hiring and elevated mortgage rates 
continue to weigh on sentiment.

Data Pulse: the week that was  
The headline PCE price index rose 0.49% m/m (3.6% y/y) in September, while the core PCE 
price index increased 0.32% m/m (3.1% y/y). Core PCE rose 2.9% on a 3m annualised 
basis, up from 2.2% previously. Market-based PCE inflation rose to 0.34% m/m from 0.24%, 
pointing to a reversal in recent disinflationary momentum. 
Nominal consumer spending rose 0.5% m/m in September, but higher prices drove the rise, 
leaving real spending flat (0.0% m/m). The softness was concentrated in real durable goods 
spending, which fell 0.8% m/m, led by a 2.0% decline in motor vehicles. Personal income 
increased 0.2% m/m, but real disposable income fell 0.3% m/m. The savings rate declined to 
3.8%, its lowest level since October 2022, down from 4.0% in August, leaving households 
with a thinner savings cushion to support consumption. 
The Conference Board’s consumer confidence index improved to 91.7 in October from 84.2 
in September. The rise in the headline index was driven by improved views of current and 
expected business conditions. The share saying jobs were “plentiful” increased to 26.7% 
from 24.3%, while the share saying jobs were “hard to get” remained at 21.4%.
