After the AI-related risk repricing: Where to invest in credit  
Price action since mid-October has shown that there is a threshold beyond which 
AI-related cost-of-capital concerns can threaten the broader growth narrative. Over 
these past few weeks, this overhang has affected the non-AI landscape, with spreads 
decompressing (i.e., lower rated cohorts underperforming) and correlations moving 
notably higher. And as our rates strategy colleagues have noted, the past few weeks have 
also catalyzed a rally in duration as Treasuries served as a hedge amid the risk-off tone.  
While developments in recent days have helped re-anchor risk assets, credit price action 
has remained choppy and sentiment is still mixed to weak. In our view, the key question 
is whether the macro backdrop is firm enough to support performing credit outside of 
the AI universe. 
Our answer is a qualified yes. Our baseline macro view remains benign, but the so-called 
‘left tail’ of the growth distribution appears thicker than before. The recent spread 
widening has created more attractive entry points in trades that offer exposure to credit 
without taking outsized AI-related exposure. That said, on a historical basis, valuations 
are far from cheap. 
Wider spreads and higher correlations

correlations of spread changes reached their highest level since the 1Q2026 Iran

risk-off tone, and EUR credit correlations have similarly risen (Exhibit 2).

Macro hedges: Still worth holding in a bifurcated market 
The segmented weakness in AI-related credit relative to the broader performing market 
has led many investors to question which hedges are best suited for their portfolios. 
With so much focus on idiosyncratic risks tied to AI-related issuance and the funding 
arithmetic of the buildout, a key question is whether macro indices remain effective 
hedges. We think they do. The past few weeks have shown that, when AI risks become 
disruptive to aggregate demand, most acutely when the supply of capital needed to 
fund the buildout is put in jeopardy, the impact on growth is felt more broadly and 
spreads across the credit market move together (again, Exhibit 1). 
Hedging has become more expensive, with implied volatility now sitting close to 
post-COVID medians in USD credit and at the 65th percentile in EUR credit (Exhibit 8). 
That said, the pricing backdrop has improved in other respects. As spreads have moved 
off their tights, skew has flattened, which improves the economics of outright payers 
and, in turn, the payoff profile of hedges. A flatter term structure has also made 
relatively longer-dated hedges more attractive. 
In the near term, the recent firmness in spreads suggests that a better entry point to 
reset hedges may emerge soon, and investors may be able to use local rallies to add 
protection at more attractive levels. Taking a step back, however, macro hedges have 
shown their value even in this bifurcated credit market, and we would continue to hold 
them.
