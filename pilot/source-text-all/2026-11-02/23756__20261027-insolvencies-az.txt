27 October 2026
•	
2026 marks the fourth consecutive year of rising global business 
insolvencies, with a moderately slowing pace. Our Global Insolvency 
Index looks set to increase by +5% this year (broadly in line with our previous 
estimate), down from +6% in 2025 and +10% in 2024, leaving it 21% above 
its pre-pandemic average. North America and Asia-Pacific now drive 79% of 
the global increase. The US is the main engine, accelerating to +12% (from 
+7% last year), while Canada keeps declining (-3%). Asia-Pacific contributes 
28% of the headline rise, driven by Taiwan (+36%), South Korea (+12%) and 
Japan (+6%), though China is near-stabilizing at +1%. Western Europe adds 
15% to the global increase: +4% regionally, led by Germany (+8%) and France 
(+3%), with five countries - including France (about 71,000 cases), Switzerland 
(15,000) and Australia (11,850) - reaching their highest levels since 2000. 
Overall, 24 of 44 monitored countries see rising insolvencies, and 30 now 
exceed their pre-pandemic averages.
•	
Large companies keep on failing at a record rate. Through the first three 
quarters of 2026, 376 firms with turnover above EUR50mn have gone 
insolvent, more than one every 18 hours. That’s a record that surpasses the 
2024 peak by 38 cases. Retail (69 cases), services (62) and construction (51) 
bear the brunt, accounting for nearly half of the total. Western Europe alone 
registers 229 major cases. Agrifood, automotive, transportation and energy 
show particularly sharp relative increases across the continent. Looking at 
companies of all sizes together, there are some signs of softening in Europe: 
Only 43% of 216 monitored sectors posted year-on-year rises in Q2, down 
from over 60% on average through 2024. But construction, transport and B2C 
services remain the most broadly affected.
•	
The jobs cost is significant: roughly 2.7mn jobs are directly at risk 
worldwide in each year over 2026-2027, up 180,000 from 2025 and a 13-
year high in both Europe and North America. This includes 2.3mn jobs at 
risk due to companies being liquidated in and 0.4mn due to restructuring. 
Europe accounts for 1.6mn, with France (about 268,000), Germany (239,000) 
and the UK (213,000) first in line, followed by North America at 650,000 and 
Asia at 305,000. To put this in perspective, the jobs at risk represent 8% of the 
unemployed in the US and 6% in Europe, but the picture varies enormously 
by country, from just 1% in Spain versus 10% in both France and the UK. 
Construction, retail and services are again the most exposed sectors.

We do not expect a meaningful decline in business insolvencies anytime soon. Our headline 
index is forecast to edge up by +1% in 2027 (+1pp compared to our previous estimate) before 
dipping just -1% in 2028. 2028 will be mark the first year without a meaningful increase since 
2022 but it is hardly a recovery. The index would still sit 22% above pre-pandemic norms, driven 
by several structural forces. With enduring tensions in the Middle East, global GDP growth is 
expected to underperform compared to the 2023-2025 period, while energy costs remain high 
and governments offer limited fiscal support. Moreover, the stock of active firms expanded by 
roughly 10% between 2021 and 2025, broadening the pool of companies exposed to failure 
– fewer than half of young companies survive five years in Europe. Construction, accounting 
for 15-20% of national insolvency counts, also continues to drag on the outlook. Regional 
trajectories will continue to diverge: the US (+4%) and Asia-Pacific (+2%) will continue climbing, 
while Western Europe edges down (-1%), with Germany flat at a high level (about 25,900 cases), 
France still inching up (+1%, 71,400 cases) and the UK declining by -3% (24,800 cases).
•	
Higher-for-longer interest rates are choking the most vulnerable firms. Persistent interest 
rates keep straining (re)financing issues for already fragile companies, notably SMEs and those 
in already high-risk sectors such as in Latin America and Western Europe. In the US, fewer than 
one in two SMEs is profitable; in Europe, small firms are more likely than large ones to report 
falling earnings. Relief will be scarce: 60% of countries will maintain higher policy rates in 2027 
than in the prior 12 to 24 months, inflating funding costs and squeezing margins. Already, 16% 
of US sectors and 25% of European ones have an interest coverage ratio below 3 and a shock of 
400bps in the US or 300bps in Europe would push the rest under. In Western Europe, a +100bps 
rise in bank lending rates adds between EUR6bn (Spain) and EUR10bn (Italy and France) in 
interest expenses, compressing margins by -0.3pp to -0.9pp of gross value added (-0.3pp in 
Germany, -0.5pp in the UK, -0.6pp in Spain and -0.7pp in France).
•	
Higher-for-longer energy costs also maintain the pressure on margins. The 60% rise in energy 
costs since January 2026 has hit transportation hardest, with net margins falling by about -17pps 
in air transport (US and Europe) and by -15 pps (US) and -10pps (Europe) in road transportation. 
Energy-intensive European industries would suffer more than their US peers, notably chemicals 
(-4pps vs. -2pps) and metals & mining (-3pps vs. -2pps). Other sectors, including agriculture, 
retail, pharma and construction, would see more limited losses of -1 to -3pps. These figures are 
an upper bound since firms will try to compensate by reviewing pricing strategies (selling prices, 
suppliers terms), implementing cost-cutting and/or optimizing internal operations to improve 
efficiency.
•	
AI Darwinism is not yet a visible driver of insolvencies at the macro level, but there are some 
signals coming from smaller markets and early adopter sectors. An initial analysis across 
eight EU economies and nine sectors finds very low correlation between AI adoption rates and 
insolvencies at this early stage. Macro and sector-specific fundamentals still dominate. However, 
AI adoption can accelerate business insolvencies in smaller and less diversified markets due to 
the competitive wedge it creates between early adopters and laggards. Belgium and Sweden, 
which have the highest average AI adoption (~35%), show a clear positive link between AI 
intensity and worsening insolvency trends. This suggests that in smaller and less diversified 
economies, AI could act as a competitive accelerator, reinforcing some firms positions while 
intensifying pressure for non-adopters. Information & communication, the most AI-intensive 
sector with 70% adoption, could be the canary in the coal mine: it shows worse insolvency 
performance than the national average in five of eight countries surveyed.

A decelerating but persistent global rise in 2026. 
As expected¹, business insolvencies are set to rise for 
the fourth² consecutive year in 2026, with our Global 
Insolvency index³ increasing by +5% y/y, after +6% in 
2025 and +10% in 2024. This outcome represents a small 
revision on the upside compared to our previous estimate 
(+0.3pp). Preliminary data available as of mid-October 
confirm the trajectory, showing a gradual slowdown of 
our headline indicator from +10% y/y in Q4 2025 to less 
than +6% y/y/ in Q3 and +6% year-to-date (excluding GCC 
countries and Israel due to the lack of official statistics on 
insolvency proceedings). At this stage, upside surprises in 
North America and parts of Asia-Pacific offset lower-than-
expected figures in Latin America, Central and Eastern 
Europe and Africa. Overall, this continued but decelerating 
upside trend would bring our headline index slightly below 
its Great Financial Crisis (GFC) level and 21% above its pre-
pandemic average (2016-2019). 
North America and Asia-Pacific make up 79% of the 
global rise. Three dynamics are at play: a noticeable 
acceleration in North America, a persistent rise in Asia-
Pacific and Western Europe and a deceleration in Latin 
America and Central & Eastern Europe. North America 
accounts for 51% of the global increase in 2026. This 
prominence stems partly from methodological reasons 
due to the region’s significant share in global GDP (28%) 
and consequently in our headline indicator (33%). The 
US rebound is set to accelerate to +12% (from +7% in 
2025) while Canada is set to decline further (-3%). Asia-
Pacific follows with 28% of the increase (+2.6% regionally). 
Australia (+1%), Japan (+6%), South Korea (+12%) and 
Taiwan (+36%) more than offset a near-stabilization in 
China (+1%) and a sharp drop in Singapore (-23%). Western 
Europe still contributes 15% (+4%), boosted by Germany 
(+8%) and France (+3%), while Latin America (+2%), Central 
and Eastern Europe (+2%) and Africa (-4%) are slowing 
sharply from their 2025 pace (+18%, +10% and +6%, 
respectively).
Overall, insolvencies would rise in 24 of the 44 countries 
we monitor, which account for 83% of our sample's 
GDP, but by a lower average of +11% (simple average), 
down from +18% in 2025. The largest relative increases 
are expected in Taiwan (+36%), Switzerland (+32%), 
Türkiye (+31%), Greece (+26%), Chile (+15%) and Russia 
(+14%). In absolute terms, the largest increases are in 
Switzerland (+3,660 cases), the US (+3,059), France (+2,312) 
and Germany (+1,836). The 20 countries with falling 
insolvencies post an average decrease of -9%. Double-digit 
drops occur in Slovakia, Singapore, Colombia, Hungary, 
Denmark, Norway and Lithuania, and Hungary (-3,088) 
and the UK (-1,145) see the largest absolute declines.

In that context, sector‑level insolvency dynamics tend 
to display more divergence from national trends. The 
country-wide situation used to set the trend for most 
sectors, in particular during the post-Covid catching-
up phase, but this is less the case since 2025, with more 
sectors showing differences in intensity and timing. In the 
Americas, Canada saw 11 out of 20 sectors reporting a 
noticeable decrease in insolvencies in the first part of the 
year, often larger than -10%  y/y, but with manufacturing, 
transport/storage, B2B services and hospitality as 
exceptions on the upside. Simultaneously in the US⁴, the 
UBS Corporate Bankruptcy Monitor saw less than half 
of their sectors recording a rise in insolvencies in the 
first three quarter of 2026, with the largest increases in 
healthcare and chemicals – and still numerous cases in 
particular in real estate, construction, retail and hospitality. 
In Asia, Japan sees eight out of 10 sectors contributing to 
the national rise, but with manufacturing (-36 cases year-
to-date to 830 as of Q3), real estate (-8 to 243) and finance 
(stable) as exceptions. 
At the regional level, all sectors in the 28 countries⁵ we 
monitor in Europe still recorded a rise in the first half of 
2026 compared to the first half 2025, but with more signs 
of softening and divergences. The slowing momentum 
gained traction across European sectors, with 43% (i.e. 93 
out of the 216 sectors monitored) posting a year-on-year 
rise in Q2, compared to 48% and 53% in Q1 and Q4 2025, 
respectively – and more than 60% on average in 2024. The 
H1 2026 picture also hides slightly wider disparities. Half of 
the countries report insolvencies either on the upside in all 
sectors (Belgium, Finland, France, Italy and Romania), or 
in almost all of them (Germany, Greece, Sweden) or mostly 
on the decrease (the Netherlands, Hungary, Spain, the 
UK). The other half of countries report the opposite trend 
in insolvencies across sectors, notably the Netherlands, 
Austria and Norway. Overall, construction, transport/
storage and B2C services stood out in H1 2026, with a 
prolonged strong and broad-based rise, while information/
communication, hospitality and industry recorded much 
slower increases at regional level, with more countries 
posting less insolvencies compared to H1 2025. In that 
context, Western European countries still record the largest 
numbers of sectors above pre-pandemic levels of business 
insolvencies, notably Germany, France, the UK, Spain, 
Sweden and Austria, with Italy, Portugal, the Netherlands 
and Denmark as the main exceptions on the opposite side 
of the spectrum.

Importantly, large firms are not immune, with another 
high number of major insolvencies in Q3 2026 (129), 
contributing to a year-to-date record (376 cases, i.e. +38 
compared to the previous high recorded in 2024). Our 
internal reporting on insolvencies of major companies – 
those with a turnover exceeding EUR50mn – translates 
to more than one case per day (one case every 18 hours), 
underscoring the risk of a domino effect on their extensive 
networks of suppliers and subcontractors, potentially 
boosting insolvencies among smaller firms. In absolute 
terms, retail (69 cases year-to-date, +21) and services 
(62,+9) remain the most affected sectors, particularly 
in Western Europe and North America, along with 
construction (51,+5), especially in Western Europe and 
Asia. This is not a surprise since these sectors have more 
large-sized companies. In Europe, for example, these three 
sectors account for 58% of companies with 50 or more 
employees, while accounting for 47% of the number of 
major insolvencies in the region in 2026. However, these 
business demographic factors mask other fragilities. In 
relative terms, several sectors are seeing large(r) increases 
or high(er) levels in comparison to historical standards or 
the existing stock of large firms. In Europe, this list includes 
agrifood, automotive, energy and, to a lesser extent, 
transportation and paper. Overall, Western Europe is 
leading both the rebound (+21 cases, followed by Central 
and Eastern Europe with +19 cases) and the global count 
in the number of cases (229 cases, followed by Asia with 
51 cases) while the Americas and China still record the 
biggest cases, together accounting for 17 of the top 20 
insolvencies for the first three quarters of the year.

At this stage, AI adoption and business insolvencies 
are uncorrelated at the aggregate level. The pooled 
correlation between AI adoption rates and insolvency 
gaps across 66 sector-country observations spanning 
eight European economies  and nine sectors  is essentially 
zero. Macroeconomic conditions and structural sector-
specific drivers – not technology – remain the dominant 
determinants of business insolvencies.
However, the most AI intensive information and 
communication sector is showing signs of higher 
insolvencies, implying that non-adopters of AI are 
starting to pay the price.Trade (24% average adoption) 
underperforms the national insolvency trend in six of seven 
countries (mean gap: −2.8pps), with Germany as exception, 
while professional and scientific activities (48% adoption) 
underperform in five of seven (mean gap: −0.6pp) 
and construction (15%) in five out of eight (mean gap: 
-1.9pps). At the opposite end of the spectrum, information 
and communication, the most AI-intensive sector (70% 
adoption), actually outperforms in five of eight countries.
The Belgium-Sweden exception suggests that high AI 
adoption can accelerate business insolvencies in smaller 
markets. The two countries with the highest average 
AI adoption rates in the panel (Sweden and Belgium, 
35%) both display strong positive correlations (r ≈ +0.55) 
between AI adoption and relative insolvency deterioration, 
meaning their most digitized sectors are also their worst 
performers on insolvencies. This suggests AI functions 
as a Darwinian selection accelerator (i.e. reinforcing 
established firms in large and mature ecosystems such as 
Germany and the Netherlands but intensifying competition 
and eliminating fragile players in smaller markets such as 
Belgium and Sweden). 
A collapse in the current AI-driven economic boom could

North America is to post the sharpest acceleration of any 
region in 2026, with the regional index now expected 
to rise +11% y/y – above the +4% in 2025 and our prior 
forecast of +7%. The revision is almost entirely driven by 
the US, which accounts for 93% of the regional total, where 
the official figures from US Courts for the first half of 2026 
revealed a clear upside (+19% y/y). The macroeconomic 
and financial outlook should confirm this trajectory, 
driven by the restrictive-for-longer monetary stance, the 
multi-speed GDP dynamics (booming ICT versus weak 
residential investment and softening consumption), the 
elevated input costs and limited pricing power outside 
tech, particularly in consumer-facing B2C sectors and 
for SMEs that have used up the buffers accumulated 
during the strong post-Covid recovery. US insolvencies 
are projected at 27,700 cases in 2026 (+12%, up from +9%

though still well below the 2000–2019 average of 34,650.

Appendix 2 – Underlying factors driving business insolvencies: 
Heatmap methodology
• The heatmap is designed as an analytical tool for identifying patterns, clusters and outliers rather than as a 
predictive model.
•  44 countries scored on 16 factors across 3 categories. Each factor is rated from 1 (very favorable, downward 
pressure on insolvencies) to 5 (very unfavorable, upward pressure), with 3 = neutral. 
•  No composite score is computed as individual factor weightings are country-specific.
•  Each factor is assessed using a consistent scoring grid, with predefined quantitative thresholds or qualitative 
criteria determining the assignment of scores from 1 to 5, and ensuring that the same score carries the same 
meaning and intensity of risk signal regardless of the country which it is applied. This uniform calibration 
guarantees cross-country comparability, with thresholds set to distribute countries across the full 1-to-5 range for 
each factor while preserving meaningful differentiation between downward, neutral and upward conditions.
-GDP momentum (YoY): Whether real GDP growth in 2027 is accelerating or decelerating 
relative to 2026
-GDP momentum (vs. 2023–25): Whether 2027 real GDP growth exceeds or falls short of the 
average pace over the preceding years
-Rate momentum (YoY): Whether the monetary policy stance is easing, stable or tightening 
relative to 2026
-Rate level (vs. 2025): Whether the cumulative level of interest rates remains elevated 
compared to the 2025 benchmark
-High-risk sectors: The breadth of sectors carrying elevated risk ratings within each country's 
economy, based on Allianz Trade ratings
-State support capacity: The fiscal room and policy willingness of governments to backstop 
the corporate sector
-Business dynamics: The intensity of firm creation and churn, with a higher score indicating a 
larger cohort of young, fragile firms
-Stock of firms vs. pre-Covid: How much the active business population has grown beyond 
its 2019 level, capturing the post-pandemic firm creation overshoot
-Construction risk: The stress level in the construction sector, one of the most insolvency-
intensive industries globally, based on Allianz Trade ratings
-B2C sectors risk: The average risk across consumer-facing sectors such as retail, hospitality 
and personal services, based on Allianz Trade ratings
-CV insolvency levels: Whether current insolvency levels are near their historical norm or at 
an unusually extreme point
-Insolvency growth rates: The historical volatility of year-on-year insolvency changes, 
reflecting the predictability of the insolvency trajectory
-Procedure usage rate: The degree to which formal insolvency procedures are used relative 
to total business distress, with low scores indicating that statistics understate true failures
-Avoided insolvencies (Covid): The estimated stock of insolvencies deferred during 2020–22 
by pandemic-era support measures and still feeding into the pipeline
-Catch-up by end Y-1: How far the normalization of deferred insolvencies has progressed by 
end-2026, with 1 meaning the backlog is fully absorbed
-Regulatory reform: Whether legislative changes to insolvency frameworks are expected to 
materially raise or lower recorded insolvency counts
