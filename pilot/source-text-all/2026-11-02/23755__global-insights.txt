With Treasury Yields at Multi-Decade Highs, What’s Happening Overseas?
Rising US Treasury yields have dominated the market’s attention this year, but the bond sell-off 
has been a global phenomenon. Some factors are universal: higher energy and food prices have 
been key catalysts behind the repricing of the global rate outlook. These pressures are evident 
nearly everywhere, even in commodity-exporting countries such as the US. As inflation has 
intensified, sovereign yields across most major markets have climbed to levels not seen in years, 
or even decades. But inflation is only part of the story. Monetary policy, fiscal dynamics, and other 
country-specific factors are creating greater dispersion across global bond markets. For investors, 
these differences are becoming increasingly important. Looking ahead, we expect a gradual 
easing of geopolitical tensions and falling commodity prices to cool inflation and provide much-
needed relief for bonds. Still, the path for yields is likely to remain uneven. Today, we examine the 
key forces driving bonds across major markets and offer our outlook for yields.

Treasury Yields Are Pricing In Excessive Fed Hawkishness  | As 10-year Treasury yields peaked near 5.3%, they reached levels not seen 
since 2002. Behind the move was a sharp repricing of Fed expectations that we believe has gone too far. Markets are pricing in more than 
three rate hikes over the next 12 months, while we expect only one. That disconnect should allow 10-year yields to moderate toward 
4.50%-4.75% over the course of 2027. Longer term, however, the federal government’s sea of red ink remains a structural challenge. The 
FY26 budget deficit reached a post-COVID high of nearly $2 trillion, and the Congressional Budget Office projects deficits to rise in both 
absolute and relative terms over the next decade. So far, neither party is showing much willingness to address the problem.
Fiscal Dynamics Explain Divergence In French And German Yields | Budgetary issues played a more central role as France’s 10-year yield 
topped 5% for the first time since 2002, while the spread versus German bonds widened to levels last seen during the 2011/12 European 
debt crisis. The catalyst was a warning from France’s independent fiscal watchdog. Even if GDP growth improves from 0.5% this year to 
1.0% in 2027, the projected 2027 budget deficit would still reach 5% of GDP, well above the EU-wide 3% limit. We see little political appetite 
to address the issue ahead of next April’s presidential election, leaving French bonds vulnerable to further pressure. In contrast, Germany’s 
deficit stayed below 3% until this year and is still only modestly above that level. Simply put, investors view Germany, rightly in our opinion, 
as a safer credit than France. Germany faces its own challenges, including sluggish growth, but its fiscal position remains on firmer footing.
Despite A Hefty Increase, Japanese Yields Remain Low By International Standards | After starting 2026 just above 2%, Japan’s 10-year 
yield topped 3% for the first time since 1996. Even after this hefty increase, it remains the lowest yield among G7 countries and one of the 
lowest across the G20. Japan’s bond market reflects a tug-of-war between monetary and fiscal policy. The Bank of Japan has raised rates 
twice YTD and six times over the past three years. At the same time, the government is pushing for more fiscal stimulus, likely adding to an 
already elevated public debt burden (already the world’s highest ratio of public debt to GDP). As the BOJ continues to normalize rates, the 
path of least resistance may be for yields to continue to drift higher.
China Is The Exception That Proves The Rule | Brazil (which we discussed last month) and China are the only G20 countries whose yields 
are down YTD. For Chinese bondholders, that has been a positive, but it also underscores the economy’s underlying sluggishness. 
Consumer inflation has remained at 1.3% or less throughout 2026, as higher commodity import costs have been offset by domestic 
manufacturing overcapacity. That helps explain why China’s central bank is among the few to have kept rates steady this year. At the same 
time, the prolonged property downturn is depressing loan demand, giving banks greater capacity to invest in government bonds. Over the 
longer run, however, fiscal pressures could push yields in the other direction. China’s debt/GDP ratio is already among the highest in 
emerging markets and continues to rise.
Bottom Line | The rise in Treasury yields this year has been part of a broader global trend. Across nearly all major economies—Brazil and China 
are the notable exceptions—bond yields have climbed to multi-year or even multi-decade highs. As oil prices return to prewar levels and 
inflation subsides, we expect yields to move lower—although the extent will vary across markets. For investors, however, today’s higher yields 
offer a silver lining: the opportunity to lock in generationally attractive yields. Bonds can provide ballast against equity risk, generate income, 
and help mitigate the impact of further increases in rates. For US-based investors, investing in international bonds can introduce currency risk, 
which can meaningfully impact both returns and volatility as foreign exchange rates fluctuate.
