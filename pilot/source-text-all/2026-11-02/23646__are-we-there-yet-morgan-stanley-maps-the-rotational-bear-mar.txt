 
'Are We There Yet?' Morgan Stanley Maps The 
Rotational Bear Market Breaking Europe Under The 
Surface 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 29, 2026 - 02:45 PM 
Look at the STOXX 600 and nothing happened: the index is barely 3% off its all-time high. 
Look under the hood, and it's a crime scene. In a new note titled "Are We There Yet?" (available 
to pro subs), Morgan Stanley's European equity strategist Marina Zavolock and team write that 
the rotations beneath the surface have reached bear-market proportions, and they have done 
so across six trades at once: high vs low momentum, AI capex plays vs the "debated disrupted", 
Strait of Hormuz closure vs reopening, cyclicals vs defensives, bond proxies and the highly 
levered, and French sovereign risk. 
Whilst European equities are only c. 3% off SXXP index highs, bear market-level rotations under 
the index surface have greatly challenged consensus positioning. 
In other words, the index is fine, the people who own Europe are not. Which, as regular readers 
know, is a mirror image of what is happening in the US, where Goldman's Bobby Molavi called 
the past few weeks "unplayable" on Monday, and where Morgan Stanley's own Nick Savone 
tried to convince clients on Sunday that the glass is half full: 
Zavolock's European team is, if anything, even more glass-half-full: the bank "broadly see[s] an 
attractive risk-reward for a rotational rebound" and, helpfully, publishes the list of stocks to buy 
(and the ones to dump) if it comes. We get to that at the end. First, the carnage. 
"All One Trade" 
Start with momentum. European 12x1M price momentum, top quintile vs bottom, has suffered a 
14% drawdown from its June highs, 12% of it in October alone. And the two legs moved like 
mirror images. 
 

 
In a sign that it is all one trade, top & bottom momentum unwinds look like parallel images of 
each other. Top momentum stocks fell -7% relative from June highs to recent lows, bottom 
momentum +9% over the same period. 
 
Put differently, whatever investors were selling, they were buying the exact opposite, which is 
what a positioning purge looks like when it goes through a single trade. Regular readers will 
recall that Savone's colleagues first laid out how to survive "The Great Momentum Unwind" 
(Aug 3). Three months later, the unwind is still going. 
How does it compare to history? MS tried to find precedents where top momentum fell, bottom 
momentum rallied and the index went nowhere, and the honest answer is: not well. The current 
episode, now 4.1 months old, has seen top vs bottom fall 16%, a bit worse than 2011 and 2021 
(-13%), a fraction of the dot-com unwinds (-27% to -35%), and the only one besides 2006 where 
cyclicals got crushed (-12%) alongside momentum: 
 

 
 
And for those hoping history tells you what comes next, MS notes that comparable unwinds 
have preceded both index breakdowns and melt-ups. Diversification at its finest. 
One consolation: MS's preferred measure of "fundamental momentum" (earnings and 
price-target revisions plus idiosyncratic momentum) has pulled back 13% from its summer 
highs, but unwinds there "tend to be tactical/short-lived", so recoveries are "more consistent" 
than in plain price momentum. 
Half The AI Trade, Gone 
At the core of the unwind is, what else, AI. Using its global AI mapping framework, MS splits 
Europe into core AI enablers (capex beneficiaries: semis, part of cap goods and tech hardware) 
and the "AI challenged" (the disruption-debated software, business services, media and 
medtech names). The ratio between the two has been a one-way street since late 2022... until 
June. 
At the core of recent momentum unwinds, European AI enablers erased half of their peak 
relative gains since the AI thematic took off & nearly 2/3rds of peak relative gains YTD (to last 
Thursday's lows). The ratio of core European AI enablers vs AI challenged has fallen c. 40% 
since June. 
 

 
The details are brutal: the enablers-vs-challenged ratio is down 38% from its June peak (-42% 
at the lows), including a 23% drop in October alone (-29% at the lows). At the trough, the ratio 
had erased 52% of everything it gained since the AI theme took off in November 2022: 
 
Meanwhile the AI "losers" staged the comeback of the year, recovering nearly 90% of their YTD 
relative losses at the peak, which is exactly what you get when the market stops asking who 
wins from AI and starts asking who pays for it. That, of course, is the question we have been 
asking since last October, when AI quietly became the largest debt sector in the market. 
Growth Scare: Complete? 
The second leg of the purge is the classic rotation into defensives. European cyclicals (including 
banks, excluding defence) are down 13% relative to defensives, which MS says is comparable 
to, and slightly worse than, every recent growth scare: -10% during the "AI disruption dominoes" 
and the start of the Iran war earlier this year, -12% around Liberation Day and -11% in the 
"Sahm Rule" summer of 2024. 
 

 
 
Strip out the AI-challenged names (which, as MS notes, have "behaved as anti-momentum 
defensives of late") and the hit to cyclicals deepens to 15%: 
 

 
 
So what got squeezed? Medtech, Life Sciences, Pharma and Tobacco are up 14% from their 
recent lows, and Food & Beverage, Food Retail and HPC are up 8%. Look at the light-green 
Life Sciences line going parabolic in the chart below: nothing says "defensive" like a 
near-vertical line. 
 

 
 
MS also flags a squeeze in bond proxies as yields came off their highs, which "appears to be at 
the top of a tactical range", and a small rally in Europe's most levered stocks that, based on the 
bank's deep dive into past yield spikes, is "likely to be short-lived, even if bond yields continue to 
decline somewhat." 
Hormuz: Back To April Lows 
The third driver is the Iran war. MS refreshed its Strait of Hormuz sensitivity screen (oil 
correlation, inverted bond yield correlation and stock behavior around key escalation and 
de-escalation dates), and found that the ratio of stocks hurt by a Hormuz closure vs those 
helped has fallen back to its April lows, when investor sentiment on the conflict was last at its 
lowest: 
 

 
 
MS links this directly to the bond rout: oil and the war, together with US 10Y yields breaking 5%, 
"drove a growth scare in the market exacerbating moves in everything from OAT spreads to the 
AI complex." Yields are now back below 5%, which the bank qualifies with a well-placed "(for 
now)". 
If a deal to reopen the Strait comes, MS's sensitivity work says Airlines, Aerospace, 
Discretionary Retail and Luxury win the most, with the DAX and mid-caps the most exposed 
indices:

reported that the US has told several Gulf states to be prepared for a short, intense military 
campaign after the midterms, while Reuters said Qatar presented Iran with a new draft deal... 
which a senior Iranian official promptly denied. And this morning European natgas did this: 
(More on the €100 "psychological mark" here.) That is not what a reopening trade looks like. 
Liberté, Égalité, Oversold 
Then there's France. French stocks with the highest OAT-spread sensitivity and domestic 
exposure are back near the relative lows of the June 2024 snap election, and on a 52-week 
z-score basis they recently hit -3.9 standard deviations, the deepest reading on MS's chart 
going back to 2021 (-3.2 currently): 
 

 
 
We have been all over the French bond rout this month, from European banks tumbling as it 
"reactivated the doom loop" to the OAT-Bund spread hitting EU crisis-level highs ahead of 
Moody's review last Friday. Which ended like this: 
MS argues that European corporates, banks included, have limited direct exposure to OAT 
spread widening, and that the French rotation was mostly about global financial conditions via 
rising bond yields. With yields off the highs, the pressure "eases some" just as French equities 
come off "highly oversold levels." 
And banks, which are by far the sector most inversely correlated to OAT spreads, just had a 
30-day drop that MS says looks strikingly like the post-Russia/Ukraine pullback of 2022. 
Historically, a 13-15% 30-day decline in the SX7E has been a trough signal: 
 

 
 
That's an 89% hit rate one month out (+7.4% on average) and 84% six months out (+19.8%). 
Then again, the 2022 analogue also includes a Credit Suisse moment seven months in, but let's 
not spoil the party. 
The real risk, per MS, is not the spread but the tax bill. On the table: LFI's proposal to massively 
raise the corporate tax surcharge (which MS estimates would hit ~8.8% of CAC 2027 earnings 
and 135bp of MSCI Europe's), a revenue-profit "mismatch" tax, a 33% buyback tax and RN's tax 
on "excess" dividends that could cut the CAC's 2027 dividend yield from 3.3% to 2.3%. Clarity is 
"unlikely until mid-next year at the earliest." 
 
Spoiler alert: France won't do a damn thing unless a bond crisis forces it to.

So what if it all reverses? MS combined price momentum, fundamental momentum, AI enabler 
vs challenged exposure, Hormuz sensitivity and cyclical vs defensive into one score. Semis, 
Tech Hardware, Cap Goods, Metals & Mining and Banks would lead a rebound; Defensives, 
from Medtech and Tobacco to Food & Bev, are most at risk: 
 

 
 
By country, the Netherlands, Austria and Ireland would lead (mostly a function of their sector 
mix), with Denmark, Belgium and Norway bringing up the rear: 
 

 
 
And the stock list. The top of MS's buy-the-reversal screen reads like a who's who of European 
AI hardware: Nokia, VAT Group, Nebius, Infineon, STMicro and ASM International, followed by 
Boliden, Siemens Energy, Atlas Copco and ACS. Banks fill much of the back half (BPER, ABN 
Amro, Raiffeisen, Santander, ING, UniCredit): 
 

 
 
At the other end, the stocks MS sees most at risk if the rotation reverses are the very defensives 
that just squeezed: Lindt, EssilorLuxottica, Philips, Smith & Nephew and Centrica, plus a long 
list of telcos, tobacco and pharma, including Novo Nordisk and AstraZeneca. 
Are We There Yet? Probably Not 
MS's case for a rebound is simple: cyclicals vs defensives has completed "a full growth scare 
cycle", Hormuz-sensitive stocks are back at the April lows, bond proxies are stretched, French 
stocks are nearly 4 sigmas oversold and banks are flashing a historical trough signal. On any 
one of these, it's a reasonable contrarian call. 
The problem is that all of them hang on the same handful of things that haven't been fixed: i) 
who funds the AI capex wall, ii) whether oil and gas keep the bond market on edge and iii) 
whether Paris can pass a budget that doesn't torch corporate earnings. On the evidence of just 
the past 24 hours (a US military campaign pencilled in after the midterms, European gas at 
€100 and France still without a budget), the market has priced the growth scare, but probably 
not the next one. 
 

 
So yes, the unwind could unwind. More likely, the "rotational bear market" is the part of the 
market that has been doing the pricing correctly all along, and the index 3% from its highs is the 
one that's late. Then again, the index has never been the one that pays for being wrong.
