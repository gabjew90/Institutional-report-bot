This week in Europe: Could the ECB put QT on hold?
This week was relatively calm in terms of data. German exports (August numbers) 
disappointed relative to expectations and could be interpreted as a first hint that 
the robust performance of the German economy in 2Q was also driven by 
temporary factors, not repeated in 3Q. But these are very volatile time series and we 
would caution against this conclusion at this stage, not least because survey 
indicators are signalling optimism in German manufacturing (see here). 
If data has not been the main focus this week it was certainly (again) euro area 
sovereign debt markets and here France in particular. After all, OAT Bund spreads 
had widened to almost 160bp last Friday. And surely, in all our investor 
conversations this week the question came up how the ECB is likely to see that 
situation. On that front, we got some interesting data points from the central bank 
this week. First, Philip Lane’s speech from Monday this week (here) and the ECB 
accounts of the September meeting (here) suggest that the ECB it starting to see 
the recent increases in euro area sovereign yields as a significant tightening of the 
monetary policy stance (see here our cross-assets discussion on the implication of 
higher yields for the economy). Second, the French governor Moulin (here) and the 
Finnish Council member Rehn stressed that the widening seen in OATs is due to 
fundamentals and needs addressing in Paris rather than Frankfurt. Finally, President 
Lagarde was reported as having reassured euro area Finance ministers yesterday 
that the ECB has tools to address disorderly market developments, subject to clear 
conditions (see here). We would note that this does not fully qualify as a verbal 
intervention, as she did not explicitly go public on the topic. But it does confirm our 
sense that the ECB is closely monitoring the situation (see here and here). So, how 
should we think about the ECB’s reaction function from here? 
Let’s start with the observed tightening through the euro area sovereign yield curve. 
Philip Lane attributed some of the increase in long rates to the increasing 
competition for capital due to the investment surge in AI. These investments 
predominantly take place in the US (and it is mostly Asia-based manufacturers that 
are benefitting). So, there is more growth in the US and Asia, less so in Europe. 
Hence the increase in European rates represents more of a tightening in Europe than 
it does in the US or Asia. On some simple metric, the increase in the synthetic euro 
area yield curve seen since the start of the Middle East conflict is broadly equivalent 
to six ECB rate hikes (using the rule of thumb that a rate hike by 25bp leads to 
around 12.5bp of increase in long rates). What has happened since the ECB 
September meeting adds another two hikes on top. Of course, this is a very narrow

perspective on just the long end of the curve and one can debate the exact amount 
of rate hike equivalents. Still, qualitatively the assessment remains, the ECB's 
monetary policy stance is significantly tighter than what is implied by the actual 
policy rate increases. So if the increase in euro area long rates continues at the 
present speed, we could soon be reaching a situation in which the ECB wonders 
whether the tightening went too far too quickly. 
So, what should the ECB do? Cutting rates is not an option, as it needs to keep the 
tightening bias in place given the upside risks to the inflation outlook. But there is a 
far more elegant solution available: the ECB can, temporarily at least, stop QT. Such 
a decision would mean the ECB starts again reinvesting maturing government 
bonds. This should help limit the upward pressure on yields. The justification is 
simple and lies squarely within the realm of monetary policy: the observed 
tightening through the increase in long rates goes beyond what the ECB sees as 
needed at this stage. A more measured pace of tightening is in order. In such a 
situation, improving the degree of control over financial conditions takes 
precedence over the aim of reducing the size of the balance sheet. And here another 
coincidence comes in handy: Excess liquidity is close to a critical threshold of EUR 2 
trillion (see here), so pausing QT would not come at a bad moment. And pausing QT 
now would also help take away some widening pressure from sovereign spreads. It 
would send a clear signal to the market that the ECB puts a premium on an orderly, 
controlled monetary tightening, a potentially very powerful message in current 
market conditions. Still, while we think such a decision would be a sensible 
approach for the ECB and could come as early as in the October meeting, we would 
also stress that some more long end tightening is likely needed for the ECB to 
decide to go that way. 
What about specific reactions to spread developments, i.e. the activation of the TPI? 
We think we are still very far from the point the ECB would put the TPI even on the 
agenda of the Governing Council. We heard from Council members this week (and 
could also read in the accounts of the September meeting), that the spread 
widening was seen as justified by fundamentals. The French deficit is likely to 
increase to 5.4% of GDP this year, with a primary deficit of around 3% of GDP (see 
here). Roughly the opposite (a primary surplus of 2% of GDP) would be needed to 
stabilise the French debt to GDP ratio. Small wonder the market is worried about 
the French fiscal situation. Absent a significant further spread widening and clear 
signs of stress in funding markets for French banks, the ECB is unlikely to 
contemplate any action here. And even then, it will very likely want to see some 
political signals suggesting more consolidation efforts are under way. 
But what applies to France does not necessarily apply to other euro area countries. 
Wider OAT Bund spreads have brought also wider BTP Bund spreads. The bar for 
ECB action is much lower in other jurisdictions with fewer fiscal headaches. But 
even here, we think, the TPI remains a remote option for now. Instead, the ECB 
could opt to skew reinvestments away from, say Bunds, into sovereign issuers 
affected by the widening of OATs. And this is yet another reason why pausing 
reinvestments appears a smart route for the ECB to take. Pausing QT in October 
would put the ECB in a good place to act, if needed, as it would open up a wider 
array of intervention possibilities, all with a view to increase the degree of control

Next Week: Postcards From Bangkok
Euro area | Final September HICP and ECB speak: Next week will be light in the data 
space. We will be focused on the details of the final September HICP prints across the 
euro area, to confirm that  signs of indirect and second round effects from high energy 
costs to core and food inflation remain scarce (see our Inflation Watch here). We will also 
keep an eye on EA industrial production which we think was resilient in August (0.2%M), 
in line with solid business surveys. All attention will be on policymakers who will gather at 
the IMF 2026 Annual Meetings in Bangkok, from Monday 12 October to Sunday 18 
October (full schedule here). On that occasion, we will hear from many ECB Governors, 
including Zigman, Moulin, Lagarde, Escriva, Makhlouf, Pereira, Kocher, Simkus, Nagel 
and Rehn.  
 UK | GDP & MPC speak: July GDP print was a notable upside surprise, in large part driven 
by white-collar services sectors. While these have been instrumental in UK growth post-
Covid, we do think there is scope for reversing some of their July strength in the August 
GDP reading. We look for a 0.2% drop in monthly GDP, leaving the 3M/3M print at a still 
robust 0.5%. We continue to track 3Q growth at 0.4%Q, and see a further slowdown into 
year-end, as the domestically oriented economy retrenches a bit on very tight financial 
conditions. Robust AI-related growth is the main upside risk, we think, to our forecast. 
Elsewhere, a number of MPC speakers will be at the IMF meetings next week, and we 
will be focused on their messaging. For now, all MPC members that are discussing a rate 
hike (MPC ex-Taylor and Dhingra) talk of the need to lean against the risk of second-round 
effects, but imply that such "risk management" action would entail only modest tightening. 
The BoE is not, we think, close to discussing whether higher productivity growth might 
translate to higher neutral rate, considering they remain very bearish on the supply-side of 
the UK economy, despite what we see as a relatively fast pace of AI adoption. With global 
policymakers (i.e. the Fed) more focused on potentially higher neutral rates, and also 
present in Bangkok, our focus next week will be on whether BoE speakers stick to their 
existing script on what could motivate rate hikes.
