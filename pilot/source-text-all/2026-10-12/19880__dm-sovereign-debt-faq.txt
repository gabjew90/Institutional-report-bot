Key Takeaways
One year ago, the MS DSA viewed debt levels as problematic but not yet 
unsustainable. The view today is largely unchanged. 
Although interest rates are higher, nominal growth is also higher, so  debt 
sustainability risks  actually vary by country depending on the interaction.  
Interest rates are one of three key variables  for debt sustainability; recently, 
strong nominal growth and, in some cases, resulting narrower deficits help. 
For example, US debt, empirically, looks more sustainable today than a year ago 
because of upside surprises in nominal growth.  
By contrast, Japanese yields are hitting new highs, and in the context of  Japanese 
debt being more interest rate sensitive, the impact is more notable.

Sovereign  Debt in Context  
MS Global Economics regularly publishes a DM Sovereign Debt monitor to assess debt 
sustainability across major advanced economies. Our first update,  three years ago, 
followed a material rise in sovereign debt after COVID.  That note  characterized sovereign 
debt metrics as "flashing yellow" because elevated debt levels were, at the time, stabilized 
by strong nominal growth and consolidating fiscal trajectories. The most recent model 
published in  October 2025, Losing G≤Rip, argued risks  were turning from amber-to-red:  
debt-to-GDP was running several points above previous forecasts, the cost of debt rose 
more than 20bps vs. prior  baselines, and the R-versus-G gap (the difference between the 
effective interest rate on debt and nominal GDP growth) was closing toward zero in 
several economies. The title's pun was the thesis: when R catches up to or exceeds G, debt 
ratios stop stabilizing on their own, and the fiscal math becomes far less forgiving. Given a 
further rise in yields since publication, market discussion has understandably sharpened 
around debt sustainability;  this note takes stock of what's actually changed over the past 
year.  
Empirically, rates matter but can't be read in isolation from growth, and both need to 
be read on a horizon longer than a couple quarters. Taking a five-year view, rates have 
actually stayed more contained than one might expect, especially considering how strong 
nominal growth has been recently. The AI Capex wave drove upside growth surprises and 
a combination of AI, energy, and tariffs pushed inflation higher than previously forecast  - 
the implication being nominal growth has run above baseline trend growth estimates 
(note models in the US are often anchored on the US CBO - Congressional Budget Office - 
for forecasts beyond two years; the MS DSA is built as such). So while the "cost of debt" is 
elevated, one could argue it has not entirely detached from growth, given the debate 
around upside risks to US trend growth.  
In context of recent nominal growth levels, higher interest costs are not 
unprecedented - much of the US yield curve has only retraced to levels seen a few 
years ago.  The case of Japan is different because there yields are pushing to historical 
highs.  But again, context matters.  Japan had one of the biggest "buffers" in their debt 
stabilizing fiscal math - a function of the long tenor of Japanese sovereign debt and  more 
than a decade of low funding costs.  Consequently in Japan, rates need to stay elevated for 
quite a long time before the stock of outstanding sovereign debt reprices.  
Herein lies the challenge on debt sustainability:  multiple variables are at play.  In a 
simple mark to market of interest rates (assuming these levels hold for an extended 
period) and realized growth, we see +/- 10pp swings in Debt to GDP estimates up to  5 
years out across developed economies.  Using the sensitivity tables in our previous 
publication model, we can see the wide range of possible outcomes. The key observation 
is that two years of higher than expected nominal growth in many cases has had a more 
positive impact on  sustainability metrics than the drag from higher interest rates.  Should 
growth falter, then clearly higher rates adversely affect debt, but for now the answer is 
not as straightforward as it may seem. 
Our US rates strategists recently highlighted that in instances where debt to GDP rose 
materially, yields have behaved differently (and often fallen) depending on the 
context of the monetary system, sovereign alternatives, and investor psychology.

These observations are important in the context of the math of the DSA model.  The 
market's focus on US debt and fiscal situation today but lack of focus a couple years ago 
arguably has as much to do with expectations as it does with the interest rate level, since 
most of the yield curve that affects US funding has mostly retraced to levels last seen a 
couple years ago. 
 In this context, it's therefore interesting to note that empirically US debt is actually 
better on a five-year horizon today than it was a year ago. The aforementioned upside 
surprise in nominal growth has an immediate impact on DSA math, while higher interest 
rates  will take time to impact DSA math because it takes over five years for those rates to 
propagate into funding costs. If rates remained elevated for five years, then clearly the 
benefit of higher nominal growth wears off; and, importantly, the risk of a growth 
slowdown increases over the same time horizon.  This is a key observation: in the US, 
nominal growth has quick, short-term impacts on the DSA, while rates have a slower but 
more meaningful implication for debt sustainability.  In Japan, by contrast,  the short-term 
debt outlook is worse because their debt stack makes their DSA more susceptible to 
interest costs, while medium-term stability is balanced with higher nominal growth. 
Ultimately, debt sustainability is not a straightforward reaction function of just 
interest costs.  Most debt scenarios need to balance the interaction of the key variables: 
cost of debt, growth, and fiscal position. The question of debt sustainability will continue 
to grapple with the same macro questions that markets continue to ponder: Is growth 
structurally strong because of AI? How much of a disinflationary  force might AI be? Oil 
prices have contributed to nominal growth rises with surprisingly limited effects on real 
growth - will that continue?  (for an in depth discussion on scenarios, see our mid-year 
outlook).

FAQ  
1. DM Debt is unsustainable, isn't it? 
DM Debt is not particularly sustainable, but that does not inherently mean it is 
unsustainable. Interestingly there is a case to be made that some debt outlooks are 
better  today than  one year ago because of high nominal growth.  DSA math implies that 
it is about paths and duration of change.  For most countries, higher rates usually take 
years to affect interest burdens. Indeed this is why, historically, emerging markets have 
been more susceptible to debt crisis - their debt tenors were historically much shorter 
than developed markets.  Additionally, deficit credibility matters.  Growth deceleration is 
the one risk that is likely equally problematic around the world. If growth decelerates, 
fiscals likely deteriorate in tandem, and the recent benefit on DSA math from higher 
nominal growth reverses - fast. 
Empirically, sustainability isn't a single yes/no threshold; it's a function of the gap 
between the effective interest rate on debt (R) and nominal GDP growth (G), 
combined with the primary balance. When R exceeds G, a country needs to run primary 
surpluses just to keep debt-to-GDP from rising, whereas others who still benefit from 
R<G, where debt can stabilize or even decline even while running primary deficits. 
2. How much does the recent rise in rates impact debt 
levels? 
Less than intuition suggests, and more slowly. Two things blunt the immediate impact: 
first, sovereign debt is overwhelmingly fixed-coupon, so a rate move only affects debt as 
it's issued or refinanced — not the entire outstanding stock at once. The US Treasury's 
average maturity on marketable debt is ~6 years, with varied proportion of debt coming 
to maturity within any given 12-month window, so higher rates phase into the average cost 
of debt gradually as older, cheaper debt rolls off. Second, the size of the effect is 
genuinely large over a multi-year horizon even if small in any given quarter. Our own 
sensitivity work shows how debt evolves over time - one could view the current 
environment as a +50bps shift in our most recent sensitivity table, though those adverse 
outcomes are diluted by higher nominal growth.  The bottom line is that the compounding 
effect takes time to build. The practical read: a rate shock that reverses quickly (a few 
quarters) leaves a small, mostly permanent scar on debt-servicing costs from whatever 
was issued during the window; a shock that persists for years is a much bigger deal, 
because it works through the entire refinancing schedule rather than one slice of it.
3. Can Japanese debt  be managed with these interest rate? 
Yes, on the current numbers — but with less margin than a year ago. Japan remains the 
DM outlier because debt servicing costs are low enough after years of monetary policy 
support that R stays below G.  Debt servicing is supported not only by still-low borrowing 
costs but also by the recovery in nominal GDP growth, which has had a structurally 
positive impact on the fiscal position through higher tax receipts.  The implication:  debt 
sustainability does not require a primary surplus, and the roughly ~9-year average

That said, the direction of travel is not free: debt costs are rising as the yield curve 
steepens amid BoJ policy normalization, and the R-v-G relationship, while still favorable, 
has been deteriorating. Fiscal policy also bears watching (for a deeper fiscal discussion, see

4. Isn't US Debt too high? 
The level is genuinely high and rising, but the answer depends heavily on which 
horizon you're asking about — and that's arguably the most counterintuitive finding in 
our latest work. US debt-to-GDP peaked at ~130% in 2020, fell back to ~120% by 2023, 
and our October 2025 base case put 2030 at roughly 144%, driven by the One Big 
Beautiful Bill Act making the 2017 tax cuts permanent and adding further deficit-
expanding measures, layered on top of already-elevated rates. Structurally, that's a real 
problem: stabilizing the debt would require a primary balance near zero, and structurally 
lower tax revenues plus rising interest payments make that difficult without further 
spending cuts or tax increases. But the near-term trajectory has actually improved on a 
mark-to-market basis — our updated 2030 projection now sits closer to ~137%, roughly 
~7pp better than the October base case, mainly because nominal growth has come in 
above trend over the past year (remember our models use CBO estimates in the out 
years). The long-run (2040) picture, however, has gotten worse, not better.  If rates were 
to stay elevated and fully reprice funding costs, then, once the entire debt stack reprices, 
the R vs G relationship looks different.  Ever-larger debt loads absent policy change are 
not sustainable, but arguing debt is  "too high" cannot be a static argument.  The bottom 
line is that empirically, relative to one year ago,  recent nominal growth surprises have 
bought some medium-term breathing room, even as the long-run trajectory continues to 
widen.  
5. What is the fiscal picture in the Euro area? 
Discussed in great depth in our recent Blue Paper, From Cheap Debt to Hard Choices, 
we see precisely that - hard choices - on the horizon for the Euro Area. European fiscal 
policy is shifting from an era of cheap debt to one of hard allocation choices. For 
several decades, governments benefited from falling debt-servicing costs and declining 
defence spending, while expanding the welfare state. That combination allowed public 
sectors to grow without an immediate market impact. The next decade looks different. 
Governments now need to fund three structural pressures at the same time: ageing-
related expenditure, higher defence spending and rising interest payments.
The numbers are large. We estimate that, absent offsets elsewhere, ageing, defence and 
interest payments would mechanically widen deficits by at least 3pp of GDP by 2040 in 
most euro area countries. Spain and Portugal face the largest long-term pressures, at just 
over 5pp of GDP, while Austria is the least exposed at around 1.5pp. The composition of 
spending pressures differs by country, but the direction is the same: the fiscal tailwind 
from falling rates and lower defence spending has turned into a headwind. Similarly, 
ageing is not only a drag on potential growth, it is also a fiscal cash-flow problem. Euro 
area ageing-related expenditure is set to rise by 1.1pp of GDP by 2040, driven mainly by

The most achievable fiscal adjustment is likely to be gradual rather than abrupt. Outright 
spending cuts are difficult to implement at scale. A more feasible route would be to slow 
the growth rate of selected expenditure items, allowing them to decline as a share of GDP 
over time. Furthermore, growth is central to any fiscal outlook, as the same fiscal strategy 
might create more fiscal space when real GDP growth is stronger. Strategic allocation of 
government spending and prioritising "high economic multiplier" spend could help 
optimize some of the difficult spending choices ahead. 
6. What to look for in terms of Debt Sustainability? 
We propose five focal areas in a DSA model:
• The R-v-G differential, not the rate level in isolation. A high or rising rate matters 
far less if nominal growth  rises just as fast. This is the single biggest source of 
"surprise" in either direction across our updates this year.
• Whether growth strength is structural or cyclical. The past year's upside growth 
surprise has done more to improve near-term sustainability metrics in the US and 
parts of Europe than the rate rise has done to hurt them — but if that growth is a 
cyclical or AI-driven productivity story that fades, the medium-term picture reverts 
toward the more pessimistic baseline. This is a key macro question and one that 
may have different answers in different countries. 
• The gap to the debt-stabilizing primary balance (DSPB). Some countries (Italy, 
historically) can run persistent primary surpluses; others (France, the US) have 
structurally run deficits even in good times. The DSPB tells you how big an 
adjustment is actually required, independent of the rate/growth debate.
• Debt duration and refinancing schedule. Because debt is fixed-coupon, a rate 
shock's bite depends on how much debt is rolling over and when — this is why 
Japan (long duration) and the US (shorter vs Japan) respond to the same size of 
rate move on very different timelines.
• Market pricing behavior itself, not just the arithmetic. Our rates strategists' point is 
important here: historically, episodes of material debt-to-GDP increases haven't 
produced a uniform yield response.  For example, yields have had different 
reactions higher or lower depending on the monetary regime, the availability of 
sovereign alternatives, and investor psychology around the specific issuer. The DSA 
framework tells you the mechanics; it doesn't tell you how markets will price those 
mechanics in real time, and that gap is often where the near-term risk actually 
shows up.
