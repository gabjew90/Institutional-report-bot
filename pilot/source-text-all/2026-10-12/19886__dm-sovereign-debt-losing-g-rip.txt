We mark to market debt projections across advanced economies 
using a debt sustainabilty analysis framework we introduced in a 
note 18 months ago.  Debt stability has deteriorated because the 
cost of financing is higher and fiscal deficits are wider than 
before.  "R" could equal  "G" by 2030.  
Approximately 18 months ago, we wrote how sovereign debt metrics were "flashing 
yellow" after Covid.  The fiscal stimulus that accompanied the pandemic drove debt 
to new all-time highs.  We used a traditional debt sustainability analysis (DSA) model 
to project debt outlooks across advanced economies.  At the time, we concluded 
that while debt was elevated, high nominal growth and consolidating fiscal 
trajectories were keeping the rise of debt in control.  The proverbial R v G 
relationship remained negative (cost of debt "R" versus nominal growth "G" — for a 
technical discussion, see Projecting Debt Levels ).  Refreshing the numbers, we find 
that the outlook has worsened: Debt levels, cost of debt, and fiscal trajectories 
have all deteriorated.  The R v G relationship could turn positive by 2030, which 
would drive an ever-accelerating increase in debt stocks if fiscal frameworks do 
not adjust.

Comparing the 2030 headline data from our previous DSA exercise (in April 
2024) to the recent analysis, we find that across our sample: 
• Debt-to-GDP is projected 3pp higher at ~130% 
• The cost of debt is 23bps higher, nearing ~3.5%
• Nominal growth is higher  but real growth is lower 
• R v G deteriorated by ~15bps in 2030, nearly reaching balance by 2030 
• Primary balances deteriorated  materially  
Higher cost of debt and weaker primary fiscal balances are  constant themes 
across almost all of the economies in our coverage. To reverse the trend, there 
are three avenues that can help stabilize debt:  lower rates, higher growth, or 
smaller deficits. 
The primary balance deterioration is notable because primary balances nearly 
doubled their deficit outlook in a short period of time.  Across the sample, the 
weighted average primary deficit went from -1% to -1.7%. There are risks that the 
deterioration is even greater in the medium term because  we use fiscal projections 
from  national budget agencies that use "projected" fiscal consolidation policies that 
can sometimes be politically difficult to achieve.  Weaker fiscal outlooks complicate 
the R v G math because it raises the bar on where debt remains sustainable. Much 
of the deterioration in primary balances is driven by the revised fiscal policies in the 
US and Germany, though the latter does not show signs of debt instability.  
Sticky yield curves are driving an accelerated mark to market in the cost of 
financing; coupled with decelerating nominal growth, we find the R v G situation 
reversed from one year ago and a major headwind for debt sustainability. Yield 
curves have remained elevated in the past couple years, driving a higher cost of 
financing.  Compared to forwards one year ago, the cost of financing is higher in the 
latest DSA.  Governments have tried to offset the cost by shortening the tenor of 
debt, with weighted average maturities decreasing in response. But issuing shorter-
dated paper has not fully offset the generally higher costs.  Nominal growth has 
helped by rising concurrently, though the mix of nominal vs. real growth is not as 
supportive.  Again, though, the higher nominal growth was insufficient to fully offset 
the higher cost of debt, driving a worse R v G relationship.  The clear  implication is 
that if there is a recession prior to 2030 — and the probability of that happening is 
high —  debt levels could fall into risk frameworks that have historically been more 
closely associated with emerging markets.

Projecting Debt Levels
 Rajeev Sibal, Arunima Sinha 
We review the key drivers of the debt sustainability analysis (DSA) framework.  A DSA 
framework projects debt levels by calculating the interaction of nominal growth rates, 
cost of debt, debt composition, deficits, and stock/flow adjustments. The most widely 
used model is the IMF DSA framework, though the European Commission DSA 
frameworks during the eurozone crisis (see here). We use a classic DSA to contextualize 
the recent rise in debt and test sensitivities, projecting where debt is likely to go under 
different scenarios. We use Morgan Stanley forecasts to 2026 and national budget office 
estimates/outlooks to make the projections thereafter. Specifically, the formula estimates 
the interaction of (1) the past level of debt-to-GDP, (2) current primary deficit, (3) nominal 
cumulative returns on outstanding government debt (R), and (4) nominal GDP (G, 
calculated as real GDP growth plus a GDP deflator, for which inflation can be a proxy).  
The box below shows the formulaic interpretation of the variables.2 
2. Stock-flow adjustments are also a critical variable, which we exclude for simplicity. A stock-flow adjustment explains the difference between deficits and change in the level 
of debt. Stock flow adjustments played an important part in the EM debt note because of contingent liabilities of the state that are not always transparent in EMs. The stock-flow 
should theoretically be less relevant in a DM framework because fiscal transparency is better (see for example the discussion Weber 2012). This note will not specify stock flow 
adjustments errors within the calculations to simplify and focus on the core variables of the equation: nominal growth, cost of debt, and primary fiscal deficits. More technical 
DSA frameworks will differentiate between cash and accrual accounting, primary deficits (policy-driven spending), and headline deficits. Furthermore, estimating debt servicing 
costs can be complicated because they depend on a range of factors, including the composition of debt instruments and government choices on the tenor of debt issuance.

DSA Math (Box 1)
Arunima Sinha
The change in the ratio of government debt-to-GDP at time t, dt, is expressed as:
Here, rt is the average nominal interest rate on government debt, gt is the nominal 
growth rate of GDP, pbt is the ratio of the primary balance of the government 
relative to the GDP, and sfat denotes the ratio of the stock flow adjustments to 
GDP. Note, the primary budget excludes the interest expense on the debt, which is 
captured in the first term. 
In DSA, interest and growth rates can be measured in different ways. In our 
analysis, the growth rate g is the forecast for the nominal growth over the outlook 
horizon, and r is the nominal implicit interest rate, which is the ratio of government 
interest payments in the current year and the government debt stock in the 
previous year. 
The stock flow adjustment (sfat) term arises because the government fiscal 
balance may not reconcile fully with the change of debt, which may be because of 
a lack of consolidate state-linked accounts (often the case in EM) or accounting 
cash vs. accrual issues (more often the case in DM, see discussion in Weber 2012). 
Specifically, in most accounting exercises, the data on debt levels and interest 
payments are obtained from changes in cash accounting, while tax revenue, 
expenditure and primary deficits are computed from accrual-based accounts, 
reflecting the need to plan budgets in advance (Casalin, Cerniglia and Dia, 2022). A 
large empirical academic literature on stock-flow adjustments has found that these 
are of a relevant size for DM and EM, and influenced by macroeconomic factors 
output, inflation and debt dynamics. 
These variables are commonly condensed into “R v G,”  a simplification3 but one that 
captures  the point that if nominal GDP growth exceeds the interest expense on the debt, 
the debt-to-GDP ratio will fall over time if the primary budget is in balance. Put 
differently, if R is less than G, then we can find the level of the primary deficit such that 
the debt-to-GDP ratio is stable. Conversely, if R is greater than G, there is a level of 
primary surplus that will stabilize the deficit. 
As debt levels increase and/or the cost of debt rises, the debt stabilizing primary fiscal 
balance (DSPB) takes on more importance because debt sustainability becomes more 
nuanced. The DSPB is the size of the primary balance required to keep debt-to-GDP 
constant. If the primary deficit is larger than the DSPB, then debt-to-GDP will rise.4
3. Note that while economic convention is to use "lower case" R v G, we use the two interchangeably and lean into R v G for ease of reading.
4. Even if R is less than G, the difference must also be less than the primary deficit for debt not to increase. If the primary deficit is too high, the extra spending offsets the benefit 
of higher nominal growth than interest costs. Similarly, if R is greater than G, the amount of the primary surplus needed to keep debt stable is even higher. The fiscal deficit is an 
important third leg of a debt sustainability tripod. See Box 2, "Estimating DSA Sensitivity," for further discussion.

What's Changed Since Last Year? 
The fiscal sustainability outlook deteriorated following the enactment of the One 
Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), which made previously temporary tax cuts 
permanent and introduced additional deficit-expanding measures over the next five 
years. Tariff revenues are not enough to change the ongoing trend.  
US public debt as a share of GDP began trending upward just before the Covid-19 
pandemic. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017 gradually widened fiscal deficits and 
increased indebtedness. This trend accelerated during the pandemic, driven by large-scale 
stimulus measures and the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, with primary deficits averaging 
around 6% of GDP between 2020 and 2024.
The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), enacted in July 2025, reinforces this trajectory. It 
not only made the TCJA’s temporary tax cuts permanent but introduced new short-term 
tax exemptions, further boosting deficits in the near term.
Incorporating the Congressional Budget Office’s (CBO) scoring of the bill into our 
forecasts, we project debt-to-GDP ratios exceeding 140% by 2030 and 160% by 2040. In 
short, the additional revenue from tariffs falls well short of offsetting the deficit 
expansion caused by the bill. Stabilizing the debt would require a primary balance of zero, 
but structurally lower tax revenues and rising interest payments make fiscal equilibrium 
difficult to achieve without further spending cuts or tax increases.

Our view, unchanged, is that fiscal sustainability continues to improve as the 
nominal economic growth rate rises. Japan's fiscal dynamics are improving as the 
government debt-to-GDP ratio is gradually declining and fiscal deficit narrows, 
supported by sustained moderate inflation alongside nominal GDP growth. While 
the average interest payment cost (interest payments relative to outstanding 
government debt) rises, tax revenues are increasing at a significantly faster pace 
due to the recovery in nominal GDP growth.
Japanese debt has been an outlier for many years. The debt-to-GDP ratio is currently  
reverting after the Covid spike. Despite the elevated debt level, Japan’s debt dynamics has 
been anchored. This can be explained by the R v G differential. If nominal growth is larger 
than the cost of debt (i.e., R - G is negative), then a primary surplus is not necessarily 
essential to stabilize debt-to-GDP ratios, and the economy can be on a sustainable path 
with primary deficits. The long period of low nominal interest rates helped keep Japanese 
debt from toppling over and allowed the country to extend duration. The question 
becomes, As Japanese nominal growth reflates, what implications that may have for Japan, 
particularly if the cost of debt gradually rises?

investors for funding. The elevated international funding implies that fiscal sustainability is 
under close scrutiny. The UK needs a primary surplus to stabilise its debt and continues to 
face challenges in the market when that surplus deteriorates.

Germany experienced a notable pivot in its fiscal policy stance in March 2025, when the 
"€1 trillion fiscal package" was legislated (see our deep dives here and here, and latest take 
here). The package consists of three pillars: First, it excludes defence spending above 1% 
of GDP from the federal fiscal rule. This allows military spending to increase without 
breaching borrowing constraints, and creates fiscal space for other areas.8  Second, a 
€500 billion infrastructure fund was created with an allocation period of 12 years 
(spending can happen thereafter). The fund will finance related investment spending of 
the central government (€300 billion) and subsidies to local governments (€100 billion) 
and make annual transfers to another federal special fund, "Climate and Transformation" 
(KTF) (€100 billion). Third, borrowing constraints on local governments will be softened 
to 0.35% of cyclically adjusted GDP (from currently zero). Overall, the adjustments to the 
national borrowing space mark a clear departure from the previous fiscal environment 
where the national debt brake  lead to years of primary surpluses. Exhibit 60

What's Changed Since Last Year? 
Not much has changed since last year: Australian government debt remains 
relatively low and stable. While public spending growth has been strong, it has 
been largely matched by revenues to date, and the Government has recently 
shifted focus to reform measures that could raise revenue further. While the 
starting point of debt and deficit remains benign relative to most other DMs, the 
imbalance between structural spending and cyclical revenues is a longer-term risk. 
Australia has a very short legacy of government debt, holding debt below 10% of GDP 
before the GFC and below 30% pre-Covid. Debt briefly touched 40% at the peak of Covid 
but is already back to approximately 35% and has stabilized. The fiscal outlook is neutral 
— while public spending growth remains strong, the government is still enjoying some 
revenue tailwinds, and has recently move to focus on revenue raising as a way to replace 
this as tailwinds fade.  As such, we expect the primary balance to remain lightly negative 
across the forecast horizon, though even with the deficit the debt remains on a sideways 
to downward trajectory.
