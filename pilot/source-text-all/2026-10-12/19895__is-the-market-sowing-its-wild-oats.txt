Around the world, interest rates have been rising … real rates, nominal rates, 
sovereign yields, and private yields. The drivers include central banks, oil prices, 
economic growth, and surging debt issuance to pay for the AI build-out and fiscal 
deficits. The fiscal angle has been most acute recently, centered on French sovereign 
debt (OATs). At wider than 150bps, the 30yr OAT-Bund spread surpassed the 130bp 
mark reached in the euro area debt crisis a decade ago. The market is reacting to 
years of fiscal slippage combined with the current political uncertainty. And if the 
market sees a justification for the spread widening, the ECB likely will as well, 
slowing any monetary policy response to offset the moves. 
And yet the selloff is increasingly testing the ECB’s tolerance for tighter financial 
conditions. We do not think the situation will trigger an immediate ECB response, 
but it helps to work through possible scenarios. The French election is still some 
way off, leaving plenty of time for market scrutiny to intensify. Nor is France alone: 
difficult fiscal arithmetic is becoming more common across OECD economies.
France has missed its fiscal targets for years, and structural pressures make 
consolidation harder with each passing year (see the deep dive into euro area and 
French Sovereign Debt  – From Cheap Debt to Hard Policy Choices). Higher yields 
tighten the screws. The draft budget assumes an average 10-year yield of 4.3% in 
2027, but market rates have topped 4¾% in recent weeks. That gap helps explain 
why Morgan Stanley Research expects the deficit to remain at 5.4% of GDP in 2027, 
rather than fall to the government’s 5.0% target. Without credible consolidation, 
the feedback loop from debt to deficits to risk premia could continue  (2027 Budget: 
A Long Way to Go, 1 Oct).
Lower rates alone will not solve the issue. The primary deficit, i.e., before interest 
expense, remains wide. Debt stabilization would require a primary surplus of 
roughly 2% of GDP, a turnaround of about 5 percentage points. That shift would be 
larger than the adjustment  required in Italy in the early 2010s. The willingness and 
ability to undertake such a move is what the market will look for in the election 
results. 
What about the ECB? Wider sovereign spreads could in principle disrupt the 
transmission of monetary policy. This past week, ECB officials stressed that wider 
OAT spreads that reflect fundamentals are not a reason to intervene. The bar was 
higher in the past: Italian spreads approached 250bp in 2022 without triggering the 
Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI) (Assessing the Impact from Sovereign & 
Funding Markets, 5 Oct; Why the ECB may soon opt to pause QT, 9 Oct).

response will likely be gradual. A temporary pause in QT could be a first step; full 
reinvestment would ease some pressure on yields. Broader market spillovers would 
change the calculus more quickly. Wider OAT spreads are already putting pressure 
on Italy’s BTPs, and action would be easier to justify where fundamentals do not 
warrant the same repricing. Renewed reinvestments could potentially be tilted away 
from Bunds towards affected issuers, subject to operational and legal constraints. 
Exogenously tighter financial conditions could also mean that less policy-rate 
tightening is needed. Calling off the hiking cycle would reset rates overall, but that 
would be a significant departure from the ECB’s current stance. Invoking the TPI still 
looks far off. 
The French election is still roughly six months away, and market scrutiny is likely to 
remain intense. We think the bar for direct ECB intervention is very high, but France 
may not be the last test. Fiscal challenges are tough all over and are  not new. 
Markets are less willing to look through them, and investors are now pricing greater 
risks ahead.

Japan Core Machinery Orders: We forecast core machinery orders increases

Australia Labor Force: We expect some moderation in employment growth 
in September, with employment rising 20k following August’s near 40k 
increase. With participation ticking down to 67.0%, we expect the

Euro Area Industrial Production: We expect EA industrial production to

UK Monthly GDP: Some of the July GVA gains –  in administrative and ICT 
sectors in particular –  do look excessive and prone to reversal. Still, we think 
even with a decent correction, the ICT sector would be ~6%Y up, pointing to 
decent underlying strength in the sector most exposed to AI adoption. We 
are still tracking 0.4%Q 3Q growth.

0.2 pt. For retail control, the slower growth in labor market income, and the
