Deep Dive into Higher Bond 
Yield Implications
We dive into implications of higher bond yields across equities, 
credit, economics, & select 'bond proxy' &/or otherwise exposed 
sectors. We assess risks to leverage, growth, multiples & 
compare to cases of sharply rising bond yields over the last 30Y
+. We find a continued general picture of fundamental corporate 
resilience, both relative to history & in absolute terms e.g. c. 
2/3rds of IG bonds maturing 2030 or later.

Key Takeaways
We dive into 30Y+ of history, finding 12 periods of sharply rising bond yields, 3 of 
which overlapped most closely with current dynamics (e.g. Fed & ECB hikes, 
elevated or rising oil prices, high GDP growth, similar starting multiples, etc) - 
mid-1990s & 2022.
No one-size-fits-all historical parallel: We find huge variation across performance 
patterns, earnings trends, & macro rotations in times of sharply rising yields. The 
similarities: EU equities decline moderately initially, albeit we are approaching 
stabilization levels vs history. 
The key difference: Earnings resilience & corporate preparedness is far higher 
today. In most comparable periods, investing in areas of positive earnings 
revisions was a successful strategy. 
European corporate leverage levels are still near cycle lows, the vast majority 
debt is in fixed coupon bond form & nearly two-thirds of EUR IG bonds mature 
beyond 2030. 
In equity strategy we stay constructive. In credit, we turned constructive earlier 
this week on improved risk reward. In Euro Area economics we note material 
growth risks 2-3Y out should bond yields remain elevated. 
In the event that bond yields peak, our analysts suggest 12 fundamentally-
resilient stock ideas to buy (Exh 12). This group is trading at -2.6 S.D. oversold vs 
the European index.

Credit strategy: The recent backup in yields & spreads have significantly 
improved the risk-reward & thus we removed our cautious call earlier this week 
(see here).  Leverage levels for investment grade issuers (universe that overlaps 
most with listed equity markets) is still near cycle lows, the vast majority debt is in 
fixed coupon bond form &  nearly two-thirds of EUR IG bonds  mature beyond 2030. 
Against a backdrop of strong & still rising earnings growth, we estimate that just 5% 
EBITDA growth can more than offset the impact of current levels of yields.  We see 
risks of more bifurcation for weaker-rated HY names (B- & CCC), which show a 
weaker mix of fundamentals & a greater exposure to near-term maturities. We 
maintain a more cautious stance on the real estate sector given the limited risk 
premiums and higher rates sensitivity in the sector.
Utilities: We see positive risk-reward after c.14% relative underperformance since 
April & a de-rating from a 13% P/E premium to market parity. We acknowledge the 
historical pattern: Utilities underperformed in 68% of large upward yield moves. 
However, higher yields are primarily a valuation rather than earnings headwind: debt 
is overwhelmingly fixed-rate with staggered refinancing, higher returns on new 
capex provide buffers, a meaningful share of utilities’ earnings is linked, directly 
or indirectly, to inflation through real regulated returns, inflation-indexed CfD/ROC 
revenues & commodity exposure, providing a partial natural hedge, utilities' ability 
to pass through rising rates has been proven during '21-23, regulatory frameworks 
are more adapted, lessons from prior sharp increases in bond yields have been 
learned & corporates better prepared. In our France coverage, cost of debt is more 
aligned to broader European bond yields than OATs with a high level of fix-rate 
debt & staggered refinancing in coming years. 
Construction & Materials: Balance sheets are materially stronger vs LT  history. Net 
debt/EBITDA has fallen materially from prior-cycle highs while EBITDA/net interest 
coverage has moved from c.5-7x historically to >10x for much of the recent period, & 
cash conversion has improved. Leverage dispersion is stock-specific rather than sub-
sector-specific - Wienerberger (small cap, not part of MSCI Europe) stands out as 
the most leveraged name in our coverage, while most peers remain at materially 
lower levels. Sector sensitivities, as with other sectors, largely feed through 
multiple with market focus on the potential sensitivity of end-market demand to 
rising yields.
Real Estate: While we are constructive on the sector we are yet to be minded to 
prefer more highly geared names. Average in-place cost of debt is still materially 
lower  than marginal debt costs for the lion's share of listed property companies.  The 
pace at which higher rates feed through to earnings depends on in-place cost of 
debt, hedging & debt duration which ranges from an average of 3 to 7 years across 
most of our coverage ( Exhibit 81 ). Higher rates can affect valuation yields and 
therefore companies' NAV, in turn increasing Loan to Value ratios, one of the key 
leverage metrics for the sector. Companies have few levers to reduce gearing; 
dividend payout ratios are generally high (often a requirement to sustain REIT 
regimes) while transaction volumes  in underlying real estate markets have slowed 
for most asset classes.

Insurance: Within P&C, rising rates primarily benefit insurers through higher fixed-
income reinvestment yields. Investors generally look through MtM losses, which can 
hit P&L (especially for Nordic/UK names), as bonds are often held to maturity. Post-
IFRS 17, higher yields also increase the discount on claims when booked, boosting 
P&L upfront; this discount unwinds over time, often with a lag. In life, higher rates 
support traditional savings/hybrid sales, new business & CSM growth, but greater 
competition from bonds can pressure savings sales & retention, as seen at Generali/
AXA/Allianz in 2022/23. Updated lapse assumptions should make the potential 
earnings impact more manageable vs history. As for France risks, French OATs & 
Italian BTPs likely represent only a fraction of most insurers’ sovereign bond 
portfolios.
Luxury & Apparel Retail: Higher bond yields represent a headwind to discretionary 
spending, particularly for the aspirational and middle-income luxury consumer. 
Higher yields typically translate into tighter financial conditions through higher 
mortgage and borrowing costs, while also increasing the incentive to save rather 
than spend. A sustained higher-yield environment could therefore prolong the 
sector’s current K-shaped demand polarisation & raise the hurdle for the sector to 
re-rate.
Euro Area Economics I Growth impacts over 2-3 years:  A key debate in economics 
is trying to identify factors that move yields, as they would result in different 
impacts on the macro output. We flag  recent comments from the ECB (here) 
suggesting Europe seems less likely than the US to benefit from higher trend 
growth (ie higher r*) thanks to AI. We think a plausible estimate is that a "pure" term 
premium shock of 100bp lowers  GDP in EA countries by -30/-40bp after 2 to 3 
years, mostly via investment. We also note that the ECB  estimates that tighter 
financial conditions (more broadly than yields) in 2026 could reduce GDP by -55bp 
to -130bp cumulated up to 2028 (here). Lastly,  a rough sequencing of the ECB 
reaction to stress in sovereign debt markets, if seen as warranted,  would likely be 
verbal intervention, QT adjustment and TPI activation. 
UK Economics: Mortgage rates - the main factor for the cash-flow channel of 
monetary policy - are bench-marked off of 2Y & 5Y swaps. SMEs  - which account for 
~50% of employment - still predominantly borrow at variable rates, from UK banks.  
While GDP growth sensitivities to rate rises are pronounced, large corporates 
increasingly  benefit from multiple financing channels. Bank lending growth to UK 
corporates has started slowing, but remains at robust levels. The annual growth 
rate of borrowing by large businesses stood at  8.9%Y in August, by SMEs 4.6%Y.

Credit Strategy: Higher Yields and Wider Spreads Improve 
the Risk-Reward 
Aron Becker, Ellie Dann, Jonathan Loke, Yagyesh Modi
 We have been tactically cautious on European credit since late summer, as we saw the 
headwinds from widening French sovereign spreads, rising energy prices, worsening 
relative valuations versus the US and seasonal headwinds from accelerating issuance as 
factors that could lead to more weakness in cash spreads.
But with the sharp underperformance of European credit over the past month, the 
risk-reward looks much more compelling now, and we  removed our cautious call 
earlier this week (see European Credit Strategy: Sovereign Risks Increasingly Priced In). 
With credit spreads now at the two-year wides, European growth gathering momentum, 
corporate and bank fundamentals in a strong position supported by strong earnings  
tailwinds, we think credit has room to perform in the medium term.
With our  economists having revised their forecasts globally for central bank paths in 
recent months, expecting a more hawkish path across the Fed, ECB, BoE,  and our rates 
teams having also revised higher their government bond yield forecasts, expecting global 
rates to stay higher for longer ( Exhibit 16 ), the question now is what this will mean for 
credit markets.

Within IG credit, real estate remains the key sector to watch, in our view. The sector 
continues to carry significant leverage and its asset valuations are closely tied to rates,

didn't subsequently materialise. But with the sector's discount to the index remaining 
closer to cycle tights and the sector's still elevated rates sensitivity, we think caution 
remains warranted in the space.

Positive risk reward skew from here
Whilst admittedly contrarian we see a positive risk reward skew for Utilities in the bond 
yield environment from here. The sector has underperformed the market ~14% since April 
highs, now in-line YTD, and de-rated from 13% premium to market P/E to parity largely 
due to headwinds of bond yields and related politics. Whilst this is consistent with 
average relative multiple over past 25 years, Utilities now has more growth and less 
commodity risk. 
We see two likely pathways for Utilities from here. Either that bond yields fall, consistent 
with MS forecasts, and long duration Utility stocks see inverse correlation hold and re-rate 
reversing some or all underperformance since April. Alternatively, if bond yields remain 
elevated then Utilities may outperform the wider market as bond market weakness 
potentially spreads to equity markets. 
Consequently, we are wary of turning too negative at what may be the crossroads in 
relative performance, one way or another. We consider Utilities medium risk to higher 
yields, with valuation impact for long duration equities significantly more relevant than 
limited earnings sensitivity. 
Within the various potential pathways; 
• We prefer long duration stocks for reversal in bond yields, notably Overweight 
Elia, National Grid and Orsted. Renewable names such as EDPR (albeit not 
Overweight rated) could also fit in this category. 
• For defensive robustness in a weaker market we like Overweight rated National 
Grid, E.ON, but also Iberdrola. Terna,  Redeia and gas networks names also offer 
defensive robustness, albeit with less attractive underlying equity stories.
• For short duration if yields continue to rise, then Fortum, Verbund, CEZ, Naturgy 
would be best  positioned given their commodity exposure and/or their historical 
correlation to bond yields.
• If yields continue to rise, we see names with limited ability to pass through 
increase in rates as potentially more vulnerable amongst regulated/integrated/
renewable names, namely Underweight-rated Enagas and Equal-weight rated 
Redeia due to the nature of the Spanish regulatory framework

The European utilities sector has a higher financial leverage than the rest of the market.

We also see limited earnings sensitivity to bond yields in the 
sector thanks to utilities' resilience and ability to adapt
We think that the impact of higher bond yields on sector EPS should remain relatively 
contained for several reasons:
• Inflation-linked revenues – a meaningful share of utilities’ earnings is linked, 
directly or indirectly, to inflation through real regulated returns, inflation-indexed 
CfD/ROC revenues and commodity exposure, providing a partial natural hedge 
against higher nominal rates; this is less the case for businesses operating under 
nominal return frameworks, including the US, Spain and, to some extent, Germany.
• Fixed-rate debt – European utilities have a high proportion of fixed-rate debt, 
limiting the immediate impact of higher market rates on interest costs - see Exhibit 
65 .
• Limited and staggered refinancing needs – refinancing is spread over multiple 
years, meaning that higher rates should feed into the P&L only gradually as 
existing debt matures, as shown on Exhibit 66 .
• Disposals provide an additional lever – utilities can use asset disposals, minority 
stake sales or farm-downs to fund capex and de-lever, reducing the need to raise 
incremental debt at higher rates. 
Overall, we think higher bond yields are likely to remain more of a valuation headwind 
than an earnings headwind for European utilities, with the impact on EPS building only 
gradually and varying materially by regulatory framework, debt maturity profile and 
balance sheet flexibility.

reinvestment yield in their fixed income investment portfolios. Although there are also 
MtM losses (which can make their way through P&L, especially for Nordic/UK names),

maturity. The pace at which higher yield make their way into investment income is 
determined by the duration of the asset portfolio  – those writing shorter tail lines of 
business like London Market insurers, Nordic insurers, UK P&C are likely to see a faster 
earn-through of higher yields (see Exhibit 103 ). That said, post-IFRS 17, there are also

Exhibit 100 we show the extent to which insurer's earnings are driven the discount 
benefit - those who are more levered here would see more earnings benefits immediately 
as rates rise. Over time this discount will be unwound and thus  offset by an increased drag 
on the IFIE (insurance finance income/expenses) but this often happens with a lag, 
depending on the duration of claims and accounting choices.

Life: For life insurance, there is also a mixed impact from higher rates. The more obvious 
effect is that higher rates make it easier to sell traditional savings/hybrid products and 
therefore lead to stronger new business and thus CSM growth. In Exhibit 104 we show 
the split of FY25 new business value among insurers that disclose it. This growth would 
gradually feed into earnings over time through a higher CSM release. The stock of CSM 
itself is unlikely to be materially directly impacted by rate movements due to asset-
liability matching and thus we generally observe fairly low CSM sensitivities to interest 
rate movements. On the flip side, there are potential headwinds to sales of savings 
business and the retention of existing customers. As yields rise, there is increased 
competition (from bonds/banks) on customers savings and the risk of lapses increases 
(especially in Italy/France, where lapsing is easier). We previously saw this in 2022/23 for 
the likes of Generali/AXA/Allianz. That said, since this time, companies have updated their 
lapse assumptions so the potential impact to CSM (and thus earnings) should be more 
manageable than it used to be. 
Within UK Life specifically, higher rates improve pension scheme funding levels, so more 
schemes potentially reach full funding on a buyout basis and are able to transact, which is 
supportive of BPA demand and volumes for UK life insurers, however UK BPA markets 
remain very competitive in our view.

Luxury & Apparel Retail  
Natasha Bonnet, Edouard Aubin, Grace Smalley
Headwind to consumer demand for broader Luxury & Apparel Retail: Higher bond yields 
represent a headwind to discretionary spending, particularly for the aspirational and 
middle-income luxury consumer. Higher yields typically translate into tighter financial 
conditions through higher mortgage and borrowing costs, while also increasing the 
incentive to save rather than spend. This comes at a challenging point for the sector: the 
lower-income and aspirational / middle-income consumer has already been under pressure 
for some time, reflected for example in weaker demand for luxury products and greater 
resilience at the very high end. A sustained higher-yield environment could therefore 
prolong the sector’s current demand polarisation, with brands more reliant on lower-
income and aspirational consumers potentially seeing a slower recovery in volumes, while 
exposure to high-net-worth consumers should remain relatively more defensive.
Higher yields are also a valuation headwind for a long-duration sector (Luxury). Luxury 
valuations are particularly sensitive to changes in discount rates given the significant 
proportion of DCF value captured in the terminal value.  This matters for Luxury given that 
premium multiples implicitly capitalise expectations of superior growth, returns and brand 
longevity well into the future. 
Higher yields therefore create a double headwind: potentially weaker near-term 
demand and earnings, alongside a lower multiple investors are willing to pay for those 
earnings. In our view, a sustained higher-yield environment raises the hurdle for sector re-
rating and increases the importance of earnings delivery as the key driver of stock 
performance.

Consumer Staples

Historically, Staples has proven to be a bond proxy sector, with the key corporates seen as 
offering reliable, steady compounding growth ( Exhibit 111 ). However, over the past few 
years, the sector has faced a variety of structural challenges including:
1) a rise in health and wellness trends, including GLP-1, which is resulting in changed 
consumption habits e.g. alcohol moderation, increased protein consumption. Companies 
have thus needed - and the process is still ongoing - to evolve their portfolios to meet this 
evolution
2) a shift in the balance of power between retail and CPG, with increased consolidation 
among retailers, which has given them increased negotiating leverage on pricing and 
payment terms. Retailers have also increased their focus on private label, creating a more 
competitive environment for CPG.
3) commodity volatility, first in the post COVID/Ukraine conflict period, and now in 
relation to the Iran conflict. Changing weather patterns has prompted more extreme 
weather (floods, high/low temperatures) which has also impacted agricultural crops: El 
Nino is likely to result in further cost inflation in some key commodities.
As a result, the sector has become less reliable and less defensive, meaning that the 
relationship of relative valuation vs yields has become less tight - structural issues 
outweighing pure correlation ( Exhibit 112 ). However, we also note that increasing market 
duration has also contributed to a weaker relationship between Staples’ relative 
performance and bond yields, as the broader market has become more sensitive to 
changes in yields too. The exception here has been tobacco, where cash flows are shorter 
duration, and thus less impacted by longer term yield considerations, but as they shift into 
NGPs, which offer value crystallisation on a longer term basis, the relationship with yields

Leverage Levels across the Telco and Tower Space
Historically, Telcos have embarked on significant investment cycles tied to the rollout of 
new technology (3G, 4G, 5G, fibre). The most recent upgrade cycle - 5G and fibre - is 
largely complete across most of Europe (with a few laggards - eg. UK & German fiber). 
Capex intensity is therefore declining (see Exhibit 119 ), translating to decent YoY growth 
in FCF generation (see Exhibit 120 ). 
Consequently, Telco leverage (measured via 12m fwd Net Debt  / EBITDA) has been on a 
steady downwards trajectory. Net Debt in the sector is currently ~2.4x EBITDA, falling to 
~2.2x next year, its lowest level since 2018. On a 10-year view, we note the majority of 
operators boast leverage ratios towards the bottom end of historical ranges. However, we 
would  note the following: 
• Telefonica remains a core outlier, with 2027 leverage of 3.7x (including 100% of 
hybrid bonds, and employee commitments). 
• The largest Telco, DT, is targeting leverage of 2.75x today (as per its 2024 CMD 
guidance). This is higher than its previous 2021-24 CMD target of 2.25x-2.75x. 
• In the Towers space, we note leverage levels tend to be more elevated due to the 
contract nature of the business (long-term contracts with escalators, high revenue 
visibility, very high operating leverage from additional tenants, insulation from 
technology shifts etc). Over the past 10 years, there has been high variability 
across Net Debt / EBITDA levels, ranging from -0.1x (Rai Way) up to 8.4x (Cellnex, 
2022a). More recently, we have seen European TowerCos become more aware of 
managing debt levels, with Helios Towers bring leverage down (from >5x in 2022, 
following its acquisition in Oman, to <3 x today), and Cellnex slowly de-leveraging 
via a mix of asset sales (Austria, Ireland, Scandinavian minority) and organic 
growth. Nevertheless, leverage remains elevated at both Inwit (~6x) and Cellnex 
(>7x today). 
• Orange will potentially see its leverage rise from 2.0x (Dec 2025a, incl. hybrids) to 
2.7x, post the acquisitions of  MasOrange (50% equity stake), Scorefit and a portion 
of Altice France (Dec 2027e, pending antitrust approval & completion of the Altice 
France break-up).

Higher yields, but not only: Higher sovereign yields ultimately raise borrowing costs for 
households and weigh on rate-sensitive spending. We provide below macro elasticities we 
think useful to get a sense of potential risks for the macro outlook. A caveat is that the

growth expectations, tighter (expected) monetary policy, higher inflation or fiscal risks. 
Those factors might also in turn provoke reactions of monetary or fiscal policies in one 
direction or the other.  And other channels may aaslso add to the impact of yields, for 
instance uncertainty. In short, looking at yields in isolation does not tell the full story.

UK Economics: Restrictive - but is it enough?  
Bruna Skarica  
Front-end matters more, but ... UK is an economy linked to the front-end of its yield 
curve. Mortgage rates - the main factor for the cash-flow channel of monetary policy - are 
benchmarked off of 2-year and 5-year swaps, mimicking the 2- and 5-year fixed-term 
structure of the secured loan products extended to households. SMEs in the UK - which 
account for ~50% of employment - still predominantly borrow at variable rates, from UK 
banks (~60% of their lending, vs. 90% in 1Q08). Large corporates increasingly  benefit 
from multiple financing channels, and just ~25% of their outstanding lending is extended 
by UK banks, with ~40% stemming from the capital markets. In aggregate, hence, BoE's 
models place a higher weight on the moves in the front-end Bank Rate pricing, then on 
term premia alone. 
One illustrative example of the Bank's modelling sensitivities was published in the August 
2019 Monetary Policy Report, which showed that, in isolation, a persistent 50bp repricing 
of the market path for Bank Rate can lower GDP by 40bp over 2 years. More recent 
analyses show an even more sizable impact - 100bp move in Bank Rate can lower GDP by 
130bp over two years. Meanwhile an isolated (term premia-related) 20bp move in the 10-
year yield is ascribed an impact of ~10bp. Broader financial conditions - i.e. tighter credit 
spreads (illustrative 10bp fall in bank funding spreads, a 10bp fall in UK investment-grade 
non-financial corporate bond spreads, a 40bp fall in UK high-yield non-financial corporate 
bond spreads) can impact GDP growth by another 10bp.
 Who is impacted by what? Tighter financial conditions undeniably  impact the UK 
economy. BoE's Money and Credit data shows mortgage approvals have - in the aftermath 
of 2-year mortgage rates moving by ~1pp higher over the past 7 months - fallen to their 
2023 lows. The total mortgage re-financing hit this year compares to 2H23, although it is 
important to note that the share of households impacted by the cash-flow channel is 
~10% of all households at most per year. A very potent channel of policy in recent years 
has been the substitution channel - households increasing their desired savings in 
response to higher cash returns. Having said that, 1H26 brought a drop in the savings rate, 
as households smoothed their consumption through the initial stage of the energy hit. We 
are modelling a modest inflection higher in the savings rate, but appreciate two-sided risks 
around this call. 
Bank lending growth to UK corporates has started slowing too, although it does remain at 
quite robust levels. The annual growth rate of borrowing by large businesses stood at  
8.9%Y in August, and that of SMEs at 4.6%Y. We think solid loans growth chimes with 
data showing resilient business capex - particularly in the utilities and ICT sectors. This 
reflects more structural themes such as electrification and clean energy transition 
investment, with the latter, we think, reflecting AI-related growth tailwinds. Part of this 
borrowing is likely less elastic to borrowing costs than typical cyclical business capex-
related funding. However, it is important to stress that despite corporate lending rebound 
in the last 2 years, corporate debt in the share of UK's GDP is at multi-decade lows. We 
think it is a fallacy to point to this as a sign that monetary policy is less impactful - private 
sector de-leveraging is precisely a result of tight financial conditions.

Drivers of Higher Rates in 2026 and Forecasts
As we have repeatedly highlighted throughout 2026, in our view the rise in euro-area 
yields has primarily reflected higher monetary policy expectations. Our 10y Bund 
decomposition in Exhibit 145 reinforces this view, showing that neither the term premium 
nor the swap spread has exerted meaningful upward pressure on yields. 
If anything, the cross-market component of the term premium has moved in the opposite 
direction, potentially reflecting the reduction in tariff-related risks over the last year. This 
is against a persistent narrative that supply/demand imbalances and governments' fiscal 
policies  have been the main drivers of the sell-off. In reality, when we updated supply 
forecasts in our 2026 mid-year outlook (see here) we kept our numbers unchanged, as 
government support for households and corporates in response to higher energy prices 
remained modest, particularly compared with the measures introduced in 2022.
Swap spreads, which were a powerful driver of the upward repricing in Bund yields 
between 2022 and 2024, have also played little role in the latest move. This is consistent 
with our fair-value model, which has pointed to broadly sideways price action in swap 
spreads over the past few quarters.

What would it take to see 10y Bunds at 4%, a level not seen since 2008?
As discussed above, we expect ECB policy expectations to remain the primary driver of 
10y Bund yields. In our view, a material move higher in yields would therefore require a 
meaningful reassessment of the monetary policy outlook—for example, if central banks 
were perceived as becoming more tolerant of inflation. We see this as relatively unlikely 
at present; if anything, we think the latest ECB meeting delivered a hawkish surprise 
relative to market expectations.
A second potential catalyst would be a shift towards more expansionary fiscal policy, 
putting upward pressure on term premia. However, our forecasts point to little change in 
the fiscal stance also for 2027. A material deterioration in the fiscal outlook would 
challenge this view: significant fiscal slippage could drive a renewed build-up in term 
premia while also putting further tightening pressure on swap spreads.
