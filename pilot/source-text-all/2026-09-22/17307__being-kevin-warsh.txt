Being Kevin Warsh
Blog
Jason Draho, Head of Asset Allocation, CIO Americas, UBS Financial Services Inc. (UBS FS)
In the 1999 movie Being John Malkovich, the John Cusack character 
stumbles upon a portal that leads into the mind of the titular character. 
The movie is described as a “surrealist fantasy comedy drama.” Investors 
might characterize watching the Fed over the past few months the same 
way. Many would welcome a portal into Fed Chair Kevin Warsh’s mind 
to understand what exactly he’s thinking. After his press conference last 
Wednesday, one might be assured that he’s hawkish and committed 
to tightening monetary policy until inflation is back to 2%. Maybe, but 
there's a difference between what someone says and what they believe. 
Without a portal into Warsh’s mind we can only speculate as to what 
he’s thinking because he refuses to provide forward guidance. That’s 
unfortunate because what he believes matters for the medium-term 
investment outlook.
Before speculating on Warsh’s beliefs, a quick recap of the Federal Open 
Market Committee (FOMC) meeting. The Fed raised rates 25bps as widely 
expected. The decision to hike was unanimous, which was on the hawkish 
side of expectations, as was the Summary of Economic Projections (SEP). 
The median FOMC forecast called for two rate hikes in 2026 and no cuts 
in 2027, while expectations for growth and inflation were revised higher. 
Finally, Warsh’s comments reiterated the Fed’s commitment to getting 
inflation back to 2%.
But this is where a portal into Warsh’s mind would be helpful because 
otherwise we’re left with subjectively interpreting his comments. Many 
Fed watchers viewed his statement that they were “removing a dose 
of accommodation” as evidence that he believes policy is currently 
accommodative—i.e., the Fed funds rate is below the neutral rate—
suggesting that multiple hikes might be necessary. But does he actually 
believe current policy is accommodative? Unclear. When Warsh was asked 
about the SEP forecast that core PCE inflation will get to 2% only by the 
end of 2028, he replied that it wasn’t his forecast. Does that mean he 
thinks inflation will fall faster or slower than that forecast? That matters for 
how many hikes he thinks will be necessary.
Trying to deduce Warsh’s thinking may not matter for the near term 
outlook because there’s not much uncertainty about the Fed’s task. 
Deciding to hike 25bps isn’t hard when growth is resilient (3Q GDP is 
tracking around 3%), core PCE inflation over 3%, and the market is 
pricing over 90% probability of a hike. Given what the Fed has already 
communicated, hiking rates again later this year shouldn’t be a hard 
decision. But what the Fed does beyond that is much more uncertain. The

market is pricing for three more hikes, while the median “dot” in the SEP 
has one more hike this year and the Fed on hold in 2027.
The essential issue for the medium-term outlook is whether the Fed, and 
Warsh specifically, thinks it’s necessary to aggressively tighten financial 
conditions in order to bring inflation down. Doing so requires equities to 
go lower, credit spreads to widen, and rates to go higher. That decision 
may not be entirely the Fed’s to make if persistently high oil prices force 
their hand. Barring that scenario, inflation should trend lower in 2027 at a 
sufficient pace for the Fed to stay on hold after hiking rates in December.
The bottom line: Financial markets handled the 25bp Fed rate hike last 
week reasonably well. It helped that Fed Chair Kevin Warsh was clear about 
the rationale for hiking and did what investors expected. But what he 
wants to do from here is a guessing game. The markets should be able to 
comfortably handle another 25bp rate hike this year since it’s already priced 
in. But the difference between a relatively constructive market outlook 
(our base case) and one facing strong headwinds hinges partly on whether 
Warsh thinks that financial conditions need to tighten significantly. There 
isn’t compelling evidence that he does, but one has to be Kevin Warsh to 
know for sure.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
