Americas Transportation: Higher Railcar Prices Supportive of Valuation; 
Refreshing EV/Replacement Methodology for Railcar Coverage; 
21 September 2026 | 3:01AM EDT
Bottom Line: Given the fairly sharp rise in railcar asset prices over the 
post-Covid period (Exhibit 1), driven by higher inflation, elevated commodity 
prices, and favorable industry supply dynamics, it is likely that owners of railcar 
assets stand to benefit from the increased value of their fleets. Industry supply 
fundamentals have been supported by a more consolidated railcar manufacturing 
base (2 railcar manufacturers comprise 90% of industry backlog), reduced 
speculative railcar building relative to prior cycles, and broadly improved utilization 
of assets by railroads themselves (and therefore less need for excess railcar buffer 
capacity) following Precision Scheduled Railroading (PSR) implementations. Against 
this backdrop, we refresh our EV-to-replacement-cost analysis and now include 
TRN and GBX in our analysis in addition to GATX. While our primary price-target 
methodology remains P/E-based (see GATX, TRN and GBX), we believe the EV to 
Replacement-Cost analysis helps provide a longer-term perspective on the value 
of these companies’ durable asset bases, particularly given railcars’ 40-50 year 
useful lives. 
 
On a relative basis, we continue to favor GATX. Although both TRN and GBX own 
meaningful leased railcar fleets of approximately 100k and 20k units, respectively, 
GATX remains the largest owner and lessor of railcar assets globally with a 
consolidated lease fleet of roughly 250k railcars. We think the scale of GATX’s

Methodology: 
1. Note that our first method compares GATX/GBX/TRN’s enterprise value against the 
estimated current replacement cost of their leased railcar fleet. On this basis, GATX 
trades at approximately 0.5x replacement value, TRN at roughly 0.6x, and GBX at 
approximately 1.0x. 
2. To enable comparisons with equipment rental companies outside the rail sector, such 
as URI and HERC (both not covered), our second method compares each company’s 
enterprise value against an inflation-adjusted asset base by restating book values using 
respective Producer Price Indices (i.e., railroad rolling stock PPI for GATX, TRN and GBX 
versus construction machinery PPI for URI and HERC). Viewed through this lens, railcar 
lessors as a group trade at an average EV-to-adjusted-asset-value multiple of 
approximately 1.0x, which screens as attractive relative to roughly 2.0x for construction 
equipment rental peers. While we are Buy-rated on GATX and Neutral/Sell-rated on 
TRN/GBX, we believe this valuation gap highlights the potential relative attractiveness of 
the railcar equipment leasing group as a whole relative to other equipment sectors.

Attractive EV-to-Replacement Cost: 
Based on our calculations and estimates for new railcar prices, the market appears to be 
valuing GATX at the enterprise level at ~0.7x its North American railcar fleet’s 
replacement cost and ~0.5x its Worldwide railcar fleet unadjusted (Exhibit 2). Given we 
have not included replacement cost valuations for aircraft engines that GATX owns 
(~40+ fully-owned aircraft engines and 50% interest in ~500 additional aircraft engines), 
one could argue the company does not receive much incremental credit for the engines 
when considering that its Enterprise Value is more than absorbed by the implied 
replacement cost of its North America railcar fleet alone. Said another way, at current 
stock price levels, one could own GATX at an EV of ~0.7x its North American railcar 
fleet’s replacement cost and 0.5x its Global railcar fleet (including Europe and 
India), with additional optionality/opportunity around the aircraft spare engines. 
Considering peers’ EV/replacement cost multiples range from ~0.6x to ~1.0x with an 
average of ~0.8x, we would consider GATX’s multiple as attractive on a relative 
basis. 
If we adjust GATX’s consolidated 2026E global railcar fleet of ~250k lower to ~187k 
as a proxy to account for the fact that some incremental fleet growth in North America 
in 2026 (i.e., NAM fleet in 2025A was ~109k compared to ~200k in 2026E) is attributed 
to its joint-venture partner with Brookfield (in practice, the total acquired Wells Fargo 
fleet remains consolidated on GATX’s financial statement), we still find GATX trading at 
an EV/replacement value of ~0.7x on its Global fleet (Exhibit 4); should this adjusted 
replacement cost of ~$25B be multiplied by a range of ~0.8x-1.0x with the high-end 
of the range closer to GBX’s implied multiple, the implied EV of ~$20B-$25B would 
put GATX’s hypothetical valuation closer to a range of $241-$380 (note: we leave 
our current price target of $223 unchanged, representing ~25% upside) as 
illustrated in Exhibit 5.  
We note that due to TRN’s large lease fleet of nearly ~100k wholly-owned railcars, 
the company similarly shows hypothetical stock price upside at the various multiple 
ranges while GBX shows moderate downside, reflecting the differences in 
lease-fleet sizes (Exhibit 6).

A Look Into GATX Profit Trends and Drivers 
As illustrated in Exhibit 7, GATX’s earnings growth path has accelerated versus its 
recent historical trend (from a ~flat pre-Covid EPS CAGR to a projected double-digit 
growth trajectory through 2028E) with the recent step-up reflecting a combination of 
higher lease rates, stable fleet utilization, higher gains on sale, and now scale benefits of 
integrating the Wells Fargo Rail acquisition. As GATX has expanded its consolidated 
global railcar fleet to ~250k, its ability to generate elevated gains on asset sales has 
scaled as well (Exhibit 8). And while Rail assets (North America and International) 
remain the bedrock of GATX’s profitability, the company’s Aircraft Spare Engine 
Leasing segment has emerged as a powerful growth driver of late as well with 
Engine Leasing’s share of primary segment profits nearly doubling from 2014 to 
2028E (Exhibit 9 - Exhibit 10). The broader trends around inflation and in particular for 
assets that are in relatively favorable supply/demand positions (railcars and aircraft 
engines) provides GATX with opportunities (we think) to continue increasing lease rates 
and is supportive to sustainably elevated to growing gains on sale; and given GATX’s 
larger overall consolidated fleet alongside elevated asset values of late, we also see 
EV/replacement cost as supportive to longer term valuation.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.

their respective areas of coverage.  The addition or removal of stocks from such Conviction Lists are managed by the Investment Review Committee or 
other designated committee in each respective region and do not represent a change in the analysts’ investment rating for such stocks.

return potential, price target and associated time horizon are stated in each report adding or reiterating an Investment List membership.
