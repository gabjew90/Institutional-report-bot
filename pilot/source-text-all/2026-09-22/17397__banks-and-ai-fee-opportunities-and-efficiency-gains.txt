Banks and AI: Fee Opportunities and Efficiency Gains 
Following our June review of how investment-grade non-financial firms discuss AI, we assess US 
and European bank commentary in earnings calls and other disclosures. Our key conclusion is that 
AI is likely to be credit-positive for banks through two channels. First, they can monetize the AI 
capex cycle through advisory, underwriting, syndication, asset-management and selective lending 
revenues. Second, they can deploy AI internally to support automation and cost discipline. The AI 
capex theme is most pronounced among US banks, but the efficiency angle is evident in both 
regions. 
Facilitating the Capex Cycle 
US banks are far more likely than non-financial firms to discuss AI capex on earnings calls, reflecting 
the commercial opportunities created by the buildout. Banks can arrange construction facilities, 
bridge financing and project packages while distributing longer-dated risk to institutional and 
private-market investors. The potential opportunity set includes a wide range of capital markets 
and financing activity (debt and equity), trading intermediation, and hedging. The Fed’s January 
2026 Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey points to banks’ greater willingness to lend to firms 
expected to benefit from AI, and we expect them to take a more active lending role if capacity in 
traditional syndicated markets becomes constrained. Given the scale of the anticipated AI-related 
capex need, we also continue to expect the private markets will play an increasingly important role 
in the financing. Our equity colleagues recently highlighted a growing supply-demand imbalance 
for AI and expect the capex cycle to remain elevated through the end of 2027.  
Reaffirming Our Overweight Stance on Banks 
AI-related activity has contributed at the margin to higher USD bank supply, in contrast to relatively 
flat EUR IG bank supply. Elevated USD bank issuance reflects both opportunistic funding amid tight 
spreads and greater balance-sheet use for prime-brokerage financing and C&I lending across the AI 
ecosystem. While this is a technical headwind for USD banks, we think it is outweighed by the 
associated fee, financing, and efficiency benefits, which should support credit fundamentals. 
Banks’ overall share of USD IG gross supply has also remained broadly stable at roughly 40%, while 
technology’s share has increased sharply towards 20%.

Banks and AI: A Credit-Positive Outlook 
 
 
In June, we reviewed earnings transcripts and regulatory filings across investment-grade 
non-financial corporations in the USD and EUR credit markets. Our key takeaway was 
that, while management teams increasingly frame AI as a productivity opportunity, 
evidence that these benefits are materializing at scale remains limited. 
We also argued that the AI buildout is becoming a major driver of corporate funding 
needs. AI-related issuers have already contributed meaningfully to debt supply this year, 
and we expect the funding burden to broaden as more firms finance their own AI capex. 
Reflecting this dynamic, we recently raised our 2026 USD IG gross supply forecast to 
$2.3 trillion from $2.1 trillion and penciled in $2.4 trillion for 2027, setting the stage for 
two consecutive years of record gross issuance. 
This week, we turn to banks. Our review of earnings calls, regulatory filings and 
transcript data across the largest investment-grade US and European banks suggests 
that AI is credit-positive for the sector through two channels, which directly inform 
fundamental metrics: 
Fee generation and ecosystem lending: Banks can capture advisory, debt and 
n
equity underwriting, and syndication revenues from the AI capex cycle while 
generally limiting retained long-dated infrastructure risk. At the same time, banks 
may expand lending and financing across the broader AI ecosystem, including 
hyperscaler suppliers, equipment providers and clients trading AI-related securities. 
While banks may deploy balance sheet lending capacity through bridge facilities, 
construction lending, and underwriting commitments, we expect them to remain 
selective in retaining large, long-dated exposures. 
Internal operating efficiency: Banks are deploying AI to support process 
n
automation, cost discipline and efficiency gains. 
Both themes are particularly evident among US banks. Reflecting this, while EUR IG bank 
supply remains broadly flat year to date, USD IG bank issuance is running at a record 
pace. We think this reflects opportunistic pre-funding of upcoming maturities amid tight 
spreads, as well as, at the margin, greater balance-sheet needs related to AI-linked 
trading and lending activity. While this elevated supply represents a technical headwind 
for USD banks, we think it is outweighed by the associated fee, financing, and efficiency 
benefits. This reinforces our preference for banks vs. non-financials. 
More broadly, recent headlines have focused some market participants on the potential 
pace of the AI buildout. Our equity colleagues recently highlighted a growing 
supply-demand imbalance for AI and expect the capex cycle to remain elevated through 
the end of 2027.  
This multi-trillion-dollar scale of the AI infrastructure buildout remains too large for 
bank balance sheets and public syndicated markets to fund alone, in our view. We 
therefore expect private credit, specialized infrastructure platforms, real estate funds 
and institutional asset managers to provide an increasingly important share of 
long-duration capital. Large banks should nevertheless remain pivotal as originators, 
structuring agents, underwriters and liquidity providers across the AI financing 
ecosystem.

1. Banks Outpace Non-Financial Issuers in AI Mentions 
AI has become an increasingly important theme in bank earnings calls. On average, AI 
was mentioned roughly nine times per US bank call and four times per European bank 
call in Q2 2026 (Exhibit 1). Banks have consistently discussed AI more frequently than 
non-financial firms since 2025H2, coinciding with the broader adoption of more 
advanced AI models. 
 
On a sector basis, US banks ranked behind only technology, excluding hyperscalers, in 
terms of AI mentions in Q2. European banks ranked behind technology and 
communication services (Exhibit 2). However, there remains meaningful dispersion 
across firms. In the US, nearly every bank in our sample mentioned AI at least once, with 
the largest banks mentioning AI the most (Exhibit 3).  Conversely, in Europe, several 
smaller European banks did not raise the topic at all.

2. AI Capex: Capital-Light Participation, With Scope for Broader Lending 
Looking closely at transcript themes, US banks are roughly nine times more likely than 
non-financial corporations to reference AI capex on earnings calls (Exhibit 4). By 
contrast, European banks discuss AI capex less frequently, reflecting the fact that the 
physical infrastructure buildout, particularly hyperscale computing and data center 
capacity, remains concentrated in the US. 
The increased focus of US banks on AI is also consistent with the Federal Reserve’s 
Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey, where banks reported a greater willingness to lend 
to firms expected to benefit from AI exposure and less willingness to lend to firms 
adversely affected by AI. This suggests that AI considerations are increasingly entering 
underwriting frameworks (Exhibit 5).

Our review of the eight largest US and ten largest public European banks suggests that 
direct retained balance-sheet exposure to AI infrastructure remains limited so far. These 
banks account for roughly 50% of US bank debt outstanding in the Bloomberg USD 
credit index, and 40% of European banks in the EUR IG corporate credit index. So far 
and based on our review, only one US bank disclosed direct lending to data-center 
projects, while another highlighted a strategic partnership related to the AI buildout 
(Exhibit 6). Most other banks discussed AI capex in broad commercial terms. European 
banks were somewhat more forthcoming on specific facilities, but overall disclosures 
suggest that direct exposure remains modest (at least for now).  
The Chicago Fed supervisory data support this conclusion: direct outstanding exposure 
to AI-adjacent sectors among large US banks represents just 0.8% of total bank assets, 
or roughly 9% of Tier 1 capital, rising to 25% when undrawn commitments are included. 
However, this likely understates banks’ broader AI-related balance-sheet exposure. 
Beyond direct lending to data centers and hyperscalers, banks may provide C&I lending 
to suppliers expanding capacity for the AI infrastructure buildout, as well as 
prime-brokerage and other financing to clients trading AI-related equities. Such activity 
is less likely to be disclosed explicitly as AI-related lending, but could become a more 
meaningful source of balance-sheet usage as the capex cycle broadens. 
For now, disclosures point to a predominantly capital-light, originate-to-distribute 
model. Banks can structure construction facilities, bridge financing and project packages 
for data centers, semiconductor manufacturing and power generation before 
distributing a meaningful share of longer-dated credit risk to institutional investors. The 
opportunity is nevertheless meaningful, particularly in the US. Several of the largest US 
banks have announced financing initiatives linked to data centers, semiconductor 
capacity and related infrastructure (Exhibit 7). Our US Banks equity research analysts 
estimate that AI-related activity has accounted for roughly 50%-55% of year-to-date 
growth in investment-banking volumes and fees, as well as around 40% of 1H2026 
Equities revenue growth. 
As financing volumes rise and traditional syndicated capacity is increasingly utilized, 
banks may deploy balance sheet lending capacity through bridge facilities, underwriting 
commitments, trading inventory, hedging and other capital-markets activities. This

3. Productivity and Operating Efficiency Take Center Stage 
When discussing internal AI deployment, we found that banks focus primarily on 
efficiency gains through automation, as well as cost control. Every bank in our sample 
cited operating efficiency or process automation as a key objective. Management teams 
often bundle AI initiatives with broader digital transformation and IT modernization 
programs, with this theme more pronounced in the US than in Europe (Exhibit 8). 
This is consistent with the conclusions of our European bank analysts, who expect the 
main benefit of AI for banks to come through cost containment and efficiency-ratio 
improvement. This differs from non-financial firms, where our earlier review found that 
fewer than 10% linked AI directly to cost cutting.  
Banks provide limited disclosures on AI expenditures. Only one European bank disclosed 
AI-specific expenses, while roughly one-quarter of banks include AI spending within 
broader technology budgets. The remainder do not disclose dedicated AI budgets at all 
(Exhibit 9).

4. AI Disruption Concerns Have Shifted Towards Cyber Risk 
Finally, earlier this year, banks faced increased scrutiny over concerns about AI-driven 
disruption to bank business models, including faster deposit repricing or lower customer 
switching costs. However, these now appear to have subsided in earnings-call 
discussions (Exhibit 10). Within risk disclosures, only one major bank identified structural 
business-model disruption as a material AI risk. Even then, the concern was primarily the 
risk of falling behind peers in AI adoption. 
Instead, cyber and operational resilience have become the more prominent focus areas 
vis-à-vis potential AI-related risks  (Exhibit 11). Banks are increasingly focused on the 
potential for AI-enabled cyberattacks, fraud, software vulnerabilities and operational 
failures. This also appears consistent with the growing supervisory focus on AI-related 
operational and cyber risk. For instance, the ECB has directed significant institutions to 
submit formal remediation plans addressing AI-enabled cyber risks, information and 
communication technology (ICT) governance, and operational security weaknesses.

Credit Implications: Reaffirming Our Overweight Stance on Banks  
AI adoption reinforces our constructive view on the banking sector. While the 
opportunity is more pronounced in the US, we think banks in both the US and Europe 
stand to benefit relative to non-financial firms in their respective markets. Banks can 
capture advisory, underwriting, syndication and asset-management fees from the AI 
capex cycle while generally limiting retained long-dated infrastructure credit and 
duration risk. AI-related efficiency gains and cost discipline should also support further 
improvement in efficiency ratios (Exhibit 12). 
We therefore reiterate our overweight recommendation on banks vs. non-financial 
sectors, particularly technology. USD bank spreads have outperformed technology year 
to date, and we expect this relative performance to continue (Exhibit 13). The key 
consideration is the divergent trajectory for leverage and supply: technology companies 
are utilizing their significant balance sheet flexibility to fund AI capex, contributing to a 
material increase in debt issuance. Technology now accounts for roughly 20% of USD IG 
gross supply this year, up from around 5% in 2024 (Exhibit 14). 
 
By contrast, we see little indication that banks are increasing leverage materially. Record 
USD IG bank supply reflects opportunistic issuance into tight spreads and, at the margin, 
AI-related growth in balance-sheet-intensive activities, including prime-brokerage 
financing of AI-related equity trading and C&I lending to companies supporting the 
broader AI infrastructure buildout. These activities may increase banks’ use of balance 
sheet lending capacity through bridge facilities, underwriting commitments, trading, 
hedging, and lending across the AI supply chain, but we expect banks to remain selective 
in retaining large, long-dated AI infrastructure exposures. From a relative standpoint, 
banks’ share of total USD IG supply has also been broadly stable at roughly 40% (again, 
Exhibit 14).  
Even if direct lending increases more materially, the scope of the AI buildout will likely be 
too large for bank balance sheets and syndicated debt markets to fund alone. We expect 
private markets—including infrastructure, real estate, credit and equity—to provide an 
increasingly important share of long-duration financing. Large banks should 
nevertheless remain central to originating, underwriting, syndicating, financing and 
hedging the AI capex cycle, with the greatest opportunity likely accruing to global firms
