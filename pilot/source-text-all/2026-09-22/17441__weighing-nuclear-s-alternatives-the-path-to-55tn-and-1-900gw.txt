Weighing Nuclear's Alternatives: The Path To
$55tn And ~1,900GW by 2100
Following on from the '26 WNA Symposium (here, here), we revisit a key
question consistently raised around nuclear: how will it compete vs other
sources of generation (fossil, renewables, etc.) through to 2100. 1) Global
nuclear capacity grows to ~1,900GW by 2100 in our base case. 2) Renewable
growth is set to continue, sodium-ion, SMRs and fusion create uncertainties
in long-term projections; 3) The pace of fossil fuel displacement will be a key
swing factor
Nuclear & Renewables: Feedback from WNA was that governments increasingly view nuclear as an
essential part of the energy mix going forward, rather than a competing alternative to renewables.
Amid rising energy demand (robotics, automation, heating/cooling, industrial applications, etc.),
nuclear's advantages are now being weighed more holistically vs. previously perceived challenges
(i.e. high rate sensitivity, construction risk, etc.). As we note here, nuclear economics depend
disproportionately on governments, given high upfront capex and long pre-revenue construction
periods. This likely makes policy support structurally more important, and the growing government
backing vital. Investor feedback has focused on near-term execution risks.
Structural Tailwinds: We model solar and wind continuing to lead capacity additions through
to 2100, growing by ~22x and ~12x, respectively. We expect these sources to be the dominant
contributors to the growing share of (non-nuclear) generation. Nuclear's growth, although moderate
in comparison (~4.8x), is however a significant increase over recent decades in our forecast.
Across fossil sources, we project coal as the only source in decline (~55% down), with retirements
outpacing new builds. We model the share of gas growing to ~2,015GW by 2100 (i.e. up ~12%).
Fossil Displacements: The rate of fossil retirements and which generation sources replace them
will have a meaningful impact on nuclear's share of generation. In our base case, total electricity
demand grows at a 1.7% CAGR through 2100 (vs. 2.8% in our high scenario). With ~50% fossil fuel
displacement by 2100, and nuclear capturing 20% of new electricity demand, we estimate ~1,903
GW of installed nuclear capacity by the end of the century (~4.8x today's fleet).
Innovation & Learning Curves a Swing Factor: Commercialization of Sodium-ion as a source of
storage is in our view, likely from 2030 onward. A 30%-50% reduction in battery costs through
sodium-ion adoption would materially improve wind and solar-plus-battery economics, affecting
nuclear's market share. Similarly, grid-scale fusion could impede long-term growth in fission
capacity, depending on adoption rates and cost details. Nuclear itself could also realise disruptive
cost curves through small modular reactors or other innovations.
Regional Variations: China accounts for 48% of reactors currently under construction globally and
targets 110GW of operating capacity by 2030 (from ~62GW today), a pace no other major market is
attempting. In our projections, the US will successfully increase its operating fleet, despite no new
units currently under construction. Near-term capacity is set to come from restarts (Duane Arnold,
Palisades, Crane/TMI) and uprates. We believe the US will scale to ~397GW by 2100, broadly in line
with the stated 400GW government target. We model growing reactors in Europe (France's EPR2,
the UK's Sizewell C, Poland, Czechia, etc.), Japan, and RoW through 2100 through a combination
of restarts, uprates, and new entrants.

Policy Sets WACC
As we highlighted previously, we believe that WACC is destiny for capital-intensive industries such
as nuclear. Nuclear economics depend largely on financing structures, given the high upfront capex
and long pre-revenue construction periods. This makes policy support structurally more important
for nuclear than for other generation sources with shorter construction cycles and faster cash flow
realization. China has successfully reduced WACC through government support, which explains its
leadership in new capacity additions. Western developers face higher WACCs, which has historically
created a structural disadvantage. The policy environment now appears to support lowering WACC
for nuclear globally.

Global Energy Demand & Nuclear's Role
We forecast electricity demand growing 1.7% annually through 2100 (base case) and up to 2.8% in our
high scenario. The takeaway for nuclear is not that it will capture the largest share, but rather that it is
entering a meaningful growth phase within an overall sector that is expanding. Feedback from WNA
confirmed that this view is shared by the nuclear industry.

Across fossil sources, we project coal as the only source in outright decline (~55% down), with
retirements outpacing new builds. We model the share of gas growing to ~2,015GW by 2100,(i.e. up

Europe's existing fleet is the largest of any region in absolute terms today. We believe the under-
construction pipeline understates growth through to 2050, given various announced projects yet to
reach first concrete. Some of these include France's EPR2 programme (up to 14 units/~22GW), the
UK's Sizewell C, Poland's first plant (~3.75GW), and Czechia's Dukovany expansion. Europe expands
more than 2.5x in our central case.
 
Japan is set to see meaningful growth driven by the restart of reactors rather than the construction
of new plants. Kansai Electric Power Co declared Japan as back, at the recent WNA Symposium. The
7th Strategic Energy Plan (approved February 2025) targets nuclear at roughly 20% of generation by
2040, up sharply from 8.5% in 2023, with government financing covering an estimated 30% of new-
build project costs where new-build does occur. Kashiwazaki-Kariwa Unit 6 restarted in February 2026.
We model Japan as growing to 107GW by 2100 in the base case.
 
Rest of the World - Our RoW grouping includes notable countries with nuclear plans such as Russia,
South Korea, Canada, Turkey, Egypt, Bangladesh, Pakistan, and the UAE. This group of countries has a
visible pipeline, with new units such as Akkuyu in Turkey, El Dabaa in Egypt and Rooppur in Bangladesh
(11GW combined) set to be online between 2027 and 2034. Continued Russian, South Korean, and
Middle Eastern expansion increases this further. We expect this region's share of global electricity
demand to rise over the century, with nuclear growing to 280GW by 2100 in the base case
