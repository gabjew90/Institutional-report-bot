Fusion: Investing before ignition
Fusion’s first commercial opportunities are emerging before
ignition. We map suppliers that already have exposure to
critical enabling technologies and companies whose
capabilities could benefit as the industry moves from
prototype development towards commercialisation.

Key takeaways
• Fusion’s first commercial opportunities are emerging in the value chain. Our base case
remains commercial fusion in the 2040s (Fusion: Tomorrow's solution to today's problem),
but procurement, qualification and demonstrator construction are creating supplier revenue
today.
• There are near-term opportunities in enabling technologies.HTS tape and magnets,
precision manufacturing, plasma heating and cryogenics screen strongest as fusion
developers are already placing orders today and the technical requirements limit the number
of qualified suppliers. Fuel-cycle systems, advanced materials and lasers offer emerging
opportunities, but revenue visibility depends on qualification, reactor design and programme
progression.
• Dual-use suppliers provide the most resilient exposure on the route to
commercialisation. Given estimated timelines for commercialisation of fusion, suppliers that
combine fusion exposure alongside established businesses in other sectors such as
healthcare, defence, industrials, semiconductors and grids, provide an opportunity for
investors to participate in fusion’s progress without relying on commercial deployment
occurring on developers’ timelines.

Fusion value chain: investable before reactors are
commercial
Our base case remains commercial fusion in the 2040s, with a bull case for the 2030s. We
continue to view positively the transformative potential of this technology and the magnitude
of the economic opportunity it presents (Fusion: Tomorrow's solution to today's problem,
Barclays Brief podcast on fusion). Despite the clear advances being made, no fusion system has
yet demonstrated whole-system engineering breakeven, with materials durability, specialty
component manufacturing, tritium self-sufficiency and unit economics remaining unresolved
challenges. Unlocking these supply chain bottlenecks will take time and, perhaps more
importantly from the perspective of this report, substantial investment, which will generate
significant opportunities for value chain companies.
Commercial fusion might be years away, but the fusion procurement cycle has already
begun. The fusion industry requires a complex value chain. As Western governments seek to
avoid the China dependence that has emerged across parts of the clean energy and critical-
minerals supply chains, significant investment will be needed to build domestic capacity in key
fusion-enabling technologies (The new geo-economics of energy). Fusion companies require
both high-specification hardware (magnets and HTS tape, power electronics, vacuum and
cryogenic systems, precision components and fuel-cycle equipment) as well as services
(engineering, construction and qualification), supplied by companies across capital goods,
electrical equipment, industrial gases, specialty materials and engineering & construction/
technical services. Many prototype and demonstration programmes are already under
construction, with fusion companies reporting c.$540mn in supply-chain spend in 2025,
projected to rise to $680mn in 2026 (Figure 2). Recognising the opportunity presented by
growing fusion order books, companies in the value chain are increasingly pivoting their
business model towards fusion specific components and services (Figure 3).
Dual-use end-markets broaden the revenue base. Superconductors, cryogenics, isotopes,
advanced materials and engineering also have uses in other markets, such as healthcare,
aerospace and defence, conventional nuclear, semiconductors and grids (Figure 4). This means
that many suppliers can build the capacity and manufacturing know-how that fusion will
require while generating revenue through exposure to other sectors, lowering the risk of

High temperature superconductors (HTS) tape and
magnet systems
High-temperature superconductors (HTS) tape and magnets are key enablers on the path
to the commercialisation of fusion. Fusion magnets have historically been produced using
copper conductors or low temperature super conductors (LTS), which limit the magnetic field
that can be produced. HTS tape is used to create the powerful magnet fields needed to confine
and control the plasma where fusion takes place. The stronger the magnetic field, the easier it is
to sustain the fusion reaction. It also allows for more compact and efficient reactor designs, an
important part of the journey towards commercialisation as it allows companies to build
machines that would historically have only been possible in government laboratories. We view
the market landscape as consisting of two layers: the HTS tape manufacturers and the magnet
developers and integrators. The applications for HTS tape and magnet systems extend beyond
the fusion industry to a diverse range of sectors, from healthcare to power transmission to
defence.
Revenue today with expansion plans underway. This is not a technology of tomorrow but is
already being supplied to fusion developers today. A single fusion prototype, such as CFS's
SPARC, requires c.10,000km of REBCO HTS tape; a commercial fusion plant would require
significantly more HTS tape. Additionally, prototype machines require full scale magnet
manufacturing and testing. While global manufacturing capacity has been increasing, it still
falls short of what would be needed for commercialisation of fusion and suppliers are investing
in additional production capacity. HTS tape and magnets are core to the commercialisation of
fusion but the applications for these products extend beyond fusion, offering the potential for
diversified revenue streams from sales into healthcare, proton therapy, particle accelerators,
scientific instruments, energy systems and grid applications.
We identify the main companies involved in HTS tape and magnets.Many of the companies
we set out in Figure 6 have exposure to other markets, providing more diversified revenue
opportunities and a more defensive way to gain exposure to the fusion ecosystem.Fujikura,
which we believe has the most exposure to this market relative to companies in Figure 6,
produces REBCO HTS tape and has supplied to CFS. The company announced further
investments in this space, consisting of JPY11.6bn of REBCO-related capacity investment, of
which JPY6bn is intended to triple production by 2027 and JPY5.6bn is for a new plant planned
that is expected to begin operating in 2028. Additionally, the company is part of the consortium
that invested in CFS and has also entered into collaboration with Kyoto Fusioneering. Faraday
Factory Japan is a major supplier of HTS tape to fusion companies, including to Proxima, and
has delivered over 10k km to customers. The company has c. 250 customers in c.30 countries
and has increased production by 10x since 2020. The list below includes those that already
supply to the fusion industry; others that are involved in this area include SuNam, Sumitomo
Electric and American Superconductor. Additionally, Toshiba has partnered with Helical
Fusion on developing HTS magnets.

Precision engineering, vacuum systems and high-
spec components
Precision manufacturing is one of the clearest areas of current supplier revenue, with
developers already commissioning components for research and demonstration machines.
Every fusion reactor, whether it be magnetic- or inertial-confinement1 , requires large, complex
and highly customised components that can tolerate extreme conditions, including
temperatures on the order of 100mn°C. Fusion machines require vacuum vessels, cryostats,
blanket structures, divertor components, pumps, seals, specialist valves, plasma-facing parts
and large welded assemblies, each at a specification closer to those of aerospace parts than
conventional power. These are bespoke items where technical qualification, delivery capability
and quality assurance are central procurement considerations alongside price. The companies
that can make these components already supply to nuclear fission, defence, aerospace,
semiconductors, scientific facilities and quantum hardware. As such, this represents one of the
clearest examples of revenue generation in the value chain accruing before fusion reaches
commerciality.
Revenue is already accruing today via three routes. The first is through the direct
manufacturing of components for demonstration machines and test facilities. Second is the
paid design, feasibility and qualification work that precedes any build. Third, supply of vacuum,
sealing and precision-machined parts into first-of-a-kind (FOAK) engineering. By contrast with
fuel-cycle or blanket systems, monetisation is not predicated on a working reactor given these
goods and services are procured as the machine is assembled.
Fusion-grade precision components are supplied by a relatively small group of qualified
manufacturers. Few companies can meet the required tolerances, materials specifications and
quality-assurance standards and many of those that can also serve aerospace, defence and
automotive customers. This can create capacity constraints and longer lead times for fusion
developers. For suppliers, the relevant read-through is that qualified manufacturing capability
is essential, although commercial outcomes will depend on contract terms, execution and the
transition from bespoke work to repeat orders.
We identify the main companies involved in the manufacturing of precision components
(Figure 7). Freemelt has progressed from feasibility work to production, securing an order from
Fusion for Energy for over 30,000 tungsten components for the JT-60SA tokamak (c.$5.7m base
value, up to c.$8.7m with options), alongside paid studies for TAE Technologies and
membership of Proxima Fusion's Alpha Alliance.2 Vinci, through its Nuvia subsidiary, co-leads
the ILIOS consortium and holds a share of the UK's STEP Phase 1 award, worth c.£100mn.3
Mitsubishi Heavy Industries supplies major components and testing equipment to public
fusion programmes, including ITER-related magnet, divertor and blanket activities.4

Power electronics and plasma heating
Fusion occurs at extremely high temperatures, and fusion reactors therefore need
specialised systems to create, heat, compress and control plasma. This requires
components from across a range of areas including high-voltage power supplies, capacitors and
capacitor films, pulsed-power equipment, power semiconductors, radio frequency (RF) heating,
gyrotrons and klystrons, waveguides and transmission equipment, electrical insulation and
specialist cables and connectors. These technologies are not peripheral to fusion, but core to
the process. Many of these technologies have applications in a diverse range of sectors, with
applications spanning industrial, power, defence and healthcare.
Revenue today with growth opportunities. Power systems and components are essential
both for research facilities and for companies moving towards commercial power plants. A
survey in the Fusion Industry Association's value chain report identified power systems/power
components as one of the most sizable constraints. For companies serving the fusion industry,
revenue can be generated from equipment sales to fusion prototypes, as well as to research
facilities. High-voltage component and capacitors and RF and plasma heating equipment are
also revenue sources. The underlying technology for power electronics and plasma heating can
be applied across a range of sectors, offering more diverse revenue streams.
We identify the main companies involved in power electronics and plasma heating.
Because these technologies can be used in a range of sectors, the companies in Figure 8 have
exposure to a range of end-markets. An example of this is Thales, where the fusion components
part of its business is a small part of its broader operations. This is similarly the case for
American Superconductor. Ampegon has worked with Fusion for Energy since 2013 to design,
manufacture and commission the high-voltage power supplies for ITER's Electron Cyclotron
Heating system, with the first European unit handed over to the ITER Organization in 2026.5
However, one company in the list is purely focused on fusion. Kyoto Fusioneering supplies
advanced technologies to fusion developers and operates independently of reactor designs. It is
a pure-play fusion company, so does not benefit from the sector diversification of some of the
other companies in the list. The company leverages the capabilities of the manufacturing
industry in Japan rather than producing everything themselves and focuses on bringing
together the individual technologies into complete systems.

Cryogenics and industrial gases
Cryogenics are a critical piece of a fusion reactor's architecture. High-temperature
superconducting (HTS) magnets only reach their performance at temperatures near absolute
zero, so every magnetic-confinement machine needs a large cryogenic plant to cool and hold
them there. That cryogenic plant effectively regulates the operating environment in which the
reactor sits. This includes the helium and liquid-nitrogen refrigeration, cryostats, insulated
distribution, storage, valves, seals and the ultra-high-vacuum systems alongside them. These
must be installed and commissioned before a machine can attempt first plasma6 , effectively
pulling the spending forward into the construction phase. In our view, this timing element
underscores the attractiveness of cryogenics for investors seeking exposure to fusion as one of
the earliest, and most predictable, places in the value chain where suppliers derive revenue.
Revenue is already live. Developers buy bulk cryogens (principally helium) to run research and
demonstration facilities, commission complete cooling plants, and procure specialist
components such as cryostats, valves and seals into FOAK engineering. Critically, none of these
orders are contingent on a working reactor. By contrast with fuel-cycle or blanket systems,
where revenue waits on demonstrated performance, cryogenic infrastructure is a prerequisite
for switching a machine on. It is therefore ordered regardless of whether the underlying reactor
fleet ultimately reaches commercial scale.
What sets this segment apart is that the fusion opportunity sits inside businesses that are
already large and diversified. The firms capable of engineering cryogenic systems at fusion
scale are the same industrial-gas majors that supply healthcare, semiconductors, hydrogen,
LNG and aerospace procurement. Fusion contracts represent incremental demand layered on
top of an established order book, rather than a concentrated bet. As such, in our view,
cryogenics also fit the criteria of an attractive way to gain thematic exposure without
underwriting the technology risk of any single developer. For cryogenics,the revenue arrives
early, the capability is scarce and the customers span multiple industries, which cushions the
wait for commercial-scale fusion. The strength of materiality is an important caveat though,
with fusion demand unlikely in the near term to move the needle for companies the size of Air
Liquide or Linde.
We identify the main companies involved in cryogenics and industrial gases (Figure 9). Air
Liquidedescribes its cryogenic technology as an enabler of magnetic-confinement fusion and
points to its role delivering the ITER cryogenic system as evidence of its ability to integrate
infrastructure at that scale.7 Lindebrings comparable helium and large-scale cryogenic
capability, evidenced in big-science projects including CERN, though we have not identified a
disclosed fusion contract to date. Among the specialists, CPC-Cryolab(Dover) supplies
cryogenic pipework, valves and components applicable directly to fusion facilities, while
Cryomagneticsand Omniseal Solutions (Saint-Gobain) provide cryogenic magnet systems and
extreme-environment seals, respectively.

Fusion fuels, isotope enrichment and fuel-cycle
systems
Fuels are a core consideration for the fusion industry. Deuterium and tritium are the most
commonly used fuels for fusion. Deuterium is common and is readily available in seawater.
Tritium is extremely scarce in nature, in part because it decays with a half-life of approximately
12 years. As a result, commercial deuterium-tritium fusion will require both secure initial
supplies and systems capable of breeding, recovering and recycling tritium within the plant.
Some fusion developers plan to work with external suppliers to develop fuel-cycle technologies
while others expect suppliers to provide the key components.
Revenue growth potential. There is not yet a commercial market for fusion fuels so companies
operating in this space can generate revenue through engineering services, tritium handling
expertise, isotope processing technologies and licensing work. While this is a critical area, many
of the fusion fuel companies today look more like engineering consulting companies than more
traditional fuel suppliers. Near-term, revenue is likely to be in the tritium management, fuel-
cycle engineering, isotope processing and licensing support, with actual fuel sales coming once
there is commercial fusion deployment.
We identify the main companies involved in fusion fuels, isotope enrichment and fuel cycle
systems. With the exception of Eni and Kinetrics (division of BWX Technologies), the
companies in Figure 10 are private. Eni has a JV (RH3OVA) with the UK Atomic Energy Authority
(UKAEA) that provides operational and consultancy services.8 Eni is also working with the UKAEA
on the H3AT Tritium Loop Facility to advance tritium processing and recycling; while BWX
Technologies is the design and fabrication partner for that project.9 SHINE Technologies
benefits from its exposure to markets such as healthcare, allowing it revenue opportunities
from the production of medical isotopes and from radiation-effects testing and neutron
imaging.

Thermal management and blankets
Thermal management and blankets are key enabling technology for fusion. As fusion
occurs at extremely high temperatures, it is essential for a reactor to be able to manage heat
and protect the reactor structure. Thermal management functions include removing heat from
the first wall facing the plasma, cooling the divertor that gets the highest heat fluxes, protecting
the superconducting magnets from overheating and converting fusion heat into electricity.
Because large reactors produce more energy, but also more heat, thermal management is a
core competency for commercial plants.
The blanket is the structure that lines the inside of the reactor behind the first wall. The blanket
acts as a shield, a heat absorber and a fuel production plant all in one system. Without a
blanket, most of the fusion energy would be lost and the reactor would not be able to function
as an economically viable power plant. The blanket has lithium-containing materials and when
the fusion neutrons hit the lithium, tritium is produced. This can be recovered and recycled
back into the reactor as fuel, creating a closed loop fuel cycle.
One to watch, but currently has a more limited investment landscape than superconductors,
cryogenics or fuel-cycle systems. The supplier base includes heat exchangers, integrated
blankets, thermal-management systems and specialist pumps. Monetisation can come through
the development and testing of integrated plant systems and engineering and manufacturing
thermal systems.

Plasma-facing and advanced materials
Materials performance is one of the main constraints on the operating life and economics
of a fusion plant. The first wall and divertor sit closest to the plasma and must withstand
extreme heat, energetic particles and neutron bombardment. These conditions can erode
surfaces, weaken materials and require components to be replaced. Tungsten is the leading
material for the most exposed areas because of its high melting point, thermal conductivity and
resistance to plasma erosion, but it remains vulnerable to cracking, embrittlement and changes
in its surface structure under fusion conditions. Ceramics, beryllium compounds, specialist
polymers and structural composites are also required for thermal protection, electrical
insulation, neutron management and other high-stress applications. No single material solution
has yet been fully qualified for long-term operation in a commercial fusion plant, leaving
material development and component qualification as continuing areas of supplier spending.
Suppliers can generate revenue well before commercial reactors are built. Near-term
opportunities include material-development studies, prototype manufacture, qualification
programmes and the supply of components for public research machines and private
demonstrators. The opportunity extends beyond raw-material production. Processing difficult
materials, joining dissimilar metals, proving performance under representative conditions and
manufacturing finished components can carry more technical value than supplying the
underlying commodity. This is particularly relevant for tungsten, which is difficult to machine
and join using conventional processes, and for ceramics and composites that must retain
insulating or structural properties under radiation, heat and repeated operating cycles.
Raw-material demand could become significant if fusion plants are deployed at scale, but
the timing and material requirements of future reactor fleets remain uncertain. By comparison,
prototype orders and qualification work are already visible. Once a manufacturing process or
material has been accepted for a major fusion programme, replacing the supplier can require
further design work, testing and approval. This creates an advantage for companies that
establish manufacturing experience and performance data during the demonstration phase.
Existing revenue from semiconductors, aerospace, defence, nuclear and medical applications
can also support investment in materials and manufacturing capacity while fusion demand
develops.

Diagnostics, testing, simulation and digital control
Diagnostics, simulation and control systems are used throughout the development cycle.
The ability of operators to see inside the fusion plasma and act on it in real time is critical to the
build. That requires a dense layer of diagnostics, such as plasma measurement, spectrometry,
and fuel analysis. These feed control systems that stabilise a reaction behaving on millisecond
timescales. Around this sits a growing market in simulation, digital twins and AI-led
optimisation used to design machines, shorten experimental cycles and predict component
wear. These activities can generate work across design, testing and operation, although
contract structures and the balance between internal and outsourced development vary by
programme.
Revenue opportunities span multiple development stages. Diagnostics and control software
are sold into every stage, from research devices to demonstration machines and test facilities,
and are upgraded repeatedly across a machine's life as experiments evolve. By contrast with
vacuum vessels or magnets, which are large one-off purchases tied to construction, this is
closer to a recurring income stream as the sensors, control platforms, modelling contracts and
testing services are sold continuously as programmes iterate. This cadence is arguably what
makes this segment attractive in spite of small individual ticket sizes, as customer relationships
are sticky and the same tools are re-sold across a widening set of developers.
Fusion demand represents the extension of a proven business that already powers particle
accelerators, cancer therapy and defence. The physics of measuring and controlling high-
energy systems is common across these fields, which is why the credible suppliers are rarely
fusion start-ups. Cosylab, for instance, builds control systems for particle accelerators and
proton-therapy cancer treatment as its core business, with fusion a natural adjacency. This
transferability does, however, cut both ways for investors. It lowers the technology risk, because
these firms are already commercial, but it also dilutes the fusion thematic purity, since fusion is
a small and hard-to-isolate part of a broader software and instrumentation business. Supplier
differentiation may be lower than in specialist magnet or precision-component manufacturing,
because some developers may build core software and control capabilities internally.
We identify the main companies involved in diagnostics and digital control (Figure 13).
Siemens AG provides the clearest named listed exposure through its collaboration with
Commonwealth Fusion Systems and NVIDIA on a digital twin of SPARC, where simulation is
used to model plant behaviour before it is built.12  Amentum provides a broader route through
fusion diagnostics, simulators, control and instrumentation, although fusion remains part of a
much larger defence, government and large-science business. Among the specialists, most of
which are private, Cosylab supplies turnkey control software and electronics into complex
fusion facilities, drawing on the same platform it sells to accelerators and cancer-therapy
systems; digiLab applies AI optimisation, sensor qualification and digital-twin techniques to
fusion design; and RadiaSoft provides modelling and control across particle beams, lasers, X-
rays and plasmas. Next Step Fusion is a rare dedicated player, focused specifically on
simulation, diagnostics and real-time plasma control.

research stage onwards. Sensors, control systems, simulation software and digital-

from accelerators, healthcare and defence reduces reliance on fusion alone. The principal
limitation for investors is access, since most specialist providers are privately held, while fusion
currently represents only a small part of the wider activities of listed companies such as
Siemens AG and Amentum. Public-market exposure is therefore relatively broad today, but

Engineering, construction and regulatory services
The engineering, construction and regulatory services segment is currently where some of
fusion's largest contracts are being awarded. Before a machine can be built, someone must
design the plant, integrate the systems, write the safety case, secure the licence, manage the
construction programme and supply the nuclear-qualified workers. That work is unavoidable,
front-loaded, and paid on long-dated professional-services contracts rather than component
orders. In our view, this is one of the most under-appreciated part of the fusion build-out.
Revenue arrives earlier here than other parts of the value chain. Engineering firms are
engaged at concept, which is years before the first component is fabricated, and stay through
licensing, construction management and commissioning. Public programmes such as ITER,
STEP and the national laboratories remain the primary customer base today, with private-
developer mandates a growing but smaller share. Importantly, this revenue is not contingent on
any reactor working, as it is spent designing, licensing and de-risking machines regardless of
whether they ultimately reach commercial scale.
The differentiating factor is the ability to license a fusion plant. Plenty of firms can manage
a construction programme but very few can write a nuclear safety case and take it through a
regulator. Fusion's regulatory position is still being settled, with several jurisdictions, including
the UK, having moved to regulate fusion separately from fission.13 This is commercially
favourable but still novel from a procedural perspective, meaning the first movers will need to
set the precedent for others to follow. Firms with existing nuclear licensing track records, ITER
experience and security-cleared staff hold an advantage that cannot be bought quickly, because
the constraint is qualified people rather than plant. We are, however, cognisant of the
limitations of expressing a fusion thesis through this sector. Most companies in this space are
diversified engineering groups where fusion is a rounding error against conventional nuclear,
defence and infrastructure work. We therefore view the fusion -specific capability as scarce and
the earnings exposure as dilute.
We identify the main companies involved in engineering and regulatory services with the
potential to benefit from fusion demand (Figure 14). Vinci, through Nuvia, leads the ILIOS

Lasers and optics
Lasers and optics are the core enabling technologies for inertial fusion, with supplier
revenue emerging well before commercial power generation. Inertial fusion uses short,
extremely high-energy laser pulses to compress and heat a small fuel target until fusion occurs.
This requires a tightly integrated chain of laser sources, amplifiers, diode pumps, optical
coatings, mirrors and diagnostic equipment. Each component must deliver high power with
exceptional precision while surviving repeated exposure to intense energy. The technology has
already demonstrated scientific progress at facilities such as the US National Ignition Facility,
but a commercial plant would need to operate far more efficiently and fire repeatedly rather
than conduct isolated experiments. The central challenge is therefore shifting from proving that
laser fusion can occur to developing laser systems that can operate reliably, frequently and at
an acceptable cost.
Suppliers can earn revenue during the research, development and demonstration phases.
Public laboratories and private fusion developers are commissioning laser systems, optical
components and modelling tools to test reactor concepts and determine the operating
parameters required for future plants. Revenue currently comes from the design and delivery of
experimental laser infrastructure, funded development programmes and specialist components
for research facilities. The same technologies are also used in defence, aerospace,
semiconductor manufacturing, healthcare and industrial materials processing. These adjacent
markets allow suppliers to develop laser performance, manufacturing capacity and optical
durability without depending solely on the fusion timeline.
A commercial plant would require high-energy lasers to fire repeatedly, potentially operating
across modular arrays rather than relying on the low-frequency systems used in many research
facilities today. Optics would also need to survive repeated pulses without degradation, while
laser efficiency, target manufacture and overall plant energy consumption would need to
improve. These requirements create demand for higher-repetition-rate laser architecture,
efficient diode pumping, low-absorption coatings and large optical components that can
withstand repeated exposure. However, reactor designs are still evolving, and the eventual
configuration of commercial laser systems remains uncertain. The most defensible near-term
exposure therefore sits with suppliers receiving funded development work or supplying
established research infrastructure.

Operations, robotics, remote handling and life cycle
services
This segment of the value chain has the potential for the most frequently recurring
revenue over a plant's lifetime, because fusion machines consume their own internal
components. The neutrons released by the fusion reaction bombard the structures closest to
the plasma such as the first wall, divertor and blanket, degrading the materials and making
them radioactive. This has two key implications. The first is that these components wear out
and must be replaced on a repeating cycle over a plant's life. The second is that once a machine
has operated, the materials can no longer be safely approached by people, so every inspection,
removal and replacement must be carried out by robotic systems working behind shielding.
Around that sits waste management, safety systems and eventual decommissioning which
positions this segment as a structural requirement of running a fusion plant.
The revenue earned is already material, but is currently concentrated in nuclear fission
rather than fusion. Remote handling, hazardous-environment inspection and
decommissioning are established businesses built on nuclear clean-up programmes, defence
and space, and it is primarily these contracts that fund the capability today. Fusion-specific
revenue is the latest arrival in the value chain, because it depends on machines having operated
long enough to require intervention. There is a pipeline of fusion decommissioning programs
already beginning to form, most notably with JET, the UK tokamak that we visited with clients
on our fusion field trip. JET is effectively the first fusion decommissioning programme, and the
techniques developed there transfer directly to the machines now being built.
Because the first wall, divertor and blanket wear out and must be swapped robotically, the
speed of that operation directly sets how much of the year a plant can generate electricity. If a
replacement takes months rather than weeks, output falls and the economics of the plant
deteriorate accordingly. Developers have recognised this, and are now designing machines
around ease of maintenance from the outset rather than treating it as an operational question
to solve later. For suppliers, that pulls remote-handling expertise forward into the design phase,
giving firms with proven nuclear robotics experience an earlier entry point than this segment's
position in our ranking would suggest.

fusion, with remote handling, maintenance and waste management alongside its industrial-

Power conversion, turbines and grid infrastructure
This is potentially the largest end-market associated with commercial fusion, but
currently the least direct way for investors to gain exposure to the theme. Every
commercial fusion plant will eventually need turbines, generators, transformers, switchgear,
grid connection and power-conversion equipment. In principle that is a substantial opportunity,
since these are among the most capital-intensive items in any power station. But this
equipment is bought last, is not fusion-specific, and is supplied by companies whose order
books are already being reshaped by far larger forces (such as the AI data centre buildout). In
our view, fusion in the near-term adds minimal incremental demand to businesses that are, for
entirely separate reasons, well positioned in the energy transition (See our Network Security
series).
Revenue here arrives only once plants are commercially deployed, which makes it firmly a
long-duration play. Magnets, vacuum systems and cryogenics are all procured during the
construction of prototypes. By contrast, power conversion is only ordered when a machine is
designed to export electricity to the grid.On our base case of commercial fusion in the 2040s,
meaningful revenue exposure for most power-equipment suppliers remains a next-decade
opportunity. The equipment is less fusion-specific than magnets, vacuum systems or cryogenics
and is supplied into several established power-generation markets. Fusion-specific
differentiation may therefore be more limited.
The starting point is that these companies are already benefiting from the same
underlying driver that makes fusion compelling. Grid modernisation, electrification and
data-centre demand have driven a step-change in orders across the power-equipment complex,
and it is that dynamic, rather than fusion, that is setting the earnings trajectory for companies
such as GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, ABB and Hitachi Energy today. In the longer run, should
fusion deliver abundant firm power at scale this would reinforce the electrification of industry,
heat and transport, expanding the very demand pool that these companies serve. Their
equipment is also the mechanism through which that power reaches the system, since every
plant requires conversion, transmission and grid connection. On that basis, we view fusion as
less of a separate revenue opportunity for this segment than an accelerant to the electrification
thesis already underpinning it.
