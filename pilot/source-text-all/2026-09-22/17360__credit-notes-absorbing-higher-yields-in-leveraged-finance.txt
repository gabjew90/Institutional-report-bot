Credit Notes: Absorbing Higher Yields in Leveraged Finance
21 September 2026 | 12:12PM EDT
The rise in global bond yields and recent central bank rate hikes have renewed credit 
investors’ focus on the interest rates backdrop—specifically on the potential 
downside risks. In the past few weeks, the 10-year US Treasury yield revisited the 
local highs of late 2023, surpassing the closely watched 5.0% threshold. And the 
30-year US Treasury yield climbed to nearly 5.4%—a level not seen since the summer 
of 2007 (Exhibit 1). In Europe, the 3.5% yield on 10-year German Bunds is the highest 
in roughly 15 years. 
Our US economists expect another hike at the October FOMC, following the hawkish 
September meeting. And our European economists forecast another 25bp rate hike 
from the ECB in December, bringing rates into mildly restrictive territory.

Debt service costs are likely more relevant vs. business model pressures, in our view 
While interest rate considerations are a standard part of credit investors’ relative value 
calculus—as there is a direct impact on total returns, the widely-discussed ‘yield-based 
demand,’ and debt service costs—we sense that the tone of the focus has shifted in a 
more negative direction, especially as it relates to fundamental risks facing floating rate 
borrowers. 
There are two key channels of fundamental impact for this cohort, in our view. First is on 
the debt service cost, as floating rate borrowers will experience a headwind in the form 
of higher interest costs immediately, while the fixed rate borrowers will experience 
higher interest costs only if/when they refinance their lower-coupon debt. 
Second is the impact on specific business models. The increase in interest rates may 
weigh on consumer demand in interest rate sensitive areas of the economy, where 
financing is a key ingredient to purchases, such as real estate, autos, and areas of home 
improvement (high-priced appliances, for example). The pressure on discretionary 
income may be especially visible at the lower end of the income spectrum, which spends 
a larger share of their budget on energy costs. That said, our US economists have noted 
that household balance sheets are strong, and the net worth-to-disposable personal 
income ratio is still near its all-time high—somewhat tempering this risk. 
As a result, we view the state of the economy as ‘solid enough’ to sustain a constructive 
backdrop for issuers in the USD leveraged finance market—at least in aggregate. More 
likely than broad-based disruption, we expect increased dispersion depending on the 
customer and category end-market focus in interest sensitive sectors. 
Given that framing, for this analysis, we focus on the debt service and credit metric 
impacts from higher base rates to issuers in the USD leveraged loan and private credit 
markets. 
The key takeaway: Expect fundamental improvement to stall somewhat; focus more on 
the drivers of higher rates 
We believe the fundamental improvements visible over the past several quarters are 
likely to stall somewhat from here for issuers in the broadly syndicated leveraged loan 
and private credit markets, given the incremental rise in the policy rate anticipated (and 
delivered, so far). Higher base rates are an especially unwelcome development for 
issuers aiming to refinance upcoming Software loan maturities. That said, given the solid 
state of the economy and the relatively contained amount of rate hikes expected by our 
economists, we view the fundamental headwind as largely manageable and unlikely to 
spark a meaningful rise in defaults above our current forecasts (Exhibit 2).

As has been the case over the past few years, the drivers of any rate increases are far 
more important than the absolute levels. Higher rates reflecting resilient growth are 
more easily digested by credit fundamentals. 
Higher base rates may support increased demand for floating rate credit, such as direct 
lending loans (within private credit) or broadly syndicated loans (either outright or as 
part of structured investments). That said, it is unlikely to be the key driver of investor 
demand, in our view, and will need to be accompanied by a supportive macroeconomic 
backdrop and compelling relative value. 
Framing the recent improvement in debt service costs, credit metrics 
Heading into the most recent rate hike from the Fed, floating rate borrowers in the 
broadly syndicated loan and private credit markets demonstrated an improvement in 
interest coverage metrics over the past several quarters. The cumulative 175bp of Fed 
rate cuts from September 2024 through December 2025 materialized through lower 
floating-rate borrowing costs—easing some of the fundamental pressure which 
occurred following the Fed’s 525bp rate hiking cycle from March 2022 through July 
2023. 
For example, the weighted-average institutional rate for new USD leveraged loans fell to 
6.5% in 2Q2026, from 9.3% in 3Q2023 (Exhibit 3).

As we noted recently, private credit fundraising reached a new record in 2Q2026 (Exhibit 
9), which we viewed as reflective of a broad-based improvement in sentiment toward 
the asset class. In our view, concerns surrounding redemption activity in retail BDCs were 
(inappropriately) extrapolated to the institutional segment of the asset class, which 
represents the bulk of AUM. More broadly, the rebound in fundraising also likely 
reflected widening spreads on new private credit origination opportunities and potential 
tightening of terms and conditions—developments which are attractive from the 
lenders’ perspective. 
 
The experience of the past few years in the broadly syndicated loan market shows how 
the linkage between higher base rates and demand can be nuanced. Exhibit 10 captures 
two key measures of demand for broadly syndicated leveraged loans: fund flows into 
loan products, and issuance of collateralized loan obligations (CLOs) which buy 
leveraged loans and repackage them into securitized products. 
Notably, loan funds reported outflows during the 2022-2023 rate hiking cycle, as 
recession risk concerns (at the time) overshadowed higher yields on loans. Given the 
magnitude and speed of the rate hiking cycle during 2022-2023, there was also a more 
pronounced focus on debt service and default risks.
