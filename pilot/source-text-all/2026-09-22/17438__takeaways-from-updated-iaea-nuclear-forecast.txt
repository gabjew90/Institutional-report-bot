Takeaways From Updated IAEA Nuclear Forecast
Summary
The International Atomic Energy Agency ("IAEA") raised its long-term
nuclear power projections as we continue to see a bright future. There
was also a big step-up in expected SMR adoption. While the long-term
is positive, we are lowering PTs on reactor developers for higher costs of
capital and slower expected buildout over the next 5-10 years. This biases
us towards favoring fuel and supply chain linked players (BWXT, EU, LEU,
MIR, URG) who can generate nearer-term financial results.
Key Points
We cut PTs on IMSR ($15 to $8), SMR ($16 to $10) and NNE ($37 to $22) to
reflect higher costs of capital (i.e. higher terminal share count) and slightly
slower buildout in the 2030-2035 timeframe.
Our key takeaways from the IAEA's report are further discussed on the next
pages.
1. Forecast is built bottom-up and not to fit a target climate narrative (i.e.
net zero), which we think enhances credibility.
2. A key driver for positive revisions was the growing recognition of nuclear's
role in energy security and long-term economic growth.
3. By 2050, global nuclear power is estimated to increase to 641-1,045 GWe
from today's 377 GWe, a 2%-4% CAGR. Relative to last year's issue, the
high case was increased ~5% and the low case was increased ~14%.
4. North America was revised positively, including a 2050 low case that
shifted from contraction to growth.
5. Newbuild pace is picking up, albeit not at the levels needed to meet long-
term targets, but directionally heading the right way.
6. SMR's share of new capacity by 2050 increased y/y from 5%-24% to
23%-28%.
7. Within the fuel chain, we see positive implications for both enrichment
(Centrus) and uranium (EU, URG), but the uranium market has a brighter
outlook of the two, if forced to pick.

IMPORTANT DISCLOSURES AND ANALYST CERTIFICATION ON PAGE 4

• Important Framework Point: We think it is important to highlight that the IAEA's forecast is built with
a bottom-up methodology taking into account regional energy demand and regional/national nuclear
policies, but potentially accounting for emerging or assumed policies. Most critically, the forecast is not
based on hypothetical scenarios that target net-zero or 1.5°C/2°C projections, which we think make these
projections more credible/realistic.
• Energy Security a Key Theme: A key driver for the increased growth forecast was the growing
recognition of nuclear's role in energy security and long-term economic growth. We find this to be a
meaningful change from last year's report, where much of the justification for nuclear growth was its
role in addressing climate change. While its zero carbon attributes are compelling, we think the energy
security investment case is stronger and more resilient than climate-based investment, which can ebb
and flow with political regimes.
• Global Growth Revised Up - Again: The IAEA increased its longer term global nuclear forecasts for the
6th straight year. Importantly, the revisions are more material on the low-end case, which suggests a de-
risking of the long-term growth narrative, in our view. The tone change from last year's report to this year's
has seemed to move from cautious optimism of nuclear having a "moment", to now being at this moment,
with uncertainty shifting from "if" to "when". By 2050, global nuclear power is estimated to increase to
641-1,045 GWe from the current ~377 GWe, a 2%-4% CAGR. The high case was increased ~5% from
last year's 992 GWe while the low case was increased ~14% from last year's 561 GWe. Central and
Eastern Asia (including China) are the dominant growth driver over the timeframe, expecting to increase
from ~114 GWe today to 268-332 GWe, adding more capacity than any other region. The IAEA called
out an improved financing regime, including the World Bank, as part of the reason it raised the outlook.
Financing has historically been a major obstacle for new nuclear, especially in emerging economies, so
the financing solution is key.
• North America De-Risking Growth: North America was revised positively, including a low case that
shifted from contraction last year to now growth. Nuclear capacity is projected to grow from ~110 GWe
today to 136 GWe in the low case (up from 98 GWe last year) and 259 GWe in the high case (up from
236 GWe last year) by 2050.
• Newbuild Pace Increasing, Needs More Work: The pace of newbuilds is picking up, albeit not at the
levels needed to meet longer-term targets, but the world is directionally heading the right way. At year-
end 2025, there were 77.2 GWe under construction (up from 64.4 GWe in 2024) with 14.3 GWe beginning
construction in 2025 (up from 10.1 GWe in 2024). About 50% of capacity under construction is in China. To
meet the 2060 forecasts, gross additions need to average ~15-29 GWe/Year. We think it is encouraging
that we are already at the low-end pace.
• Rise of the SMR: We view the significant increase in SMR growth as a key takeaway from this year's
update. In last year's report, SMR market share of new nuclear capacity by 2050 ranged from 5%-24%
with low-end SMR builds a paltry 16 GWe. The new report estimates global SMR market share of
23%-28%, a higher and much tighter band, implying ~120-285 GWe. SMR market share is expected to
be highest in North America, with ~60% of new nuclear capacity coming from SMRs. This equates to
75-167 GWe of SMR capacity being added by 2060 in North America.
• Western World Enrichment Demand Growth Dictated by Europe: We use IAEA's forecast to imply
enrichment needs in the western world. We assume Chinese and Russian enrichment is generally not
available to the west and thus, only focus on nuclear power growth in North America and Europe. For
Europe, IAEA splits into 2 regions, one of which (Eastern Europe), includes Russia, so we exclude that
from our analysis. Remaining SWU demand in North America and Europe (excluding Eastern Europe)
is ~28.5 million SWU relative to current SWU supply of ~24.8 million SWU (per Urenco), suggesting
we are already in a deficit market. In IAEA's low case, SWU demand is expected to be relatively flat to
2050 as North American growth is offset by declines in Europe. The high case projects SWU demand
growing at a 2.8% CAGR to ~57 million SWU, implying a shortfall of ~32 million SWU that will need to

• Global Uranium Needs are Significant: Current global annual uranium demand is ~168 lb. On average,
0.4-0.5 million lb are needed annually per GWe. Since uranium is a more global commodity than

641-1,045 GWe by 2050, a 2%-4% CAGR. This implies annual uranium production needs of 288-470
million lb by 2050, which we think is a substantial ask from the industry, especially considering additional
investment is needed just to offset depletion of today's mines. Accordingly, we are bullish on uranium
prices that will be needed to incentivize additional supply. This is already playing out today with long-term
uranium prices at all-time highs at $96.50/lb, vs. the prior high of $95.00/lb in 2007.
