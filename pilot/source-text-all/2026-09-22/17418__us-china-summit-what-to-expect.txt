US Policy: Setting Expectations & Broader Framework  
 
Managed competition or competitive confrontation...defining the relationship: We 
think the overall US-China relationship will continue to be characterized by mutual 
dependencies underpinning a "managed competition" dynamic in the near term. That does 
not preclude the broader backdrop of competitive confrontation (that we discuss in more 
detail below), but we think reflects the nearer-term incentive to not dislodge the 
equilibrium that governs both sides' decision-making. Simply put, the US maintains a 
dependence on China for certain rare earths, while China continues to depend on the US 
for some of its chip exports. At the same time, both are slowly de-risking and building two 
distinct tech ecosystems (the "Two Worlds" divergence) that we discuss here and here,  
with important implications for AI sovereignty as we detail later in this section. That 
means we can continue to see tactical escalation within the broader pre-existing 
boundaries, as evidenced by recent policy actions (MOFCOM's June 22 rare-earth listings, 
the FCC humanoid-robot and inverter ban, and more). Hence, the goal heading into the 
summit we think is managed competition as a tactic, with competitive confrontation 
as a longer-term structure. That reflects the outcome of the May summit ("Limited Truce
+"), which saw selective cooperation in lower-sensitivity areas, incremental trade, and 
investment commitments well short of comprehensive agreements, with harder issues 
either deferred to a later date or narrowly managed rather than fully resolved. We think 
this upcoming meeting will be more of a follow-up to those commitments than the 
introduction of many new deliverables.
To that point, we see fewer deliverables outside of traditional trade commitments & 
the rare-earths truce extension (see more detail in the next section). The focal point for 
us is November 10, when three measures all expire simultaneously: 1) the BIS Affiliates 
Rule suspension, the pause on Chinese rare earths controls, and the 178 item Sec 301 
exclusion list. We expect both sides to prioritize rolling the deadline forward. On tariffs, 
we think the US likely prioritizes keeping China's effective rate above other trading 
partners (which is a strategic imperative, as we've argued in the past). 
Tail risks into the summit, outside of tactical escalation: Iran is the clearest variable, in 
our view. Treasury launched Operation Economic Outcast on August 24, threatening 
secondary sanctions on Iran's enablers (and laying out a clear escalation ladder), as 
Secretary Bessent explicitly declined to exempt China and later warned that Chinese 
banks intermediating Iranian oil revenue would be in scope. In our view (and as our China 
equity strategists describe below), the greatest tail risk would be the US targeting non-
USD based transactions. This is not our base case before the summit or in the immediate 
aftermath, absent a material change in the US-Iran conflict dynamics.
AI policy is on the agenda, but efforts are nascent. Institutional progress could be 
plausible because the dialogue itself was a deliverable from May. What does the US 
want? In this context, we see the US asking for cooperation on monitoring AI-directed 
cyber attacks and has floated asking US/Chinese labs to self-regulate and share threat 
information. The administration has also flagged concerns around Chinese distillation of 
proprietary US models (see more here) via  a public advisory. In that vein, we see the US 
prioritizing a "middle path" for restrictions that basically leaves the below-frontier

Two Worlds & AI Sovereignty
Our thesis, in short, is that as AI progresses into the national security arena, we 
see an increasing probability of both US and Chinese AI policy intervention, 
which could lead to a greater bifurcation of the global market. US restrictions 
likely lean toward controls over advanced chips and semi equipment as well as 
limits on cloud, compute access, procurement, and distribution, while we think 
Chinese areas of focus are more likely to be around regulation and local model 
integration/platform and app store access, for example. From a US perspective, we 
see a middle path that seeks to balance two objectives: 1) permitting a certain 
degree of open-weight models (for a discussion of the merits of open vs. closed 
weight, see here) to facilitate proliferation, model development, and access; and 2) 
safeguarding US frontier LLM IP. In our view, that could be potentially be achieved, 
for example, by prohibiting usage of certain Chinese AI model developers' models 
by US firms. Although technology restrictions and geopolitical tensions may create 
benefits for some companies to the detriment of others across the technology 
stack, duplicating AI capacity across the stack leads to a larger and longer-lasting 
global AI capex cycle. The biggest beneficiaries are likely to be infrastructure 
providers and companies that can enable localized AI deployment while 
maintaining compliance, security, and governance requirements.

China Economics: Deliverables & The Trade Relationship
 
The US-China relation has underdone another round of escalation/de-escalation cycle 
since 2025. The pivotal moment was the October 2025 Busan meeting, which arrested an 
escalation cycle involving triple-digit tariff threats, US technology restrictions and 
increasingly expansive Chinese rare-earth export controls. The outcome was essentially a 
one-year stalemate: Washington reduced some tariffs and suspended its penetrating 
Entity List rules, while Beijing suspended its second wave of rare-earth controls and other 
retaliatory measures. The May 2026 Beijing summit moved the relationship toward what 
we described as a “Limited Truce+”: the two sides established Trade and Investment 
Boards, announced some agricultural and aerospace purchases, and established a broader 
concept of “constructive strategic stability.”. On the other hand, little progress was made 
on the core strategic disputes around advanced semiconductors and critical minerals.
The consensus has largely held since Beijing, though interspersed with targeted 
escalations. China has further strengthened its strategic-mineral framework, and used 
entity listings and export controls against US defense, rare-earth and drone companies. 
The US has tightened restrictions around robotics, inverters and drones. That said, the 
agreement achieved in Busan remained intact. We think the relationship is increasingly 
characterized by sector-specific competition inside a broader understanding, echoing the 
mutual dependence embedded in the “chips-for-rare-earths” equilibrium. Both sides have a 
strong incentive to prevent narrow disputes from escalating into an economy-wide 
conflict. 
The upcoming Washington summit may be  a first attempt to validate and substantiate 
the "constructive strategic stability": Our base case for Washington is therefore an 
extension of managed competition rather than a grand agreement. 
• Some form of extension of the Busan consensus: Three key components of the 
Busan deal will expire on November 10:  178 US Section 301 tariff exclusions on 
Chinese imports, the suspension of the BIS Affiliates Rule, and China's suspension 
of its second-wave rare-earth controls. As November 10 falls ahead of the 2026 
APEC Summit on Nov 18-19, when the two Presidents will likely meet again, we 
think both sides have incentives to work towards more clarity on such an extension 
at the Washington Summit.  As a result, we would view a rollover, potentially with 
some modifications, as a base case. 
• On tariffs, we expect some targeted rollback, but broad tariff framework 
remains intact for now. The US tariff structure has already shifted from the 
legally challenged IEEPA regime toward more durable Section 301 and Section 232 
authorities, while China may tolerate certain US tariff levels provided they remain 
broadly consistent with the existing framework. Consequently, the more plausible 
deliverable is targeted reciprocal relief under the Board of Trade. Chinese and US 
teams are currently negotiating a framework covering roughly US$30bn of non-
sensitive trade on each side. This would be economically modest relative to total 
bilateral trade, but politically useful as it cements the "constructive" side of 
strategic stability, where tangible (albeit modest) results can be achieved without 
touching strategically sensitive areas. That said, potential Section 301 overcapacity

• We expect a modest "commercial package": We see scope for additional

The main risks to the current understanding, and potentially to the Washington 
summit itself, would arise if either side viewed the other's  measures as broadening 
beyond the implicit boundaries of managed competition. US arms sales have been 
established by Beijing as a clear red line. Should current non-tariff and technology 
restrictions broaden significantly,  the current equilibrium would be more difficult to 
sustain. For example,  the FCC has increasingly moved from restrictions on individual 
Chinese companies toward category-wide and component-level controls covering drones, 
routers, robotics and power inverters, while reportedly considering further restrictions on 
Chinese optical transceivers used in AI data centers. A further expansion into foundational 
technology and digital infrastructure supply chains could be viewed by Beijing as a 
broader tightening of US technology restrictions, potentially prompting reciprocal 
measures on rare earths, critical minerals, dual-use goods or other supply-chain 
chokepoints. This could test the current equilibrium.
Bottom line: Washington summit is a test of durability of current trade truce. Busan 
arrested further escalation; Beijing added a light layer of institutions and a modest 
commercial agenda; Washington needs to show the framework can survive despite 
targeted escalations and move beyond the original one-year expiry.

China Equity Strategy: Scenarios & Market Implications  
Laura Wang, Chloe Liu
The current trade truce expires on Nov 10. Both sides are reportedly treating an extension 
as "almost certain" but disagree on scope/duration. We believe that likely base-case 
deliverables include a rollover of China's rare-earth export restriction pause, continued US 
tariff suspension, more  purchases on agricultural/aircraft/energy sides, and further US 
tech-export approvals — assuming no serious trade disagreement occurs beforehand. 
That said, the latest cycle (FCC bans/forced-labor listings vs. Chinese export controls/
blacklists) illustrates how vulnerable the trade truce might be, which makes us believe 
that a scenario analysis of potential different outcomes and the implications on the 
market would be helpful for risk-reward analysis. 
We leverage the framework we designed in March ahead of the first Trump-Xi meeting 
during President Trump's second term (China Equity Strategy: US-China Presidential 
Summit: Scenario Framework & Investment Implications (15 Mar 2026)).  
What's changed vs. the original March (rescheduled to May) backdrop
• New sector in scope: Grid/power equipment. EO 14420 (Aug 26) adds a fifth 
"swing" sector alongside Semiconductors, Internet/Datacenters, Healthcare, and 
Materials — DOE's 120-day implementation window (~late Dec 2026) is a key 
signpost independent of the summit outcome itself.
• Iran-linked secondary sanctions risk is now a distinct escalation lever not 
present in the March framework, given China's role as the largest importer of 
Iranian crude and Treasury's explicit warnings about bank-level sanctions.
• AI competition is running on its own track and  has become a headline summit 
topic, not just an economic afterthought — Trump has said AI will be discussed, 
while a first-ever official US-China AI safety dialogue (led by Treasury Secretary 
Bessent) is being arranged around mid-September.
• Possible deliverables as the Bessent–He Lifeng pre-summit meeting confirmed: 
Treasury Secretary Bessent testified (Sep 15) he will meet Vice Premier He Lifeng 
this weekend (Sep 19–20) to discuss Iran sanctions, continuing their multi-city 
working-level channel (Davos, Paris, London, Seoul, Geneva). Bloomberg reports 
the two sides are discussing tariff cuts on ~US$30bn of goods (including US 
energy/agricultural exports and Chinese manufacturing inputs), pointing toward an 
extension of the truce rather than a breakage. AI cooperation could very well be 
another focal point.

Asia Thematic and Equity Strategy  
Daniel Blake  
Key Points
• From a Multipolar World perspective, the ongoing interdependence of the 
US and China economies helps preserve the current paradigm of managed 
competition. 
• The likelihood of either a grand agreement or a re-escalation look limited, 
given structural constraints and differing objectives.
• However, US and China efforts to reduce chokepoint dependence will 
continue for years, underpinning our Investment Super-Cycle outlook, 
alongside AI Capex and Defence Modernization. 
• Other Asia Pacific nations are generally looking to maintain economic 
optionality, pursuing opportunities in global value chains while 
strengthening national resilience. 
• We highlight opportunities in our global sub-themes of Economic Security & 
Reindustrialization and AI Sovereignty & Semi Localization. Our Critical 
Minerals sub-theme ranks less well on growth/valuations for the listed 
cohort, but we expect ongoing strategic support. 
• Key supply chains in focus - Rare earth elements and processing equipment, 
Indium phosphide & optical transceivers, High-performance computing & AI 
accelerators, High-bandwidth memory.
Stability Management, Steady De-Risking
Managed Competition the Default Option
Heading into a string of Presidential bilateral meetings through year-end (DC, Shenzhen, 
Miami), investors remain focused on how the US-China relationship may evolve. But 
increasingly, there is attention paid to the implications for the region, for example in the 
Takaichi Grand Strategy or Korea's Mega Projects. 
On US-China, we think the default option of stability management  will prove the most 
likely outcome. Two rounds of escalation in 2025 demonstrated the leverage available to 
both sides, with discretion ultimately proving the better part of valor on both occasions.  
As a result, we expect the US and China will bring limited ambitions to the September 
summit and subsequent meetings. Instead, relatively limited transactional wins in areas 
less sensitive to national security may be pursued through the forthcoming Board of 
Trade (eg agriculture, energy and commercial aircraft). 
On the AI competition and safety debate, we think the time is not ripe for exploring a deep 
partnership. Recent warnings of existential AI risks to humanity should ensure AI safety is
