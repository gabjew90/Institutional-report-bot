Two trades the summit cannot kill
The Trump-Xi summit will generate headlines around tariffs, technology and critical minerals. Markets
may initially trade the tone of the meeting, but the more durable opportunity lies beneath the
diplomacy.
Washington and Beijing may agree to manage the rivalry. Neither side can afford to stop preparing for
it.
Two expressions of that reality: own China’s domestic technology complex over its consumer internet
champions, and buy the critical-minerals supply chain after a significant reset.
China tech, not China consumption
KSTR versus KWEB separates two very different China exposures.
KWEB is principally a bet on large-cap internet platforms, the Chinese consumer and an improvement
in the broader regulatory and macro environment. KSTR provides exposure to the STAR 50:
semiconductors, advanced manufacturing, biotech and other companies central to China’s domestic
technology buildout.
That distinction matters going into the summit. A warmer relationship may help both, but strategic
competition in AI is unlikely to disappear. Even limited cooperation on AI safety does not remove
export controls, technological rivalry or Beijing’s incentive to localize the semiconductor stack.
The summit may improve the relationship. It will not make China abandon technological self-
sufficiency.

The leverage has not disappeared
Rare earths have become one of Beijing’s most effective negotiating tools. China controls most global
refining and processing capacity, while Washington still wants more reliable access to Chinese supply.
A summit agreement could temporarily reduce the scarcity premium, but it cannot eliminate the
strategic response. Governments and companies will continue spending on non-Chinese mining,
refining and supply-chain resilience because the events of the past year demonstrated the
vulnerability.
REMX has sold off sharply since peaking around the May Trump-Xi summit. The geopolitical
importance of the underlying assets has not declined with it. The ETF gives global exposure to miners,
refiners and producers of rare earths and strategic metals.
The summit may improve access to Chinese minerals. It cannot restore confidence in relying on a
single supplier.
The mineral left behind
Critical minerals sit at the intersection of the AI infrastructure buildout, electrification and national
security. Yet REMX has sharply decoupled from both semiconductors and copper.
The ETF peaked around the May Trump-Xi summit and has since fallen well below its 200-day moving
average. Over the same period, SOX and copper have remained considerably stronger. Investors
continue paying for the chips and the dominant electrification metal while largely discounting the
strategic-minerals supply chain surrounding both themes.
This is not a clean relative-value trade. A summit agreement improving access to Chinese supply could
initially pressure the scarcity premium further. But it would not reverse the longer-term spending
required to diversify mining, refining and processing capacity away from a single dominant supplier.
China’s control of rare-earth processing and Washington’s push for more reliable access remain
central to the talks.
The market has not abandoned technology or electrification. It has abandoned one of the least-owned
and most politically sensitive parts of the supply chain.

The trade logic
Own KSTR versus KWEB as the cleaner expression of China’s continuing technology self-sufficiency
drive. Own REMX as the more contrarian catch-up trade after the entire May summit premium was
erased.
One owns China’s response to US technology restrictions. The other owns the response to Chinese
mineral leverage. Different markets, same strategic rivalry.
