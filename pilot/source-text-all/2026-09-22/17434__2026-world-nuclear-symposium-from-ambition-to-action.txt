2026 World Nuclear Symposium 
Highlights From The World Nuclear Association Symposium: 
From Ambition To Action  
Our Conclusion 
From September 9 to September 11, the World Nuclear Association hosted 
its annual World Nuclear Symposium in London, U.K., which we attended. In 
this note, we summarize our key takeaways from the Symposium. 
The Industry Has Shifted From Advocacy To Execution: The clearest 
takeaway across the symposium was that nuclear energy has largely won 
the policy and market acceptance debate, with attention now turning to 
execution at scale. Speakers noted that the sector already has the key 
ingredients required for expansion: stronger government support, growing 
investor interest, rising electricity demand, and increasing recognition of 
nuclear's role in energy security and decarbonization. Panelists noted that 
success will depend less on technological breakthroughs and more on 
delivering projects on time and on budget, maintaining public confidence, 
and demonstrating that nuclear can be deployed repeatedly and reliably. 
Standardization, Industrialization, And Fleet Deployment Are Becoming 
The Industry Playbook: Large-scale nuclear growth will require the industry 
to adopt manufacturing-style deployment models built around 
standardization, repeatability, and long-term supply chain planning. 
Presentations emphasized that project-by-project customization increases 
costs, complexity, and delivery risks, while fleet deployment enables learning 
curve benefits, supply chain investment, and more predictable construction 
outcomes.  
Financing And Risk Allocation Are Improving, But Remain Critical 
Bottlenecks: Access to capital is becoming less of an obstacle than the 
allocation of risk between governments, developers, utilities, and investors. 
Discussions on newcomer countries, mainstream finance, and project 
delivery repeatedly highlighted that investors are increasingly willing to 
support nuclear projects, provided regulatory frameworks are stable, 
financing structures are well defined, and construction risks are appropriately 
managed. The launch of the World Nuclear Investment Guide reinforced the 
industry's focus on creating practical financing frameworks capable of turning 
project interest into actual final investment decisions. 
Supply Chains, Fuel Infrastructure, And Workforce Capacity Are 
Emerging As The Key Constraints To Growth: While industry demand 
and policy support have strengthened substantially, the greatest challenges 
now lie in building the physical and human infrastructure needed to support a 
nuclear build-out. Across both established and newcomer nuclear countries, 
speakers stressed that meeting global growth targets will require coordinated 
investment in people, industrial capabilities, and supporting infrastructure 
years before reactors enter commercial operation.

WNA Symposium Day 1: Finance Summit 
A Scalable Investment Framework Is Needed; WNA Investment Guide Launched 
The first day of the symposium focused on the financing aspect of nuclear supply with a $6T 
opportunity over the next 25 years to achieve targeted expansion of tripling current nuclear 
capacity of 423GW to 1,457GW by 2050 (called the Tripling Declaration, which was first 
launched ahead of COP28, in order to help countries achieve climate initiatives). The keynote 
speaker highlighted the challenges of financing nuclear projects, highlighting the 10-15 year 
timeframe to deployment, billions in capex, first of a kind and regulatory risk, as well as the 
benefits being largely public (decarbonization, energy security, sovereignty and employment). 
Speakers noted that the way to demystify investment into nuclear is to treat it like any other 
project. Investable projects reduce four of the five major kinds of risk (design, licensing, 
regulatory, supply chain), leaving the fifth risk (project execution) to be borne by the builders 
and investors. With that in mind, the WNA launched The World Nuclear Investment Guide to 
help establish a framework on what makes a good nuclear project.  
Nuclear Has Reached An Investment Inflection Point, But Financing Remains The 
Bottleneck 
A central theme throughout the Finance Summit was that nuclear energy has largely won the 
policy and public acceptance debate and is now increasingly viewed as a critical solution for 
energy security, decarbonization, industrial competitiveness, and rising electricity demand 
from AI and data centers. Panel attendees highlighted the scale of the opportunity, with 
estimates of up to $6T of investment potential across the nuclear value chain and annual 
capital requirements needing to rise from roughly $80B today to more than $250B per year 
through 2050. Industry leaders argued that financing has become the sector's primary 
challenge. The discussion consistently emphasized that nuclear must transition from being 
viewed as a specialized energy investment to a mainstream infrastructure asset class 
supported by transparent financial metrics, standardized project structures, and a larger base 
of institutional capital. 
Risk Reduction, Standardization, And Government Support Are Essential To Unlock 
Private Capital 
Attracting large-scale private investment depends on reducing and properly allocating project 
risks. Key concerns cited by investors included licensing, regulatory uncertainty, supply chain 
readiness, cost overruns, and project execution. Participants argued that mature designs, 
proven technologies, fleet deployment strategies, and structured risk-sharing arrangements 
can materially lower financing costs and improve investor confidence.  
Governments continue to provide primary support for nuclear initiatives in many countries, 
rather than sole financiers, with their role focused on absorbing early-stage development 
risks, providing policy certainty, and supporting first-of-a-kind projects until sufficient 
performance data and deployment experience allow private capital to take a larger role. The 
industry's path to lower financing costs is linked to replication, standardization, and the 
development of financing frameworks that can be applied across multiple projects. 
Next Phase Of Nuclear Growth 
Panelist indicated a clear need for the industry to shift from ambitious announcements to 
successfully delivering on new projects and extension of existing assets. Existing reactor life 
extensions, uprates, restarts, and new-build programs are the most immediate opportunities 
to expand nuclear capacity while SMRs progress toward commercialization. Corporate 
electricity consumers are becoming increasingly important in project development, with 
companies such as Google and Nucor demonstrating how long-term power purchase and 
offtake agreements can improve project bankability and support financing.

Launching The Nuclear Investment Guide 
The World Nuclear Association launched the World Nuclear Investment Guide as a practical 
resource designed to bridge the gap between the nuclear and finance communities, providing 
tools, frameworks, case studies, and best practices to help projects reach financial close 
more efficiently. Speakers emphasized that investor interest in nuclear has shifted 
significantly, with discussions now focused less on whether institutions will invest and more 
on when they will invest, reflecting growing recognition of nuclear’s role in energy security, 
climate goals, and long-term infrastructure development.  
WNA Symposium Day 2: Turning Ambition Into Action 
The opening remarks on Day 2 of the 2026 World Nuclear Symposium emphasized that the 
nuclear industry has entered a new phase where the focus must shift from ambition to 
execution. Speakers highlighted record industry momentum, including more than 1,000 
delegates from 50 countries, over 35 new World Nuclear Association members, record global 
nuclear generation in 2025, and more than 80 reactors under construction in 2026. The 
discussion stressed that growing energy security concerns, rapid electricity demand growth 
from AI and industrialization, and global decarbonization goals have led over 50 countries to 
include nuclear energy in their long-term energy plans. A recurring theme throughout the 
session was that achieving the goal of tripling global nuclear capacity by 2050 will require 
industry-wide collaboration, stronger cross-sector partnerships, and successful project 
delivery to maintain public, government, and investor trust. 
Ensuring Success In Deployment  
Panelists highlighted noted the marked positive change in sentiment around the nuclear 
industry. The industry now has the policies, financing frameworks, customer demand, and 
technology needed to scale nuclear globally, with the remaining challenge being disciplined 
execution while maintaining an uncompromising focus on nuclear safety. Panelist noted that  
optimal execution hinged on standardized reactor designs, repeatable project delivery (fleets 
vs one-offs), long-term visibility for supply chains, and capturing lessons learned to reduce 
costs, schedules, and risks over time. Participants highlighted the importance of creating 
integrated delivery teams, maintaining stable designs and long-term supply chain 
partnerships across projects, strengthening the role of the "intelligent customer," and 
establishing financing frameworks that provide greater certainty and risk sharing.  
The Multi-dimensional Nuclear Build-out Required 
The panel emphasized that achieving the industry’s goal of significantly expanding global 
nuclear capacity will require multiple pathways to advance simultaneously, including lifetime 
extensions of existing reactors, restarts, large-scale new builds, and SMR deployment, while 
maintaining strong operational performance and safety standards. Speakers highlighted 
tangible progress across the sector, including the Czech Republic’s move toward 80-year 
reactor operations and new build projects, Poland securing €17B in financing for its first 
nuclear plant and advancing plans for both large reactors and SMRs, and Urenco’s major 
enrichment capacity expansion driven by growing long-term fuel demand. Panelists stressed 
that international collaboration and shared learning are essential to avoid cost overruns and 
delivery delays. 
A Focus On Fuel, Standardization And Spent Fuel Management 
Interestingly, Orano noted the need for new technologies to standardize fuel across 
developers, warning that creating unique fuel types for every reactor design would undermine 
economic viability and slow commercialization. A major focus of the presentation was spent-
fuel management, with the speaker contending that the nuclear industry has a responsibility 
to address waste within the current generation rather than passing liabilities to future 
generations, while highlighting reprocessing as both a practical and economically competitive 
solution, noting that spent fuel should be viewed as a strategic resource for future nuclear 
fuel supply.

Regulators Should Be Taking A System-wide View 
The panel emphasized nuclear should be viewed not as isolated energy projects but as 
economic development platforms. Speakers highlighted the importance of regulatory 
harmonization, supply chain coordination, and workforce development, with suggestions 
including a “license once, review once, build many” framework to enable standardized reactor 
deployment. Examples from the UAE, South Korea, and China illustrated how collaboration, 
repeatable designs, and shared capabilities can lower costs, strengthen supply chains, and 
accelerate project delivery.  
The Cornerstone Of Delivery: Workforce, Leadership And Capability For The Nuclear 
Scale-up 
The panel argued that workforce capacity and leadership are becoming the critical 
constraints to achieving global nuclear growth, emphasizing that the industry will need 
hundreds of thousands of skilled workers and that workforce shortages are fundamentally a 
cost-and-schedule challenge requiring early action from both governments and industry.  
A Look At Nuclear Sentiment Across The Globe 
China’s Blueprint For Nuclear Deployment Focus On The Fleet 
Ma Yuanhua of State Nuclear Power Technology Corporation (SNPTC) outlined how China is 
translating ambitious nuclear growth targets into large-scale deployment, noting the country 
had 62 reactors operating (66GW) and 58 reactors under construction (69GW) as of July 
2026, with plans to reach 110GW of operating capacity by 2030 and 150GW by 2035. He 
identified four key challenges to fleet-scale nuclear expansion: balancing standardized 
designs with continuous improvement, supply chain constraints for critical components, 
managing multiple simultaneous projects, and shortages of specialized nuclear talent. To 
address these issues, SNPTC has adopted a fleet-based construction model centered on 
standardization, specialization, and centralization, emphasizing safety, engineering, 
procurement, construction, commissioning, and workforce development. Key performance 
targets include 80% standardized design reuse, a 56‑month construction schedule, factory 
equipment acceptance rates above 93%, and first-time success rates above 95% for both key 
construction processes and commissioning tests. 
TEPCO’s Nuclear: “Japan Is Back” 
Toshihiko Fukuda of TEPCO outlined the company’s post-Fukushima strategy, highlighting 
ongoing decommissioning work at Fukushima Daiichi. But with that said, Mr. Fukuda boldly 
announced that “Japan is back” returning to operation in February 2026 after a 14-year 
shutdown following extensive safety upgrades, emergency preparedness programs, and 
organizational reforms implemented after the Fukushima accident. Looking ahead, Japan’s 
Seventh Strategic Energy Plan has shifted policy toward maximizing nuclear energy use, 
targeting nuclear power to supply 20% of electricity by 2040 while supporting deployment of 
next-generation reactors and decarbonization goals. In response, TEPCO plans to restart 
additional reactors, resume construction of Higashidori Unit 1, evaluate advanced reactor 
technologies, and strengthen the nuclear fuel cycle and supply chain to support Japan’s long-
term energy needs. 
Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) 
Chan hyuk Jeon of Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) argued that the world is 
facing a dual challenge of climate change and rapidly rising electricity demand from AI and 
data centers. He warned that years of project cancellations and underinvestment have 
weakened the global nuclear supply chain and could create bottlenecks similar to those seen 
in the semiconductor industry if governments fail to provide stable, long-term nuclear policy 
support. To enable a nuclear renaissance, he outlined four priorities: consistent government 
backing for nuclear power, streamlined regulatory and licensing processes, greater

Powering Prosperity In Africa 
His Excellency, Faure Essozimna Gnassingbé, President of the Council of the Republic of 
Togo attended the symposium and delivered a key note address. Togo will host the third 
Nuclear Energy Innovation Summit for Africa (NEISA) in Lomé from June 1-3, 2027, and the 
President officially declared that Togo is signing on to the initiative to triple global nuclear 
energy capacity by 2050, positioning the country as an active participant in Africa’s growing 
nuclear agenda. The President noted that nuclear energy can play a critical role in 
strengthening energy security, reducing emissions, and supporting economic growth across 
Africa, particularly as the continent pursues industrialization, digital infrastructure 
development, urbanization, and improved healthcare systems. He pointed out the potential of 
emerging technologies, such as small modular reactors (SMRs) and microreactors, to provide 
tailored energy solutions for African countries, while emphasizing that Africa seeks 
partnerships and investment rather than technology handouts.  
Scaling Nuclear Energy: Rosatom In Action 
Rosatom argued that achieving the global ambition to triple nuclear capacity will require 
coordinated action across four key areas: project execution, supply chain capacity, workforce 
development, and financing. Drawing on its experience operating 33 reactors in Russia and 
managing an international portfolio of 45 units across more than 10 countries, the company 
emphasized that successful nuclear deployment depends on collaboration between vendors, 
customers, regulators, and host countries, particularly in newcomer markets where regulatory 
frameworks are still developing. The presentation highlighted the importance of expanding 
manufacturing capacity, localizing supply chains, and building a skilled workforce through 
international education programs, noting that Russian universities train over 2,000 
international nuclear students annually and that localization levels have reached 
approximately 30% in Bangladesh and 60% in Turkey. 
New Routes To Nuclear – Newer Jurisdictions 
The panel emphasized that successful nuclear deployment in newcomer countries depends 
on long-term political commitment, strong institutions, realistic financing frameworks, and 
human capital development rather than simply selecting a reactor technology. Examples 
provided were from Rwanda, Uzbekistan, and El Salvador, where governments are building 
regulatory capacity, training nuclear professionals, and following international frameworks to 
prepare for future nuclear programs. Participants stressed that financing remains a key 
challenge, with development banks playing an important role through capital, risk-sharing, 
policy support, and technical assistance, while cautioning against unrealistic expectations on 
costs, timelines, and emerging technologies such as SMRs. The discussion also underscored 
the importance of regional cooperation, knowledge sharing, and coordinated infrastructure 
planning to help countries reduce risks, strengthen energy security, and support long-term 
industrial and economic development. 
WNA Symposium Day 3: Fuel Cycle And Beyond 
Nuclear Growth Is Increasingly A Deployment And Supply Challenge, Not A 
Technology Challenge 
Across the sessions, speakers consistently emphasized that nuclear energy has entered its 
strongest growth environment in decades, supported by climate, energy security, and national 
security priorities. While long-term demand expectations remain robust, including forecasts 
for 1,457GW of global nuclear capacity by 2050, the industry's primary challenge is now 
converting momentum into completed projects.

Fuel Cycle Security Has Emerged As A Strategic Priority 
Expanding the nuclear fuel cycle is critical to supporting future reactor deployments. Panelist 
highlighted years of underinvestment in uranium mining, conversion, enrichment, and fuel 
fabrication, resulting in a structural supply deficit that could constrain growth. Developing new 
uranium supply is a lengthy process, with mines often requiring eight to 20 years to move 
from discovery to production, reinforcing the need for long-term contracting and earlier 
investment decisions. 
Regulatory Reform, Standardization, And Collaboration Are Essential Enablers of 
Growth 
Panelists noted the need to modernize licensing and regulatory processes while maintaining 
strong safety standards. Regulators highlighted efforts to streamline reviews, reduce 
unnecessary requirements, and increase international cooperation through joint technology 
assessments and reactor design reviews. Speakers highlighted recent reforms, including the 
U.S. NRC’s cultural and process changes under the ADVANCE Act, Finland’s major 
regulatory overhaul that reduces binding regulatory requirements from roughly 8,000 to 1,500 
requirements, and European initiatives such as ENGARD that aim to harmonize reactor 
design reviews and enable greater reuse of regulatory work across countries. 
New Sources Of Capital And Demand Are Expanding The Nuclear Investment 
Opportunity 
Hyperscalers, industrial companies, and large energy users are increasingly moving beyond 
power purchase agreements by investing directly in projects, sharing development risk, and 
helping support new deployment models. Demand growth from AI-driven data centers, 
industrial electrification, and emerging applications such as nuclear-powered shipping is 
creating additional long-term opportunities for the sector. At the same time, participants noted 
increasing support from governments, development banks, and private investors. 
Ensuring And Expanding The Future Fuel Cycle 
The panel focused on the opportunities and challenges of expanding the nuclear fuel cycle to 
support the global goal of tripling nuclear capacity by 2050, emphasizing energy security, 
supply chain resilience, and the need for investment across the fuel cycle. Scott Moe, 
Premier of Saskatchewan, highlighted growing electricity demand, supportive public attitudes 
toward uranium mining, and the importance of responsible regulation, skilled labour 
development, and community engagement to bring new uranium projects online, while 
industry participants noted that new uranium mines can take eight to 20 years to progress 
from discovery to production.  
Dastan Kosherbayev of Kazatomprom stressed that years of underinvestment have 
contributed to a structural uranium supply deficit and warned that producers will increasingly 
prioritize customers that provide clear demand signals and long-term commitments. He noted 
that eastern markets are backing nuclear growth ambitions with concrete action and said 
Kazatomprom would no longer turn down attractive eastern deals in anticipation of potential 
western demand. Meanwhile, Matsuda of Kansai Electric underscored Japan's nuclear restart 
progress, fuel cycle ambitions, and plans for new reactor construction through the 2040s and 
2050s.  
The Fuel Cycle Innovation Frontier 
The panel discussed how innovation across the nuclear fuel cycle, from uranium enrichment 
to recycling and waste management, will be critical to supporting long-term nuclear growth 
and reactor life extensions. Constellation Energy highlighted initiatives including plant 
uprates, extending reactor operating lives to 80 years and beyond, adoption of higher-assay 
low-enriched uranium (LEU+), and the deployment of accident-tolerant fuels to improve 
performance and safety. Industry participants emphasized the need for new enrichment 
capacity, fuel recycling, and international collaboration, with Orano stressing that

reprocessing can both reduce waste and improve resource efficiency, while Posiva pointed to 
Finland’s progress toward operating the world’s first deep geological repository as proof that 
long-term spent fuel disposal is achievable. 
Beyond Offtake: New Roles For Energy Users 
The panel discussed the evolving role of energy users, hyperscalers, and industrial 
companies in advancing nuclear deployment, highlighting how end users such as Amazon, 
Equinix, ExxonMobil, and others are moving beyond traditional offtake agreements by 
investing capital, sharing risk, and helping shape deployment models for new nuclear 
projects. Developers including X-energy and TerraPower emphasized recent milestones such 
as fuel fabrication licensing, construction progress, regulatory approvals, and partnerships 
that aim to make advanced reactors more repeatable, scalable, and financeable. Looking 
ahead, participants expressed optimism that increased customer involvement, growing 
demand from AI-driven data centers and industrial users, and continued progress on 
demonstration projects will accelerate commercialization and create a pathway toward 
repeatable nuclear deployment at scale. 
From Ambition To Action: Realizing The Tripling Nuclear Era 
The panel concluded that realizing a “tripling nuclear era” will require the industry to move 
from ambition to execution by focusing on financing, project delivery, and stronger 
collaboration across the nuclear value chain. Speakers emphasized the need for clearer 
storytelling around nuclear energy’s role in decarbonization, energy security, and economic 
growth, while maintaining a strong commitment to safety, innovation, workforce development, 
and diversity. A major theme was that access to capital remains the industry's biggest 
challenge, with participants highlighting the importance of de-risking projects, creating 
bankable investment opportunities, and improving cost and schedule predictability to attract 
private financing. The discussion also stressed the growing acceptance of nuclear power 
among governments, financial institutions, and energy-intensive industries, alongside the 
need for international cooperation, fleet deployment strategies, regional partnerships, and 
broader cross-industry advocacy to accelerate deployment worldwide.
