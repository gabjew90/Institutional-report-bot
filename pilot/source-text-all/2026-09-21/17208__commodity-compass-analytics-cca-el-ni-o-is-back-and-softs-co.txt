El Niño is back, and softs commodities are in the firing line 
 
◼ Already a record. In June, the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) breached the 1.5°C threshold, 
entering strong El Niño territory nearly two months earlier than in 2023, when the same 
level was only reached in September. Agricultural markets quickly turned their 
attention to the potential implications for next year's harvests. Since June 30, grain 
futures have risen by 18%, while softs have gained 14%. In our view, grain markets have 
been influenced primarily by summer US crop surveys and escalations near the 
Ukraine/Russia border. In parallel, both grains and softs likely benefited from spillover 
effects from higher energy prices, which rose by 34% over the same period. 
Nevertheless, soft commodities are far more exposed to regions historically affected 
by El Niño, particularly South America, Africa and Southeast Asia, whereas grains 
remain largely a US and China story, where El Niño's influence is generally weaker and 
can even be supportive of yields under certain rainfall configurations. 
◼ Beyond agriculture. While El Niño's strongest economic effects are typically felt 
through crop yields, it can also disrupt global logistics. Lower water levels have already 
constrained traffic through the Panama Canal, prompting the canal authorities to limit 
vessel drafts and cap daily transits. Year-to-date rainfall in Panama during 2026 
remains below 2023 levels, when El Niño-driven drought conditions severely restricted 
canal operations. Another important consequence is the increased risk of disease 
outbreaks. Studies show that ENSO-related climate anomalies, including shifts in 
rainfall and temperature, can create favourable conditions for diseases such as 
chikungunya, hantavirus, Rift Valley fever, cholera, plague and Zika to spread across 
regions including Southeast Asia, Tanzania, Brazil and the western United States. 
◼ Assessing the impact on supply. Against this backdrop, we focus on four key soft 
commodities, cocoa, cotton, coffee and sugar. By examining the historical relationship 
between ENSO, as captured by the Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI), and subsequent 
production outcomes, we assess what current climate conditions imply for supply in 
the 2026/27 marketing year. We find that the most severe disruption is concentrated in 
cocoa, where projected production declines in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana are large 
enough to generate a meaningful tightening of global balances. Cotton and sugar also 
exhibit negative supply responses, but the magnitude remains relatively modest, 
reflecting weaker weather sensitivity and greater resilience in producing regions. By 
contrast, coffee appears to benefit from current ENSO conditions, with production 
expected to remain above trend in both Brazil and Colombia. 
◼ Price impact. Translating these supply shocks into prices, our model points to gains 
across the softs complex, ranging from 6.5% for cotton to 34% for cocoa, with sugar 
(+7.5%) and coffee (+8%) falling in between. Overall, the results suggest that the 
current El Niño is likely to have a net bullish impact on softs, although the opportunity 
set remains highly concentrated, with most of the upside centred on cocoa. By 
contrast, coffee prices may also move higher despite a favourable supply impact from 
El Niño, reflecting the market’s greater sensitivity to broader softs-sector flows and 
investor positioning.

Meet ENSO: El Niño and La Niña 
The story of El Niño begins along the Pacific coast of Peru and Ecuador. During the nineteenth 
century, local fishermen observed that every few years unusually warm waters would appear 
near Christmas, replacing the cold, nutrient-rich waters that normally support one of the world's 
most productive fisheries. Because this warming typically occurred around December, they 
named the phenomenon El Niño, Spanish for "The Boy Child", in reference to the Christ Child. 
What began as a local observation is now recognized as one of the most influential climate 
phenomena on Earth. El Niño is the warm phase of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a 
recurring interaction between the tropical Pacific Ocean and atmosphere that affects weather 
patterns around the globe. Its counterpart, La Niña, represents the cold phase of ENSO and is 
generally associated with opposite climate impacts, although both phases typically develop and 
peak during the same seasonal window. 
Under normal conditions, trade winds blow from east to west across the tropical Pacific, pushing 
warm surface waters toward Indonesia and Australia while allowing cold, nutrient-rich waters to 
rise along the coast of South America. During an El Niño event, these trade winds weaken, 
allowing warm waters to spread eastward across the Pacific. During a La Niña event, the opposite 
occurs: stronger trade winds reinforce the accumulation of warm water in the western Pacific and 
enhance cold-water upwelling in the east. 
Because ENSO influences atmospheric circulation across much of the globe, its effects extend 
well beyond the Pacific Ocean. Changes in Pacific sea-surface temperatures can alter rainfall 
patterns, temperatures, tropical cyclone activity and agricultural conditions across multiple 
continents. As a result, ENSO is closely monitored by meteorologists, governments, commodity 
producers and financial markets alike. 
Although ENSO affects the entire Pacific basin, particular attention is paid to the Niño 3.4 region, 
located between 5°N-5°S and 170°W-120°W. Situated in the central equatorial Pacific, this region 
lies at the heart of the ocean-atmosphere interactions that drive ENSO. Temperature changes 
occurring there are often an early indication that broader shifts in global weather patterns may 
follow. From a historical perspective, current conditions are unprecedented. As of early 
September, sea surface temperatures in the Niño 3.4 region were approximately 2.9°C above the 
long-term average, surpassing the previous September record anomaly of 2.1°C.

Measuring ENSO: understanding the ONI 
The most widely used measure is the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), which tracks sea-surface 
temperature anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region. Rather than relying on individual monthly 
observations, the index uses a three-month moving average to smooth short-term fluctuations 
and focus on persistent changes in ocean conditions. 
The ONI is expressed as the difference between observed temperatures and their long-term 
average. Sustained positive anomalies indicate warmer-than-normal conditions associated with 
El Niño, while sustained negative anomalies indicate cooler-than-normal conditions associated 
with La Niña. In practice, values above +0.5°C generally signal El Niño conditions, whereas values 
below -0.5°C indicate La Niña conditions.

production and commodity markets. 
To put current conditions into perspective, top right chart compares the evolution of 2026 with 
the five strongest El Niño events on record. As of July 2026, the Niño 3.4 anomaly has reached 
approximately 1.8°C above its long-term average, the highest value ever observed for this stage 
of the year since records began in 1950 and ocean temperatures in the Niño 3.4 region are running 
above those observed during the build-up phases of the major El Niño episodes of 1982-83, 1997-
98 and 2015-16. 
Looking ahead, forecasts from the Climate Prediction Center indicate that El Niño is likely to 
strengthen further through the end of the year, with a greater than 90% probability of a very 
strong event during the Northern Hemisphere fall and winter of 2026-27. The agency further 
estimates a 75% probability that the event reaches a historically unprecedented intensity, 
exceeding the peak strength of previous El Niño episodes recorded since 1950. Taken together, 
current observations and forecast projections suggest that 2026 is tracking toward one of the 
most significant ENSO events of the modern observational era. 
These alarming forecasts justify a closer examination of the potential disruptions to agricultural 
production and supply chains.

Regional impacts of ENSO: where and when it strikes 
As discussed earlier, El Niño affects weather patterns well beyond the tropical Pacific through 
what is known as atmospheric teleconnections. As a result, some regions typically experience 
above-average rainfall and flooding, while others face below-average rainfall and heightened 
drought risk. 
The map below highlights these patterns, areas shown in blue are generally associated with 
wetter-than-normal conditions, whereas areas shown in orange tend to experience drier-than-
normal conditions. Although the magnitude and timing vary across events, these recurring 
patterns provide a useful guide to identifying regions most exposed to El Niño-related weather 
disruptions. 
 
• US Corn Belt (winter): wetter than normal. El Niño typically brings wetter conditions to the 
southern and eastern US during winter. This generally has a limited direct impact on corn 
yields, as the crop is dormant, but improved soil moisture recharge ahead of spring planting 
can be beneficial for the following growing season. 
• Brazil (spring): drier than normal. Central and northern Brazil, including major soybean and 
second-crop corn regions, often experience reduced rainfall during the spring planting period 
(September to December). Delayed planting can shorten the growing season and reduce yield 
potential, particularly if dryness persists into the flowering stage. 
• West Africa (summer to autumn): drier than normal. El Niño is frequently associated with 
below-average rainfall across parts of West Africa, including Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, which 
account for around 60-65% of global cocoa production. Dry conditions during pod 
development can reduce yields and increase crop stress, making cocoa one of the 
commodities most exposed to El Niño-related weather risks.

• Southeast Asia (second half of the year): drier than normal. Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand 
and neighbouring countries tend to receive less rainfall during El Niño episodes. This can 
negatively affect palm oil production through lower yields, reduce rice output where irrigation 
is limited, and increase stress on other crops including cotton and sugar. The impact is often 
strongest several months after the onset of drought because perennial crops such as oil palms 
respond with a lag. 
• China: a notable exception. Much of central and eastern China typically experiences wetter-
than-average conditions during El Niño events. This can be supportive for crops such as rice, 
particularly in southern regions, provided rainfall remains moderate. However, excessive 
precipitation can also increase flood risks and hinder harvesting, meaning the overall impact 
depends on the intensity and timing of rainfall. 
 
Supply impacts of ENSO across key agricultural commodities 
ENSO effects vary significantly across commodities and producing regions, creating both winners 
and losers in global agricultural markets. In the following sections, we examine the historical 
relationship between ENSO phases and supply conditions for four major crops, focusing on 
cocoa, sugar, cotton, and soybeans, and highlighting the key producing countries most exposed 
to El Niño and La Niña driven weather disruptions. 
Framework 
We test whether the El Niño or La Niña state can predict supply deviations from trend in the 
following year. To do so, we use the Relative ONI index, defined as the deviation of the ONI Index 
from its seasonal norm. This measure captures the difference between observed sea surface 
temperatures and their historical averages and is expressed in degrees Celsius. We then regress 
the June-July-August Relative ONI value against an indicator of next year's supply shock, defined 
as annual production of the two top producers relative to its trend over the previous five years.

This first step produces an expected supply shock, expressed as a percentage deviation from 
trend and potentially either positive or negative. In the second step, we regress this supply shock 
against subsequent 12‑month price returns. The resulting framework provides not only an 
estimate of the supply impact associated with El Niño or La Niña conditions, but also an 
indication of the potential price response. We now apply this approach across our set of softs 
markets. 
Cocoa: The Clear Loser 
Cocoa remains the soft commodity most exposed to ENSO-related supply shocks. The 
transmission channel is both direct and powerful: El Niño conditions tend to increase heat and 
moisture stress across key West African growing regions, disrupting flowering, pod development 
and tree productivity. Unlike annual crops, cocoa trees can carry the effects of weather stress for 
multiple seasons, amplifying the eventual impact on harvested output. 
Consistent with this mechanism, our framework identifies a pronounced negative relationship 
between ENSO intensity and next-year production. Based on current RONI levels, our model 
points to a significant downside risk for the 2026/27 crop year. Production could fall 14% below 
trend in Côte d'Ivoire and 20% below trend in Ghana, making cocoa the commodity with the 
largest estimated ENSO-driven supply disruption in our softs universe.

Price-wise, the implied supply shock is substantial. Applying our historical supply-to-price 
framework, the model points to a 34% increase in front-month cocoa futures, by far the 
largest price response across the soft commodity complex and consistent with cocoa's high 
sensitivity to weather-related production shocks. 
Cotton: Modest Supply Risk, Limited Price Upside 
Cotton is exposed to ENSO primarily through its influence on rainfall patterns across Asia's main 
producing regions. In India, El Niño tends to weaken monsoon rainfall, increasing the risk of 
moisture stress during planting and early crop development. China also exhibits a measurable 
relationship with ENSO conditions, although the transmission channel is less direct and output is 
generally less sensitive to climatic variability. 
Consistent with these historical relationships, our framework points to relatively limited 
production impacts from the current El Niño backdrop. Based on current RONI levels, cotton 
production in both India and China is projected to be around 4% below trend in the 2026/27 
crop year. While meaningful, these supply losses remain modest relative to those observed in 
more ENSO-sensitive commodities.

Price impacts are therefore expected to remain contained. Translating the estimated supply 
shock into market outcomes, our model implies an increase of approximately 6% in front-
month cotton futures over the next twelve months. 
Sugar: Resilient Fundamentals 
Sugar appears relatively resilient to ENSO-related disruptions. The main transmission channel 
operates through rainfall patterns in Brazil and India, the world's two largest producers. 
However, sugarcane's multi-year growing cycle and the geographic diversification of production 
help mitigate the effects of short-term weather shocks. 
Our model suggests only modest production deviations from trend in the upcoming crop year. 
Based on current ENSO conditions, Brazilian sugar output is expected to be around 3% below 
trend, while Indian production is projected to be roughly 2% below trend. These estimates 
point to a manageable reduction in global supply rather than a major production shortfall. 
The implied price response is correspondingly moderate. Based on the historical relationship 
between supply shocks and prices, our framework points to a 7% increase in front-month 
sugar futures over the coming twelve months. 
Arabica Coffee: A Relative Winner 
Surprisingly, Arabica coffee stands out as one of the few soft commodities that may benefit from 
the current ENSO environment. In several key producing regions, particularly Colombia and parts 
of East Africa, El Niño is often associated with drier conditions that can support flowering and 
reduce disease pressure, creating a more favourable backdrop for crop development. 
The statistical relationship captured by our model is positive. This means that based on current 
RONI levels, Colombian production could rise around 5% above trend, while Brazilian output 
is expected to run approximately 2% above trend in the next crop year. However, the recent 
earthquake in the El Cairo region of Colombia could offset some of the benefits associated with 
current El Niño conditions mainly in the form of transportation and export logistics disruptions. 
Despite the positive supply outlook, the historical relationship between supply surprises and 
prices indicates a potential 8% increase in front-month Arabica coffee futures. While this may 
appear counterintuitive, it suggests that supply shocks alone are insufficient to explain price 
movements. Broader market dynamics, including inventory levels, demand trends, and investor 
positioning, must also be taken into account. We also acknowledge that this result highlights the 
importance of the beta effect. As a relatively small market within the broader agricultural 
commodities complex, coffee is more sensitive to investor flows and broader softs market 
dynamics, resulting in a higher beta and greater price volatility. 
Conclusion 
 
The current El Niño backdrop points to a very supportive environment for soft commodity prices. 
Our framework suggests that cocoa, cotton and sugar are likely to experience supply losses 
relative to trend, while Arabica coffee stands out as the only commodity expected to see a supply 
increase.

interest. The height of each column represents the current construction ratio (CR) – the absolute change in aggregate open interest 
expressed as percentage of total volume traded during the week. A low CR could be a sign of heightened intraday trading activity. To 
provide some context for the (CR), it is compared to its one-year average, represented by the black dots in the chart.

Commodity Outlook (09 September 2026 – link) 
Trading blows, counting down 
Oil. Our base case scenario sees a narrow US-Iran agreement around the November 
midterms, followed by a more comprehensive resolution by year-end, progressively 
easing the current Hormuz-driven supply disruption. Our alternative scenarios 
comprise a stalemate until the end of the year on the one hand, and a prolonged 
escalation in tensions on the other. In our base case scenario, robust non-OPEC supply 
growth, the phased unwind of OPEC+ voluntary cuts and moderating demand, aided by 
rising EV adoption, should steadily rebalance the market even as inventories remain 
historically depleted. We expect Brent to average around $100/bbl in September-
October, with significant spikes highly probable, before gradually falling back in 
November-December toward $80/bbl. Next year, we see prices holding steady at 
$80/bbl, with a moderate decline in the second half to $77/bbl. 
Gold. We remain constructive on gold through 2027. Recovering retail demand, a softer 
US dollar and resilient physical demand continue to support the market, while supply 
growth remains gradual despite historically elevated prices. We expect gold to reach 
$4,750/oz by Q4 2026, continue appreciating through the first half of 2027 ($4,750-
5,000/oz), and surpass $5,000/oz during the second half of 2027, lifting the average 
annual price above $5,000/oz in 2027. 
Copper. We remain constructive on copper through 2027. While headline inventories 
appear comfortable, around 70% of exchange stocks remain concentrated within 
COMEX warehouses, leaving ex-US markets comparatively tight. At the same time, 
investment in power grids, electrification and AI infrastructure should keep demand 
growing faster than supply, while El Niño risks and increasing uncertainty around 
Chilean production threaten supply reliability. We therefore raise our 4Q26 target to 
$14,750/t and expect prices to trade around $14,750-15,000/t during the first half of 
2027, before rising further to $15,250-15,500/t by Q4 2027.
