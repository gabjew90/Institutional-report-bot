In Summary 
• The ongoing oil-price shock shouldn’t prove structural, with flows likely to normalize in 
2027. But several pressure points are pulling oil and gas prices in different directions 
and the balance will decide where prices go next. Brent jumped to nearly 110 USD/bbl 
after Saudi Arabia was forced to shut its East-West pipeline, pushing the net hit to Hormuz 
flows to about 4.3mbpd vs pre-war levels. For now, pressures points (including near historic 
lows strategic reserves and an underinvested refining base) are outweighing the two relief 
valves (shadow traffic in Hormuz and demand destruction). As we expect the Saudi 
pipeline to be repaired rather swiftly and volumes steady in Hormuz,  Brent should average 
87 USD/bbl in Q4 2026 before consolidating to 76 USD/bbl in 2027. But prices would hold 
near 100 USD/bbl if the outage extends toward six weeks and Hormuz remains in flux. 
• European gas prices are climbing as the region races to refill, though the resulting 
macro risk looks contained for now. Gas prices have rallied from the mid-40 EUR/MWh in 
July to over 80 EUR/MWh as Hormuz LNG loadings stay stuck at 15-25% of pre-war levels. 
We expect TTF averaging 65 EUR/MWh in Q4 2026 before easing to 36 EUR/MWh in 2027 
amid larger supply from both the US and the Middle East. A sustained move above 120 
EUR/MWh for six months would risk tipping the Eurozone into recession. 
• A separate refining and export bottleneck is keeping refined product prices elevated. 
US and European crack spreads have surged to 62 EUR/bbl and 74 EUR/bbl. On top of 
Hormuz disruptions, a significant share of Russia’s refining capacity has been hit by 
Ukrainian strikes, resulting in a diesel export ban. Until June, China also had export 
restrictions in place amid fears of supply shortages. A structurally underinvested refining 
base is adding to the multi-layered crisis. As a result, global oil demand is expected to 
contract 2.5mbpd in 2026 as demand from Asia has collapsed. 
• Diesel-reliant transport and agriculture, Asian petrochemicals and fertilizers are the 
most exposed sectors with very few short-term substitutes available, while producers 
with domestic feedstock flexibility are comparatively insulated. Between March and 
August, fossil-fuel importers paid an estimated extra USD330bn. High diesel prices are 
hitting trucking, agriculture and construction, and the Gulf’s 40% share of global sulfur 
exports is impacting food production via fertilizers. The near-term adjustment is mainly 
through inventory drawdowns and output cuts rather than genuine substitution. 
• Households are only starting to feel the full inflationary hit, and the pass-through will 
remain sticky into 2027. In the US, gasoline alone drove over a third of August’s CPI 
increase, with pump prices set to reach 3.84 USD/gallon in 2026 (+24% y/y, about 355 USD 
more per household annually). In Europe, regulated tariffs are catching up with a lag and 
lower-income households remain disproportionately exposed. 
• Bonds are already pricing in most of the energy premium, but sustained inflation and 
oil-price spikes could lead to further rate stress. Much of the initial repricing has already 
occurred, we estimate that a further increase in Brent to 120 USD/bbl would only lift yields 
by 5-10bps (vs 17-18bps on inflation swaps and 8-11bps on expected policy-rate pricing). 
This suggests that a significant further rise in yields would likely require a renewed large 
supply shock or evidence that inflation is delaying monetary-policy normalization. 
• Looking ahead, over the longer run, the unfolding events are fundamentally a 
Hormuz/Gulf shock that will reshape the global energy. The conflict is reshaping global 
energy trade routes: Gulf states are building out bypass infrastructure (ADCOP, the Kirkuk-
Ceyhan/Development Road corridor, Duqm) while China, India, Japan and South Korea 
diversify away from Gulf-transiting barrels; LNG has no equivalent bypass, so gas exposure 
remains structural.

Roller-coaster oil markets  
The recent attack on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline (Petroline), combined with stalled talks between Iran 
and Gulf states on Hormuz shipping, is compounding risks, but we expect the shock to fade. Brent has spiked to 
nearly 110 USD/bbl, widening an already-significant Persian Gulf supply gap (see Figure 1): despite lower Gulf 
production, exports proved more resilient, but the net hit to Hormuz flows still stands at about 4.3mbpd versus pre-
war levels. It remains uncertain when the Saudi pipeline will resume normal flows of 7mbpd - a mild scenario sees 
volumes ramp back to 5-6mbpd within weeks, while a worst case keeps the pipeline shut for about six week but we 
see this as a temporary shock either way.

OECD commercial stocks remain the main shock absorber, but that buffer is thinning fast. US Strategic Petroleum 
Reserve draws have slowed to just 0.3mbpd over the past month - reserves are now near their lowest level in 
decades, so Washington has little scope to repeat the scale of releases seen in 2022 or earlier this year even if the 
deficit widens further; while Japan and South Korea are rebuilding their own reserves rather than drawing them 
down. China’s crude imports, meanwhile, are only just restarting after months of weakness and remain about 
4mbpd below year-ago levels; refiners are taking advantage of firm margins and spare quota to buy and reprocess 
crude for re-export rather than to meet stronger domestic demand, so this is not yet a genuine demand recovery. 
Against this backdrop, we forecast Brent averaging 88 USD/bbl in Q3 and 87 USD/bbl in Q4 2026 before easing to 
76 USD/bbl in 2027 - but a deeper or more prolonged Saudi export disruption, without an offsetting reserve release, 
would keep the market in deficit for longer and raise the probability of Brent holding near 100 USD/bbl.

A natural gas squeeze looming over Europe? 
Natural gas prices are ticking up as Europe races to refill storage. European gas is exposed to the same Middle 
East disruption through a different channel: LNG loadings through Hormuz have remained stuck at 15-25% of pre-
war levels for two months, even as Qatar sends more empty tankers into the Gulf. We expect Qatari and Emirati 
LNG exports to normalize only in Q1 2027, so for TTF to keep pricing enough Asian LNG demand out of the market 
to free cargoes for Europe, prices must stay elevated for longer: the rally from the mid-40 EUR/MWh in July to over 
80 EUR/MWh has already cut some demand from the rest of the world. With Europe still playing catch-up on 
storage (see Figure 2), we expect TTF to average 65 EUR/MWh in both Q3 and Q4 2026 before consolidating to 36 
EUR/MWh in 2027 as Hormuz flows normalize and incremental US LNG supply arrives. The key near-term risk is 
storage: a cold winter combined with a lower-than-usual fill could deplete storage before spring, front-loading price 
pressure into early 2027.

For now, the macro risk from gas prices looks contained. In 2022, gas prices averaged close to 130 EUR/MWh for 
a full year, yet the Eurozone still grew about 3.4%, cushioned by a post-pandemic demand boom and a fiscal 
response worth an estimated 0.4pp of growth. Today’s backdrop is less forgiving: fiscal space has narrowed and 
household demand is far less dynamic. We think TTF in the 80-100 EUR/MWh range could be absorbed for nine 
months or more without a growth shock, but a sustained move above 120 EUR/MWh for six months or longer would 
tip the Eurozone into recession. Our base case, TTF averaging 65 EUR/MWh through Q3 and Q4 2026, does not test 
that threshold, though a winter upside scenario would keep the tail risk alive. 
Refined products to remain expensive  
A two-layer infrastructure bottleneck is hitting refined products and will keep driving regional prices up, 
independent of crude. In August, Brent averaged 89 USD/bbl, yet clean-product crack spreads surged to record 
levels, the US benchmark reached 62 EUR/bbl and European diesel crack 74 EUR/bbl (68% and 92% above their 
five-year averages) showing the main story is refining availability, not crude input costs. Three regions are driving 
the crunch. In the Middle East, the US-Iran conflict and Hormuz blockade have hit both refinery operations and 
product exports: pre-conflict flows of roughly 6mbpd through the Strait have fallen to 1.1mbpd, and the Saudi 
pipeline attack compounds this by also halting flows to the Yanbu refineries. In Russia, Ukrainian strikes cut refinery 
runs to multi-year lows by mid-May, and Moscow’s diesel export ban - extended through September, with no 
guarantee against further extension - has squeezed a market that depends on Russia for 11% of global diesel. In 
China, export restrictions imposed in March to preserve domestic inventories cut clean-product exports by 24.3% 
year-on-year over the first five months, though Beijing’s June release of 13mn tons should ease global supply, lifting 
China’s exports full-year total to 32mn tons, 0.6% above last year.

Global clean-product inventories are drying up as the crisis extends through 2026. European (ARA) inventories 
are well below the five-year average. In the US, refiners are running at record utilization (98% in September), but 
most of the extra output is being exported for higher margins rather than sold domestically — gasoline stocks are 
6% and diesel 14% below seasonal norms, and US diesel topped 6 USD/gallon for the first time. Rising domestic 
prices could eventually pull barrels back into the US market and curb these exports, with some early – tough strongly 
pushed back by government officials for now - talks about potential diesel export restrictions to support domestic 
supply. Additionally, there is little relief from spare capacity: years of underinvestment and refinery closures have 
left the West structurally unprepared, global refining investment is on track to fall below 40 USD bn this year (-15% 
y/y), and capacity is set to grow only marginally through 2040. Unlike 2022, when Europe and Asia reallocated 
capital toward LNG regasification within months, refining capacity cannot be added quickly enough to ease this 
shortage in the short or medium term, and rebuilding inventories will take time even once Hormuz flows normalize. 
We now expect world oil demand to contract by 2.5mbpd in 2026 as high product prices force further demand 
destruction, concentrated in middle distillates and petrochemical feedstocks in Asia. Longer term, persistently 
expensive products should accelerate substitution toward EVs and heat pumps, and put a premium on refineries 
with the flexibility to shift crude grades and product mix.

Downstream sector impact and potential substitution 
After six months, duration, and not just the peak price, is now the central macro risk. Between March and August, 
fossil-fuel importers paid an estimated 330 USD bn more than pre-war forward prices implied, split roughly evenly 
between crude and refined products - a real income transfer from importers to exporters that squeezes household 
purchasing power and corporate margins, weakens currencies, and strains public finances wherever governments 
subsidize fuel. The Hormuz crisis is unusual in disrupting many linked-but-hard-to-substitute commodities at once 
(crude, gas, LPG, naphtha and sulfur) spilling into fertilizer and petrochemical shortages. Substitution can happen 
at three levels : (i) switching energy carriers, (ii) switching industrial feedstocks, or (iii) consumers switching final 
products. With the first working fastest but simply redistributing scarcity, and the other two developing more slowly 
but reshaping industrial structure permanently. Inflation itself spreads in three stages: a fast first-round hit to 
energy-intensive industries (speed depending on taxes and subsidies), a slower production-cost effect as old-price 
inventories and contracts run out, and a second-round effect as wages, inflation expectations and price-setting 
behavior adjust. 
The immediate impact is on energy-intensive industries. Gulf states are major exporters of both fuels and 
feedstocks: Europe is less directly exposed to Gulf crude than East Asia but still feels it through global oil prices, LNG 
competition and product shortages; the US is more insulated on crude but exposed via global product prices, hitting 
lower-income consumers hardest while producers and refiners gain from higher margins. East and South Asia face 
the largest physical exposure, with China, India and Japan recording the highest net additional fossil-fuel import 
costs since the conflict began – USD 31.7 bn, USD 14.4bn and USD 10.4bn respectively. Diesel is the most powerful 
transmission channel given its role in trucking, agriculture, mining and construction - sectors that are hard to 
electrify, especially for long-haul transport and in emerging markets - while shipping and aviation face higher 
bunker/jet-fuel costs and rerouting. Among industrial inputs, East Asian petrochemical crackers reliant on imported 
naphtha are most exposed, whereas the US benefits from domestic ethane and China retains partial cover via coal-
based chemicals; metals, cement and glass are exposed through electricity and high-temperature heat costs, hitting 
aluminum hardest. Gulf countries supply more than 40% of global sulfur exports and about 20% of 
ammonia/nitrogen-fertilizer exports, so the crisis strikes food production through fertilizer, diesel, irrigation and 
packaging costs simultaneously. 
The second-round effect on the broader economy will be felt with a lag and last longer. Food shows this best: 
packaged food embeds fertilizer, farm fuel, irrigation, refrigeration, processing heat, packaging, transport and 
labor, each repricing at a different speed : fresh produce reacts within weeks, grain can take up to a year via fertilizer 
and harvest effects, and restaurant prices adjust slowest but most persistently as labor and rent costs catch up. 
Large retailers can hedge and absorb costs better than small ones, and real household income falls as consumers 
trade down and defer spending. Export restrictions, subsidies and price controls can temporarily shield domestic 
food prices but tend to redistribute scarcity and worsen the burden on lower-income, food-importing economies.

Construction and real estate are hit both directly (cement, plastics, metals, diesel) and, more durably, indirectly 
through higher interest rates raising financing costs and lowering the present value of future rents. Compared with 
2022, the economy enters this shock with more renewable capacity and European LNG infrastructure - renewables 
installed since 2020 reportedly avoided USD 36bn of fossil-fuel imports in the first five months of the crisis - but 
public debt is higher and buffers are thinner after the pandemic and the 2022 crisis. 
Substitution will spread across fuels, feedstocks and products but initially redistributes inflation rather than 
eliminating it. The first response is inventory drawdowns, switching to costlier available inputs, output cuts and cost 
pass-through. Over time it becomes more structural, reorganizing downstream markets around flexibility, lower 
energy intensity and redundancy - persistently expensive oil and gas improve the economics of electrification, even 
though electrification changes commodity dependence rather than removing it. Because capital, regulatory 
approval and margins all constrain the pace of change, expect a two-stage adjustment: curtailment and demand 
destruction first, structural substitution and capex later, alongside rising demand for fuel/feedstock flexibility even 
where it costs more than a specialized, single-fuel plant.

Households to face higher inflation 
Six months in, households are losing purchasing power in stages. Petrol and diesel reprice within days or weeks, 
but gas and electricity bills follow regulated tariff calendars and contract-renewal dates, so the current inflation 
reading is not yet the peak. Exposure varies sharply by household - an EV- and heat-pump-owning homeowner is 
comparatively insulated, a gas-heated renter won’t feel the full effect until tariffs reset, and a rural household reliant 
on diesel cars feels it immediately. Petrol and diesel are the fastest channel: European pump prices typically pass 
through crude costs within one to two months, roughly one-for-one. In the US, gasoline alone drove over a third of 
August’s CPI increase, diesel peaked above 6 USD/gallon in September, and average pump prices are on track for 
3.84 USD/gallon in 2026 (+24% y/y) - about USD 355 more per year for a typical household; hitting rural, low-income 
households hardest given limited access to public transport. 
Gas and electricity bills rise on institutional timetables, so the pass-through is felt with a lag that varies by 
market. In France, the regulated gas reference price rises 6.3% on October 1 (to 182.88 EUR/MWh including tax, 
about 60 EUR/year for indexed contracts), though roughly 40% of households are on fixed-price contracts and won’t 
feel it until renewal; French electricity moves more slowly, with the next adjustment scheduled for February 2027. 
The UK’s quarterly price cap rises by about 5 GBP/month from October, partly offset by government VAT relief, 
while roughly 35% of households on fixed contracts are shielded for now. Japan and India rely more on subsidy 
calendars than market pass-through, which smooths the household shock across time without removing its fiscal 
cost. More broadly, real energy bills were still about 4% above 2019 levels in 2024, so this shock lands on budgets 
that had not fully recovered from 2022; and unevenly: an estimated 120mn households in advanced economies 
spend over 10% of income on home energy, rising to 20% including transport for the poorest, and the bottom income 
quintile spent 12% of disposable income on gas, electricity and heating in 2022 versus 4% for the top quintile.

In the longer term, the energy crisis impacts inflation beyond energy bills. A second inflationary wave builds as 
transport and industrial costs feed into food, goods and services with a lag of around two years: a permanent 10% 
rise in oil prices raises headline CPI by about 2.3%, food CPI by 0.3% and core CPI by 0.1%, and after expenditure 
weights, headline CPI rises by almost 0.4% - consistent with the roughly 0.5% inflation contribution the last oil move 
added in the Eurozone, UK and Canada. Looking ahead, households are likely to weigh energy resilience more 
heavily in purchasing decisions, shifting spending from variable fuel costs toward upfront capex on electrification 
and insulation - even as higher prices simultaneously squeeze the ability to finance that switch, widening inequality 
in energy exposure. Electricity is set to become the central household energy price, trading oil and gas exposure for 
exposure to power prices, network charges and time-of-use pricing, with government subsidies and price-cap 
calendars still deciding when the crisis is actually felt.

The energy-yield loop 
Energy shocks often have an immediate effect on rates through several components. The first one is inflation 
expectations: Higher commodity prices tend to increase inflation and are priced directly on the derivatives market. 
Historically a 1% rise in Brent lifts the 2Y inflation swap by 1.65bps in the US and 1.77bps in the Eurozone, and the 
1y1y forward inflation swap by 0.64bp in the US and 1.09bp in the Eurozone. During special shocks the effect 
intensifies: The US 2Y inflation swap moved 1.86bps per 1% of oil during the Ukraine shock and 2.01bps during the 
Iran episode. The EU 2Y inflation swap moved by 2.60bps during the Ukraine shock, but only 0.36bp during the Iran 
episode. 
The first channel then impacts the expected short rates path. When inflation tends to increase suddenly, the 
central bank faces more pressure to be hawkish because of market pricing. Historically a 1% rise in Brent adds 
1.02bp to US monetary pricing (proxied by 2Y OIS − Fed Funds) and 0.83bp to EUR monetary pricing (proxied by 2Y 
OIS − €STR). During crises, this pressure concentrates region by region: US pricing repriced hardest during the 
Ukraine shock at 2.82bps per 1% of oil, close to three times its long-run sensitivity, while in the current Iran episode

Bond markets have already absorbed a large portion of the current energy shock through higher inflation 
compensation and a repricing of the expected monetary-policy path. While additional oil-price gains would still 
exert upward pressure on rates, the scenario analysis suggests that the marginal impact is now smaller than during 
the initial phase of the shock. As a result, a sustained and significant further rise in yields would likely require a new 
escalation in supply disruptions or clearer evidence that energy-driven inflation is feeding into broader price 
dynamics and delaying the normalization of monetary policy.  
How the conflict is reshaping the global energy map 
The Strait of Hormuz has always been the Gulf’s structural vulnerability, and this crisis is accelerating a shift 
from managing chokepoint risk to actively engineering around it. The attack on Saudi Arabia’s own hedge (i.e.  
Petroline) is a reminder that existing bypass infrastructure is now a target, not a guarantee. Riyadh’s repair timeline 
will determine how quickly that redundancy returns, but the episode has already revived talk of expanding capacity 
rather than just repairing it. The UAE’s ADCOP pipeline to Fujairah, which sits outside the Strait entirely, remains the 
cleanest working bypass and has absorbed a large share of redirected flows, with further capacity additions 
planned for 2027. Iraq is leaning on the Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline and the broader Development Road corridor to 
Türkiye, while Oman’s Duqm port is the other candidate for a Gulf pipeline that bypasses the Strait altogether.

There is no easy fix for natural gas. Unlike crude, LNG cannot be pipelined around Hormuz, which is why Qatari 
and Emirati LNG loadings have collapsed even as oil exports partially recovered via redirection. With normalization 
pushed out to Q1 2027, Qatar’s pattern of sending more empty tankers into the Gulf is one of the few signs of an 
eventual ramp-up, but there is no Petroline-equivalent for gas and the exposure is structural. 
On the demand side, the pressure to de-risk predates this crisis — it dates back to the war in Ukraine — and is 
now intensifying, though Russia/Ukraine plays no direct role in the current Hormuz shock beyond the refined-
products channel already noted. China has spent the past decade building overland alternatives (the ESPO 
pipeline from Russia and the Kazakhstan-China link) specifically to cut seaborne Gulf and Malacca dependency, 
and its crude imports remain about 4mbpd below 2025 levels even at firm prices — though the recent uptick in 
Chinese buying is a refining-margin and re-export story more than fresh demand. India is diversifying toward US, 
West African and discounted Russian barrels via the Cape route while expanding strategic reserves, and Japan and 
South Korea are rebuilding SPRs drawn down earlier in the crisis. The India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor 
is likely to see fresh political momentum as a longer-term, non-maritime link between the Gulf and Europe. None of
