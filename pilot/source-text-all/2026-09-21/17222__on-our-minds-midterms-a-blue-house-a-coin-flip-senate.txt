The national environment looks like a wave year, but the Senate map forces Democrats 
to win in battleground red-leaning states. Prediction markets are currently favouring 
Democrats to flip four of the competitive races and win control over Senate, although the 
SG model with a broader range of inputs is less certain about this outcome. Energy prices, 
tariffs, data centres and AI are all key issues that will determine control for the Senate. 
 
2026 U.S Midterms 
As we approach Election Day on November 3, the race for control of Congress is entering its final 
stretch. All 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are on the ballot, including special elections in 
Florida and Ohio. Republicans hold a 53–47 Senate majority. They are defending 22 of the seats 
up this year, while Democrats are defending 13. To win the Senate, Democrats need a net gain of 
four seats, because the Vice President breaks a 50–50 tie. Given a much slimmer 218-214 
Republican advantage in the House, Democrats need to flip a net of just three House seats in the 
fall to take back the House.  
Historically, midterms have acted as a check on the party that holds the White House. Since the 
World War II, the president’s party has lost House seats in all but two midterm (1998 and 2002). 
Some shifts have been large: Republicans gained more than 50 House seats in 1994 and 63 in 
2010, and Democrats gained around 40 in 2018. The Senate follows a different pattern, because 
only a third of seats are contested and the result depends heavily on which states are on the map. 
In 2018, the president's party gained Senate seats even while losing the House. 
Given that the very narrow Republican majority in the House provides ample opportunity for the 
Democrats to flip that chamber of Congress, the Senate races have become key to the outcome 
of this year’s midterm elections. This year's map leans toward states that have voted Republican 
in recent presidential elections, which gives Republicans a structural cushion in a challenging 
national environment. With cost-of-living concerns dominating voters’ views and several races 
tied within the margin of error, however, both chambers remain in play, with many political 
pundits predicting a blue wave. As of September 17, prediction markets and forecasters suggest 
a highly competitive Senate battle, with Kalshi implying roughly a 43% chance of Republican 
control, Polymarket about 42%, and the Cook Political Report rating the chamber a toss-up, while 
two national generic-ballot polls released this week showed notable Democratic momentum, 
including a New York Times/Siena College survey with Democrats ahead by 8 points and an 
Economist/YouGov poll showing a 12-point Democratic advantage.

As of this week, the SG election model suggests we’re in for a very tight race: there’s a 44% 
probability we’ll end up with Democratic majorities in both chambers of Congress and a 43.3% 
chance we’ll have a divided Congress based on a Democratic House (left-hand side chart above). 
This is close to what prediction markets have been suggesting this week. There are, however, 
nuanced differences when focussing on the nine key Senate races – see the right-hand side above. 
For example, for Texas our model is less optimistic the senate seat there will end up in Democratic 
hands compared to prediction markets. 
The difference between the prediction markets and the SG model can be attributed to the inputs 
and weighting that feed into the margin calculated by the model. The internal model takes a 
greater weighting for the polling data compared to the weighting for prediction markets 
depending on the abundance (or lack of) polling data and the liquidity of the contracts being 
traded on prediction markets, whereas markets can move based on a variety of factors (one of 
which is the polling data).

Another highly watched race is Texas. It is shaping up to be one of the most expensive Senate 
races of this year’s midterms, with Republican groups now spending more than $50 million on 
Ken Paxton after months of hoping he could defeat Democratic candidate James Talarico. For a 
consistently red state, the race remains remarkably close, with recent polls showing a tight 
contest and some even putting Paxton (R) behind. Given the diminishing impact of funding on 
polls, Trump’s unpopularity among Texas Hispanics, and voters’ affordability concerns, 
Democrats maintain a narrow lead so far. Both prediction markets (right map above) and the SG 
model (left map above) point to a close race, though the SG model still considers the state to be 
more of a tossup because it incorporates older polls with lower weighting, while prediction 
markets react more heavily to recent polling and investor sentiment. With Republican donations 
surging and Donald Trump continuing to back Paxton, it will be interesting to see whether the 
probability gap between Republicans and Democrats winning the seat begins to narrow.

Comparing the likeliest scenarios of a full Democratic Congress and a divided Congress with 
Democratic House in the table, generally the differences are nuanced. In both cases the 
administration’s fiscal policy discretion will be heavily hampered, although with a Democratic 
sweep of Congress uncertainty in this area likely will end being more severe, including a higher 
likelihood of government shutdowns. The biggest difference between these two scenarios will 
play out when it comes to new appointees for the Supreme Court and the Federal Reserve: here 
the administration will, in principle, have a lot less room for maneuvering if both chambers of 
Congress turn blue.

several races at once. While local dynamics will matter, there are a few key issues that stand out 
nationally such as affordability, foreign policy, data centers, healthcare, and AI. Recent polling

The Iran conflict and energy prices. Energy costs have become a visible part of the cost-of-living 
debate following the escalation of the Iran conflict earlier this year. Concerns over disruptions to 
shipping through the Strait of Hormuz initially pushed gasoline and diesel prices higher, feeding 
into transportation, food, and other household expenses. While some of the earlier price pressure 
eased during ceasefire negotiations, renewed tensions have kept energy markets volatile. Given 
the link between fuel costs, inflation, and consumer sentiment, energy prices remain an 
important factor in how voters assess the economy, particularly in rural and transportation-
intensive states. 
Tariffs, trade, and affordability. Affordability remains one of voters' primary concerns after 
several years of elevated prices. The renewed US-Canada trade dispute has added another layer 
to that debate, with retaliatory tariffs raising costs for a range of consumer goods, industrial 
products, and agricultural inputs. While tariffs are often discussed through a geopolitical lens, 
voters are more likely to experience them through higher prices and supply-chain disruptions. 
The issue is especially relevant in agricultural and manufacturing-heavy states that are directly 
exposed to cross-border trade. 
Data centers and the AI buildout. Investment tied to artificial intelligence continues to support 
economic growth, capital spending, and construction activity. At the same time, the rapid 
expansion of data centers has become a local political issue in many regions as rising electricity 
demand contributes to higher utility bills and increased grid investment. Public opposition to 
new projects has grown in several states, turning data centers into a broader proxy for concerns 
over AI, infrastructure costs, and the distribution of economic benefits. The issue is particularly 
visible in states with large concentrations of data-center development. 
Recent New York Times/Siena College polling highlights how these concerns translate into voter 
perceptions of party competency. Democrats maintain their largest advantage on healthcare,

Assessment 
The outcome of 2026 midterm elections remain a tight race between the two main political 
parties when it comes to control of the Senate, while the House is almost certain to revert back 
into Democratic hands. When we aggregate projections from analysts, polls and prediction 
markets and considering historical prediction uncertainty in our model, it currently remains a 
close call between a Democratic sweep of both the House and Senate vs a divided Congress with 
the House under Democratic control. Going forward we will publish weekly updates from the SG 
election model up until the week of the midterm elections. 
 
 
Appendix: SG Election Model Methodology 
The SG election model shows the most probable balance of power in Congress to be a Republican 
controlled Senate and a Democrat controlled House. Given the close Senate election, the model mainly 
focuses on simulating the 9 most competitive Senate races (as categorized by Cook Ratings) by taking 
inputs from three sources: Cook Political Report, NYT Polling Data, and prediction markets (Kalshi and 
Polymarket). 
 
The Cook and polling data is weighted respectively based on the time to election and the sparsity of the 
polling data so that as the election draws closer, the polling data is given more weight compared to the 
Cook ratings. The formula below shows this relationship. 
𝛼ℎ𝑦𝑏𝑟𝑖𝑑𝑖= 𝑤𝑃𝑖× 𝑃𝑜𝑙𝑙𝑀𝑎𝑟𝑔𝑖𝑛𝑖+ (1 −𝑤𝑃𝑖) × 𝐶𝑜𝑜𝑘𝑃𝑟𝑖𝑜𝑟𝑀𝑎𝑟𝑔𝑖𝑛𝑖 
The combined margins of Cook and NYT data are then weighted relative to the margin calculated through 
the prediction markets. The model takes into consideration the possibility of national and race-specific 
shocks through Monte Carlo simulations with the shock distributions calibrated using forecast errors based 
on historical polling misses. The national shock is drawn once and applied to every competitive Senate race, 
representing a nationwide polling miss of political wave that moves all races in the same direction. The 
race-specific shock captures local factors such as candidate quality, campaign dynamics, or state level 
polling errors. The hybrid margin calculated through the weighted inputs and results captured given each 
simulation’s national and race-specific shock leads to the probabilities shown above. More specifically, the 
hybrid margin is calculated as: 
𝜎𝑡𝑜𝑡𝑎𝑙𝑖= √𝛽𝑖
2 × 𝜎𝑁2 + 𝜎𝑖2 
𝛼𝑚𝑎𝑟𝑘𝑒𝑡𝑖= 𝜎𝑡𝑜𝑡𝑎𝑙𝑖× 𝜙−1(𝑃𝐾𝑎𝑙𝑠ℎ𝑖𝑖) 
𝛼ℎ𝑦𝑏𝑟𝑖𝑑𝑖= (1 −𝑤𝐾𝑖) × 𝛼𝑠𝑡𝑟𝑢𝑐𝑡𝑖+ 𝑤𝐾𝑖× 𝛼𝑚𝑎𝑟𝑘𝑒𝑡𝑖 
where sigma_N and sigma_i represent the national and idiosyncratic shock, respectively. 
Output: Each simulation produces a winner in every race and party control of each chamber. The resulting 
probability is the share of simulations in which each outcome occurs.
