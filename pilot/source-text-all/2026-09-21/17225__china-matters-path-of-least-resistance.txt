The Gap Keeps Growing 
China’s August activity data showed a widening divergence between supply and 
demand, and between manufacturing output and household consumption. Industrial 
production growth accelerated from 4.5% yoy in July to 5.2% in August, while retail sales 
growth slowed from 0.6% yoy to 0.4%. Among major activity indicators, only export 
value and industrial production growth exceeded 5.0% yoy in August. By contrast, many 
domestic demand gauges continued to post large year-over-year declines (Exhibit 1). 
For exports, we can cross-check the data against trading partners’ reported imports 
from China. Such “mirror data” support strong Chinese export growth. For domestic 
demand, however, there are statistical reasons why official data may understate the true 
weakness. For example, in retail sales, large retailers above the designated size are 
surveyed comprehensively, whereas small retailers are sampled and estimated because 
of their large numbers and the difficulty of surveying every small retailer each month 
(see our latest edition of “Understanding China’s Economic Statistics” for more details). 
As a result, officially reported sales growth at small retailers tends to be smoother than 
that at large retailers and to hold up better during economic downturns. In August, sales 
at large retailers declined by almost 4% yoy, while sales at small retailers increased 3% 
yoy (Exhibit 2). This implies an even greater divergence between strong exports and 
weak domestic demand than official data indicate. 
 
The remarkable divergence in the Chinese economy extends beyond exports versus 
domestic demand. Within tech-related manufacturing, for example, industrial robot and 
semiconductor output climbed 34.6% yoy and 20.6% yoy respectively in August, while 
microcomputer and smartphone output dropped 25.6% yoy and 22.3% yoy respectively 
(Exhibit 3). Similarly, within retail sales, communication equipment sales increased 
27.3% yoy in August, while auto sales fell 18.5% yoy (Exhibit 4). These extreme 
divergences, driven in part by the ongoing AI capex boom and policies such as the 
consumer goods trade-in program, make it difficult to characterize the overall strength 
of the Chinese economy.

policymakers do not appear to feel much urgency to introduce additional easing 
measures, absent a sharp deterioration in the labor market. At the September 15 press 
conference, NBS officials highlighted industrial robots and semiconductors—but not 
smartphones or microcomputers—when discussing industrial production, and 
communication equipment—but not autos—when discussing retail sales. With top 
leadership continuing to emphasize advanced manufacturing, the path of least

domestic demand, and between strong industrial output and weak household

Property Markets: Where to Go Now? 
Significant divergence also applies to property prices. Based on the NBS 70-city 
property price indices, new home prices in Tier-1 cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, 
and Shenzhen) rose 2% from January to August this year, while home prices in Tier-2 and 
Tier-3 cities continued to decline (Exhibit 5).1 Our previous research shows that typical 
large-scale housing busts last six years, with real house prices falling around 30% from 
peak to trough. By this standard, China’s property market should be nearing a bottom as 
we head into 2027, six years after the 2021 housing peak, with real house prices having 
declined by more than 30%, according to our tracking. 
However, we think China’s property market may not follow this typical experience over 
the next few years. Take the US as an illustrative case of housing market bottoming in 
5-6 years: although inflation fell in the first few years after house prices peaked, it largely 
moved higher in subsequent years (Exhibit 6). The broader US economy was recovering, 
with the unemployment rate falling, wage inflation normalizing, and rent inflation 
recovering. By contrast, five years after Chinese property prices peaked, China’s labor 
market remains very weak, if not weakening, and rent inflation remained negative (-0.6% 
yoy) as of August 2026. If property has dragged down the overall economy over the past 
five years, we are now seeing more signs that the broader economy - especially 
employment, wage growth, and rent inflation - is increasingly weighing on the property 
market recovery.

Policymakers do not appear eager to unleash significant easing to boost housing 
demand. In fact, the recent policy announcement to curb developer financing would 
likely slow land sales and local government revenue before reducing housing supply, 
making it a net negative for the housing market in the near term, in our view. Therefore, 
the path of least resistance may be continued house price declines outside a few 
top-tier cities and selected segments, such as new luxury homes in highly desirable 
locations. 
 
Implications of China’s Economic Transformation 
China’s bank loan growth has slowed dramatically over the past three years, from above 
10% yoy in 2023 to just 5% yoy in August 2026 (Exhibit 7). Even after factoring in 
increased government bond issuance to swap out bank lending to local government 
financing vehicles (LGFVs), total social financing (TSF) growth also declined, from close 
to 10% yoy in 2023 to 7.2% yoy in August 2026. PBOC Governor Pan Gongsheng recently 
argued that the credit slowdown is a natural outcome of China’s structural 
transformation: as credit-intensive sectors such as property and infrastructure, which 
rely heavily on bank loans, give way to sectors such as AI and high-tech manufacturing, 
which are less credit-intensive and rely more on equity and corporate bond markets for 
financing, bank loan growth will inevitably decelerate without a material slowdown in 
GDP growth. 
This argument has merit. But by the same mix-shift logic, China’s structural 
transformation is negative for the labor market. For every unit of value-added created in 
the economy, labor intensity is more than 20% higher in services and more than 100% 
higher in construction than in manufacturing (Exhibit 8). As policymakers press ahead 
with this economic transformation, industrial production and exports are likely to 
continue benefiting from technological advancements and AI-enabled manufacturing, 
supporting headline GDP. However, employment, wage income, and household 
consumption are likely to remain sluggish as the economy shifts further toward less 
labor-intensive sectors. 
Reversing this trend requires significant and systematic efforts to reform the social 
safety net, improve income distribution, and raise labor’s share of GDP. These are 
multi-year or even multi-decade projects, and we have seen few signs that this process is 
moving quickly. In the meantime, China’s structural transformation implies continued

Growth Target Dilemma 
In recent weeks, government bond issuance has accelerated somewhat, and 
policymakers have signaled plans to step up infrastructure investment, especially on the 
“Six Networks” projects.3 The State Council held meetings on building grid and logistics 
networks on July 31, communication networks on August 21, underground networks on 
August 31, and computing networks on September 11. But even after penciling in a 
pickup in sequential growth in the coming months on accelerated investment, our 
forecasts for Q3 and Q4 real GDP growth are both 4.4% yoy, below the government’s 
full-year target range of 4.5-5.0% (Exhibit 9). Our forecast for 2026 full-year real GDP 
growth is 4.5%, exactly at the lower bound of the target range. Two years after the 
September 2024 policy pivot, when policymakers meaningfully loosened monetary, 
fiscal, property, and equity market policies to boost confidence and halt the continued 
decline in GDP growth, meeting the growth target is becoming a challenge again. 
Herein lies the dilemma in setting annual growth targets. China’s economic planning 
system centers on the GDP growth target, which helps coordinate resources across 
ministries and different levels of government. Shifting to other targets, such as inflation 
or employment, would face various implementation challenges. At the same time, 
pressure to reach the growth target each year often leads to accelerated infrastructure 
investment, which is under policymakers’ control and can translate into GDP quickly. 
These dynamics make rebalancing away from an investment-driven economy difficult, 
but a fundamental shift to a much lower annual growth target would put the long-term 
goal of “doubling income by 2035” at risk, as this goal requires a 4.17% annual growth 
rate from 2025 to 2035.

A decisive move to rebalance the domestic economy may involve either expanding fiscal 
spending substantially to boost consumption or drastically shifting fiscal spending from 
investment to consumption. Policymakers are reluctant to do the former because of 
debt sustainability concerns, given that China’s augmented fiscal deficit has run in 
double digits as a share of GDP every year since 2015. They are also reluctant to do the 
latter because of growth target concerns, as the GDP impact of reducing investment 
would be immediate, whereas the benefits of strengthening the social safety net would 
come much later. The path of least resistance may be to gradually lower the annual 
growth target, for example from 4.5-5.0% in 2026 to around 4.5% in 2027. More signals 
on next year’s growth target should emerge as the December Politburo meeting and 
Central Economic Work Conference approach later this year.
