In Summary 
• The AI supercycle will pull long-term yields down in both the US and Europe, but for 
very different reasons and by very different paths. We model this across five transmission 
channels – three macro (neutral rate, inflation expectations, fiscal collateral) and two 
micro (AI-driven duration supply and crowding-out) – applied to an AI upside and 
downside scenario. Over a 10-year horizon the net effect is around −50bp for both regions 
in the upside scenario (roughly +5pp of cumulated US potential growth, +1.6pp for the 
eurozone), and a prolonged, smaller drag in the downside (−2.5pp and −1.3pp 
respectively). The US result depends on AI productivity actually materializing to keep its 
fiscal trajectory intact; the eurozone gets to the same place even without the AI dividend, 
since its fiscal position doesn't need repairing in the first place. The US is the levered, 
volatile expression of the AI trade; Europe is the safer one. 
• The market has already priced the AI productivity story for the US, but not the fiscal 
relief that should come with it. The USD 5y1y forward has moved from ~3.0% to 4.9% since 
mid-2025 some 150bp above our median neutral-rate estimate and 90bp above the top 
of our estimate range, and more than the full +67bp our model assigns to the Ai enhanced 
neutral-rate range over the next decade. What isn't priced at all is the fiscal collateral 
improvement, worth −85bp to the US 10Y by the end of the horizon. Either the market 
doesn't believe AI will repair the fiscal trajectory, or it's pricing a less favorable version of 
the same story with higher AI-driven job displacement pushing up social protection and 
unemployment spending enough to offset the tax-revenue gain. 
• Fiscal collateral – the extent to which a stronger economy makes a government's debt 
easier to repay – is what actually decides the outcome, in both scenarios and both 
regions. The transatlantic rates converging seems counterintuitive at first view as Europe's 
growth impulse is a third the size of the US. But its fiscal position benefits more, because 
the improvement lands on a stable debt path rather than the expansionary US path. For 
every point of growth AI delivers, Europe's fiscal position improves about 1.6 times more 
than that of the US. 
• The way AI interacts with interest rate isn't a straight path, and its shape differs on each 
side of the Atlantic. Both curves rise well above their eventual resting point before 
reversing, the US 10Y's round trip is 135bp (versus 80bp for the eurozone) even though the 
net 10-year effect is only −50bp for both. The peak trade also looks different in each 
market: a butterfly in the US (buy the belly, sell the wings, as front-loaded micro effects hit 
the wings while macro effects lag into the belly) versus a flattener in Europe. By the end of 
the period both curves converge to a similar, milder flattening.

AI is no longer only a sector story, but a macro-scale claim on the economy. 
Artificial intelligence (AI) has become a major macroeconomic force. AI investment will pass USD1tn globally in 
2026, of which more than USD500bn in the US alone. That is 1.8% of US GDP and 0.9% of world GDP, on a path to 
2.8% and 1.4% respectively by 2028. In terms of growth, tech and data center activity will represent 36%-50% of US 
year-on-year growth this year. Globally, the direct AI investment impulse is worth roughly 10% of global growth. 
From the financial markets side, AI has mostly been examined through the lens of equities (sector analysis, 
valuation, concentration risk), or private credit  (risk profile and risk pricing of AI investments types (see our latest 
reports here linked here). This paper asks how the AI supercycle transmits into interest rates.. 
US and eurozone vary in the level of macro impact, but share similar profile 
We model the effect against a baseline without AI effects, measuring the difference versus an AI upside scenario 
and one in which AI shifts to the downside. We use several published estimates to derive scenarios for the impact 
on potential growth, trend productivity and trend employment. We define an upside scenario with cumulated 
potential growth gains of +4.94pp in the US and +1.60pp in the eurozone; in the downside we assume -2.47pp in the 
US and -1.35pp in the eurozone. The US macro scenario shows much greater sensitivity to AI, while Europe has less 
to gain in terms of productivity. The pattern is similar for inflation, where in the upside scenario the US faces a 
stronger supply- and energy-driven uptick, before higher productivity gains take over and reverse the AI impact into 
a disinflationary trend. Inversely in the negative scenario, the negative AI demand shock brings US inflation further 
down than it does in the eurozone (Table 1).

Our framework rests on five major transmission channels 
We identify two major channels of transmission: 1) a macroeconomic channel through potential growth, inflation 
dynamics and the fiscal position (plus a spillover effect for the eurozone), and 2) a microeconomic channel through 
the changes AI financing brings to the structure of fixed income markets. The latter has gained importance recently 
as market participants have discussed the possibility that AI debt issuance was competing with US government debt 
and therefore contributing to driving up yields. The macro channels capture the changes in the way long-term rates 
discount economic variables. The micro channels capture how the AI funding model (market size, self-funded vs 
debt issuance) affects the price of duration. Macro and micro channels work on different lags, and their effects can 
change sign over our 10-year forecast horizon. Generally, the macro channels affect the expected rate component 
of long-term rates, while micro channels act on the term premium. The exception is the fiscal position which, despite 
being a macro channel, affects the term premium component of long term government bond yields. 
1) Neutral rate r* is the real policy rate at which the economy neither overheats nor stalls. It is the level the 
expected path of short rates is anchored to. If AI raises trend productivity, capital earns more, firms want to 
invest more, and the neutral rate balancing saving against investment rises. A lasting productivity gain 
therefore lifts r* which feeds through the entire yield curve. It has a stronger effect at the front end as it 
transmits through expected short rates, and productivity gains have a long-term mean reverting trend. The 
effect is inverted in case AI would fail to produce productivity gains in an economy facing a debt overhang 
from AI investments.  
2) For inflation expectations we use an adaptive expectation approach where the estimation windows adjust 
with previous estimation errors. As AI has a strong supply impact at first, we get upside push in expectations

that fades with time when productivity gains take over. As the phase in becomes smoother, the change in 
expectation is less pronounced to the downside. These factors also influence the expected rate component 
of government bond yields. 
3) Fiscal collateral is defining the fundamental value of sovereign bonds as discounted claims on future 
primary surpluses. The channel captures its change in response to an AI-driven up- or downside productivity 
shock. A productivity shock has an asymmetric effect: tax revenues improving the fiscal position are elastic 
to GDP, while the largest spending lines (pensions, benefits, debt service) are sticky.  
4) Duration supply is a market-microstructure model tracking AI capex as a generator of new duration, 
through both long-dated IG bonds and pay-fixed swap structures in private credit. The curve effect sits at 
the long end as 65% the supply lands beyond 10 years. The swap spread effect depends on fixed-rate 
issuance, which cheapens the sovereign against swaps while pay-fixed hedging from private credit, 
enrichens it. Duration supply affects the term premium, it is more pronounced at the longer end of the curve. 
5) Crowding Out is a sector-level displacement model affecting long-term rates via the swap-market. 
Financial institutions issue fixed-rate bonds and then ask for floating, receiving fixed in swaps. That receive-
fixed leg is how financial issuance moves duration out of the sovereign market. AI borrowers sit on the 
opposite side of the swap curve, paying fixed rather than receiving it. So both channels lift swap yields 
against the sovereign: one adds fixed-payer supply, the other removes fixed-receiver demand. Like 
duration supply, crowding-out affects the term premium at the longer end of the curve. 
6) Spillover Effect is added for the eurozone, acting mainly at the long end, where global term premia and 
rate levels remain structurally correlated and anchored to the US benchmark. It captures how AI effects 
propagate to global fixed income markets via the US Treasury market, even where AI exposure is limited.

The AI super cycle is a bull market for duration, but it is not a directional trade 
Our results show that AI is a bull market for duration in both scenarios, but the path matters. In the upside scenario, 
AI lowers long-term rates by around 50bp over a 10-year horizon in both the US and the eurozone. In the downside 
scenario, rates are also held down for a prolonged period. In both cases, however, yields rise before they fall: the 
US 10Y makes a 135bp round trip in the upside and 72bp in the downside, against 80bp and 41bp for the eurozone. 
The AI effect on rates is therefore not a long-term directional trade, the right timing is essential. The timing depends 
on which channels dominate when, how long they take to act and where on the curve they bite hardest. That makes 
the shape of the curve as important as the yield level for positioning (Figure 1).

The end points for the US and the eurozone are remarkably close, even though the two start from very different 
positions in the AI race. The fiscal collateral channel explains why. Europe gets only a moderate growth impulse, 
but it lands on a stable fiscal position, which improves the value of its fiscal collateral significantly. The US gets a 
much larger growth impulse, yet the gain is partly offset by continued fiscal expansion over the next 10 years (we 
use the CBO forecast of debt/GDP rising 20pp over the next 10 years). With a stable debt path, the increase in US 
fiscal collateral value would subtract 125bp rather than 85bp, lifting the total effect to -90bp and leaving the 
eurozone far behind. The transatlantic AI rates convergence is thus a late-cycle outcome: at the peak, the AI effect 
is more than twice as large in the US as in the eurozone. 
 
The US curve rotates between the peak and the end of period due to micro effects 
At the peak of the upside scenario, the US curve shifts up, but with the middle of the curve (belly) holding up better: 
the 2s30s slope barely moves while 2s5s flattens 13bp and 5s30s steepens 12bp. The peak AI trade on the US curve 
is therefore a butterfly (buy the belly, sell the wings). In the eurozone the peak AI effect on the curve is a flattener 
instead: 2s30s −20bp, with the 2Y up 51bp against the 30Y up 31bp. This shape difference is driven by the stronger 
and front-loaded US micro channels, set against macro channels that arrive with a lag. In addition to the time lag, 
macro channels (neutral rate, inflation expectations) act mainly at the short end, while the micro channels hit the 
long end because AI issuance is long-dated. The 5Y sits in between: it absorbs little of the duration-supply and 
crowding-out pressure, yet is long enough to benefit from the improving fiscal collateral effect on its term premium. 
By the end of the period, both curves flatten to a similar degree as fiscal collateral value becomes the dominant 
force, pulling the belly and the long end down while the 2Y is barely affected (US 2s30s −41bp, EA −30bp) In the

Microeconomic channels and inflation move first, the fiscal channel decides the end point 
Having looked at the way AI effect the changes in yield levels and the influences the shape of the curves, we now 
turn to the decomposition of the moves: which channels drives the yield and curve dynamics, how much each 
contributes and when. The sequence is the same in both scenarios: micro channels and inflation expectations drive 
rates early on, while the fiscal channel and the neutral rate r* only kick in later.  
In the upside scenario, the peak arrives when productivity gains lift r* while inflation expectations are still elevated 
and the micro channels are still at work but before higher growth has significantly fed through to tax revenues rising 
the value of the fiscal collateral. For the US at the peak in 2032, r* alone contributes +67bp against −18bp from the 
fiscal channel. By the end point the picture reverses: the fiscal channel contributes −85bp, r* slows down to +42bp 
and inflation expectations flip to −18bp as the disinflationary effect of productivity gains sets in. Europe follows the 
same sequence at roughly half the amplitude, reflecting its lower sensitivity to AI. 
In the downside scenario, rates first move up, driven by inflation expectations on the back of AI investment and 
energy demand. In the US, the micro channels add another 20-30bp from AI-related issuance. The tipping point is 
in 2031: productivity gains fail to materialize and the debt overhang starts weighing on growth, which later erodes 
tax revenues, increases government spending and reduces value of the fiscal collateral. In the eurozone the micro 
channels are negligible, but the spillover from US rates matters. 
The greater US sensitivity also shows up in the gross channel activity, i.e. the sum of all channel contributions in 
absolute terms. In the upside it amounts to 158bp in the US against 78bp in Europe for a broadly similar final 
outcome. In other words, much more happens under the surface in the US, but most of it cancels out: only 30% of the 
US channel activity is left once the channels are netted against each other, compared with 58% in Europe. The AI 
journey is therefore likely to be bumpier for US rates than for eurozone rates, even if both end up in a similar place. 
The AI effect should therefore translate into relatively higher US rates volatility (Figure 4 & 5).

The fiscal channel has the last word – and the US needs the AI dividend more than Europe 
We find no transatlantic divide in the long-term AI effect on rates. At the end of the period the US and eurozone 10Y 
effects are only a few basis points apart in both scenarios. But the path to that point differs materially in level, drivers 
and timing. The convergence of the end points is attributable almost entirely to fiscal channel. Fiscal collateral 
contributes −85bp to the US terminal effect in the upside and −46bp to the eurozone, in both cases more than the 
channels working in the opposite direction, principally the neutral rate r* effect. 
The mechanism is the productivity beta of government debt. A positive productivity shock raises tax revenues by 
more than the GDP gain, so the present value of future primary surpluses improves and the bondholder is effectively 
long AI productivity. Europe is disadvantaged here, as it tax revenues are less sensitive to GDP (also because AI 
growth is tilted versus US and less taxable by European governments) but its spending is also more sticky, as Europe 
is more exposed to negative labor market effects caused by AI. On the other hand, the fiscal trajectory of Europe is 
more favorable, with a rather stable debt to GDP ratio projection of 86% for the next 10 years. Over the same time 
period, the CBO sees US debt growing to 20pp of GDP. 
What differs between the two markets is the debt path on which that improvement lands: deteriorating in the US, 
stable in the eurozone. The US is therefore the more dependent of the two on the AI productivity call actually being 
realized, as it needs the additional revenue to sustain its current expansionary fiscal stance, whereas the eurozone 
would maintain its debt trajectory even without the AI dividend. 
On our calibration, Europe’s fiscal-collateral beta is 1.6× the US per point of growth delivered, notwithstanding the 
more favorable US elasticities (higher revenue elasticity, lower spending elasticity). The difference lies in the 
denominator: a smaller debt stock (EUR14tn against USD40tn), a lower starting yield, a lower baseline real rate

The neutral rate: bigger in the US, more decisive in Europe 
The r* channel is larger in the US but more decisive in Europe. In the US upside it is the biggest single positive 
contribution at both moments, +67bp at the 2032 peak and +42bp at the 2036 end point, but it sits inside 158bp of 
gross channel activity and is therefore largely netted away by the other channels. In the eurozone it is effectively 
the only positive channel left at the end of the period, at +15bp. Both paths rise with a delay, starting two to three 
years from now. The US path runs much higher but also mean-reverts more strongly later on, so the gap to the 
eurozone, which follows a smoother path, narrows over time. In the downside the paths are less pronounced and 
the two regions are closer together: the absolute impact is stronger in the US, while the eurozone shows the higher 
sensitivity relative to its own starting point.

The market prices the AI productivity gain, but not the fiscal relief that should come with it 
In the US, the rates market has already fully priced the productivity leg of the AI story. The 5y1y forward swap has 
climbed from 3.0% in mid-2025 to 4.9%, against a median neutral-rate estimate of 3.4% and range of 2.7-4.0%, which 
puts the market-implied neutral rate some 150bp above the median and 90bp above the top of the band. Our AI 
models would add +67bp to current estimates pushing the range to 3.3-4.67% – a range the market already exceeds. 
What is missing from the current AI narrative pricing is the other side of the same story: the fiscal collateral 
improvement that these productivity gains should bring, worth −85bp on the US 10Y over the next ten years. That 
leg arrives with a much longer lag, as it takes a visible turn in tax receipts to shift the narrative (Figure 9). We would 
therefore read the current level as a temporary mispricing rather than a challenge to the fiscal leg of the AI story. 
The front-loading of the productivity channel builds up correction potential.
