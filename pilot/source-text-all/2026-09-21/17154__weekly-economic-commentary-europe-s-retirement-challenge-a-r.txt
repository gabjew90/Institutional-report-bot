Europe's identity has always been shaped by age.  It is home to ancient institutions, 
historic cities and traditions that stretch back centuries.   
Europe’s economic identity is being shaped by age in the present day.  Demographically, 
it is now the oldest continent, and this development is reshaping everything from labor 
markets to public finances.  But the impact of Europe’s aging is most visible in the debate 
over pensions.   
Across the continent, governments are confronting a difficult reality: systems designed 
when workers vastly outnumbered retirees are becoming harder to sustain.  By 2070, 
there will be barely two working-age adults for every person over 65, down from around 
three today, according to the European Commission.  Birth rates have fallen well below 
replacement levels across most of the continent, while life expectancy continues to rise.  
The median citizen of the European Union (EU) is already 45 years old, at least six years 
older than other continents’ populations.   
The economic costs of ageing are already substantial.  Public spending on retirement 
benefits, healthcare and long-term care accounts for roughly one-fifth of the EU's gross 
domestic product (GDP).  Across the EU, nearly half of all social protection spending is 
devoted to old-age and survivor benefits.   
Most member states rely heavily on public pension systems financed on a pay-as-you-go 
basis.  Workers contribute through payroll taxes, and those funds are used to pay today's 
retirees.  Retirement security therefore depends largely on government promises and the 
willingness of future workers to finance them.  Public systems cover more than 90% of

Contributions flow primarily toward current benefit payments rather than investment portfolios. 
Europe's pay-as-you-go model worked well when populations were younger and workforces were 
expanding.  As populations age and labor force growth slows, pension finances come under 
increasing strain. 
The United States follows a more diversified approach.  Social Security, also primarily a pay-as-
you-go system, provides a safety net.  Employer-sponsored defined contribution (DC) plans, such 
as 401(k)s, play a much larger role.  Some argue that Americans bear greater investment risk 
under this system, but they have also accumulated a far larger pool of retirement assets.   
Europe's pension challenge is not just about fiscal sustainability; it also represents a missed 
economic opportunity.  Much of Europe's retirement wealth remains invested conservatively, often 
concentrated in government bonds and other low-risk assets.  As a result, less capital finds its way 
into productive investment, which Europe could use a lot more of. 
Across the EU, funded retirement assets amount to just over $5 trillion, less than 30% of GDP.  
America's 401(k) plans and pension funds manage roughly $45 trillion in assets, equivalent to over 
150% of GDP.  Only a handful of European countries, including the Netherlands, Denmark and 
Sweden, have built investment pools that are comparable to the size of their economies.  
Significant portions of these portfolios are invested in equities and other growth-oriented assets, 
helping create some of Europe's deepest and most dynamic capital markets.   
The implications extend beyond market structures.  European governments today face growing 
demands for defense spending, infrastructure investment and debt servicing.  Every euro devoted 
to pension support is unavailable for other priorities.   
Europe is beginning to confront these challenges.  The Savings and Investment Union is an effort 
to channel more household savings into long-term investment capital.  A key part of the strategy is 
to improve retirement outcomes and create larger pools of capital that can be invested in Europe's 
future.

Yet reform remains politically difficult.  France provides perhaps the clearest example.  President 
Macron's decision to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 triggered nationwide protests and 
political turmoil.  Germany is now grappling with similar pressures.  Recent reforms seek both to 
strengthen private long-term savings and to reduce the burden on the public system.  Policymakers 
are expanding the role of capital-market investment in old-age provision while continuing efforts to 
extend working lives as life expectancy rises. 
Preserving retirement promises at the heart of Europe’s social model will require difficult choices.  
Governments increasingly acknowledge that people must work longer, save more or accept lower 
benefits.  While the economic case for reforms is straightforward, the politics surrounding it are not.  
The future of Europe's pension systems will determine more than retirement incomes.  It will shape 
public finances, capital markets and the continent's capacity to invest in its future.  Europe's identity 
has long been defined by age.  The challenge now is whether it can come of age. 
Crash Course 
I typically avoid business books when I am on vacation.  But I made an exception during our 
sojourn last month for Andrew Ross Sorkin’s 1929.  Nearly a century after the Great Crash, that era 
is still being studied for lessons that might be relevant in the current day.  The potential for financial 
instability is ever-present. 
Sorkin’s work isn’t the most authoritative account of the economic and financial events of that time: 
John Kenneth Galbraith’s The Great Crash, 1929, published in 1955, provides better background 
on what happened before and after that fateful October.  Sorkin focuses more on the prominent 
personalities involved, from bankers to speculators and government officials.  Even Winston 
Churchill makes a cameo. 
Here are the main things I took away from the reading. 
• Bubbles are only obvious in retrospect.  The market excesses of the 1920s are apparent 
from the distance of history, but they were not clear in the moment.  The hot stocks of the 1920s 
were new technology companies like RCA that promised to transform the economy. Traditional 
models were deemed inadequate to project the performance and valuation of these kinds of 
firms. There were voices that suggested that markets were overdone, and speculators who 
profited handsomely when this was proven true. But they were in the great minority.   
• The Federal Reserve performed very poorly during the crisis. The Fed was a relatively 
young institution at the time, having been chartered in 1913 to foster financial stability. Sorkin 
describes an institution that was very much in the background during the crash, more intent on 
teaching lessons on the evils of excessive leverage and moral hazard than taking steps to limit 
the damage. 
Years later, Anna Schwarz and Milton Friedman found that the Fed had presided over a huge 
contraction of credit.  While the reserves supplied by the Fed to the economy continued to grow, 
the conservatism of lenders and the mass failure of financial institutions resulted in a credit 
crunch that made the situation much worse.

Subsequent research by Ben Bernanke confirmed this conclusion, and informed the Fed’s 
response to the 2008 financial crisis. Facing criticism over the moral hazard raised by his 
actions, Bernanke famously observed that “There are no atheists in foxholes, and no ideologues 
in financial crises.” 
• Financial regulation was completely ineffectual.  Bank failures in the United States had been 
rising in the years before 1929, revealing poor regulatory oversight.  Securities markets were 
largely self-regulated by the major exchanges, creating a significant conflict of interest. The 
association of commercial banking and investment banking was unhealthy. 
In the wake of the Great Crash, government supervision of finance was expanded.  The Federal 
Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) was chartered in 1933 and the Securities and Exchange 
Commission was created in 1934. The FDIC took a lead in overseeing smaller banks around 
the country, which had not been well-looked after by state regulators. 
Ever since, periods of heavy regulation have typically seen few bank failures and periods of 
deregulation have seen more.  We are in the midst of one of the latter episodes, which has 
raised concern in some corners.  Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr recently gave a 
speech ominously entitled: “Deregulating in a Financial Boom: What Could Go Wrong?” 
During his book tour, Sorkin was asked if the timing of publication could be taken as any kind of 
warning about current market circumstances.  He, and I, are very reluctant to make that 
association.  But the experience of 1929 should not be lost on investors and institutions as they 
operate in the present day. 
We Know It When We See It 
Every runner has a natural pace: the speed that we maintain under optimal conditions like flat 
terrain, cool temperatures and a good night of sleep. Runners can train to speed up to meet a 
target time, or slow down for endurance. 
Interest rates also have a natural setting.  When they are raised above this level, the pace of the 
economy slows to ensure endurance.  When they are lowered, the speed of economic growth will 
increase.  The neutral rate of interest (or r*) cannot be precisely measured, but understanding its 
influence is critical. All current estimates suggest that the neutral rate is moving up, carrying other 
yields with it and complicating central bank decisions.

than the supply of savings. For much of the 2010s, abundant global savings, weak productivity 
growth and demand for safe assets held neutral rates down.  
Today, investment opportunities have reignited, led by technology: Businesses are investing 
heavily in data centers, software, semiconductors and power infrastructure to support AI 
deployment. Stronger expected productivity growth raises the prospective return on investment, 
increasing demand for capital and compounding the run-up of yields.  
Growth of government debt is also pushing up r*. Investors need additional compensation to hold 
more government securities as deficits grow; the rising risk-free benchmark rate pulls up other 
yields with it. 
A higher neutral rate raises the bar for monetary policy to be considered restrictive. This week, Fed 
Chairman Kevin Warsh characterized the Fed’s rate increase as “removing a dose of 
accommodation,” implying the rate had been below neutral. The Fed estimate charted above 
implies a nominal neutral rate of over 4%, affirming an outlook of at least one more hike in store. 
The structural factors pushing up r* are unlikely to subside. Demand for AI is still growing, and 
deficits are difficult to reduce. If the Fed is to steer the U.S. economy to an appropriate pace, a 
push to stay above neutral will be necessary.
