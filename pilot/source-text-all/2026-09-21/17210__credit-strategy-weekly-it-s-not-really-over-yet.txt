Credit Strategy Weekly 
It’s not really over yet 
The list of worries keeps growing. The global sovereign bond sell-off eased somewhat this 
week, but as our Rates colleagues point out, the next peak in long-end yields is likely still 
ahead. And, earlier this week, we explored the rising risks to the credit markets, which include 
AI disruption (industries and new issue volumes), potential damage from private credit 
markets, geopolitical issues ranging from tariffs to wars, to oil price volatility, and, more 
recently, the ongoing sovereign bond sell-off and rising yields. We can add to this, the very tight 
spread to benchmarks, which leaves very little room for error. But all of this has been known 
for a while, except for the uncertainty generated by the rising sovereign bond yields worldwide 
that are reaching multi-record highs. If the speed of the sell-off slows down, credit spreads 
should recover fully. 
But the case for credit remains solid. Credit fundamentals remain solid, and firms’ ability to 
service their debt has not changed. Yields may rise further but the overall cost of debt moves 
more slowly and remains very manageable for companies that are still generating large profits. 
Moreover, the rise in yields is making credit increasingly attractive, and with the recent rise in 
global uncertainty, companies may well opt to remain defensive when it comes to spending for 
longer, with spreads stable for longer than we initially expected. 
Strong volumes defy global markets’ volatility. The tone in the markets may have been weak 
most of the week but the new issue markets were mostly unaffected as we had a healthy feed 
of new issues daily. We saw bonds from every major market segment this week, including a 
couple of AT1 CoCos. The overall tally year-to-date stands at €680.2bn, ahead of last year’s 
volumes at the same stage, and we expect activity to remain strong into year-end, highlighting 
just how strong appetite for the asset class is.

Macro view 
All about the sovereign bond volatility: The week started 
poorly, with sovereign bonds selling off further and the tone 
remained negative until the FOMC meeting. The markets 
improved gently, and credit spreads recovered some of the 
ground lost. But the issues remain. Oil prices are currently just 
above $100 per barrel, and inflation expectations are high and 
rising. This is likely to keep pressure on sovereign bonds and it is 
difficult to see how far yields can rise. In our view, if the rise is slow, 
the credit markets will adapt and spreads are likely to remain 
tight. Next week, there will be few economic data releases and we 
expect the markets to follow the developments in the Middle East 
and the moves in oil prices. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Cash 
Ending on a better tone: The credit markets remained under 
pressure early this week, as sovereign bonds continued to slide. 
The tone improved ahead of the FOMC meeting and the increased 
stability in the sovereign bond world that followed the Fed’s 
announcements helped the credit markets improve across the 
board. The USD indices were more resilient than their euro 
counterparts since last week, and navigated the euro sovereign 
bonds and Treasuries sell-off better. But all the indices are ending 
the week on a better tone, and we suspect that credit spreads will 
continue to improve over the coming days, and test the better 
levels seen late in August or in early September, as long as the 
sovereign bond market doesn’t see renewed weakness. New issue 
volumes have been very strong and should slow down in the next 
couple of weeks, and this is likely to give further support to the 
secondary market.

a very positive performance, as we had a healthy offering every

and the blackout periods hamper activity.

Strategy Scorecard 
Regular readers will be familiar with our SG Credit Scorecard, a tool we have used for more than 
a decade to help us forecast the direction of credit spreads.  
For readers who are not familiar with the Scorecard, we construct it in three stages. First, we 
group indicators into various categories. Macroeconomic fundamentals, for example, 
comprises GDP and inflation numbers, but also indicators such as monetary policy and fiscal 
policy. Second, we compare our forecasts for these indicators over the next six to 12 months with 
the consensus view. This gives us an impact, which can range from a maximum positive of +3 to 
a maximum negative of -3. These are shown in the table as +++ and ---; a neutral reading is =. 
Finally, we assign a weight to the indicator. This ranges from 1 (relatively unimportant) to 3 
(relatively important), shown in the table as a number of boxes. The weights depend on our view 
of where we are in the credit cycle. The scorecard for each indicator is the impact multiplied by 
the weight, so it can range from +9 to -9. The average level of macroeconomic fundamentals is 
therefore the average of the two sub-indicators, GDP and inflation and policy; the average level 
of the whole scorecard is the average of the five indicators (macroeconomic fundamentals, 
corporate fundamentals, investor demand, supply, and valuations).

The week ahead 
Sovereign bonds, light economics, and the Middle East: Next week will be relatively light 
on the economic data front, and we suspect the credit markets will mostly focus on the risk 
appetite/aversion dynamics in the sovereign bond world. Based on that, any positive 
developments in the Middle East should prove supportive of risk appetite, and we expect the 
markets to look to the oil prices for guidance. 
A light economic calendar to come: Next week, we’ll have a few economic data releases 
from the major economic blocs, including PMI data on both sides of the Atlantic, as well as many 
other data releases from several European countries. However, in our view, none of the prints is 
likely to have a major impact on the markets, and even less so on the credit markets. Instead, 
we’ll be keeping a close eye on any shifts in the sovereign bond market, as mentioned above.

Market review 
Sovereign bonds, the FOMC, and inflation expectations impact the overall markets: 
It was a difficult start to the week, as sovereign bonds remained under heavy pressure after the 
ECB meeting the previous week. Bond yields on both sides of the Atlantic rose to multi-year highs 
at a quick pace. However, the markets started to calm down ahead of the FOMC meeting and later 
calmed down gently, despite the generally hawkish message (see our economists take here). 
Sovereign bonds remained nervous, but the sell-off slowed. 
Government bond yields have risen sharply in recent weeks

Despite the ongoing volatility in the sovereign bond market, equities recovered gently in the 
second half of the week. It was a similar story with oil prices, which dropped from the higher levels 
seen earlier this week, although prices remain very high and above $100 per barrel, amid no 
significant breakthrough in the war in the Middle East.
