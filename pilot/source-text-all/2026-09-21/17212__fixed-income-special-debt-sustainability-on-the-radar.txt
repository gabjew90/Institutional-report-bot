Fixed Income Special 
Debt sustainability on the radar 
 
Key points 
 Debt remains sustainable across the countries analysed, but vulnerabilities are greatest 
in the US and Italy, while Germany retains the strongest buffer. 
 Long debt maturities delay the pass-through of higher yields, although the US is more 
exposed due to its shorter maturity profile and heavier reliance on bills. 
 The withdrawal of central banks has increased sovereigns’ reliance on more price-
sensitive and leveraged investors, raising the risk of volatile flows and refinancing 
pressures, particularly in France and Italy. 
 High-debt countries are particularly vulnerable to growth shocks, while for the others 
risks to growth and fiscal slippage are more symmetric.  
Recommendation 
This document is an aggregation of analysis published gradually in FI Weeklies since early May. 
Back to basics 
As yields reach new highs across jurisdictions, questions over the debt sustainability are 
emerging. 
What does sustainability mean? A country’s public debt is considered sustainable if the 
government is able to meet all current and future payment obligations without exceptional 
financial assistance or going into default (IMF). The long-term trajectory of debt depends on fiscal 
policy (primary balance, pb), interest rates (r) and nominal economic growth (g). Intuitively, for a 
given primary balance, the larger the interest rate-growth differential (r–g), the more the debt 
ratio will increase, while when r < g, the debt ratio tends to decline. Two rates thresholds, to be 
compared with the actual cost of borrowing, are particularly relevant: 
▪ 
The stabilisation rate: the average interest rate at which a government can stabilise its 
debt-to-GDP ratio, given nominal growth, the primary balance and the debt stock 
inherited from the previous year.

We can then calculate two metrics: 1/ the stabilisation buffer i.e. the difference between the 
average interest rate and the stabilisation rate and 2/ the sustainability buffer i.e. the difference 
between the average interest rate and the stress threshold. 
Key levels to keep in mind 
Computing these metrics for the four largest EA countries, the UK, the US and Japan (Table 1, 
Graphs 1-6) yields logical conclusions:  
- 
In France, Germany, the US and the UK, average interest rates are above the stabilisation 
rate, implying a mechanical increase in debt-to-GDP (and a negative stabilisation buffer). 
This largely reflects primary deficits compressing the stabilisation rate. The opposite holds 
for Spain, Italy and Japan.  
- 
Borrowing costs remain below the stress threshold in all countries (positive sustainability 
buffer):  
• 
The gap is the widest in Germany, indicating a substantial buffer before debt 
sustainability becomes a concern, even in the context of current and expected fiscal 
stimulus. 
• 
The margin is relatively large in France, Spain, the UK and Japan. However, in 
France, the stress threshold is declining while the average interest rate is rising, 
narrowing sustainability buffer. In Spain, the stress threshold is increasing (less 
visible in the chart due to scaling), supporting a rebuilding of the sustainability 
buffer. In the UK and Japan, the sustainability buffer is lower but relatively stable. 
• 
The buffer is the smallest in Italy, leaving it closest to potential sustainability 
concerns. While the stress threshold is broadly stable, the key issue for Italy remains 
the elevated debt stock, which keeps servicing costs high. The buffer is also 
narrowest in the US reflecting a large primary deficit alongside rising interest costs. 
However, the dollar's reserve status has historically supported a higher debt 
tolerance.

Maturity of debt matters 
Short term debt means higher refinancing risks... Countries relying heavily on short-term 
instruments must refinance more frequently and are more exposed to rising funding costs than 
those issuing predominantly long-term debt. The four largest euro area countries rely mainly on 
bonds: by end-2025, debt stocks are on average composed of 95% bonds and 5% bills (Graph 8). 
This is also the case for the UK, Japan and the US, although the share of bills is significantly higher 
in the US at around 20%. Overall, short-dated debt (including both bonds and bills) is most 
prevalent in the US, where 60% of debt matures within four years, compared with 40% or less 
elsewhere (Graph 9).

… and faster the pass-through from market rates to borrowing costs. As a result, the 
weighted average maturity (WAM) of US debt is the shortest, while the UK has the longest (Table 
2). In Europe, WAM has gradually increased since the mid-2000s, supported by strong demand for 
duration in a low (and at times negative) rate environment. This has slowed the transmission of 
higher borrowing costs to the average interest rate (Graph 10), reflecting the heavy use of long-
term issuance. Consequently, the average interest rate remains below current market rates 
(Table 2). For example, in France, a WAM of around 8.5 years implies that 8.5% of the debt stock 
is refinanced each year, meaning it takes roughly a decade for the entire stock to be rolled over. 
As maturing bonds are refinanced at higher yields, only a gradual increase in the cost of debt 
ensues. Hence, the shorter the WAM, the faster the pass-through from market rates to borrowing 
costs.

Investor structure matters as well 
A growing role for price-sensitive investors. Since the start of QT, central banks – traditionally 
price-insensitive investors – have stepped back, while issuance volumes remain elevated. “New” 
investors must fill the gap, notably foreign investors, money market funds and investment funds, 
according to an OECD study (Graph 11). Sovereign bonds are therefore increasingly held by more 
price-sensitive investors, while foreign investors tend to be more responsive to political and 
geopolitical developments. This can add volatility to flows and, by extension, to yields. European 
sovereigns are, on average, more reliant on non-resident investors than the US or Japan (Graph 
12). In addition, hedge funds are playing a growing role in sovereign bond markets as liquidity 
providers and marginal buyers. As noted by the IMF, “these changes in the investor base towards 
more price-sensitive and leveraged investors could make markets more vulnerable to shocks”.

Investor base risks are rising. The IMF Investor Risk Index assesses the vulnerability of a 
sovereign's funding based on the composition and stability of its investors base. Higher scores 
indicate greater reliance on investor groups that may be more likely to reduce their holdings 
during periods of market stress, potentially amplifying refinancing risks. Since 2023, the Investor 
Risk Index has risen across most major sovereigns (Graph 13). France and Italy stand out, 
combining high debt levels with weaker investor base characteristics. By contrast, Japan's very 
high debt burden is mitigated by a more stable investor structure, while Germany remains the 
most resilient among the countries shown (Graph 14).

Scenario analysis – applying shocks 
Assessing sovereign debt vulnerabilities. We applied isolated macroeconomic shocks to the 
IMF baseline projections (table in Appendix): a +1pp on rates, a 1pp growth/inflation contraction, 
a +1pp growth/inflation pulse, and a 1pp fiscal slippage. The outputs show a structural split 
driven by debt-to-GDP (Table 3, Graphs 15-21). For highly levered sovereigns, existing debt base 
acts as a massive multiplier. As a result, cyclical shocks to the nominal growth (denominator) 
overwhelm standard changes to the fiscal deficit (numerator).  
Growth/inflation vs. fiscal slippage. Germany’s low starting debt insulates it from growth 
volatility, leaving its trajectory dominated primarily by direct fiscal slippage. Conversely, in the 
US, Japan, and Italy, a 1pp growth shock triggers an aggressive upward trajectory, making their 
debt profiles more sensitive to economic downturns. Meanwhile, France – although its baseline 
rests on highly optimistic assumptions – Spain and the UK are closer to the 100% threshold, 
creating more symmetric risks to both growth and fiscal shocks.
