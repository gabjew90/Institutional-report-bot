Market Wrap-up  
The bear case for credit  
 
iTraxx  
 
The list of issues threatening the credit markets keeps growing, and the latest 
addition, in the form of rising sovereign bond yields is putting pressure on the whole 
Fixed Income world.  Today, we look at the scenario where the credit markets finally 
enter a bear trend, for the first time since early 2022. 
Market highlights 
Another day, another sell-off.  The markets started the day in the grip of a mounting risk 
aversion which saw sovereign bonds under pressure, again, and yields moved on average 
4-5bp higher.  The 10y Bund yield rose 4bp to 3.55% and the 10y UST yield also rose by 
4bp to hit 5.03%. But in the afternoon, the tone improved gently and sovereign bonds 
gained back a little of the ground lost, with yields ending around 1pb wider. Equity 
markets continued to slide but started to pare losses in the afternoon and are closing less 
than 0.5% lower. Finally, the iTraxx indices followed the same trends, starting the day 
wider, and ending unchanged to a touch tighter.  The Main index is closing below 54.5bp 
and the X-Over around 262.5bp. 
The primary markets were open again and this time they had a clear senior financials and 
HY flavour.  We only saw one IG corporate deal but four senior financials transaction, 
including a dual tranche and a triple tranchet deals.  In total we had €6.525bn priced 
(several HY deals yet to announce final terms) pushing the total for the week to at least 
€12.275bn and we still think volumes will rise towards €20bn+ this week.

Market thoughts 
The list of threats to the credit market keeps growing, and now there are many moving parts, 
limiting the room for error further. For a while now we’ve had to deal with the possibility that AI 
disrupts whole sectors of the economy (software makers were among the first targets), and more 
broadly, the risk of a flood of paper.  So far this has been relatively mild in the euro markets (€27bn 
in the euro IG market with a high proportion of it coming in the 10y+ tenor) but still, the growth 
of the sector in both the euro and USD markets has been very strong over the past 18 months.  
And the Technology sector has underperformed all other sectors, particularly in the USD market. 
Technology sector in euros 
 
Technology sector in USD 
 
 
Source: Markit, SG Cross Asset Research/Credit 
 
 
Then there is the Private Credit threat which, admittedly, has been quiet lately, with scarce 
headlines.  However, the market remains opaque, and sooner or later we’ll see a new default 
cycle which may bring renewed nervousness.  In our view, the next default cycle will not be more 
dramatic than usual, with defaults peaking around 6-8%.  But the threat of a bigger damage is not 
completely negligible. 
The more recent threats include the geopolitical tensions on the economic front which started 
with the US tariffs announcements last year and worsened considerably this year with the war in 
the Middle East and the damage done to the oil prices and trade through the Strait of Hormuz 
(and now potential threats to the Bab al-Mandab Strait too).

The war which most expected to last just a few weeks at the time the conflict began, has turned 
into a more protracted conflict and our Commodities experts now expect a very narrow deal by 
the time of the US midterm elections before a broader agreement towards the end of the year.  
Furthermore, the war in the Middle East has not only pushed oil prices higher but is disrupting 
supply chains and inflation globally has moved higher.  And while it is far from the levels  seen 
back in 2022, central banks are reacting (see the ECB’s more hawkish tone last week following 
their 25bp hike) and sovereign bond yields have risen sharply.  Currently the 10y Bund yield has 
risen above 3.5%, the 10y UST yield above 5%, the 10y JGB above 3%.  
And this has become a new threat to the credit markets, as the rising yields make sovereign bonds 
increasingly attractive, at a time when uncertainty is very high. The 10y Bund yield is at the 
highest levels since around 2008 and the 10y UST yields is at the highest since around 2007. 
Sovereign bond yields keep rising 
 
Source: Bloomberg, SG Cross Asset Research/Credit 
The higher sovereign bond yields means that the cost of funding for corporates continues to rise 
and perhaps more importantly at the moment, the appeal of sovereign bonds (theoretically safe) 
could become very attractive compared to corporate debt (which can potentially get 
downgraded and default).  And this could trigger a migration from corporate bonds towards 
sovereign. 
Additionally, we still have the potential threat of more tariffs, and a trade war between the US 
and Europe, or China, or others and many different combinations, although at the moment this 
is not a likely scenario.   
Still, the risks to the markets are many, and growing and given the very tight spread levels (spread 
to benchmarks) it is not unconceivable that a new accident could finally spark a sharp and 
sustainable spread widening. 
Fortunately, that’s not the whole story (to be continued).
