Where does last week’s selloff stand in 
history, and what's driven bigger ones?
Despite Monday’s recovery, last week’s selloff made it one of the bigger declines 
of recent years. On Friday, the S&P 500 moved into correction territory for the first 
time since the Liberation Day selloff in April 2025. That day also saw US HY spreads 
reach their widest since that time, whilst the VIX closed above 35 again.
But there were clear differences with the other big risk-off moves of the last decade:
n
First, there wasn’t a sudden and fundamental reassessment of the growth 
outlook, like when the pandemic began in 2020, or during the Liberation 
Day selloff of 2025.
n
Second, there wasn’t a meaningful data deterioration, or even much 
deterioration at all, as happened in summer 2024, or in the 2015-16 selloff.
n
Third, there wasn’t a hawkish repricing like 2022. Admittedly, further rate 
hikes are priced in, but even more hikes were priced in early October when 
the S&P 500 and the NASDAQ hit their record highs. So that factor hasn’t 
changed.
Looking forward, it’s worth bearing in mind that the outright bear markets of the last 
50 years (S&P 500 down 20% or more) have previously occurred when there was 
at least one of (1) a recession, (2) widespread expectations of a recession, or (3) after 
a Fed tightening cycle with multiple hikes.
And much as parallels have been made with the bursting of the dot com bubble, 
remember that was also accompanied by a broader macro slowdown, and then a 
recession in 2001. Indeed, the S&P 500 peaked in March 2000, but only entered 
bear market territory in March 2001, just as the actual recession was starting.
In other words, the present conditions would be historically unusual ones for a bear 
market to occur. The last time a selloff occurred without any of those three points 
above was arguably the “Kennedy Slide” of 1962.

How this differs from the other big risk-off moves of recent 
decades
Generally speaking, the sudden risk-off moves of recent decades have coincided 
with at least one of three things:
1. A fundamental or sudden reassessment of the growth outlook.
n
The Covid-19 pandemic is the most obvious example of this in 2020, when 
the global economy suddenly went into recession, in a manner completely 
unexpected just a few weeks earlier. As is typical in recessions, there was 
huge market turmoil, and the S&P 500 declined by more than a third in just 
over a month. The last time the index fell that fast and that quickly was in the 
Great Depression.
n
Similarly in 2025, the Liberation Day tariff announcements were higher 
than initially expected, which also drove a reassessment of the outlook. 
Although the tariff hikes were then delayed the following week, the 
announcements sparked a big repricing in financial markets, with the S&P 
500 posting one of its biggest 2-day declines (-10.53%) since WWII, only 
rivalled around Black Monday 1987, the height of the GFC, and the initial 
pandemic turmoil.
n
But today we haven’t seen a similar reassessment of the growth outlook. 
Clearly there’s been specific concerns around the idea of an AI slowdown, 
but we haven’t seen a sudden policy shift or an exogenous shock that would 
cause growth to be slower than we thought a month or two ago. Indeed, in 
DB’s latest World Outlook, our economists see global growth picking up 
slightly in 2027, at 3.5%, whilst US growth is only seen slowing by a tenth 
to 2.3%.

2. A meaningful data deterioration that has ramped up recession fears.
n
Plenty of selloffs (large and small) have been driven by weak data, which 
has ramped up fears of a recession or slowdown. It happened in summer 
2024, when there was huge volatility as the yen carry trade unwound. That 
followed several weak data prints, including an ISM manufacturing print on 
1 August 2024, which was unexpectedly bad, and then an underwhelming 
jobs report on 2 August where the Sahm rule was breached. That’s a 
recession indicator, suggesting a recession has begun when the 3-month 
average of unemployment has risen by half a point within a year. 
Immediately, there were fears that a bigger slowdown was coming, and 
although that was avoided, multiple weak data points ramped up fears that 
might happen.
n
Further back in 2015-16, we had a clear data deterioration that led to 
mounting fears of a recession. Indeed, by Q2 2016, the US economy had 
only grown by +1.5% on a Q4/Q4 basis, the slowest since Q3 2011. 
Meanwhile, the ISM manufacturing spent 5 consecutive months in 
contractionary territory from October 2015.
n
Today that’s not happening, and only yesterday we saw a mostly robust set 
of flash PMIs for November. For instance, the German composite PMI hit a 
4-year high of 55.0, whilst the Euro Area number remained steady from 
October at 51.7. Then in the US, the composite PMI still stood at 54.6. So 
whilst a data deterioration is possible, and financial stress has multiple 
channels to the real economy, these are not conditions we are yet 
experiencing.

3. A hawkish repricing for central banks
n
Outside of recessions, another feature that’s coincided with several bear 
markets is some kind of hawkish central bank repricing.
n
That was very clear in 2022, as inflation kept exceeding expectations, and 
markets rapidly reassessed how high the Fed and other central banks 
would hike rates. Then in turn, fears of a recession mounted as the scale of 
monetary tightening surpassed initial expectations. So even though a 
recession didn’t happen, that hawkish policy stance and genuine fears that 
a recession might follow led to a bear market that year.
n
Another example of a non-recessionary bear market came in 1966, when 
there was also a hawkish shift in policy. As in 2022, there was a notable 
growth slowdown over 1966, and the 2s10s yield curve (a leading indicator 
of many past recessions) moved into inversion territory. Meanwhile, the Fed 
were tightening policy to try and rein in building inflation.
n
Today however, the equity declines have not coincided with a hawkish 
repricing. It’s true that markets are pricing in more Fed rate hikes ahead, but 
that was also the case when the S&P 500 hit its record high on October 6. 
Indeed, that day, there were 82bps more hikes priced by the September 
2027 meeting, and today there are only 66bps priced by then. So if anything, 
pricing has shifted slightly dovishly with the equity declines.

Conclusion
Much as last week’s selloff was severe, it’s worth remembering that it has several 
differences with many of the other big risk-off moves in recent times. We haven’t 
seen a sudden reassessment of the growth outlook, or a meaningful data 
deterioration. And we haven’t seen some big hawkish shift from central banks 
either.
Clearly, there’s been stress in the tech space. But even though parallels have been 
made with the dot com bubble bursting, it’s worth remembering from a macro 
standpoint that the bursting of the dot com bubble was accompanied by a wider 
economic slowdown, and then a recession in 2001. Indeed, the S&P 500 peaked in 
March 2000, but only entered bear market territory in March 2001, just as the actual 
recession was starting.
The last time a bear market occurred without one of those three points above was 
arguably the “Kennedy Slide” of 1962. From December 1961 to June 1962, the S&P 
500 fell -28% from peak-to-trough. Admittedly, growth slowed in H1 1962, but it 
was far from a recession. And there wasn’t monetary tightening happening either. 
The main issue at the time were concerns about extended valuations. In the 60 years 
since, bear markets have always coincided with at least one of those three factors, 
so it would be unusual to see one today without those.
