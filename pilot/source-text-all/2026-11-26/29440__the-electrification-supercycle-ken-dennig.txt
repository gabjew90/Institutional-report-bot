The Electrification Supercycle

Executive Summary
Electricity demand is inflecting after roughly 15 years of 
stagnation in the developed world, and the shift is struc­
tural rather than cyclical. In 2024, for the first time in 
three decades outside of a crisis, global electricity demand 
grew faster than the economy. We believe this marks the 
opening of a multi-decade, capital-intensive supercycle 
driven by artificial intelligence, the reshoring of industry, 
and the electrification of transport and heat—a demand 
wave notable for being unusually broad-based and less 
cyclical than its predecessors.
The scale is significant. The IEA expects global electricity 
demand to grow at roughly 3.6% per year over the next 
five years, compared with 2.8% per year over the previous 
decade, or about 1,100 TWh of additional demand each 
year, up from roughly 700 TWh historically. Its mid-year 
update put growth at 3.6% in 2026 and 3.8% in 2027. The 
capital required is enormous and, so far, unmet: clean-energy 
investment reached a record $2.3 trillion in 2025, less than 
half the $5–8 trillion a year needed by 2030. Data centers 
crystallize the story at the margin (around 1.5–2% of global 
electricity today, rising to roughly 3–5% by 2030), but 
breadth, not the data center, is the investable point: data 
centers account for roughly half of United States demand 
growth, yet only a low single-digit share in China.
We see the opportunity across the full electron value chain, 
organized around four pillars:
	•
Generation: natural gas funds the transition as 
the reliable bridge fuel, while we believe nuclear 
is the long-term destination.
	•
The grid and electrical equipment: a binding con­
straint on the entire system, and therefore a defen­
sible margin pool, given multi-year transformer lead 
times and order backlogs that keep growing.
	•
Critical materials: copper above all, alongside ura­
nium, lithium, and aluminum, moving into structural 
deficits as supply constraints meet demand that is 
becoming less cyclical and more strategic.
	•
Enablers: energy storage, behind-the-meter power, 
and grid software that firm and route the system.
The value opportunity comes down to what is implied by 
the price, and the market has capitalized the theme very 
unevenly. The re-rating has happened in the equipment, not 
in the electrons. Global industrials, home to the electrical 
equipment makers, trade on roughly 23x forward earnings, 
a premium of about a quarter to the world index; global 
utilities trade near 17x, a discount of roughly a tenth, on 
a dividend yield more than twice the market’s; and global 
materials, at about 15x, sit at an approximate 18% discount. 
Independent power producers have de-rated to around 
8x EV/EBITDA. Copper makes the case most cleanly: in 
mid-August, with the metal near record highs, the producers 
were pricing in a copper price roughly equal to spot, against 
a three-year average premium of about 17%; copper has 
set further records since. Meanwhile, metals and mining 
capital spending still runs about 30% below its 2012 peak, 
a supply discipline that underpins durable free cash flow.
The risks are real. Demand could disappoint if efficiency 
gains compress the AI curve as they did in the 2010s. A 
recession would drag consumption. Overbuild, commodity 
overshoot, and overpaying for popular pipeline remain live 
concerns. Two developments deserve particular attention. 
First, affordability has moved from rhetoric to policy: whole­
sale prices in the largest U.S. power market rose 76% year 
over year in the first quarter of 2026, Texas has subjected 
new data-center connections to a statewide audit, and in 
late September, federal regulators suspended an emergency 
capacity procurement in that largest market. Second, phys­
ical delivery is slipping even as spending guidance rises. 
Several of these are price risks rather than thesis risks, 
highlighting the need for a margin of safety.
The bottom line, in our view, is that the asymmetry is 
concentrated at the bottleneck—grid equipment, firm and 
dispatchable generation, and copper—where pricing power 
is greatest, demand is least price-sensitive, and, in parts of 
the chain, valuations still sit below mid-cycle levels. The 
debate over electrification has moved past the question 
of whether power demand is rising. What remains is who 
captures the margin on supplying it and at what price 
that margin is owned.

Introduction: From Energy 
Transition to Energy 
Addition
For most of the past decade, “energy transition” has been 
shorthand for substitution: clean power displacing dirty, 
electrons replacing molecules. That framing no longer fits. 
The world has invested more than $15 trillion in clean 
energy over the past ten years, yet fossil fuels still account 
for roughly 80% of the global energy mix on a primary-en­
ergy basis (about 64% on a final-consumption basis; the 
two figures are often, wrongly, used interchangeably). The 
transition looks slow, not because it has failed, but because 
the more powerful dynamic is additive: the world is layering 
enormous new electrical load onto the existing system, even 
as it works to decarbonize it. Energy addition explains why 
demand for almost everything that generates, moves, and 
stores power is rising at once.
Beneath this sits a quieter change: energy is shifting from a 
commodity, extracted at volatile prices, toward a technology, 
manufactured at scale with costs that fall as production 
grows. The cost curves of solar, wind, and batteries are the 
clearest evidence, and energy that behaves like a technology 
rewards scale, learning curves, and incumbency differently 
from a pure commodity.
Four imperatives compete for primacy (affordability, security, 
reliability, and sustainability), and capital follows whichever 
feels most threatened. Sustainability led in 2018–22; the war 
in Ukraine elevated security concerns; AI and data-center 
demand have since elevated reliability; and through 2026, 
affordability has come to the fore, with the U.S. Consumer 
Price Index for electricity up 3.8% in the year to August 
2026, against headline inflation of 3.4%. The theme, there­
fore, no longer depends on any single imperative. Security 
and reliability carry it even if the sustainability agenda cools. 
Affordability, however, has become the issue most capable 
of constraining how the build is paid for.
That durability favours incumbents with scale, order back­
logs, and pricing power. A decade of under-investment has 
left the system under-provisioned just as demand inflects, 
while large parts of the value chain still trade below mid-cy­
cle multiples and replacement cost. Inflecting, less-cyclical 
demand set against cheap, supply-constrained assets is 
precisely the configuration we look for as value investors.
A Brief Historical 
Development of 
Electrification
Global electricity demand nearly doubled between 2000 
and 2023, and in 2024 it accelerated again, rising roughly 
4.4% (about 1,000 TWh, close to Japan’s entire annual 
consumption) to approach 30,000 TWh for the first time. 
For an investor, the shape of the curve matters more than 
its size: after years of steady, almost mechanical growth, the 
rate of change inflected. The recent acceleration is best read 
not as a continuation of the old trend but as a break from it.

A Two-Speed World: Emerging-Market Growth, 
Developed-Market Plateau
The global doubling masked a sharp divergence. Emerg­
ing economies, led by China and India, drove the bulk of 
growth as industrialization, urbanization, and rising living 
standards pulled hundreds of millions of people onto the 
grid; China’s electrification (the share of electricity in final 
energy consumption) has more than doubled since 2005, 
reaching above 27%. The developed world told the opposite 
story. U.S. demand grew just 0.6% a year for 15 years, and 
German demand actually declined as efficiency gains and 
the offshoring of heavy industry offset population growth. 
The resulting consensus among utilities and planners that 
load growth was structurally low and that the task was to 
decarbonize a flat system shaped a decade of under-building. 
This assumption has now been overturned.
Even so, electrification continued to advance: elec­
tricity’s share of global final energy consumption rose 
from around 18% in 2010 to roughly 22% by the mid-
2020s. What changed was its pace and the source of the 
marginal unit of demand.
The Data-Center Parable: 
Why Efficiency Lulled the Market
Data centers offer a cautionary lesson. Their electricity use 
roughly doubled between 2000 and 2005, then went nearly 
flat through the 2010s, even as streaming and cloud com­
puting exploded, as the migration to hyperscale facilities, 
better power usage effectiveness, and higher server utiliza­
tion absorbed the growth in workloads. That achievement 
bred a dangerous intuition: that efficiency could forever 
neutralize demand growth. Generative AI shattered it. 
GPU-dense servers draw far more power per rack than the 
hardware they sit beside, and U.S. data-center consumption 
more than doubled between 2017 and 2023. Efficiency 
is real, but it is not infinite, and betting on it to absorb a 
step-change in workloads can prove costly.
The Hinge: Electricity Decouples from GDP
For three decades, global electricity demand outgrew the 
economy only in the disruption of crises: 2000–01, 2008–09 
and 2020. In every normal year, electricity tracked at or 
below GDP. But in 2024, for the first time outside of a 
shock, it outpaced economic growth.
This decoupling signals that electricity is no longer a passive 
by-product of economic activity but an active input into 
its most dynamic drivers: artificial intelligence, advanced 
manufacturing, electrified transport and the reshoring of 
supply chains. For a value-oriented investor, the significance 
is that the demand now building is durable and broad-based 
rather than cyclical, which changes how the assets that 
serve it should be valued.
A System Entering the Surge Under-Provisioned
The supply side, meanwhile, spent the plateau years pre­
paring for a future that did not arrive. Metals and mining 
capital expenditure ran roughly $50 billion (about 30%) 
below its 2012 peak of approximately $149 billion, starving 
the pipeline of the copper and aluminum the grid now 
requires. U.S. transmission build over the last decade was 
roughly 50% lower in miles than in the decade before, and 
the average age of transformers, transmission lines, and 
other grid equipment is 40–50 years.
The demand inflection of the mid-2020s is therefore meet­
ing a structurally under-built system, with long lead times 
on the equipment needed to expand it. That mismatch—
accelerating, less-cyclical demand on one side, constrained 
and slow-to-respond supply on the other—is the foundation 
for everything that follows. It is the source of the pricing 
power that accrues to incumbents along the value chain, 
and the reason so many of the assets that will meet this 
demand still trade below their mid-cycle worth.

The Macro Shift: Electricity Decoupling from GDP
The International Energy Agency expects global electricity 
demand to grow at roughly 3.6% per year over 2026–2030, 
compared with 2.8% over the previous decade, raising 
consumption from 28,200 TWh in 2025 to 33,600 TWh 
in 2030. Electricity consumption is projected to grow at 
least two and a half times as fast as overall energy demand, 
lifting its share of final consumption from around 21% 
toward 24%, in what the IEA now calls the “Age of Elec­
tricity.” The 2024 decoupling is forecast to persist rather 
than revert: after a weather-affected moderation to about 
3% in 2025, the agency’s July 2026 update raised the near-
term path to 3.6% in 2026 and 3.8% in 2027. Emerging 
economies account for nearly 80% of additional demand 
through 2030, with China alone accounting for close to 
half, adding over five years the equivalent of the European 
Union’s entire current consumption.

Data centers are the most consequential driver at the 
margin, and the one where credible estimates diverge most. 
The IEA puts their consumption at roughly 415 TWh in 
2024, about 1.5% of global electricity, rising to about 950 
TWh, or some 3% of global demand, by 2030, representing 
less than a tenth of global demand growth. Morgan Stanley’s 
estimates are materially higher, at about 5% of global con­
sumption by 2030 and roughly 20% of incremental demand.

a year; Morgan Stanley models about 3% a year and a 
three-quarters share. The Department of Energy’s scenar­
ios project data centers at 6.7–12.0% of U.S. electricity 
by 2028, up from 4.4% in 2023. In China, by contrast,

AI workloads are vastly more energy-hungry than the ser­
vices they replace. Yet utility load forecasts have historically 
overestimated demand, and more conservative analysts 
model AI at around 4.5% of 2030 U.S. electricity demand. 
What is striking, and directly relevant to valuation, is how 
price-insensitive this demand appears. Morgan Stanley’s 
analysis of a fully integrated 100-megawatt AI hyperscaler 
finds that raising power prices from $120 to $140 per MWh 
costs only about 100 basis points of return, with returns on 
equity still well above 20% at roughly $160 per MWh. The 
buyers of incremental power can absorb materially higher 
prices, a condition under which generators, utilities and 
equipment suppliers can earn durable excess returns. The 
corollary is that someone else ends up paying: price-insen­
sitive demand and political sensitivity to household bills 
are on a collision course.
Electrification of Transport, Heat, & Industry
Data centers dominate the headlines, but the broader elec­
trification of the economy may prove even more durable. 
Buildings, including space cooling, heat pumps, and the 
data centers housed within them, are expected to con­
tribute roughly 49% of incremental global demand over 
2025–2030, and transport’s share of demand growth is 
forecast to double to more than 10%. A home’s electricity 
consumption roughly triples once an electric vehicle and 
a heat pump are both in place.
Industry is the third leg. Industrial and commercial power 
demand has been rising faster than at any point in recent 
memory across the U.S., Europe, Japan and Taiwan, 
propelled by reshoring and the electrification of industrial 
heat. In the U.S., essentially all demand growth is now 
commercial and data-center-led: the IEA’s September out­
look has commercial consumption edging above residential 
in 2026, and its October outlook expects commercial use 
to rise a further 2.8% in 2027, while residential use stays 
broadly flat. Industrial electrification and grid hardening 
are policy-spanning and increasingly mandatory, lend­
ing the overall demand profile a stickiness that a purely 
AI-driven story would lack.
Regional Divergence
Regional dispersion is itself an opportunity for a globally 
mandated investor. U.S. demand is expected to grow close 
to 2% a year to 2030, more than twice the rate of the past 
decade, on infrastructure that is conspicuously old. The 
EU is forecast to grow at 2.3% per year, not regaining 2021 
levels before about 2028, while European data centers rise 
from about 2.5% of consumption in 2025 to roughly 5% 
by 2030, with the United Kingdom an outlier at near 12%. 
India is the fastest-growing major market at 6.4% per year; 
China moderates to 4.9% but still accounts for close to half 
of global demand growth.
The most attractively priced exposure need not sit in the 
fastest-growing market. Grid-equipment suppliers and util­
ities in slower-growth but supply-constrained European 
markets may offer a better risk-adjusted entry than assets 
already pricing in the demand they expect to capture. Euro­
pean power prices support the point: German day-ahead 
prices ran around €124 per MWh in mid-August 2026, up 
roughly 65% year over year, with calendar-2027 baseload 
above €100, revenue that flows directly to merchant-exposed 
generators operating heavily depreciated assets.
The Supply-Side Response & Its Bottlenecks
Capital is responding, but the constraint is physical rather 
than financial. Capital expenditure by the four largest U.S. 
hyperscale operators rose from approximately $410 billion in 
2025 to an estimated $725–760 billion in 2026. The grid, 
not generation, is the bottleneck. U.S. interconnection 
queues held roughly 2,060 GW of generation and storage 
at the end of 2025, with a median wait time from request 
to operation exceeding 5 years. Globally, more than 2,500 
GW sits in connection queues, and the IEA estimates 
that one-fifth of the global data-center build-out is at risk 
of delay. Tellingly, the U.S. gas queue grew 86% in 2025 
while every clean-generation category shrank.
The equipment that expands the grid is itself scarce, though 
the shortage varies by class: large power transformers remain 
in short supply for up to four years, while medium-voltage 
units have partially normalized to 14–16 months. Power 
transformer prices are up to 77% higher than in 2019,

slow-to-respond supply base. That asymmetry is where 
margin accrues, and, because much of the chain spent 
the plateau years de-rated and under-owned, it is where 
mispricing is most likely to be found.
Investment Opportunities
The demand case is widely understood; the edge for a value 
investor lies in where along the chain that demand is mone­
tized, and at what price the market lets you own it. We follow 
the physical path of an electron from the materials that 
make up the system, through generation and the grid, to the 
storage that sustains it. We apply one discipline throughout: 
favour the bottleneck, favour demand that cannot easily be 
rationed by price, and require a margin of safety to mid-cycle 
multiples, replacement cost, or net asset value. We discuss 
sectors and industries rather than individual securities.

Generation: Gas as the Bridge, 
Nuclear as the Destination
Natural gas supplies roughly 40% of U.S. electricity and is 
the practical backstop for a reliable grid. The commodity 
has been benign in North America. Henry Hub is expected 
to average about $3.44 per MMBtu in 2026 on record pro­
duction, while European TTF has roughly doubled year over 
year to around €62 per MWh on Middle East supply disrup­
tion. This divergence matters greatly for gas-fired economics 
on either side of the Atlantic. The listed exposure spans 
gas-turbine and power-equipment manufacturers, contracted 
LNG infrastructure, and independent power producers 
whose dispatchable fleets command a scarcity premium in 
a congested grid. The producers are the more interesting 
value setup today: they trade at roughly 8x EV/EBITDA 
after a drawdown of about 30% from their 2024–25 peaks, 
against about 18x forward earnings for the regulated utilities.
Nuclear is the long-term destination rather than a near-
term fix. The capital cycle is vast, with up to $3–4 trillion 
in fission investment over 2025–2050 to reach net-zero, 
and it is already being deployed: approximately 78 GW is 
under construction across 15 countries, about half of it in 
China, against an operating fleet of about 420 GW. Timing 
is the critical caveat. Licensing runs five to ten years, and 
construction typically six to eight; historical cost overruns 
have averaged 3x original estimates, and the most recent 
U.S. large-reactor project came in at 2.6x its original budget. 
All announced corporate nuclear procurement amounts 
to up to 13 GW into the mid-2030s, or about 102 TWh a 
year, less than a fifth of projected data center demand. The 
investable ecosystem spans uranium mining and enrich­
ment, reactor engineering and the operators of existing 
fleets; we treat the technology developers as optionality 
rather than core exposure.
Renewables remain the fastest-growing source of generation. 
The IEA expects roughly 1,000 TWh of annual renewable 
growth over 2026–2030, more than 600 TWh of it from 
solar, though tariffs, trade fragmentation and project delays 
temper the pace. For a global value investor, the attraction 
lies less in the developers than in incumbent European 
utilities, where tightening power markets meet heavily 
depreciated asset bases, and in regulated network operators 
as the lower-volatility expression of the same theme.
The Grid & Electrical Equipment: 
The Real Bottleneck
The grid and the equipment that builds it likely provide the 
most defensible margin pool in the chain, because the asso­
ciated lead times, backlogs and pricing power are structural 
rather than cyclical. Global grid investment runs at approx­
imately $400 billion a year on the IEA’s measure and needs 
to rise by roughly half by 2030; the European Commission 
projects €584 billion of grid investment required by 2030; 
and U.S. investor-owned utilities are projected to invest a 
record $239 billion in 2026, within roughly $1.4 trillion of 
planned spending through 2030. This is mandatory capital 
spending against a regulated or contracted return, not a 
demand forecast, and with transformer capacity additions 
arriving only in phases to 2028 and beyond, the shortage 
and the margin it confers persist.
The opportunity set is broad and far from U.S.-only: the 
North American electrical, power-management and ther­
mal-management suppliers; the European and Japanese 
electrification majors, high-voltage cable makers, and trans­
former manufacturers; and the transmission-and-distribution 
equipment makers of China, Korea, and India, which fre­
quently trade at a substantial discount to Western peers on 
similar or better order growth. Electrical equipment backlogs 
stand at about 1.7x their 2017–19 average, with book-to-bill 
ratios still above 1.0. Where the grid cannot connect load 
quickly enough, fuel-cell and distributed-generation suppli­
ers have won multi-gigawatt offtake agreements from large 
technology buyers—tangible evidence that speed-to-power, 
not levelized cost, is what the marginal buyer is paying for.
Critical Materials: 
Copper, Uranium, Lithium, Aluminum
Copper is the conviction commodity of the theme, and 
the key shift is in the character of its demand rather than 
its level. In China, the electrical network is already the 
largest end-use, accounting for 39% of consumption, and 
grid spending has risen from 32% of national copper use in 
2011 to 38% today, while construction has fallen from 26% 
to 14%. That rotation signals that strategic end-uses are 
growing at mid-single-digit percentages per year, compared 
with roughly 1.6% for core economic uses.
We would keep data centers in proportion: only 1.4–1.8% 
of copper demand today and perhaps 3.2–3.9% by 2030. 
The investable claim is a durable mix shift, not an AI story.

The market has begun to price it: LME copper set succes­
sive records through late August and September, peaking 
near $14,875 a tonne, with COMEX futures above $6.70 
a pound. The physical evidence of tightness is unusually 
clear. Over two-thirds of exchange-monitored stocks sit 
in U.S. warehouses ahead of a still-pending U.S. tariff 
decision. The Commerce Secretary has recommended a 
15% duty on refined copper from January 2027, rising to 
30% in 2028. The inventory situation now leaves roughly 
94% of global demand outside the U.S., with only a thin 
supply, and global spot treatment and refining charges 
reached an unprecedented negative $215 per tonne in July. 
Supply continues to disappoint: Chilean output, roughly 
23% of global supply, fell 6.6% in the first half, and the 
state producer has abandoned its 2030 production target. 
Scotiabank forecasts refined deficits of 435,000 tonnes in 
2026, widening to 750,000 tonnes by 2030 based on con­
servative demand growth. The listed exposure sits in the 
few remaining copper-focused producers and the diversified 
majors with meaningful copper leverage.
Uranium offers a tighter, more concentrated version of the 
same supply-deficit story. The long-term contract price 
reached $97 per pound at the end of June 2026, the highest 
in more than 18 years, and held around $96 per pound in 
September, a premium of roughly $6–10 to spot. A term 
price that leads spot is precisely what one would expect 
if utilities were contracting ahead of a structural short­
fall. Two-thirds of production originates in Kazakhstan, 
Canada, and Australia, a concentration that is both a risk 
and a moat for incumbents.
Lithium is where the demand mix is shifting fastest, and 
where volatility is greatest. Battery-grade lithium carbonate 
in China fell from about $62,800 per tonne in 2022 to 
near $10,400 per tonne in 2025, before roughly doubling 
in the first half of 2026. Demand is projected to reach 2.8 
million tonnes LCE by 2030, a 14% compound rate, with 
energy storage the fastest-growing segment at 25% a year, 
and Scotiabank’s base-case balance turns to a deficit in 
2026. We nonetheless treat lithium with the most caution 
of the four materials: in our judgement, a commodity that 
has fallen more than 80% from its peak and then doubled 
inside three years is better held as higher-beta optionality 
than as a core position, and we have yet to be convinced 
of the industry’s supply discipline.
Aluminum is the quiet beneficiary of grid build, as utilities 
substitute it for copper in high-voltage transmission. Bank 
of America expects the market to move into deficit in 
2026 and widen in 2027 as Chinese output runs against 
its 45-million-tonne cap, and the metal traded around 
$3,250 a tonne in mid-August, up roughly 25% year over 
year. Not all electrification metals have moved together. 
Nickel has been essentially flat in 2026 and remains in con­
tango as the market waits to be convinced that Indonesian 
supply will be controlled.

Valuation & The Value-Investor’s Edge
The differentiated return does not come from identifying 
the trend; it comes from buying the cash-generative core 
of the chain while it is still priced below its own history, 
and from declining to pay growth multiples for a pipe­
line that may not materialize. The market has capitalized 
on the theme very unevenly, and the dispersion is where 
the opportunity now sits.
Copper producers make the case most cleanly. At the end of 
August 2026, with copper near record highs, the mid- and 
large-capitalization producers traded at spot EV/EBITDA 
multiples of roughly 8.6x, 6.5x and 5.8x across 2026E–28E, 
and below 1.1x price-to-net-asset-value against a three-year 
average of 1.4x. Most telling, the group priced in copper 
at about the spot price, versus roughly a 17% premium to 
spot over the past three years and about 9% since 2018. 
The equities are not extrapolating the supply deficit, let 
alone incentive pricing, even as EBITDA margins approach 
50%, and the metal has moved higher since. The diversified 
miners have traded between 0.7x and 1.4x net present value 
through the cycle, toward the lower end since the financial 
crisis, so a value entry can target the trough band just as 
the demand mix turns structurally less cyclical.
The most useful valuation observation for a global investor 
is that the market has paid up for the equipment, not the 
electrons. On MSCI data at 31 July 2026, global industri­
als—home to the electrical equipment manufacturers—
traded at roughly 23x forward earnings against about 19x 
for the world index; the S&P 500 industrials sector, at 
around 25x, was the most expensive of the 11 U.S. sec­
tors and some 40% above its twenty-year average, having 
traded near 16x as recently as late 2022. The other end of 
the chain has not participated. Global utilities traded at 
about 17x, with a 3.2% dividend yield, against 1.5% for 
the index, and have underperformed the world index over 
one, three, five, and ten years. Global materials, at about 
15x, stood at an approximate 18% discount.
Positioning corroborates the point. In Bank of America’s 
mid-2026 fund manager surveys, the most crowded trade 
was long semiconductors, cited by a record 80% of respon­
dents, while power and electrification did not feature at all. 
Interestingly, U.S. contractor backlog fell to 8.0 months in 
July, with data center-exposed contractors at 11.4 months 
against 7.5 months for the rest. Growth is narrowing to a 
single end market. And financing conditions have tight­
ened: hyperscaler bond cover ratios fell from roughly 5x in 
February to below 2x by July.
Generation offers the same proven-cash-flow-versus-priced-
in-pipeline contrast. Independent power producers have 
de-rated to about 8x EV/EBITDA on asset-mix optics and 
affordability-related policy risk, despite owning scarcity-val­
ued dispatchable fleets, a classic setup for patient capital, 
provided the policy risk is underwritten rather than ignored. 
European utilities embody a durable version of the same 
disconnect: the market reflexively applies low multiples to 
commodity-exposed generators, yet German power prices 
up 65% year over year can drive earnings upgrades and free 
cash flow from heavily depreciated fleets. Underpinning this 
is supply discipline: producers that have been slow to add 
capacity channel cash toward shareholder returns instead 
of value-destructive growth. Disciplined supply, structural 
demand, and entry multiples below mid-cycle in parts of 
the chain are the combination at the heart of the case.
Portfolio Implications
We weight the links by conviction and valuation rather 
than headline growth. The core, characterized as durable, 
lower-beta, and cheap on its own history, sits in grid equip­
ment and in regulated or independent power generation, 
where pricing power, order backlogs, and mandatory capital 
spending can be bought at below mid-cycle multiples. Sat­
ellite positions, which are higher-beta and deficit-driven, 
are copper and uranium producers trading below 1.0x price-
to-net-asset-value or replacement value, alongside selected 
storage and enabler industries. Optionality, represented by 
small-modular-reactor and nuclear-fuel developers, behind-
the-meter power and grid software, is underwritten rather 
than paid for, with small position sizes and a refusal to 
capitalize full pipeline value; the 45–80% drawdown in 
listed nuclear developers this year is a live illustration of why.
Several tilts follow from the value lens. We favour firm, 
dispatchable generation where reliability is the attribute 
being priced, the bottleneck equipment and materials the 
system cannot do without, and supply discipline rather than 
growth capital as the driver of free cash flow. The breadth 
of the theme, spanning materials, industrials, utilities, and 
technology, is itself an advantage for a value mandate, offer­
ing real-asset-linked, inflation-aware exposure with secular 
demand behind it and self-funding cash returns ahead.

Risks & What Would 
Change Our Mind
Some of the risks below are genuine threats to the thesis; 
others threaten mainly those who overpay for it. Distin­
guishing the two is itself part of the value discipline.
The first risk is that demand disappoints. Efficiency held 
data center electricity use nearly flat through the 2010s; 
more efficient chips and models could compress the curve 
again, and utility load forecasts have a long record of over­
estimating demand. There is direct evidence of moderation 
ahead: UBS expects hyperscaler capital expenditure growth 
to slow from roughly 76% in 2026 to 25% in 2027 and 6% 
in 2028, and the Bank for International Settlements has 
warned that disappointing returns could trigger a sudden 
pullback in financing. An investor who underwrites the 
high case is exposed; one who buys assets priced closer to 
the low case is not.
A broader macro shock would hit demand through a dif­
ferent channel. McKinsey estimates that a recession in 
which global growth dips to zero in 2027 could lower global 
GDP by as much as 6%, or roughly $7 trillion, by 2035 and 
reduce final energy consumption by up to 4%. Electricity 
has decoupled from GDP at the margin, but it has not 
divorced itself from it.
On the supply side, the risk runs toward overbuild and late 
delivery rather than failed demand. Independent data sug­
gest that 30–50% of the U.S. data center capacity scheduled 
for 2026 is likely to be deferred (only about 5 of roughly 16 
GW is under construction) for supply-side reasons: power 
and grid constraints, construction bottlenecks, skilled 
labour shortages, and local opposition. Spending guidance is 
rising while gigawatts arrive late; both statements are true at 
once. Commodities carry their own cyclicality, as shown by 
lithium’s round trip and the return of storage cost inflation 
in 2026, and even copper demand could be rationed at the 
margin if prices overshoot far enough.
Policy and affordability are where the most material change 
has occurred. Real-time wholesale prices in PJM, the largest 
U.S. power market, averaged $136.53 per MWh in the first 
quarter of 2026, up 76% year over year; its independent 
market monitor attributes approximately $23 billion of 
customer cost increases substantially to data center load 
and expects the effects to persist at least until the end of 
2028. The latest capacity auction cleared at the $325-per-
MW-day cap and still fell 6,831 MW short of its reliability 
requirement. In September, federal regulators suspended 
PJM’s proposed backstop procurement of up to 6.8 GW 
for 2028/29 until early 2027, describing it as deeply flawed. 
In Texas, the governor’s August directive subjected some 
474 GW of large-load requests, roughly 90% of them data 
centers, to a statewide audit; regulators have since allowed 
the grid operator to proceed under a stricter proof-of-read­
iness framework, but BloombergNEF estimates that up to 
50 GW of data center capacity could be delayed. New York 
implemented a comparable pause in July. U.S. residential 
electricity prices are forecast to average 18.2 cents per kWh 
in 2026, rising to 18.7 cents in 2027. For investors, this 
cuts both ways: it threatens the rate-based capital spending 
on which the regulated-utility case partly depends and 
merchant generator margins, yet it reinforces the scarcity 
value of firm capacity and of the equipment that relieves 
the constraint. Some analyses attribute much of the PJM 
capacity price surge to market design rather than data center 
demand per se, and Texas forward prices rose only 11–17% 
over the same period.
The final risk is the one most within our control: paying 
up for the theme. The greatest danger is not that electrifi­
cation fails to materialize but that an investor buys popu­
larity—essentially capitalizing development pipelines and 
connection optionality at multiples that require flawless 
execution. The most expensive sector in the U.S. market 
is now the one most exposed to this build. The mitigation 
is not avoiding the theme, but discipline at the entry mul­
tiple: paying for proven cash flow and treating the pipeline 
as free optionality rather than the base case.
A recession or a demand disappointment would pressure 
the whole theme, and no valuation discipline fully insulates 
a portfolio from a genuine macro shock. But overbuild, 
commodity overshoot, and paying up for pipeline are prin­
cipally price risks, not thesis risks. These are precisely the 
risks a margin of safety is designed to absorb. What would 
change our mind is not volatility in the names, but evidence 
that the decoupling of electricity from economic growth 
had reversed, or that the supply bottlenecks had resolved 
quickly enough to erode pricing power. Absent that, the 
discipline is to use weakness to improve the entry, not 
to abandon the thesis.

Conclusion
For the first time in three decades outside of a crisis, elec­
tricity demand has begun to outgrow the economy. That 
decoupling looks set to define the decade ahead rather 
than fade as an anomaly. This is not a cyclical upswing 
to be traded but a multi-decade supercycle. It is durable 
because no single policy agenda drives it. It is policy-span­
ning because security and reliability carry it even where 
the sustainability impulse cools. And it is capital-intensive 
because the system that must absorb the new load spent 
the prior decade underbuilt. Energy addition, not energy 
substitution, is the organizing reality.
Recognizing the theme is no longer the hard part; the edge 
lies in structure and in price. Weighting exposure toward 
the bottleneck, the grid equipment and firm generation the 
system cannot do without, and weighting toward strategic, 
price-inelastic demand where the most defensible margin 
accrues. Copper sits at the center of the materials case, gas 
funds the bridge, nuclear anchors the long-term destination, 
and the grid remains the constraint that confers pricing 
power on those who relieve it. Across all of it, the discipline 
is the same: pay for proven cash flow, treat pipeline as free 
optionality, and require a margin of safety.
That discipline is what makes this a value opportunity 
rather than merely a growth story, and the opportunity 
has become more selective, not less, over the course of 
2026. The market has re-rated equipment makers to a 
premium, leaving the electrons behind: global industrials 
trade at roughly 23x forward earnings. In contrast, global 
utilities are near 17x, and global materials are near 15x. 
Copper producers no longer price in a premium to spot, mer­
chant-exposed utilities carry the low multiples the market 
reflexively assigns to commodity earnings, and diversified 
miners trade well within their historical valuation band. 
These dispersions reward patience and selectivity rather 
than blanket exposure to a theme.
The debate over electrification has moved on from whether 
power demand is rising; that question is settled. What 
remains is who captures the margin on supplying it and, 
for a value investor, at what price that margin is owned. 
Answering both with discipline, across a chain this broad 
and this constrained, is where we believe the returns of the 
next decade will be found.
