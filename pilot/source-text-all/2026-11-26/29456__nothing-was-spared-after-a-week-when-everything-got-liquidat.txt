 
'Nothing Was Spared': After A Week When 
Everything Got Liquidated, Goldman Asks Is The 
Commodity Puke Over? 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
MONDAY, NOV 23, 2026 - 07:30 PM 
Commodities were supposed to be the hedge. 
Instead, they turned out to be the funding source. According to Goldman's commodity desk, the 
complex got hit by two shocks back to back this week: first a hawkish Fed and strong-dollar 
repricing that lifted real rates, and then an AI-equity deleveraging that culminated in Friday's 
worst stock market session since Liberation Day. Here is how the desk sums it up in its latest 
Weekly Commodity Thoughts (available to pro subs): 
Another wild week in commodities! Commodities were caught in two sequential shocks - first a 
hawkish Fed/strong-dollar repricing that lifted real rates, then an AI-equity deleveraging that 
forced liquidation across commodities irrespective of the dollar. No sector was spared across 
the BCOM complex... 
In other words, by the end of the week, nobody was selling commodities because of 
commodities. The tell, per Goldman, is that the most crowded markets dropped the hardest on 
no headline at all: UK emissions fell 10% with positioning in the 98th percentile of its three-year 
history, and sugar lost 8% (with a Brazilian real kicker) from the 92nd percentile. And the 
high-beta names got it worst: silver down 16% on the week, copper down 10%, which is pretty 
much what Goldman's Privorotsky meant when he warned that rotation has become outright 
liquidation. 
Here is the damage by sector: precious metals and energy each down roughly 14% over 20 
days, and not a single green bar beyond a sliver of one-day energy bounce (figures read off the 
chart, so approximate). 
 

 
 
Copper: The Tightest Chinese Market In 15 Years... And It 
Doesn't Matter 
Copper's 10% weekly drop came from three directions at once: global growth fears (BCOM fell 
5%), bearish Chinese demand feedback from the CESCO conference in Shanghai, and 
expectations of US exports, which triggered heavy short-selling on the SHFE. The desk arrived 
in China with a modest short, and candidly admits what it got wrong: 
Think what we got wrong was the ease at which Trade Houses were able to divert metal to meet 
the Chinese deficit (CESCO feedback >100k MT diverted for Dec), and how negative Chinese 
sentiment is on demand. We think the US has over-imported 1.7mn MT since Jan24, absorbing 
the global surplus, but recent (small) exports have demonstrated to the market that there is a 
release valve, which is disconcerting in a negative demand environment. 
That is the key chart of the copper section: US imports, which ran well above the pre-tariff 
monthly average (at times nearly triple it) for most of the past 18 months, have just fallen off a 
cliff, and the metal America hoarded is now looking for an exit. 
 

 
 
The irony is that the physical market in China has rarely looked tighter. Per the desk, Chinese 
physical is "the tightest we've seen in the 15 years", with the cash arb at +$750/mt, the SHFE 
curve inverted and domestic inventories down 70% year-over-year. But with metal easy to divert 
and Chinese buyers in no rush to restock ahead of the Lunar New Year lull, Goldman's verdict is 
brutal: "the China tightness is at best tangential to price." 
The bull counter: committed AI capex, tight scrap, no mine-supply rebound in 2027, rising State 
Grid tenders and an administration that will "(presumably)" be proactive on trade. That 
parenthetical is doing a lot of work. 
Over at UBS, as we noted on Friday, the trading desk warned to "watch for AI fears to morph 
into a broader growth scare across assets", while UBS Wealth Management called the drop a 
sentiment and inventory story with fundamentals intact. Same bank, same metal, opposite 
conclusions. Diversification at its finest. 
Gold: An Orderly Puke, With CTAs Still Selling 
Gold is now 34% off the year's highs and just had its worst week since March. And while 
Friday's Nasdaq rout did plenty of damage, Goldman stresses this sell-off looks nothing like 
January's chaos: 
Compared to Jan, this sell-off is much more orderly with positioning balanced on aggregate and 
precious failing to perform in a high rates/tech sell-off environment. Our CTA model suggests 
that, despite a large short position, there is further selling in flat or down tapes (with small 
covering on a sharp move higher). ETF positioning remains high as is well flagged but is 
surprisingly sticky. 
 

 
Translation: the trend followers are already short and still have more to sell unless gold rips 
higher. On Friday alone, SHFE gold open interest shed 8.5k lots, a 95th-percentile one-day 
drop. The one bright spot is a growing physical bid from India and Turkey. That is a rather 
different tone from Goldman's top futures trader Robert Quinn, whose "4-5 sigma" panic signal 
we discussed in "Gold's Margin-Call Puke" yesterday, and which historically reverses within a 
week. Same bank, two clocks. 
Meanwhile the vol market is finally pricing the panic, as we flagged on Friday: 
Per the desk, 1-month gold vol rose about 7 vols on the week, with riskies 2 vols further toward 
puts. Yet "we've seen little macro interest to re-engage", with a few specialists buying upside in 
small size and almost no big downside demand. Goldman's own gold delta is small short to flat 
"as gold fails to find support for now." 
 
PGMs held up better, with palladium already extremely short and finding cost-curve support at 
5-year lows. Silver, meanwhile, just learned what a crowded short looks like when it doesn't 
squeeze. 
For perspective on just how extreme this is: 
None of this is a surprise to Goldman's analysts, whose 2027 outlook "It Is 2022 Again" 
(available here for pro subs), which we broke down on Wednesday, expects near-term 
downside and high vol as the Fed keeps hiking, with structural central-bank buying lifting gold to 
$4,650 by end-2027 and $5,600 by end-2028 once cuts begin. GIR also expects silver to 
underperform gold through the hiking cycle, though "another liquidity squeeze could create 
upside." This week was a liquidity squeeze, just in the wrong direction. 
Oil: The Market Has Learned To Look Past Skirmishes 
Crude had another soft week. It slid early on as the Trump-Putin-Xi meeting was read as a sign 
that escalation wasn't imminent, then clawed back some ground on Thursday on reports 
Washington is weighing fresh strikes and that Iran will respond if its vessels are attacked. But as 
the desk puts it, "in reality, any rally was capped by the weakness in the broader macro 
 

 
complex." The physical market seems to have found a floor, with DFLs roughly flat on the week 
and North Sea physical diffs slightly higher. 
The headlines haven't exactly calmed down: 
And yet, the desk is positioned short vol. The most interesting part is where the vol sits: realized 
vol has compressed sharply, but implieds remain elevated across the curve, with traders rolling 
front positions into the 6-12 month bucket. As a result, 12-month Brent vol is now in the 100th 
percentile of its three-year history, while 12-month 25-delta put skew sits in only the 8th 
percentile. 
 
Goldman thinks that's a fade: 
We expect the compression in realised vol to persist as the likelihood of a near-term strike that 
actually causes disruption fades. The market seems willing to look past skirmishes as long as oil 
keeps flowing, and the US administration's rhetoric is increasingly pushing strike risk beyond the 
near term (i.e. this weekend, Trump was cited on Fox saying he is inclined to strike Iran after the 
new year if there is no deal). That said, we like protecting short vol by owning call spreads live in 
the front to guard against an escalation-driven rally. 
Put differently: sell the far-dated fear, buy cheap front-month insurance in case the "after the 
new year" deadline arrives early. It fits neatly with Goldman's 2027 Oil Analyst outlook 
"Adaptation" (also available to pro subs), which we covered in "Goldman Warns Of Massive Oil 
Glut In 2027 As Gulf Barrels Flood Back Around Hormuz" last Monday: Brent averaging $88 
next year and $78 by December 2027, as ship-to-ship transfers and the Hormuz-bypass 
pipelines we have been pounding the table on since March let the market adapt. 
TTF: Very Long, Very Short Gamma, And A Right Tail That Won't 
Die 
The most colorful commentary of the week comes from Goldman's co-head of commodities 
trading Jon Yarrow, whose remarks on the bank's gas outlook call (paraphrased by the desk) 
 

 
explain why EU gas has been so violent since it topped its 2022 highs in October. The short 
version: everybody owns options and everybody runs them live. 
...the scale of OI in the options space is unusual in a couple of ways: 1) in almost every strike is 
massive - from about 40 EUR all the way to 125 EUR - particularly in the Dec contract; and 2) 
only on a couple of occasions this year did implied vol look cheap relative to realized, which 
could suggest that you have a lot of people who hold these options directionally, hold them live... 
If you have the market makers selling vol... you have them hedging the delta. And on the other 
hand, you have the people that are long the options, that are running them live, so the market is 
very short gamma. 
October's rally was "incredibly orderly" until 100 euros, when "there are just not enough people 
left to buy and then the violence hit." Winter balances are still very tight, but two things are 
pushing the other way: Hormuz flows are picking up, and the January JKM basis is so weak that 
"Asia doesn't necessarily want the gas physically in January." 
On the first point, Goldman's research team estimates Hormuz LNG crossings have averaged 
over 60% of pre-war levels month-to-date: 
 
Still, Yarrow isn't ready to call the top: "is it too early to fade the market when you still have that 
right tail intact?" Q1, he says, will be "highly volatile as a base case," and where it ends up is "a 
weather and timing issue." But once Qatar's expansion is flowing, the conclusion is clear: 
Assuming it comes back to a reasonable degree, there is no issue with storage next summer. 
There is really no shortage, especially at this price and even 15 EUR lower. And then that starts 
bringing us to the bear trade in the longer term. 
 

 
That bear trade is in the back end. Summer-29 TTF trades around 28 EUR/MWh while the desk 
sees a downside target in the high teens to low 20s (TTF already touched 20 earlier this year), 
meaning the back of the curve still embeds what Goldman calls "a scarcity premium that the 
fundamentals do not justify." The reasons: a wave of new LNG capacity from the 2025-26 FIDs, 
and demand that may never show up after two gas shocks in five years (Russia in 2022, the 
Gulf in 2026). Goldman's trade is to layer into Summer-28 and Summer-29 downside via 
max-loss structures, since summer tenors are the least exposed to weather and the most 
exposed to the supply wave. 
 
Liquidation First, Fundamentals Later 
Put it all together and this week had little to do with commodities themselves: tight Chinese 
copper, central-bank gold buying and a firm physical oil market all lost to rising real rates and 
the AI unwind. As Goldman's research team warns, even in its base case gold faces "near-term 
downside and elevated volatility... as the Fed continues hiking." 
Which leaves the question of whether this is the washout. Quinn's sigma signal says the bounce 
is close; the desk's CTA model says the systematic sellers aren't done. We lean toward the 
latter, at least until the AI unwind finds a floor. Commodities have spent most of 2026 trading as 
a war hedge, and the past week showed they also trade as a source of liquidity when equity 
margin calls arrive. With the Fed still hiking, the "real asset" trade will probably have to wait for 
the cutting cycle. 
Then again, with Iran hitting US bases in Bahrain and 12-month oil vol at three-year highs, 
someone is still paying up for disaster insurance. It just isn't in gold.
