Rethinking the 60/40 portfolio: what can bitcoin
provide?
Traditional portfolio fundamentals have changed since the post pandemic reset in 2022, with
stock-bond correlations turning positive once again and prompting investors to reassess their
allocations. Here, Jurrien Timmer, Director of Global Macro at Fidelity Investments, examines
the evolving macro environment, opportunities for diversification and the potential future role of
Bitcoin.

Key points
Higher inflation and interest rates are keeping stock-bond correlations in positive territory, weakening a key
assumption behind the traditional 60/40 portfolio and strengthening the need to re-examine portfolio construction
and allocation principles. 
Bitcoin combines the scarcity characteristics of gold with the growth potential of a technology network,
potentially positioning it alongside gold as an allocation option within a broader diversifying approach. 
Bitcoin’s moderating price swings over successive market cycles could signal its gradual maturation as an asset
class, potentially supporting a more established role in portfolio allocations.

For much of the past two decades, traditional 60/40 portfolios benefited from a dependable relationship between
equities and fixed income. Equities generated long-term growth, while bonds provided income and typically
performed well when stocks weakened, providing some defensive positioning to the portfolio.
However, the post-pandemic inflation shock, subsequent interest rate reset and current inflationary environment
have challenged that relationship in recent years. As investors look beyond bonds for diversification, Bitcoin has
entered the discussion through its distinctive combination of monetary scarcity and technology-driven network
growth.
When bonds diversify less
During the 2000s and 2010s, markets were shaped by concerns around weak growth, low inflation and a prolonged
period of financial repression, with central banks maintaining interest rates at or near zero. In this environment,
periods of equity market weakness were often accompanied by falling bond yields and rising bond prices. This
negative correlation underpinned the traditional 60/40 model. The combination proved particularly effective,
historically delivering returns above inflation with relatively moderate volatility.
That dynamic changed following the pandemic. Higher inflation prompted a sharp reset in interest rates, weighing
on bond prices while also placing pressure on equity valuations. While bonds remain viable income-generating
assets, particularly now that yields are higher, their increased correlation with equities means they may no longer
provide sufficient diversification on their own.
As a results, investors may need to re-evaluate their approach and look at allocations through a different lens.
Bonds will not need to be replaced in their entirety - or even to a great extent - but their role as diversifiers may
need to be re-examined and supplemented from other sources. For example, under a 60/20/20 framework, equities
remain the portfolio’s anchor as the largest allocation and bonds retain their income-generating role. However, in
this structure, part of the traditional bond allocation is redirected towards assets and strategies offering attractive
risk-adjusted returns and low or negative correlations with both stocks and bonds.

Why Bitcoin has entered the conversation
It is in this alternate structure that Bitcoin can come into the conversation. Bitcoin is unique because it combines
characteristics that are not typically found in the same asset. Its limited supply gives it scarcity characteristics
associated with gold and other stores of value. At the same time, it is an expanding technology network that can be
considered through Metcalfe’s law, under which a network’s potential value increases as it grows.
These dual monetary and technological characteristics mean Bitcoin may behave more like a store of value in some
environments, but like a higher-growth risk asset in others. Together, these features have earned it a place among
the assets investors may consider alongside other alternatives such as private assets.
This means that, from a portfolio-construction perspective, Bitcoin may sit more naturally alongside gold within the
diversifying allocation. Gold has historically served as a store of value during periods of monetary inflation, when
the purchasing power of bonds can be eroded. To the extent that Bitcoin is seeking to fulfil a similar role, an
allocation could therefore come from the traditional 40% bond allocation rather than the 60% equity allocation.
A different approach to valuation
Additionally, these similarities go further - Bitcoin is similar to gold in one important respect: neither asset generates
cash flows. This means that valuation methodologies can be challenging in a traditional sense and investors need a
robust methodology to rely on. Although conventional discounted cash-flow models do not apply, Bitcoin can still be
assessed through alternative valuation frameworks. One approach is to compare its price with its long-term power-
law curve and historical support trend, which can provide context on where Bitcoin sits within its broader
development and market cycle.
Bitcoin can also be assessed relative to gold. At times, a divergence between the two assets can indicate when
one appears more extended than the other. During the 2026 Iran conflict, for example, central-bank demand and
speculative flows pushed gold well above its longer-term trend while Bitcoin remained comparatively subdued,
making Bitcoin appear undervalued relative to gold under the model presented.
Bitcoin can play a role as allocations shift
In the end, the current macroeconomic environment and dynamics at play mean that investors face an even greater
challenge in portfolio construction that in the past. Reevaluating a robust allocation methodology is certainly
needed as assets no longer fully serve their traditional roles in a 60/40 portfolio. Looking at alternative sources of
diversification means expanding investment horizons outside of equities and bonds, meaning that assets such as
Bitcoin may become more attractive.
However, a key measure of Bitcoin’s maturation will be whether it increasingly behaves like an established store-of-
value asset. The slope of its rallies and the magnitude of its declines have already moderated over successive
cycles. A continuation of this trend would indicate that Bitcoin is gradually moving beyond its historically
pronounced boom and bust profile and could establish itself as a more reliable component of asset allocations in
the future.
