The LNG capacity trap
Qatar is paying competitors to replace missing supply today. Its return could pressure the new projects
built to replace it tomorrow.
QatarEnergy bought 33 U.S. LNG cargoes this year versus four a year ago.
In plain English, one of the world’s biggest low-cost LNG suppliers has temporarily become a customer
of the U.S. competitors filling its gap.
The shortage is doing more than raising prices. It is helping competing supply prove that it is needed.
The capacity winning today could therefore become tomorrow’s excess supply.
Reuters
The shortage pays
Right now, the market is short of LNG. Buyers are searching farther away for replacement cargoes,
supporting near-term prices and forcing some ships to travel longer distances.
The parts of the market with the most flexibility benefit most. These include sellers that can redirect
cargoes to the highest-paying region, companies with gas not locked into fixed-price contracts and
ships able to capture today’s higher market rates.
For now, flexibility is the asset.
The capacity trap
The risk flips when Qatar comes back.
The U.S. plants and other projects that stepped in during the shortage do not disappear just because
Qatar starts exporting normally again.
At the same time, China is now expected to need less additional LNG than analysts once thought. That
creates a simple problem: more sellers may be chasing fewer new customers.
Not every U.S. plant is equally exposed. Projects with long-term customers already secured are better
protected.
The vulnerable group is simpler: new or expanded projects that still need customers, still need final
approval or depend heavily on high LNG prices to make the economics work.

Where the money may be
This gives us a relative-value framework rather than a simple long-or-short LNG call.
During the shortage, flexibility matters. When Qatari supply returns, the advantage shifts to contract
quality. The projects most exposed are those that still need customers, or permanently high LNG
prices, to justify being built.
TME
The next knock on effect
There is one more step.
Many long-term forecasts for U.S. natural gas assume that a large number of LNG plants will be built
and will require huge amounts of U.S. gas as their raw material.
If too many LNG projects are delayed or cancelled, that future demand for U.S. gas becomes smaller.
That would remove some of the support behind higher U.S. gas prices several years from now.
This is not necessarily a bearish Henry Hub call today. It is a longer-dated risk to the demand story
currently embedded in U.S. gas forecasts.
What would prove this wrong
The new supply may be absorbed if Asian demand rebounds strongly as prices fall. Construction delays
could also prevent too many plants from arriving at the same time. And if Qatar remains impaired for
longer, competing projects will have more time to sign long-term customers.
The warning sign is simple: Qatar’s recovery becomes more visible while new LNG projects begin
struggling to find buyers or secure final approval.
Qatar’s absence is helping finance its future competitors. Its return will reveal which of
them never deserved to be built.
