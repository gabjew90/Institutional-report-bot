US Federal Reserve 
(Fed)
October Pause, December Live
The Fed raised the federal funds target range 25 bps to 3.75%–4.00% in September, unanimously 
reversing July’s 9–3 decision to hold. The Fed’s statement dropped July’s reference to supply shocks, 
stating simply that “inflation remains elevated” and the hike would support a “timelier return” to 2%.
Economic activity was characterized as solid, with resilient spending, strong productivity and robust 
capital investment. The SEP reinforced the hawkish shift, with higher growth and inflation forecasts and 
lower unemployment. The median policy-rate projection rose to 4.1% for both 2026 and 2027, versus 3.8% 
and 3.6% in June, implying another hike this year and a higher-for-longer path. The longer-run rate also 
edged higher, reinforcing the sense that policymakers see less policy restraint than previously assumed. 
The September minutes confirmed broad support for tighter policy. All participants backed the hike. Many 
viewed a higher rate path as prudent insurance against persistent inflation, several judged policy to be  
not restrictive or only mildly restrictive, and most expected another increase by year-end. Fed Chair Kevin 
Warsh echoed this, arguing inflation was “too high” and financial conditions were not restrictive, although 
he avoided committing to a preset sequence and stressed data dependence. Since then, however, the 
tone has softened at the margin. September nonfarm payrolls rose only 29,000, unemployment increased 
to 4.2%, and Federal Open Market Committee members Williams and Jefferson have emphasized 
patience, while Fed committee members Logan, Barr and others still see further tightening as necessary. 
August core PCE eased to 3.0% year over year (y/y), while consumption remained firm, leaving the infla-
tion-growth mix uncomfortable but less clearly one-sided. Markets heavily favor an October hold but are 
pricing a December hike; we expect a pause in October, with December likely to see a hike of 25 bps.
Bank of Canada (BoC)
On Hold for Now, but Hawkish Risks Building
The Bank of Canada (BoC) left its policy rate unchanged at 2.25% in September, extending the pause that 
began in late 2025. Relative to July, the message was more cautious. There were no new forecasts, with data 
evolving broadly in line with July projections. Growth had strengthened, with second-quarter (Q2) gross 
domestic product (GDP) rising 3.3%, but the BoC emphasized that new US tariffs and renewed trade uncer-
tainty could undermine the durability of the recovery. Inflation was still running near 3%, largely because of 
gasoline, yet the BoC judged that upside risks had increased as high energy prices persisted and tariff-re-
lated costs threatened broader pass-through. Macklem stressed that policy would continue to look through 
the direct impact of energy prices, but not if those pressures became more persistent or spread across the 
Consumer Price Index (CPI) basket. His September 21 remarks sharpened that message, warning that the 
BoC did not want to be “too slow to respond” if inflation persistence increased. Data since the meeting have 
been mixed. August headline CPI remained at 3.0%, while inflation excluding gasoline rose to 2.4% from 
2.2%, suggesting some broadening. Meanwhile, July GDP was flat, although Statistics Canada’s advance 
estimate points to 0.2% growth in August, leaving the growth picture uneven. Markets have shifted materi-
ally more hawkish, with a meaningful probability of a December hike, followed by an additional 75 bps by 
July 2027. While the BoC will likely remain on hold in October, risks have shifted toward renewed tightening 
if core inflation firms further and growth remains resilient.

European Central 
Bank (ECB)
From “No Brainer” to More Nuanced Tightening
The ECB raised interest rates for the second time this year in September, with a hike of 25 bps to 2.5%, 
in line with market expectations. It was a unanimous decision by the Governing Council, one that ECB 
President Christine Lagarde described as a “no brainer.” Renewed pressure on energy prices over the
summer pushed inflation firmly above 3%, warranting caution also in light of a surprisingly resilient growth 
print in Q2. The central bank’s new forecasts show that the baseline was revised up for 2027 GDP growth, 
to 1.4% (+0.2 percentage points [pp] vs. June) and for the Harmonized Index of Consumer Prices (HICP)
to 2.5% (+0.2 pp), while the core HICP was also seen higher at 2.6% (+0.1 pp).The alternative scenarios were 
also updated, with the adverse scenario seeing the HICP at 3.2% and real GDP growth at 1.1% in 2027, both 
higher than in the June forecasts and consistent with the more resilient growth picture. Technical assump-
tions included market forward rates rising to 3%, 40 bps higher than in June, leaving some theoretical room 
for further action in tightening territory now that the ECB is at the top end of the range of its neutral rate 
estimates. However, the ECB is still in a “moderate response” mode, with current futures prices for oil and 
gas sitting somewhat between the baseline and adverse scenario assumptions. Inflation risks are still judged 
to be tilted to the upside given the prolongation of the energy shock, while growth risks are seen to the 
downside, albeit likely to a lesser degree than initially thought. Importantly, indirect and second-round 
effects have remained very limited, if not absent, so far. Core HICP has remained well-behaved since the 
beginning of the Middle East conflict over seven months ago, while wage growth is expected to remain 
close to 3% in 2027, a broad equilibrium level. We continue to expect the ECB to remain non-committal 
about the rate path ahead but to continue leaning on the cautious side, without urgency, as long as energy 
price volatility remains elevated and geopolitics fluid. A December hike now appears likely, but further tight-
ening beyond that is not our base case, given that higher short- and long-term rates could weigh on growth 
and that some form of energy price normalization cannot be excluded. Some cuts in the second half of 
2027 seem likely, and the further the ECB hikes, the more of that tightening will ultimately be reversed.
On the succession of the ECB Governing Council, the race to replace Lagarde, Schnabel and Lane is now
fully under way, but it is unlikely that policymaking will be revolutionized by the next ECB leadership.
Bank of England 
(BoE)
Dragged to Hike
The BoE kept the Bank Rate unchanged at 3.75% at both its July and September meetings, broadly in line 
with expectations, as the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) remained reluctant to act unless forced to. 
The vote splits point to a more balanced debate, though, with the same 6–3 split at both meetings and the 
dissenters favoring a hike for risk-management reasons. With renewed energy pressure over the late 
summer, the tone turned more hawkish in September. Guidance was unchanged at “stands ready to act as 
necessary,” but inflation risks were considered to have increased materially since July, with a growing case 
for action before the emergence of second-round effects. Headline inflation surprised meaningfully to the 
upside, with CPI now expected at 3.75% in the fourth quarter (Q4) and above 4% in the first quarter (Q1), 
while growth was surprisingly solid in Q2, driven by high value-added services and business investment. 
The labor market still shows some slack, but with signs of stabilization. While the rise in inflation is almost 
entirely due to energy prices, a precautionary approach to prevent a broadening of price pressures is 
increasingly taking shape as the growth trade-off appears less severe. At least one hike now looks likely 
sooner rather than later, most likely in November. A further adjustment is possible if energy prices remain 
high, but a broader hiking cycle seems unlikely given the already relatively high level of Bank Rate.
Regarding quantitative tightening (QT), the BoE announced a fixed multi-year plan, replacing annual
announcements, to reduce gilt holdings to zero by year-end 2034, with an average reduction of £46 billion 
per year, including £20 billion per year of active sales. Only gilts maturing between 2035 and 2049 will be 
sold, while £120bn of longer-dated gilts will be retained to back banknote issuance. Overall, this reduces 
market attention on annual QT announcements and should ease term-premium pressure at the long end, 
while active sales will be suspended for six months, making the overall approach more dovish than expected.
Back to the Peak
Norges Bank hiked its policy rate 25 bps to 4.5% in September, following an earlier hike in May. This takes the 
rate back to its post-pandemic peak, reached in late 2023 and maintained until mid-2025, thus reversing
a short normalization period. While consistent with its June guidance that another hike was necessary, the 
September decision was a close one, as core inflation and growth momentum came in below expectations 
over the summer, making it more of a coin flip. However, even after the hike, the asymmetry remains hawkish. 
The updated rate path indicates some probability of another hike toward year-end or in Q1, with cuts only 
starting in Q4 2027 and an even more gradual normalization toward 3.25% by the end of the decade, 
signaling a higher-for-longer stance. A weaker Norwegian krone, sticky price and wage expectations, lower 
spare capacity or higher imported inflation could all trigger the move. It is always risky to underestimate 
Norges Bank’s hawkishness, but being back at the peak policy rate and signaling a prolonged restrictive 
stance could be enough for domestic inflation to converge back to target, particularly as wage growth is 
expected to continue normalizing next year, to around 4%. Therefore, we expect a prolonged hold with  
some residual risk of another hike, followed by a shallow and gradual cutting cycle from late 2027.

Sveriges Riksbank
Walking Toward Neutral
The Riksbank kept the policy rate at 1.75% at its September meeting, in line with expectations and guidance. 
The lean was hawkish, with more explicit guidance toward a rate hike in 2026 on the back of an upgraded 
outlook. Spot inflation remains subdued by last year’s budget measures, with core inflation also relatively low. 
However, strong growth momentum, a weaker Swedish krona and the energy shock all point to further infla-
tionary pressure down the line. Forecasts were upgraded for both growth, following strong data over the 
summer, and inflation, which is now seen averaging above target through 2028. The rate path was also raised 
significantly compared with June, flagging a hike in Q4 and a higher terminal rate close to 2.5%, to be 
reached already by the end of next year. A hike by year-end to 2% now looks a foregone conclusion, most 
likely already in November, while another move in 2027 is feasible. Further upward adjustments, however, 
remain conditional on firmer core inflation and continued growth momentum.
Marginally Hawkish Risks, No Hike Yet
The SNB left its policy rate unchanged at 0% in September, as expected, but the backdrop has shifted 
modestly more hawkish since June. The SNB revised its conditional inflation path higher to 0.7% in 2026 
and 0.8% in both 2027 and 2028. Since the meeting, September CPI has risen further to 1.0% y/y, a two-year 
high, although prices were unchanged during the month. The increase remains concentrated in energy, 
while core inflation edged only to 0.5%, suggesting limited broadening in underlying pressures. Growth was 
also upgraded materially, with the SNB now expecting 1.5%–2% growth in 2026, versus around 1% previously. 
The currency message also changed. In June, the SNB stressed an “increased willingness” to intervene 
against rapid and excessive franc appreciation. With the Swiss franc (CHF) around 3% weaker on a trade-
weighted basis since June, that language was dropped in favor of a more neutral commitment to remain 
active in foreign-exchange markets “as necessary.” SNB Chair Martin Schlegel attributed the depreciation 
largely to wider rate differentials and stressed that medium-term inflation pressure had increased only 
slightly. The CHF could still benefit from renewed safe-haven demand as European bond-market stress lifts 
global risk aversion and its role as a defensive alternative to the euro. Although markets shifted aggressively 
toward tighter policy immediately after the September meeting, expectations have since been pared back, 
with roughly 50 bps of hikes expected by September 2027. That still looks more aggressive than the SNB’s 
own communication, particularly with underlying inflation still subdued. Our view is that the SNB is likely to 
remain on hold through year-end, with any 2027 hike contingent on broader inflation pressure.
Bank of Japan 
(BoJ)
December Hike on the Table
As widely expected, the BoJ raised the policy rate 25 bps to 1.25% at its September meeting in a 7–2 deci-
sion. The two most recent government appointees to the Board and considered most dovish, Asada and 
Sato opposed the hike. They claimed inflation was still below 2% and economic activity not strong enough 
to warrant more tightening. The Summary of Opinions, published weeks after the meeting, suggested a 
clear divide between these two members and the rest of the Board, the latter sticking to the need for 
further rate hikes as financial conditions continue to be accommodative. Meanwhile, most recent comments 
by Sato showed her being in favor of rate hikes, albeit in conjunction with strong assessment of consump-
tion and income growth. We stick to our call for another 25 bp hike in December. Economic activity 
continues to be resilient, with the September Tankan survey showing all-industry diffusion index reaching
a 25-year high. Inflation too, barring subsidies remains on the stickier side (as evidenced in stronger than 
expected September Tokyo CPI number led by a return of the water utility bills which was suspended over 
the summer). The Board is also gradually looking at higher neutral rates than its own current nominal range 
assumption of 1.1%–2.5% (which at 2% target translates into real rates in the range of -0.9% to +0.5%). This 
sits on the lower side and will need to be revised up subsequently, in our view. In addition, persistent yen 
weakness will likely require more rate hikes going forward, than less.

Reserve Bank of 
Australia (RBA)
Powering Through with Hikes
The RBA has raised the cash rate by 100 bps cumulatively year-to-date, with the latest 25 bps hike in 
September. Its September decision was unanimous, with the post-meeting tone on the hawkish side. There 
was a mention that inflationary risks have gathered momentum since the last meeting in August, owing to 
risks in global oil supply as well as stronger growth domestically. Higher fuel prices have also been partially 
passed on to goods and services within the economy. The RBA is also cognizant of controlling inflationary 
impulse, which would require a slowdown in aggregate demand to bring capacity pressures and inflation 
within the central bank’s target. While rate hikes since starting of the year have helped tighten financial 
conditions, inflation still remains sticky and beyond comfort. The RBA therefore hiked in September to 
tighten financial conditions further, and we think that given the hawkish bent of the commentary since then 
will mean at least one more hike in the near-term, most likely in November. In addition, weak productivity 
growth, which has likely fallen 0.2% y/y in Q2 2026 is pulling potential growth lower and unit labor costs 
higher. Both these developments are keeping overall demand above potential, thereby adding to infla-
tionary pressures. These risks will leave the RBA with little option but to tighten policy enough in order to 
cool down demand for inflation to return to target. As growth begins to cool, the door for easing will eventu-
ally open up sooner, likely by end 2027.
Reserve Bank of 
New Zealand (RBNZ)
On the Way Up to ‘Neutral’
After an expected and weak Q2, growth is in a solid recovery phase in the third quarter, with surveys 
showing solid expansion in both manufacturing and services sectors. The labor market is in a resilient mode 
as well, with wages picking up and high labor-force participation rates (which pushed up the unemployment 
rate sharply but primarily owing to excess supply). Inflation, meanwhile, has spiked up above the RBNZ’s 
target with core inflation also moving upwards. Rising global commodity prices and El Niño add further risks 
to inflation. Despite softer growth, the RBNZ began adjusting policy settings in July, and has since raised 
rates by a cumulative 50 bps so far. The central bank has reiterated that it will raise policy rates further 
toward more neutral settings in order to move inflation back to the mid-point of its 1%–3% target range.
Recent policymaker comments have been hawkish, suggesting the central bank would likely want to take 
the policy rate to 3.00% as early as October. We expect one to two more rate hikes in Q1, to a terminal rate 
of 3.50% by mid-2027. Despite multiple hikes, this would still be well below terminal rates in the United 
States or Australia, indicative of the relative weaker growth outlook. In addition, the resilience in growth so 
far has not translated into higher housing prices, which remain underwhelming in this cycle. This will 
constrain the RBNZ’s aggressiveness in future action, in our view.
Bank of Korea (BoK)
Strong Growth Fueled Tightening
In back-to-back meetings, the BoK raised its policy rate, with an increase of 25 bps to 3.00% in August, 
following a similar move in July. Strong growth led by artificial intelligence demand likely drove the hike, 
as the Board raised its GDP projections to 3.3% for 2026 from 2.6% and for 2027 to 2.9% from 2.1% earlier. 
Growth continues to progress resiliently on multiple fronts, including exports and investments which are 
fueling service sector employment prospects, thereby adding to consumption as well. The August hike 
was, however, not a unanimous decision, with one member voting for a hold (6–1 split in favor of a hike). 
Interestingly, the inflation forecasts were broadly kept unchanged at 2.7% and 2.3% y/y for 2026 and 2027, 
respectively, both above the 2% target. Risks of demand fueled inflationary pressures persist. The Board’s
dot plot, which provides a six-month ahead guidance, was less certain about the extent and duration of the 
current tightening cycle. Expectations around the terminal rate were also divided. We expect one more rate 
hike to 3.25%, by end Q1 2027. Looking at the Monetary Policy Committee’s “dot plot” projections, 10 out of 
21 dots showed a preference for the terminal rate at 3.25% and unchanged inflation forecasts mean the BoK 
is comfortable with the current outlook on inflation despite the strong growth tailwinds. This sits well with 
our broad assessment that this tightening cycle will likely be a more measured one.

People’s Bank of China 
(PBoC)
PBoC Opening the Door to (Limited) Easing?
The PBoC retained its “appropriately loose” stance at its September Monetary Policy Committee meeting, 
but the language shifted toward near-term easing. Compared with July, the PBoC dropped its emphasis 
on cross-cyclical adjustment and instead stressed stronger counter-cyclical support, suggesting a greater 
willingness to respond to near-term weakness, rather than preserving policy space or leaning against
longer-term imbalances. The economic diagnosis remained familiar: Broadly stable growth characterized by 
strong supply, weak demand and structural divergence, while policy should support a “reasonable rebound” 
in prices. Incoming data remains mixed. August CPI rose 0.8% y/y and core inflation reached 1.0%, while the 
Producer Price Index accelerated to 3.8%, partly reflecting higher commodity prices. The Purchasing 
Managers’ Index of manufacturing improved to 50.1 in September, returning to expansion, although property 
and domestic demand remain weak. Meanwhile, the yuan reached a multi-year high and the PBoC became 
somewhat more tolerant of appreciation, which reduces one constraint on further easing by giving the PBoC 
more room to cut rates without immediately reigniting depreciation pressure. Post-meeting action rein-
forced the easing bias. The PBoC cut the one-year Pledged Supplementary Lending rate 25 bps to 1.5%, 
expanded its scope, and raised relending and rediscount quotas by ¥700 billion. It also has continued to 
provide ample liquidity. Finance Minister Lan Fo’an October 1 comments signaled faster fiscal spending and 
further targeted measures. Markets continue to expect limited further easing rather than large-scale stim-
ulus. We expect targeted liquidity support to remain the preferred tool, with the possibility of a 10-bp rate 
cut and 50 bp cut to the reserve requirement ratio in Q4.
Reserve Bank of India 
(RBI)
Joins the Party
The RBI joined the global tightening bandwagon, raising the policy repo rate by 25 bps to 5.50% in a unani-
mous decision in October. The policy stance was changed to “calibrated tightening” from “neutral” in a 4–2 
vote, clearly indicating that the direction of interest rates is now up, ruling out any rate cuts even as the 
Monetary Policy Committee will remain data-dependent for further action. The action and change in stance 
are measured approaches in our view, given the rise in inflation in recent months even as growth remains 
broadly resilient. Core CPI remains subdued but is rising sequentially, and headline CPI will likely breach the 
RBI’s target upper band of 6% in Q4, exacerbated by unfavorable base effects. The RBI acknowledged these 
risks, especially amid resurgent oil prices and a weak rupee as well as tighter global financial conditions on 
materially higher bond yields. The RBI raised its fiscal year (FY) 2027 (ending March 2027) CPI forecast to 
5.2% (from 5.0% earlier) while upgrading its GDP forecast to 7.1% from 6.7% previously. Given this backdrop, 
we expect two more rate hikes this cycle, taking the repo rate to 6.00% by end FY 2027. Further hikes 
cannot be ruled out, especially if inflation remains sticky and elevated. The probability of this occurring will 
go up even further if liquidity remains flux as is currently, following the RBI’s foreign-currency-denominated 
term deposit inflow scheme. Although the central bank refrained from announcing more specific measures 
in October, proper absorption of excess liquidity (still in excess of ₹5 trillion) will be required for rate hikes to 
be transmitted more meaningfully to the wider economy.

Franklin Templeton Fixed Income (FTFI) Central Bank Ranking Methodology
Each central bank is scored on three parameters: Inflation Outlook Perception, Quantitative Easing/Liquidity Management Programs, and Interest Rate Forward Guidance. Each parameter can be 
scored from a range with a minimum of –2 (dovish) and a maximum of +2 (hawkish). The methodology for scoring compares the latest monetary policy statement/press statements with prior ones to 
see how the language and tone regarding each of these parameters may have changed over time. The scores are ultimately aggregated for each central bank, with a final FTFI score ranking each 
from –6 (for most dovish) to +6 (for most hawkish). We also provide our one-year ahead policy rate expectations and compare our rankings and expectations with market implied policy rates to eval-
uate how the difference between our expectations/rankings and market expectations/rankings.

WHAT ARE THE RISKS?
All investments involve risks, including possible loss of principal.
Fixed income securities involve interest rate, credit, inflation and reinvestment risks, and possible loss of principal. As interest rates rise, the value 
of fixed income securities falls. Low-rated, high-yield bonds are subject to greater price volatility, illiquidity and possibility of default.
International investments are subject to special risks, including currency fluctuations and social, economic and political uncertainties, which 
could increase volatility. These risks are magnified in emerging markets.
Sovereign debt securities are subject to various risks in addition to those relating to debt securities and foreign securities generally, including, 
but not limited to, the risk that a governmental entity may be unwilling or unable to pay interest and repay principal on its sovereign debt. To the 
extent a strategy focuses on particular countries, regions, industries, sectors or types of investment from time to time, it may be subject to 
greater risks of adverse developments in such areas of focus than a strategy that invests in a wider variety of countries, regions, industries, sectors 
or investments.
