Global Risk Barometer*: Risk Level Remains at High
*The geopolitical risk barometer builds on our analysts’ discretion and their judgment regarding the overall geopolitical tensions. As economists, we primarily assess geopolitical risks from the perspective of the global economy and financial markets. Hence, 
in our assessment minimal to low risk would refer to an environment where geopolitics is unlikely to trigger any economic or market turbulence. When the overall risk is extreme or very high, geopolitical risks would likely dominate the markets’ agenda.
Our risk assessment and how it has changed since our previous update
Overall risk assessment: HIGH
We maintain our risk assessment at HIGH. Despite relative calm between the US and Iran, 
fighting between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia has escalated. President Trump’s 
announced “energy ceasefire” between Ukraine and Russia seems non-existent, as 
Russia continues its assault. Ukraine, on the other hand, has been able to inflict 
significant long-lasting damage to Russian refineries (read more on p. 8)
What has changed since the previous update? 
As we write in our Editorial, p. 4., central bankers convened for their annual precious 
metal conference.  CBs are increasing their gold holdings, as a response to rising 
geopolitical and market uncertainty. Not only are they scaling up their gold reserves, but 
they are also diversifying the custodians and repatriating some of the physical gold.
What to watch in October and early November?
➢Brazilians will head to the polls on the 25th of October. Jair Bolsonaro’s son, Flávio 
Bolsonaro, is the favourite to win (read more on p. 12).
➢Israeli election on 27th of October has been characterised as an “election like no other”. 
Netanyahu’s bloc and the fragmented opposition are running neck and neck in polls.
➢US mid-term election on the 3rd of November. Will Trump re-escalate attacks against 
Iran after the mid-terms?

Gold prices are far from the all-time highs reached in January, but price developments 
this year disguise a long-term trend of central banks increasing their gold reserves. 
This trend has been most pronounced in the large EM economies, where central banks 
with the exception of Russia have notably increased their gold holdings.
But not only are central banks buying more gold, they are also moving it around 
physically. In a survey by the World Gold Council (WGC), released in September, central 
bank reserve managers were increasingly saying to have made custody arrangements 
over the past year to increase domestic storage or diversify overseas storage 
locations. Also, a rising share of respondents was planning to diversify overseas 
locations going forward. Bank of England remains the most trusted custodian, followed 
by domestic storages and the BIS, but the attractiveness of domestic storages seems 
to have increased over the past 7 years.
The reasons for central banks moving their gold holdings are three-fold. Increasingly, 
as the WGC says, they are optimising across custody risk, physical accessibility and 
market liquidity. The political decision to freeze Russian reserves after the full-scale 
invasion of Ukraine, is undoubtedly something that central banks especially in the EM 
factor in as they reconsider the physical location of their reserves. 
As such, few central bankers in the West are explicitly saying they are diversifying a 
US-related risk, and the Federal Reserve has kept its position as the fourth most 
trusted custodian for gold. That said, last week, when central bankers met in Italy for 
the London Bullion Markets Association conference, Gioia Cellai, deputy director at 
Banca d’Italia, said: ”Gold is something physical that you can store in your vaults, and so 
it’s perceived somehow less exposed to sanctions or friends becoming enemies”.

➢Yemeni Houthis have escalated their warfare against Saudi Arabia. At least
12 people were killed in a Riyadh airport attack, including one US citizen. 
Yemeni government forces said in early October that they had captured
control over the Bab el-Mandeb strait, but according to recent media reports, 
fighting is still ongoing.
➢In our previous radar, we wrote about the ”Islamic NATO in the making”. On 
October 7th, Pakistan and Turkey agreed to provide ”practical measures” to 
defend Saudi Arabia against Houthi attacks. Details about what it means in 
practice, remain scarce. The Houthis have threatened to launch ballistic
missiles towards Pakistan and attack Pakistani vessels in retaliation. 
➢In early October, Saudi Arabia was able to carry 5.8Mb/d of oil via its East-
West pipeline. Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman said pipeline
capacity to outside the Gulf could reach 10Mb/d within the next 2-3 years.
➢The ISW’s analysis on Iran’s new militant leaders shows how the new regime
comprises of hardline figures, many of whom have played key roles in Iran’s
wars in the past and in violent suppression of domestic protests.
Saudi Arabia Under Attack, Trump Mulls Post-Midterms Escalation
➢Will the US escalate after the mid-terms? According to the NYT, in recent 
months, Mr. Trump’s advisors have made five proposals for major operations 
against either Iran or the Houthis, but each time the President has vetoed. 
Similarly, the US response to Iran’s recent attacks against ships in the SOH 
has been muted. The officials are now discussing the option of a 3-day 
campaign focusing on Iran’s missile and drone arsenal. In a significant 
buildup of troops, by the end of October, the US will have three aircraft 
carriers in the Middle East. Still, a renewed US attack after the mid-terms is 
hardly certain as interceptor stockpiles are depleted and senior US officials 
are increasingly sceptical that military action would force Iran surrender.

➢Ukraine said on October 14th that it had been left without US warnings of 
incoming Russian missiles two nights in a row. In the past two days, Ukraine 
has managed to intercept only four out of 44 incoming missiles. Starlink 
connections also appear limited. Trump has said the US has not cut off 
Ukraine’s access to US intelligence, but the newly appointed White House 
Press Secretary Katie Zacharia evaded the question in a recent presser.
➢In early October, Ukraine said its long-range strikes had neutralised 51% of 
Russia’s oil refining capacity. In October, President Putin said Ukraine’s 
attacks had cost Russia roughly 1% of GDP. The IEA says that as Ukraine has 
increasingly targeted secondary refining units that are more complex than 
the crude distillation units, repairs in some cases could take 6-8 months.
➢Trump invited President Putin to the December G20 meeting in Miami.
➢BOFIT released their economic projections for Russia for years 2026-2028. 
GDP growth has stagnated at around 1% this year, and will slow further next 
year. Financing the budget deficit has become more difficult but Russia is 
still able to sustain the financing and production needed for the war.
Long-Lasting Damage to Russian Refineries
➢Ukrainian intelligence says Russia is planning an intensifying campaign on 
the country’s water and energy supplies in the winter. We keep an eye on any 
talks regarding an actual energy ceasefire. Russia’s air campaign is a sign of 
weakness on the frontlines, and a reciprocal halt to strikes on energy 
infrastructure, if actualised, could form a basis for a broader ceasefire.
➢The IEA says that combined Middle East and Russian diesel exports fell to 
52kb/d in August, 75% below the same month last year. The decision by the 
Trump administration to allow Russian diesel imports to the US has faced 
strong objection. We keep an eye on how this deal evolves.

EU-China Trade Tensions Easing For Now

Danske Bank
10
➢EU-China trade tensions eased after trade negotiations in Beijing led 
to a 16-point consensus spanning trade and investment, export 
controls, and intellectual property right. EU subsequently announced 
a cap on imports of hybrid cars at 350.000. Bloomberg on 14 October 
reported Germany is pushing for caps on more products while China 
has warned of ‘new tricks’. EU leaders meet on 15-16 October where 
EU protectionist measures will also be on the agenda.
➢US-China relations looked good on the surface during the Xi-Trump 
summit in Washington in late September resulting in an extension of 
the trade truce by two months to 10 January. After a bumpy summer, 
relations look more stable again. But trust is limited between the two 
sides and a long-term rivalry continues to unfold.
➢On Taiwan, Xi urged the US to "adhere to the correct position of 
opposing Taiwan independence and handle the Taiwan issue with 
prudence“. For some time it has been speculated, Xi would use his 
good relationship with Trump to press him to change the official US 
stance from ”does not support” to ”oppose” Taiwan independence. 
Trump did not change it, though. Ahead of the summit, Taiwan 
performed its first military drills using attack drones and missiles. 
EU And China Agree On Short-Term Deal But Tensions Still Loom
➢EU leaders summit 15-16 October. Will they postpone tougher measures 
against China? Forceful actions are likely put aside for now, but some 
protection of strategic sectors and buy-EU policies are still on the table.
➢Xi and Trump meet again at the APEC Summit in Shenzhen on 18-10 
November and at the G20 Summit in Miami on 14-15 December.
➢Will Trump go through with delayed weapons sales to Taiwan? Or does he 
hold off for now to keep good relations with Xi ahead of their two meetings at 
APEC and G20. Also watch if Trump allows a stop-over by Taiwan’s president. 
Seems unlikely, though, ahead of the two summits.

Under the Radar
Bolsonaro Jr. Eyeing Election Win

Danske Bank
12
➢Brazil presidential election: Flávio Bolsonaro, Jair Bolsonaro’s son, is the 
favourite to win the presidential election on the October 25th. His victory 
would mark a significant win for the Bolsoranismo movement (the Brazilian 
equivalent to MAGA), founded and headed by his father. Financial markets 
have embraced the possibility as Bolsonaro Jr. has promised spending cuts. 
Under his watch, Brazil would become a key ally of the Trump administration, 
and jailed Jair Bolsonaro would most likely again have a say in the country’s 
politics.
➢BRICS Summit: On 12-13 September BRICS leaders met in New Delhi. The 
summit resulted in a 45-page joint communique, despite sharp division 
between members Iran and the UAE, currently mired in conflict – a diplomatic 
win for India. The statement urged ”maximum restraint” in the Middle East. 
India’s leader Narendra Modi called for a new global order saying the Global 
South was ”in the front row when it comes to facing global crisis but in the back 
row when it comes to decision making”, a sentiment widely shared by BRICS 
leaders. Chinese leader Xi Jinping and India’s Modi met for the first sit-down 
on Indian soil in seven years and continued a slow thaw in the China-India 
relationship. Xi, Modi and Putin met again at the summit, not long after their 
previous rendezvous at the SCO Summit in August in Kyrgyzstan.
Bolsonaro Jr. Eyeing Presidential Election Victory
.
➢The third Russia-Africa summit will be held on October 28-29th in Moscow. The 
previous meetings took place in 2019 and in 2023. Previous communiques had 
indicated that the summit could be held on the African continent, but this year’s 
summit will again take place on Russian soil. Russia’s military footprint is 
particularly prominent in the Sahel region and in Libya. Their modus operandi for 
Africa often combines military and economic collaboration – they offer their 
security services (Africa Corps) in exchange for access to natural resources.
➢The conflicts in the broader Horn of Africa region, namely in Sudan and Ethiopia, 
are becoming more entwined. Ethiopia recently accused Sudan, Egypt and Eritrea 
for supporting the Ethiopian People’s Forces Alliance for Survival, a heterogenous 
alliance formed by seven armed domestic groups that the Ethiopian army is 
fighting against in the north of the country. The relations between different groups 
are complex, but often reflect the ”my enemy’s enemy is my friend” principle. After 
Ethiopia’s civil war a few years ago, many Tigrayan (TPLF) fighters went into exile 
in Sudan, and this group is now collaborating with the Sudanese armed forces. 
Sudan, a country currently in a brutal civil war, on the other hand is accusing 
Ethiopia for supporting the notorious rebel group RSF (the UAE is also a known 
supporter) that its government forces are fighting against. In addition, there is 
longstanding mutual animosity between Ethiopia and both Eritrea and Egypt.

Conflict Scenarios for 2026
Scenarios
War continues (85%): Protracted conflict with 
rising spillover risks across Europe.
Deal dictated by Russia (5%): The US pushes a 
deal between Russia and Ukraine where 
Ukraine is left with insufficient security 
guarantees. Sanctions against Russia are 
eased rapidly. The risk of a new attack by 
Russia over coming years remains high.
Sustainable peace (5%): Ukraine gets 
sufficient security guarantees but is forced to 
make territorial concessions. Any sanctions 
relief for Russia is gradual.
Ukraine wins (5%): The new US administration 
plays hardball with Russia and a ceasefire 
favourable for Ukraine is achieved. Ukraine 
gets security guarantees it prefers.
Baseline (60%): Protracted limbo. Israeli/US 
war operations in the region continue, while 
the intensity of the warfare varies. We think 
the US attack on Feb-2026 marked a point of 
no return, and the US will remain tied up in the 
region for much longer than the 
administration had in plans. Geopolitical risk 
premium keeps oil prices high despite 
increased traffic volumes in the SOH.
Downside risk (25%): Fighting resumes and 
the war escalates further, as the whole of the 
Persian Gulf is affected. Strait of Hormuz 
remains too dangerous for maritime 
navigation.
Upside risk (15%): The US and Iran sign a 
permanent peace deal, and a new nuclear 
deal, and tensions de-escalate.
Baseline (70%): Tensions remain high under 
the surface but no Chinese blockade or 
military action is likely the next couple of 
years. War is extremely costly for all sides. 
Both US and China choose deterrence as 
preferred policy. US aims to deter Chinese 
invasion by making perceived cost as high as 
possible. China uses military drills and grey 
zone warfare to deter US support for formal 
Taiwan independence and subdue Taiwan. In 
the long term, a real risk of war remains. China 
will not give up reunification, and it becomes 
stronger militarily and more self-sufficient. 
Downside risk (20%): Mishaps trigger tit-for-
tat escalation into war and/or US changes 
policy and supports Taiwan independence.
Upside risk (10%): Trump dials back support 
for Taiwan and China reduces military drills.
