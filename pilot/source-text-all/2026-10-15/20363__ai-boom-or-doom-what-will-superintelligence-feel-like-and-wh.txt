October 12, 2026 
The recent panic about rogue AI agents collaborating in swarms to take over the 
internet and threaten human existence has, more than occasionally, lacked 
nuance. But it does reflect how artificial intelligence is progressing in creative 
and surprising ways, and at a pace that seems uncontrollable. 
For some in the industry, it is evidence that the technology is following a path of 
accelerating self-improvement that will lead to artificial general intelligence (AGI) 
and, perhaps, to utopia or catastrophe. 
Former Google CEO Eric Schmidt has sketched out a “San Francisco 
Consensus” based on a belief in scaling laws, with ever larger AI models 
becoming an input into their own improvement; superintelligence by the end of 
the decade; and transformative AI systems outperforming humans on many 
tasks. This loop would lead to exponential growth in scientific advancement, 
financial returns and human progress. And crucially, AGI, and then 
superintelligence, would merely be milestones in a process, far from its climax. 
If that were the case, superintelligence could make much of the cognitive work 
that dominates the world economy much cheaper and change the ways in which 
firms compete. But its economic effects will depend on how businesses and 
customers respond. 
In this paper, we analyse what the San Francisco Consensus means for investors 
and enterprises in the years to come and what could accelerate – or slow down – 
its timeline of AGI by 2030. 
1. What would superintelligence look and feel like? 
2. Can we get there theoretically? 
3. Can we get there practically? 
4. What happens if we don’t get there? The base case 
The question is urgent. 
And it is complicated by reports of AI models autonomously accessing the 
internet and hacking other companies; a public backlash over the energy-
intensive data centres springing up as part of a historic $1 trillion a year 
investment boom; and dire warnings of threats to life if superintelligent AI runs 
roughshod over its human creators to achieve its own goals.

1. What would superintelligence look and feel like? 
1.1 What are AGI and superintelligence? 
Artificial general intelligence (AGI) is a simple milestone scribbled in pencil: an AI 
system that has surpassed human capabilities. Superintelligence is the 
continuum beyond that point. It is not merely systems that exceed the best 
human performance across one cognitive task or another, but systems that 
exceed human potential across every cognitive task. 
The San Francisco Consensus takes this further: the intelligence must be able to 
produce more intelligence. The key to getting there is so-called recursive self-
improvement, where the AI is able to autonomously design and improve itself in 
a way that compounds exponentially. A first step has maybe already been taken, 
with OpenAI saying recently that AI is now writing approximately 80 percent of 
the company’s code. 
There is significant disagreement on how we will be able to identify AGI or 
superintelligence when we see it, let alone what comes afterwards.  
OpenAI’s Charter defines AGI in economic terms, as “highly autonomous systems 
that outperform humans at the most economically valuable work”. Google 
researchers proposed a five-step ladder based on performance and generality, 
with level 5 “superhuman” for a system that outperforms 100% of humans. And 
philosopher Nick Bostrom, who got the ball rolling in 2014 with his influential 
book “Superintelligence”, defined it as “any intellect that greatly exceeds the 
cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest”. 
It’s also a moving target.  
In August, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said the company would have an internal 
system that he would call AGI by later this year but subsequently told a podcast 
that “at best it’s a very poorly defined term. I was going to say it’s like an irrelevant 
marketing term”. Yet his No. 2 Greg Brockman described the new Astra model at 
the start of September as being potentially the moment that “AGI was created”. 
For this report, we have chosen to focus on a system that outperforms essentially 
all humans across essentially all cognitive tasks, including the development of AI 
itself.  
Under our scenario, by 2030 the best performing AI models would have the 
qualities of “superintelligence”, that is better than human experts in all domains. 
These would be frontier models: and using them would feel exactly like bringing 
the best human experts in a room to solve whatever business problem you faced.  
 
And the equivalents of today’s frontier models would be available, quite literally, 
everywhere, compressed enough to run on every phone, tablet or laptop 
computer.  
In early 2026, we already see early examples of what highly capable AI can do. 
They give a flavour for a world with superintelligence even if the reality would be 
a leap beyond, both quantitatively and qualitatively. At that point, 
superintelligence would mark a regime break, not a faster version of what we 
already have. Rather than blindly doing what humans specify, but just a bit better, 
it would specify the task itself.

1.2 Capability and diffusion 
If AI becomes capable of improving itself, the technology may compound faster 
and faster. The supply of machine intelligence could grow faster than business 
and physical infrastructure can accommodate.  
This balance between capability and diffusion creates different scenarios.  
 
Figure 2: The balance between capabilities and diffusion 
Source: Deutsche Bank Research 
 
Lower capability, constrained diffusion: where we are now 
Progress is steady and sector-by-sector. This is how technology usually diffuses, 
according to researchers for the New York Fed back in 2003, who tracked 20 
major technologies across leading industrial economies from 1788 and found “a 
pattern of trickle-down diffusion that is remarkably robust across technologies”. 
Lower capability, high diffusion: the cloud transition 
This is faster because the complementary investment is small, with no retooling 
and no skills at scale required. Amazon Web Services launched the modern cloud 
in 2006, offering scalable computing and storage infrastructure. Adoption grew 
and then accelerated over the pandemic. Almost all enterprises now use it in 
some form.

Higher capability, lower diffusion: the overhang 
This is theoretically possible and arguably where we are today in narrow domains. 
The technology would be applied in some sectors of the economy, while other 
sectors would move at a slower speed. Superintelligent or not, it risks being 
bogged down by physical constraints, bureaucracy and human inertia. 
Higher capability, higher diffusion: superintelligence 
In this scenario, capability becomes so high that it changes the dynamics of 
diffusion itself. AI generates not just better AI (so-called recursive self-
improvement, or RSI) but also speeds up the integration process. 
Rapid AI diffusion could work two ways. 
In the first, AI could be enlisted to do integration work itself: mapping processes, 
rebuilding data work, retraining staff, writing the control and audit layer, and so 
on. The problem with this is that absorbing and recreating undocumented 
knowledge is impossible by definition, at least for a task-based superintelligence.  
The second way that higher capability AI could be rapidly diffused is much more 
competitive – and disruptive. Rather than incumbents slowly converting AI, 
perhaps using it for some of the hard work of integration, they could be instead 
displaced by new firms that have been built AI-native from day one. New entrants 
will never have to incur integration costs because there is nothing to integrate. 
Incumbents will need to change – fast – or die.  
Change could also be forced by earlier adopters in the supply chain, as happened 
with one company after another adopting electronic data interchange (EDI) to 
link with its suppliers or customers, or by a migration of capital towards early 
adopters, repricing the cost of capital for slow-coaches and prodding them to 
move faster. 
1.3 The forcing mechanism: change or die 
A company that uses superintelligence may outperform one that doesn’t. 
Firms using superintelligence will likely have substantial cost and revenue-
harvesting advantages. Every firm will therefore have to adopt superintelligence 
or retrench to a smaller set of offerings. And any business line of lasting value 
abandoned by a non-adopter will be taken over by an adopter. Customer inertia 
will provide a safe harbour for a limited time only.  
The analogy here is electricity. There came a point in the deployment of 
electricity where firms in every sector, from entertainment to finance, from 
healthcare to transport, had to adopt the technology to remain relevant.  
Under this scenario, wait-and-see will not be an option. There will be no stable 
“non-adoption” alternative. Sufficiently high capabilities will force a tipping point 
of rapid diffusion and competitive repricing. Firms using yesterday’s production 
model will either have to change, choose a different market or die.  
Of course, caveats apply.  
Customers may not change behaviour immediately. They may even prefer 
companies which limit their use of AI, in the same way that some customers 
choose to avoid factory-produced food.

The paradox that Nobel Prize-winning economist Robert Solow observed in 1987, 
that “you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics”, 
is likely to still apply for a while – even with superintelligence. 
The reason is that the productivity J-curve identified by Stanford University AI 
economy guru Erik Brynjolfsson et al is unavoidable. In that scenario, the drag of 
early intangible investments to integrate general purpose technologies means 
that productivity initially appears to slow before rapidly accelerating.

Furthermore, “change or die” would require abundant capital and low regulatory 
entry points for nimble new entrants. But in practice, highly regulated areas such 
as banking, utilities, pharmaceuticals and defence are tough nuts to crack. 
Existing contracts and licences, high switching costs and decades of depreciated 
capital investment and infrastructure would shield incumbents at least for a 
healthy transition period. 
For an extreme example from the real world of better, cheaper technology failing 
to force change, take Pakistan’s 3.6-gigawatt imported coal power plant fleet 
installed in the mid-2010s. In that case, it came with expensive take-or-pay 
liabilities which will continue to run for decades, even though changes like 
cheaper rooftop solar have cut utilisation to below 20 percent.  
So: superintelligence will not force adoption everywhere. It will hit hardest in 
contestable sectors, say, high-volume customer services, routine software 
engineering and freelance creative work, and widen the gap between them and 
protected ones. Forced adoption will be about relative value, not an apocalypse.

1.4 What survives and what it means for portfolios 
Because AI sits on an aggressive learning curve, its price on an equivalent quality 
basis is plummeting. In a world of recursive super intelligence this is likely to 
continue. Its price will fall to the cost of compute.  
What will retain its value will be whatever the model cannot replicate: 
1. Proprietary data 
2. The right to operate, particularly in heavily regulated sectors such as 
healthcare and finance. 
3. Sticky customer relationships, such as those where mission-critical 
workflows and databases are deeply entwined. 
4. Excludable physical assets, such as machinery and land. 
5. In addition, humans have proven to have unlimited wants. Each shift in 
technological capabilities or cultural norms has delivered new needs to 
be met. Some will be needs we can articulate today, such as originality or 
empathy, but new ones are likely to arise.  
If this forcing mechanism holds, firms will separate into two “bimodal” groups: 
adopters and casualties, with very little in between. 
This dispersion of performance may create challenges for investors. The four 
years since the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022 have seen the stocks of 
semiconductor and semiconductor equipment makers surge almost seven times 
while those of software makers have only risen by 80 percent. Conventional 
diversification and volatility-based allocation may become ineffective strategies.

Diversification normally works because idiosyncratic risks are unrelated and 
average each other out. Here they would not: with one variable that overrides 
everything else, spreading capital across more names would just spread exposure 
rather than diluting   
Investors will need to consider adapting their approach in three ways: 
 
 Classify investments as adopters or casualties rather than position off 
averages.  
 
Under a normal distribution, errors are symmetric and partially 
cancel each other out. But under bimodality they do not: to put a firm 
in one category or another means getting it completely right or 
wrong. Position size should track confidence in the classification. 
 
 Avoid reading calm price action as low risk.  
 
Trailing volatility measures dispersion that has already been realised. 
Bimodal separation is a jump, where the two modes look identical 
right until the moment when they don’t. A classic example is Chegg, 
an educational software maker which traded like a normal small cap 
stock until one day in May 2023 when it halved on a single day after 
management linked slowing growth in new customers to ChatGPT. 
 
 Averaging down, ie scooping up shares whose price has fallen, may no 
longer be prudent  
 
Can we get there theoretically? 
2.1 Scaling laws 
Scaling laws have provided the blueprint for improving AI over the past decade. 
It was a relationship between the amount of data, the amount of compute and 
the complexity of an AI model somewhat predictably informed how powerful a 
model would become. Google DeepMind’s Chinchilla model in 2022 showed that 
you could compensate for having a smaller model by using substantially more 
data. 
Scaling is older than the chatbot. In 1950 Claude Shannon built Theseus, a 
mechanical mouse that learned its way through a maze by trial and error. Epoch 
AI reckons it took about 40 operations of compute, known as FLOPs, to train. Grok 
4, trained in 2025, took roughly ten trillion trillion times as much. Across those 25 
orders of magnitude, more compute has brought more capability  
 
 
A falling price may be information that a business model is obsolete 
rather than an opportunity.

It is the robustness of this relationship that has made larger investments in 
compute capex a sine qua non for AI companies. 
However, this is not a law of physics and nothing guarantees it will continue. 
Indeed, in the ChatGPT era, five different approaches to scaling have been 
applied. Older strategies yield worse results, and new techniques, such as post-
training refinements, have been developed. The consensus amongst American 
and Chinese AI labs is that these will hold.  
While this may be a consensus amongst the commercial labs, there is scientific 
disagreement that applying scaling laws to today’s large-language models can 
achieve superintelligence. 
Yann LeCun, a Turing Award winner and formerly Meta's chief AI scientist, is blunt: 
"LLMs are not a path to superintelligence or even human-level intelligence”. Even 
inside the labs, the recipe is contested. Demis Hassabis of Google DeepMind has 
said he expects "one or two more big breakthroughs" to be needed "in addition to 
scaling". Ilya Sutskever, a co-founder and former chief scientist of OpenAI, has 
said the field is going "back to the age of research again, just with big computers". 
This is a scientific argument we are not in a position to evaluate, but for the 
purposes of our scenario, we will side with the labs’ position. 
For the moment, there is strong evidence that capabilities have in fact 
accelerated over the past three years, as reasoning models have outpaced non-
reasoning models by two or three times, according to Epoch data.

2.2 Recursive self-improvement: AI improving AI 
A cocktail-party definition of recursive self-improvement (RSI) is AI that improves 
itself. The reality is that it can be used in two ways: 
1. Self-improving agents that get better at using themselves: showing 
smarter use of prompts, tools, memory and workflows. The model-
building process is untouched. 
2. True RSI improves the process that builds the models: designing 
experiments, curating data, optimising algorithms and designing chips in 
a compounding loop. 
The former is like a factory worker who gets better at the job by learning the quirks 
of the machinery, getting organised and making fewer mistakes. The latter is like 
the worker redesigning the machine tools and then using those to build the next 
generation of machines. 
True RSI is what OpenAI’s chief scientist Jacob Pachoki referred to last month in 
a blog post about “An Alien Mind”. “Automated AI research is a more dramatic 
form of scaling intelligence with compute,” he wrote. “And of course as a part of 
it, AI will improve the computational substrate itself,” he added. Pachoki 
represents a broadly held consensus view amongst American and Chinese labs 
that RSI is both possible and necessary for AI development. 
• 
OpenAI (June 2026): “AI doing AI research will become the determining 
factor of the pace of progress within the next few years.” 
• 
Google DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis (May 2026): “All the leading labs are 
quite focused on [RSI]…” Humanity is standing in the “foothills of the 
singularity”. 
• 
Alibaba CEO Eddie Wu (Sept 2026): RSI is “a concrete path forward,” with 
models autonomously designing experiments and driving a cycle of self-
evolution. 
For now, self-improving agents are the AI du jour. Noisy claims about the vast 
majority of coding being done by agents do not always stand up in the light of day 
from companies still hiring human coders at breakneck pace, albeit no longer to 
do the most mundane parts of the job.  
A better metric may be METR’s task-completion time horizon. This is measure of 
the complexity of software tasks that an AI agent can complete on its own, using 
the baseline of how long it would take a human expert to finish the task, at a 50 
percent success rate. So far the most advanced models have reached about 16 
hours, up from OpenAI’s GPT-4’s four minutes in March 2023.

But software engineers can hold off on updating their resumes a little longer: 
there is a long road between 50 percent accuracy and the 99 percent or 100 
percent needed for serious enterprise use at scale, with the horizon potentially 
getting further away the nearer you get. Also METR notes that measurements 
above 16 hours are unreliable with the current task suite. As is the case across AI, 
the instrument is running out before the question is settled.  
More broadly, there are significant limitations to RSI:  
1. Research is clock-bound.  
• 
A frontier training run can take weeks on thousands of specialised chips, 
and researchers often cannot design the next experiment until the 
previous one has been completed.  
• 
That means that the speed of the loop is set by experiments completed 
per hour or day, not by ideas generated.  
• 
Compute is a hard ceiling that needs to be bought, built and connected 
to a grid. 
2. The real world defies verifiability.  
• 
The loop compounds only when the system can check its own output 
cheaply and automatically. 
• 
It is no coincidence that AI loves maths and coding. It is much harder to 
verify outputs over a long time horizon in the real world: does this drug 
work or does this strategy succeed?  
• 
RSI compounds fastest where the economy cares least and slows where 
the returns are largest.

3. Sequences are consequential.  
• 
Amdahl’s law finds that speed-up is capped when processes run in a 
sequence.  
• 
If you automate 90 percent, the residual 10 percent still limits you to a 
ten-times faster process. Nine women cannot produce a baby in one 
month.  
• 
The Usain Bolt of AI still has to run alongside the comparative tortoises 
of the training run itself, chip fabrication and real-world validation. Ten 
times faster is still not a regime break. 
This is borne out by METR’s series. Doubling every seven months is fast 
exponential growth but a take-off would show the doubling time itself – the 
second derivative – shrinking. Horizons are not (yet) exploding. 
Philosopher Toby Ord shows that recursive self-improvement can only reach a 
singularity if each cycle of improvement takes less time than the last, shrinking 
fast towards zero. He argues that real feedback loops have a floor: chips take time 
to make and models take time to train. AI progress could still outrun an 
exponential, but it would not go vertical. 
2.3 Scaling plus RSI: a match made in the race to AGI? 
No scaling and RSI means no short timeline for AGI. Without scaling, more 
compute buys nothing and capital is wasted. Without RSI, AI capability will 
continue to improve at the pace of flesh-and-blood human researchers, 
augmented by AI tools but not replaced. Only with both will it be possible to 
produce a system that improves faster than the humans building it. Without one 
or the other, no quantity of chips, energy or capital will fix it. 
3. Can we get there practically? 
3.1 Infrastructure, chips and energy 
There is no consensus on the amount of training required to reach AGI. Estimates 
suggest that training 2026's top models required between 10^26 and 10^27 
FLOPs. If we hypothesise that an "AGI-like model" would require three orders of 
magnitude more training, this would require 10^30 FLOPs.   
Consider today’s US AI infrastructure, which runs to approximately 10^22 FLOPs. 
If all of this was conceivably used for a 10^30 FLOP training run, it would take 
nearly four years to complete.  
The physical infrastructure to practically support such a training run would 
require enormous power, perhaps around 6GW, about 50% more than the four 
Vogtle nuclear power plants recently activated in the US. The cost of the 
compute infrastructure would run to between $300bn and $500bn.  
Today’s advanced chips would simply not suffice. Back-of-the-envelope maths 
suggests that even using Nvidia’s most advanced Vera Rubin chips, training such 
a model in four months would require 7.8m chips and 26GW of facility power. 
That would cost $900bn today and a rental equivalent cost of $90bn.  
Of course, frontier compute is growing rapidly, and price-performance is 
declining. If current trends hold, a 2030-era cluster capable of such a run would

But even if AI labs crack the secret sauce of RSI, and their finance chiefs are willing

Grid connection queues, turbine lead times and transmission build are measured

Politics in the US has shifted towards slowing the pace of the AI build out and this 
force will run against the demands for larger data centres that would be essential 
for RSI.  
3.2 Capital 
Financing for AI has been abundant in the historic buildout of the past three years. 
However, it is still largely conditional not on revenue but on belief in a future 
payoff. 
RSI is a “key part of the investment thesis” in the “biggest scientific bet civilisation 
has ever made,” Google DeepMind’s chief strategy officer Jasjeet Sekhon said at 
a conference in August, The Information reported. “We have this danger we could 
hit an AI air pocket such that the expenditures happen but the revenues don’t 
show up.” 
An inflection point is approaching where the nature of investment changes from 
free cash flow and equity to debt, with all that entails in terms of exposure to 
rising rates and creditor appetite. The hyperscalers are reaching a point where 
capex is no longer covered by operating cashflow and where net debt exceeds 
earnings before interest, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA). 
Alphabet, for example, posted its first quarterly free cash outflow in nearly two 
decades in its most recent quarter, even as it lifted its full-year capex guidance to 
$205bn. It said it would continue to rent third-party capacity and reported a 
backlog of $514bn in contracted, not-yet-recognised data centre revenue. 
3.3 Data 
Data has been the new oil for some time now. But unfortunately for AI 
development it seems to be running out even faster. 
The effective stock of quality- and repetition-adjusted public human text was 
about 300 trillion tokens, with a vast 90 percent confidence interval of 100 trillion 
to 1,000 trillion, Epoch estimated in 2024. A token is roughly equivalent to three 
quarters of a word. By that count it was likely to be fully used between 2026 and 
2032.

However, since then, rather than data dropping off a cliff, there has been a rise in 
synthetic data generation, transfer learning from data-rich domains such as video 
and telemetry, and data efficiency. While the data shortage has not been 
resolved, the immediate crisis has been averted. 
AI now makes much of its own training data. When DeepMind's AlphaGo became 
superhuman at playing the mind-bendingly complex board game Go, it did so not 
by playing human experts, but by playing millions and millions of games against 
itself. 
For many domains relating to coding and mathematical proofs where automatic 
checks are possible, such synthetic experience can move things forward. That is 
the case with AI research, where the data may be the results of experiments, all 
of which are bought by compute. About 90% of OpenAI's 2024 research compute 
went on such experiments. 
And increasingly, people write the tests rather than the answers. By the summer 
of 2026, selling training data for practice tasks was a nearly $9bn revenue 
business. Firms such as Mercor hire experts to generate long tasks against which 
the AI can be trained. 
The challenge here is that trust in the checker becomes a scarce input.

As AI makes production cheaper, it also makes establishing that production is 
accurate or reliable more expensive. Domains which can be checked 
mechanically, such as mathematics, will be opened up more quickly. But those 
which rely on expert verification, such as medicine, engineering or law, may 
progress more slowly. 
The likely implications are twofold: a slower rate of diffusion into economic 
domains that are less verifiable and a larger share of production costs devoted to 
assurance. 
Another issue is that systems that have to grade themselves learn to game the 
process. This was partially what happened when an OpenAI model escaped a 
sandbox and hacked into a third-party website. Trust replaced data as a wall.

3.4 An incomplete case 
This analysis shows that the San Francisco Consensus, which emphasises RSI and 
a short timeline to AGI, is neither predestined nor impossible.  
Rather it depends on whether the science is right, on whether scaling is all you 
need, and on whether the infrastructure and capital required for that scaling is 
deployed and accessible to the leading labs.  
There is, in other words, a possible rather than impossible, path to AGI, subject to 
those conditions.  
None of this proves that superintelligence will ever arrive. A buildup at this scale 
has few peacetime precedents and vastly exceeds the likes of the Manhattan 
Project and Apollo programme.

It is uncertain whether there will be enough demand to pay upfront for the 
investment in achieving RSI that would justify that investment. And whether the 
friction of verification, and inevitable declining marginal returns, act as a drag on 
what otherwise might be a runaway process. 
4. What happens if we don’t get there? The base case 
4.1 Separating AI progress from superintelligence 
So what if that thesis is wrong? Does the AI trade depend on a journey to AGI?  
AI can remain economically important even before RSI coupled with scaling laws 
lead to super intelligence. Companies do not need to wait for nebulous 
superintelligence to generate identifiable returns from AI in the medium term as 
they fold it into their systems, streamline workflows and roll out new business 
models. 
Investors already have exposure to RSI as a capital allocation because the leading 
US labs say RSI is a key part of their product strategy. And it is these labs whose 
products are catalysing and amplifying the infrastructure boom. 
Even rapid competitive diffusion does not mean every part of the physical 
economy changes instantly. 
The smartest superintelligence might identify the optimal buyout structure 
before breakfast, but it cannot force a financially constrained buyer to use it or 
persuade the seller to accept less than they have been expecting. Those 
challenges will be even more intractable where the parties are more complex, say, 
fiscally constrained sovereigns or foreign equity holders. 
4.2 Why the base case is attractive 
Superintelligence would be a chaotic accelerant to human society, and a 
potential regime break. But it is not the only scenario in which today’s investment 
in AI infrastructure makes economic sense. 
Adoption of AI tools by businesses is progressing approximately three times faster 
than the mobile, internet and cloud computing waves. From sector revenues 
below $1bn in 2022, the AI economy, excluding China, will likely exceed $200bn 
in revenues in 2026.  
Early winners are the cloud businesses. Google Cloud revenue jumped 82 percent 
from a year earlier in the most recent quarter as companies turned AI pilots into 
workflows. Amazon Web Services sales were up 37 percent and Microsoft’s AI 
business more than doubled. 
There is also slow, but increasing, evidence of a productivity uplift. The J-curve 
cuts both ways: front-loading investments is taking one step back to take two 
steps forward. The so-called complementary intangible investment that is 
(mis)measured as cost today to the detriment of the productivity statistics will 
flatter them in the years to come as it pays off. The base case does not require a 
regime break to show up as a productivity surprise, just for the accounting to 
catch up. 
The economic gains from AI that is less powerful than the AGI mooted by the labs 
would be substantial. And in this base case, they may also be attractive.

4.3 Investor watchpoints 
The arrival of ASI would be a disruptive economic shock. Investors need to be alert 
for the early signals. 
Rather than relying on a single probability forecast, investors should watch five
