Unlike in 2023, the recent increase in mortgage rates has 
been driven primarily by higher base rates 
Mortgage rates have climbed steadily over the past few 
months, recently reaching 7.4%, their highest level since 
2023. However, a decomposition of the increase shows that 
the drivers of the recent rise are different from those in 
2023 (Exhibit 1). Using the framework discussed previously, 
we decompose mortgage rates into the following key 
sub-components - (i) 10-year Treasury yields (‘base rates’), 
(ii) Spread between MBS current coupon yield and 10-year 
Treasury yield (broken out into option-adjusted spread 
(OAS) and option cost), (iii) GSE guarantee fees (about 
60-65bp currently), (iv) minimum servicing fee (of 25bp), 
and (v) originator profits. Unlike in 2023, when wider MBS 
spreads and elevated rate volatility accounted for a greater 
share of high mortgage rates, elevated base rates are the 
primary driver today. The 10-year Treasury yield reached a 
high of 5.3% in September, above its October 2023 peak of 
5.0%, yet mortgage rates remain below their October 2023 
peak of 7.8% based on the Freddie Mac survey rate. As a 
result, the 10-year Treasury yield now accounts for more 
than 70% of the mortgage rate, an unusually high share by 
post-2008 standards (Exhibit 2).

Recent HPA trends appear to be more closely correlated with swings in the labor market than with mortgage rates 
The level of mortgage rates has traditionally been an important driver of the pace of home price appreciation (HPA). 
Mortgage rates declined to as low as 6.2% during the final four months of 2025, close to the lowest level reached in 2024. 
However, despite the decline in rates during 2H’2025, HPA remained weak. Year-over-year HPA slowed to approximately 
0.1% by September 2025 and remained around that level for the following four months. We had previously attributed part of 
the weakness in HPA to declining consumer confidence. Revised non-farm payroll (NFP) data provides further support for 
that view, suggesting that deterioration in the labor market may have weighed on consumer confidence and housing demand 
(Exhibit 3). Monthly NFP growth averaged -22k during the final five months of 2025 compared with +27k during 1H’25. 
Labor-market conditions subsequently improved, with NFP gains averaging +76k during 1H’26. This improvement coincided 
with a modest recovery in year-over-year HPA, which reached approximately 1.1% in August 2026. Our economists expect 
the unemployment rate to remain broadly stable at around 4.1% through year-end 2027, an improvement from 2H’2025, 
when it peaked at 4.5%. This more favorable labor market backdrop could support the housing market in 2027. 
Nuances in consumer confidence by generation provide an additional reason for cautious optimism around housing demand. 
The latest Conference Board release indicates that, while aggregate confidence has fallen to its lowest level since 2014, 
confidence has held relatively steady in recent months (and at higher absolute levels) among Gen Z and Millennials. As these 
cohorts increasingly enter prime homebuying years and consider homeownership, comparatively resilient confidence could 
provide support for housing demand. However, there are limitations to relying on sentiment indicators. Our economists note

Pent-up demand in a scarce housing market should establish a floor on home prices 
Challenging housing affordability in recent years has created meaningful pent-up demand, particularly among younger 
households. Over the past five years, the aggregate U.S. homeownership rate has remained broadly stable at around 65%, but 
the homeownership rate for householders under age 35 has declined by nearly 3 percentage points. The deterioration has 
been particularly pronounced over the past two to three years. Relative to Q2 2023, homeownership rates for households 
under 35 and those between 35 and 44 have fallen by approximately 3 and 2 percentage points, respectively, compared with 
a decline of less than 0.5 percentage point for households age 55 and older (Exhibit 4). These trends point towards pent-up 
housing demand from the younger households. 
A key driver of declining homeownership among younger households is insufficient housing supply at affordable price points. 
Supply remains constrained not only by the mortgage-rate lock-in effect, as homeowners who secured ultra-low mortgage 
rates in 2020-21 remain reluctant to sell, but also by a longer-running imbalance between new housing supply and household 
formation. A simple comparison of annual housing-unit completions to the increase in the number of households shows that 
household formation, a proxy for demand, has significantly outpaced housing completions, a proxy for supply, in the 
post-Global Financial Crisis period (Exhibit 5). We note that this measure may somewhat understate the housing shortage, as 
household formation in recent years may itself have been constrained by limited housing supply and affordability challenges. 
Our economists estimate that the there currently is a shortage of roughly 3-4 million housing units, broadly within the 1.5-5.5

While housing affordability remains challenged, buyers are adapting to make homeownership work 
The steady increase in mortgage rates this year has resulted in affordability reversing recent gains (Exhibit 7). At 7.4% 
mortgage rates, we estimate that housing affordability would drop to levels last seen in 2024, reversing the gradual 
improvement seen over roughly the past two years. Although existing home sales have declined by more than 40% from their 
peak 2020–21 run rate, activity has remained in a relatively narrow range of 3.86 million to 4.38 million units (seasonally 
adjusted and annualized rate) since the start of 2023 despite mortgage rates fluctuating between 6.0% and 7.8%. 
Some of this resilience likely reflects the increased share of all-cash purchases, which are relatively insensitive to mortgage 
rates. But buyers who finance their purchases also appear to have adjusted to strained affordability. The NAR’s 2025 Profile of 
Home Buyers and Sellers report notes that buyers have increased their down payments to reduce monthly mortgage 
payments and have been willing to cut discretionary spending to make homeownership more feasible. For example, the 
median down payment rose from 12% in the 2021 survey to 19% in 2025. Similarly, while only 33% of buyers reported 
making financial sacrifices in 2021, when affordability was relatively favorable, that share increased to 44% in 2023, when 
affordability was at its weakest in recent history.
