Equity Outlook 4Q 2026
Rising Rates Shift the Balance of Risks
Underlying economic drivers remain healthy, but rising yields, 
potential earnings pressure and overall market fragility may 
warrant further caution.

Equity Outlook  |  4Q 2026
AMR HANAFY, CFA
Research Analyst
Bottom Line
We believe a combination of higher yields, potential earnings growth pressure and overall market fragility has shifted the balance of risks. While we 
remain constructive on global equities, more caution appears warranted.
This Is Not a Bearish Call…
We believe underlying economic drivers remain strong, the U.S. labor market is holding firm, and that derailing the global manufacturing upcycle would be hard 
without far more aggressive interest rate hikes than we expect. We also continue to believe in AI’s potential to drive near-term economic growth and long-term 
productivity gains across the wider economy.
…Yet Risk Has Mounted
What has changed, in our view, is how much could disappoint from here as interest rates continue to rise and geopolitical tensions remain unresolved. History 
suggests that strong nominal growth, resilient market performance and higher yields can coexist—until something breaks. We worry higher discount rates and 
rising bond volatility could put pressure on equity valuations and reduce liquidity, alongside further geopolitical strife, potentially slowing AI capex and weaker 
consumer demand.
Portfolio Recommendations at a Glance
• Risk Posture: More cautious (though not bearish) as the balance of risks continues to shift
• Regions: Reduce global equities and EM to neutral; prefer U.S. large cap, Japan and China; underweight Europe
• Styles and Sizes: Trim small and mid caps to neutral; remain neutral on value vs. growth
• U.S. Sectors: Maintain barbell of quality Technology and Cyclicals, with Health Care as an AI hedge

Rate Hikes Are Precautionary, 
Not a New Tightening Regime
Fearing inflationary spillovers from the U.S./Iran 
conflict, global central banks have begun to raise 
rates (top left chart). We believe these actions 
are more precautionary than the start of a new 
tightening regime. In the U.S., expectations for 
the fed funds rate have swung by 150bps, from 
cuts to hikes, in the last seven months (top 
right chart).
The indicators we think Fed Chairman Warsh 
watches across the labor market and corporate 
fundamentals mostly point to above-trend 
economic growth. However, on inflation, we 
believe he remains concerned about the 
increasing share of PCE component prices that 
have been rising more than 3% (bottom chart). 
We think some of those reflect supply-driven 
shortages, which can prove temporary, rather 
than overheating broader demand.
Our Neuberger Fixed Income team expects 
a slow, coordinated hiking cycle and that 
markets are likely overstating both the number 
of hikes and the terminal fed funds rate. If 
inflation data proves stubborn, we would expect 
Warsh to grow more hawkish and we would likely 
revisit our equity positioning.

The U.S. Economy Can Handle 
Higher Rates
Economic growth in the U.S. is still outpacing 
the cost of money, giving the economy room to 
absorb higher interest rates without derailing 
the expansion, in our view. We see the rise in 
yields since late 2025 as normalization toward 
stronger nominal growth, with real rates—not 
inflation expectations—driving the increase. As 
we highlighted last quarter, the 10-year Treasury 
yield tends to gravitate around long-term nominal 
growth, the sum of real potential growth and trend 
inflation (top chart).
Nominal GDP growth still exceeds 10-year yields 
by roughly 100 basis points in the U.S.; Germany 
and Japan are also growing faster than bond 
yields, unlike in the U.K., Italy and France (bottom 
chart). In the U.S., we believe private-sector balance 
sheets provide more cushion, with both corporate and 
household leverage ratios near multi-decade lows. 
Meanwhile, other variables may push global rates 
higher, including rising oil prices, broad-based 
monetary tightening and reduced central bank 
bond buying. Potential fiscal unsustainability, 
hyperscaler debt issuance and a wave of mega-
IPOs have increased competition for capital, 
increasing the risk premium.

Key Risks 
Fading Global Liquidity Could 
Be a Headwind to the Global 
Economy
Liquidity is the cash and near-cash that underpins 
global credit creation, and it tends to price risk 
before earnings do: As liquidity expands, multiples 
can stretch; as it fades, valuations can compress.
China’s credit impulse, a key indicator that 
tends to lead global manufacturing activity 
by a year, has rolled over (top chart) and could 
be pointing to a broader economic slowdown, 
which in turn could weigh on equity markets, in 
our view.
We also believe heightened bond volatility could 
hamper liquidity and pressure equities. As we 
noted in a 2025 report, Treasuries often serve 
as collateral in global financing arrangements, 
meaning that higher bond volatility (gauged by 
the MOVE Index) can reduce the amount of credit 
offered against the same collateral. Our analysis 
shows that there’s a strong inverse correlation 
between bond volatility and equity returns 
(bottom chart). If this trend persists, we fear it 
could portend downward pressure on equities.

Key Risks 
U.S. Midterms: A Blue Sweep 
Could Shift Equity Leadership
U.S. midterm election years have historically seen 
lower equity returns and elevated volatility. Since 
1950, the S&P 500 Index has returned an average 
4.6% during midterm years versus 11.2% for the 
rest, with almost all gains coming after the results 
are in (top chart). So far in 2026, the S&P 500 
Index is up 14%, outrunning its historical average in 
midterm years, but trading in a narrow band since 
May. We see the soft-yet-volatile autumn tape 
as a seasonal bellwether rather than a signal to 
sharply cut risk.
Prediction markets currently peg a Democratic 
sweep as the the most likely outcome, with 
more than a 60% probability (bottom chart). In 
that scenario, we expect market volatility would 
spike initially as investors digest the possibility 
of stiffer regulation, particularly on the AI and 
financial industries. We also believe equity 
leadership could rotate toward defensive sectors, 
including Staples and Health Care, and away 
from Technology, Semiconductors, Industrials 
and Financials.

developed world at about 4% of GDP and are set 
to keep rising through 2027 as more debt rolls into 
short-term bills at higher rates (top chart). Net 
interest payments now absorb close to 20% 
of federal revenue (bottom left chart) and 
are expected to account for most of the deficit 
through 2030 (bottom right chart).
We believe fiscal credibility and flexibility may 
become a bigger concern for equity investors: 
Heavier Treasury supply and doubts about fiscal 
sustainability may keep term premiums aloft 
and yields elevated, potentially capping 
equity multiples.

Small and Mid Caps: Taking 
Profits; Trim to Neutral
We have been overweight small caps since 2024, 
but rising rates and a shifting balance of risks now 
argue for a tilt toward higher-quality, lower-beta 
stocks—and by extension, large caps over 
small caps.
Small caps tend to finance themselves via short 
and floating-rate debt: Almost 45% of small cap 
debt is floating rate with the majority set to mature 
within the next 5 years. Their ability to sustain that 
debt has decreased since the GFC: Small cap 
interest coverage ratios have fallen over the 
past decade and are now one third that of large 
caps (top chart). 
Meanwhile, we believe the Russell 2000 Index has 
gotten riskier: Over 28% of the index has negative 
forward earnings vs. just 4% of the Russell 1000 
Index, and a recent index reshuffling upped the 
weights in more speculative pockets, including 
biotech and regional banks (sectors likely to be 
pressured by elevated rates).
Finally, small cap earnings estimates are being 
revised lower, especially when compared to large 
caps (bottom chart). Since July 1, the 2026 EPS 
estimate for the Russell 2000 Index has been cut 
by over 5%, driven by 9 of the 11 sectors.

Value vs. Growth: 
Remain Neutral 
Value stocks have outperformed growth 
stocks, just not in the U.S.: Over the last decade, 
value has outperformed growth by about 30% 
outside the U.S., roughly matched it in EM, but 
has underperformed by 50% in the U.S. (top 
chart). We find that value benchmarks tend to be 
underweight technology and overweight more 
traditional, economically sensitive sectors. In the 
U.S., that meant missing the AI winners; outside 
the U.S., it meant owning the recovery in European 
banks, and in Japan, the tailwind from key 
governance reforms.
Value offers exposure to the ongoing cyclical 
manufacturing recovery, diversification away 
from the AI theme, and potential room for 
valuations to re-rate. We find the earnings of 
value companies are about 1.2 times as sensitive 
to the global economy as the earnings of growth 
companies (bottom chart). Value indices tend to 
be less concentrated in AI and tech-adjacent 
names than growth indices. And while relative 
valuations have re-rated, they do not appear 
stretched: the value-growth PE gap is only back 
to its historical average.

Japan: Long-term Potential; 
Remain Overweight
We remain overweight Japan, where corporate-
governance reform continues to support long-term 
equity growth. Macro indicators reflect an economy 
still in expansion: Manufacturers’ capex sentiment 
is rising; bank loan growth exceeds 5% a year; 
fiscal policy tailwinds may extend into 2027; and 
government debt-to-GDP has fallen from ~220% in 
2020 to ~195% (top left chart).
Earnings and sales estimates for the MSCI Japan 
Index are still being revised higher (top right chart), 
yet we think valuations remain attractive, trading 
roughly in line with the MSCI ACWI Index on forward 
P/E (bottom left chart). Capital efficiency is also 
improving: Shareholder payouts hit a record ~¥40T 
in FY2025, double 2021 levels, with buybacks nearly 
half the total and a ~4.1% shareholder yield, above its 
20-year average (bottom right chart).

Neuberger Regional Equity 
Recommendations
• Global Equities (Neutral): Downgraded from overweight. 
Fundamentals are intact, but the balance of risks has shifted: 
A market break could hit both AI capex and the wealth effect; 
valuations are exposed to rates and bond volatility; and the AI 
buildout relies on capital markets.
• U.S. Large Cap (Overweight): Strongest earnings quality and 
AI capex conviction, in our view; earnings broadening beyond 
mega-caps. Barbell of quality Tech and cyclicals, with Health 
Care as an AI hedge.
• U.S. Small and Mid Cap (Neutral): Downgraded from 
overweight after a strong run. Most exposed to floating-rate 
funding and refinancing as rates rise; the group may suffer 
from weaker interest coverage, input-cost pressure, and 
softening earnings estimates.
• Europe (Underweight): Equity performance has been 
disconnected from a hollow economy; German growth is 
driven primarily by government stimulus. Half of 2026 EPS 
growth is from banks, energy and autos; ECB risks tightening 
into weakness.
• Emerging Markets (Neutral): Downgraded from overweight. 
Index concentration in a handful of semiconductor names 
has turned broad EM into a technology proxy. We favor select 
EM tied to domestic growth rather than broad EM beta.
• Japan (Overweight): Moving from reflation to a capex boom; 
earnings and sales revisions near highs. Governance reforms 
are gaining traction; shareholder payouts are at a record high; 
still, valuations in line with ACWI.
• China (Overweight): Investment and export driven growth 
offsetting slowing domestic demand. Further deterioration 
in data likely to incentivize stimulus. “Anti-involution” healing 
the profit cycle.
• India (Neutral): Positive macro headlines offset by elevated 
real rates (~5%), marked-down EPS revisions, a widening 
current-account deficit, and the largest fiscal drag in a decade.

may cap the recovery.

out, but would rather get selective exposure, including hyperscalers, 
software companies with accelerating recurring revenue, and other 
names fortified by cash over those reliant on capital markets.

bottom of its 10-year range, utilities (as bond proxies) lose most as 
yields and bond volatility rise. We prefer to get AI power exposure 
through Industrials instead.

Index Definitions
The S&P 500 Index consists of 500 U.S. stocks chosen for market size, liquidity and industry group representation. 
It is a market value-weighted index (stock price times number of shares outstanding), with each stock’s weight in the 
Index proportionate to its market value.
The MSCI ACWI Index captures large and mid-cap representation across 23 Developed Markets (DM) and 24 
Emerging Markets (EM) countries. DM countries include: Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Denmark, Finland, 
France, Germany, Hong Kong, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Japan, Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Portugal, Singapore, 
Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, the U.K. and the U.S. EM countries include: Brazil, Chile, China, Colombia, Czech Republic, 
Egypt, Greece, Hungary, India, Indonesia, Korea, Kuwait, Malaysia, Mexico, Peru, Philippines, Poland, Qatar, Saudi 
Arabia, South Africa, Taiwan, Thailand, Turkey and United Arab Emirates. With 2,837 constituents, the index covers 
approximately 85% of the global investable equity opportunity set.
The MSCI China Index captures large and mid-cap representation across China A shares, H shares, B shares, Red 
chips, P chips and foreign listings (e.g., ADRs). With 655 constituents, the index covers about 85% of this China equity 
universe.
The MSCI Europe Index captures large and mid-cap representation across Developed Markets (DM) countries in 
Europe. The index covers approximately 85% of the free float-adjusted market capitalization across the European 
Developed Markets equity universe.
The STOXX 600 Index is a broad measure of the European equity market, consisting of 600 components that 
represent large-, mid-, and small-capitalization companies across 17 countries and 11 industries within Europe. It covers 
approximately 90% of the free-float market capitalization of the European stock market.
