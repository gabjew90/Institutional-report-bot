• Following last week's visit to Siemens Energy's gas 
turbine facility in Berlin, we stayed on the road and visited 
Forgent’s operations in Minnesota this week. A closer 
look at the company’s facilities revealed how one of the 
fastest-growing electrical equipment suppliers is helping 
address key bottlenecks across the power infrastructure 
ecosystem that underpins our theme.
• The visit reinforced our view that manufacturing capacity 
remains a key constraint across the electrical equipment 
value chain, while also highlighting how Forgent's 
vertically integrated operating model, customization 
capabilities, and rapid expansion are helping it gain 
share and strengthen relationships with increasingly 
sophisticated customers.
• Although successful execution will be essential as the 
company scales production, workforce, and capacity, 
we believe Forgent is well positioned to benefit from 
sustained demand growth and could emerge as an 
increasingly important player in the next phase of power 
and electrification investment.

Our view in summary
A stream of headlines have moved markets in both 
directions over the past few weeks. Macro developments 
including central bank rate discussions, the final stretch of 
the US midterm campaign, new announcements on power 
capacity, and a constant flow of AI-related news have all 
contributed to shifting narratives and price moves across 
various segments of our Power and resources theme. Yet we 
continue to believe the trajectory of this cycle will ultimately 
be determined by physical capacity, execution, and earnings 
power rather than headlines.
In this environment, we believe there is no substitute 
for getting right to the source where earnings power 
is generated and fundamental value becomes more 
transparent. So, following last week's visit to Siemens 
Energy's gas turbine factory in Berlin, we decided to stay 
on the road and continue tracing the buildout of the 
power ecosystem. This week, we traveled to Minnesota 
to visit Forgent Power Solutions' electrical-equipment 
operations, providing another opportunity to pressure-
test the underlying fundamentals directly on the factory 
floor and identify where demand, capacity constraints, and 
competitive positioning can translate into durable value 
creation.

Weekly performance commentary
Power and resources had a positive performance on 
an absolute basis and moved broadly in line with the 
benchmark MSCI ACWI Index in the week ending 9 
October. On a sector level, gains were primarily led by 
utilities companies, while equipment providers lagged the 
benchmark. Geographically, US names led the pack, with 
Europe slightly retracting.
Thoughts of the week
What is Forgent Power Solutions?
In this week s briefing, we take a deep dive into one 
of our recent high-conviction additions to the Power 
and resources portfolio (and recently-named Top Pick on 
CIO's US Industrials Equity Preference List): Forgent Power 
Solutions (FPS). While this piece is more company-specific 
than usual, we hope it also provides insight into how 
we research unique opportunities beyond the well-known 
incumbents for the benefit of clients.
Formed by middle-market private equity firm Neos Partners, 
Forgent is a combination of four companies making medium 
and low-voltage transformers, switchgear, and e-houses for 
utility, data center, and industrial customers. It is scaling 
production capacity at a rapid pace, helping it deliver 
product meaningfully faster than rivals. In tandem, FPS 
is building credibility with sophisticated customers, and 
recently won its first major order with a leading AI lab.
Investors question whether Forgent is simply benefiting 
from an unusually tight market or building a differentiated 
platform that can sustain growth as industry capacity 
expands. After visiting the company s new manufacturing 
plant in Minnesota and seeing its competitive advantages 
firsthand, we are increasingly confident that FPS is not 
merely a swing producer of electrical equipment, but a 
future incumbent.
The state of play in electrical equipment
Many companies in Power and resources supply the medium 
and low-voltage market with transformers, switchgear, 
power distribution units, and circuit breakers, including 
Eaton, Schneider Electric, Siemens Energy and NARI 
Technology. While supply is not quite as tight as for high-
voltage equipment, lead times for this gear can stretch 
to two years or more. And while many participants are 
adding manufacturing capacity, the challenges associated 
with training fresh labor and managing a complex supply 
chain has exacerbated delays.
This scarcity is central to our positive thesis on the 
electrical equipment industry. Manufacturing capacity—not 
just electricity supply—is a gating factor for data centers, 
grid modernization, and industrial electrification. Suppliers 
are adding significant capacity, but lead times for delivery 
remain extended and order backlogs are still growing.
Our team keeps a close eye on supply side developments, 
as properly managing the supply/demand gap is key 
to sustaining pricing power for the manufacturers and 
smoothing the profitability of the cycle. As part of this 
monitoring, we became interested in Forgent, which, with 
2.4m square feet of (mostly new) manufacturing space, 
can deliver product in 6-9 months—a major selling point. 
Unlike subscale rivals, we believe it has the potential to 
capture a larger piece of the growing opportunity set. 
More specifically, we visited to assess the improvement 
in throughput, operational execution, and the quality of 
demand.
A snapshot of the factory
Forgent’s 500,000 square foot manufacturing facility 
in Minnesota designs and manufactures engineered-to-
order electrical distribution equipment, medium-voltage 
switchgear, e-houses, and power skids. The location only 
began producing about nine months ago, but was already 
buzzing with activity on our visit, and is scaling up rapidly. 
The origin of MN operations was States Manufacturing
—a fourth-generation family business making custom 
electrical equipment. This represents about 1/5 of FPS’ total 
manufacturing square footage, with the remainder in Texas, 
Los Angeles, Maryland and Tijuana, Mexico.
Given the clean-sheet design of Forgent’s new facilities, the 
manufacturing process is exceptionally vertically-integrated, 
beginning with 2500lb boxes of coated copper and steel 
sheets, and ending with an e-house stretching up to 60 feet 
in length and weighing up to 50 metric tons. The only major 
components sourced externally are circuit breakers, supplied 
by peers such as Eaton and ABB. We observed the entire 
process from metal bending to painting to wiring to final 
assembly, and came to fully appreciate the flexibility and 
quality control that full integration provides.
Importantly, while many of the steps were standardized, the 
product designs are highly customized. Management noted

the company has about 50k custom reference designs, and 
can facilitate nearly-infinite permutations of wiring.
Floor space appeared well-utilized, but with only 9 months 
of operation so far, management sees ample room to boost 
capacity and reduce inefficiencies within the facility. While 
much of electrical equipment manufacturing is still manual 
today, we spotted modern automation in many areas of 
the fabrication process. The main visible constraints were 
circuit breakers, which are supplied by third parties, as well 
as training of new labor to add a second shift. We observed 
a well-organized “training dojo” where new recruits were 
practicing assembling switchgear, and management holds a 
favorable opinion of the talent pool in the area.
Why is Forgent gaining share? Four competitive 
advantages we took away from the tour:
1. Leading with technical know-how
By providing custom powertrain solutions and producing 
most components in-house, FPS can sit with the customer 
and design the product to meet unique project-specific 
specifications. More importantly, it can deliver those 
complex solutions at scale, and faster than rivals. 
As credibility with customers builds, FPS “content per 
megawatt” increases. Illustrating this, two years ago an e-
house used to include 25%-30% FPS content, while today 
this is 75-80%. Close collaboration with the few external 
suppliers is helpful as well—for example, the cooling system 
for the e-house is manufactured by a different business just 
down the street.
2. Complexity is a feature, not a flaw
While many peers aim to standardize their product for the 
sake of manufacturing efficiency, Forgent thrives on custom 
orders. Customers may want a specific voltage, e-house 
size, wiring pattern or brand of circuit breaker, and FPS 
can meet this need at scale while modularizing portions 
of the production process. Custom solutions only fetch a 
modest pricing premium, but management believes it can 
“indoctrinate speed” and efficiency by building an end-to-
end digitized system for quoting, entering, and completing 
the path of these orders.
3. Vertical integration supports nimbleness
Whereas several peers have been bogged down by supply 
chain constraints in recent years, Forgent rarely has to wait 
for the “golden screw” needed to complete a delivery. By 
making structures and componentry in house from raw 
materials, the production line can work around temporary 
constraints and substitute inputs as needed. While we 
understand keeping operations running smoothly while 
scaling massive capacity is a day-to-day challenge for 
management, we have been very impressed that FPS has 
avoided the sales constraints experienced by other key 
participants in the industry. In addition, the corporate 
office appears quite lean and well-connected to the factory 
floor, allowing for quick decision-making on new capital 
investments.
Vertical integration improves the value proposition to the 
customer via fewer interfaces, more coordinated delivery, 
and reduced procurement risk.
4. Capacity matters more than ever
With demand off the charts, adding manufacturing capacity 
at scale drives very high returns on investment. Forgent’s 
shorter lead times, paired with burgeoning trust from 
customers, has helped it grow backlog from USD 850mm in 
FY25 to USD 3.0bn in FY26. Backlog now covers ~90% of 
forward revenue, providing visibility to operating leverage as 
excess capacity fills out.
Moreover, while the company historically only sold to OEMs 
and EPCs, it is now winning orders from hyperscalers, 
frontier AI labs and neoclouds. Given the pace of capacity 
expansion (scaling to 120 e-houses per month), we expect 
FPS to sustain below-peer lead times despite this rapid order 
growth.
Investment implications
The visit reaffirmed our view that rising supply of 
medium and low-voltage electrical equipment continues to 
undershoot true demand, and that Forgent is particularly 
well-positioned to capture a larger slice of the pie. 
Importantly, we underappreciated the extent to which FPS 
vertical integration is a durable competitive advantage, and 
believe the company is taking a long-term strategy to 
build customer trust (particularly by avoiding price gouging 
despite these advantages). Given the sophistication of 
hyperscaler and frontier AI customers, we believe it will 
remain challenging for new entrants to unseat incumbents, 
but FPS has the greatest potential to do so, in our view.
What could challenge our thesis?
We believe execution risk on ambitious growth plans 
is the key swing factor for our thesis. Forgent must 
juggle numerous priorities simultaneously: building square 
footage, adding shifts, training fresh labor, ramping output, 
keeping quality control tight, and delivering on time 
to highly-discerning customers, all at critical stage of 
reputation-building. We believe strong talent is in place 
and up to the task, but this is no easy task. Given the 
company’s significant capital investment for the foreseeable 
future, it will take time before investors will be able to 
observe normalized incremental margin performance, but 
we believe this to be 25-30% under the surface.
More broadly, we must also monitor delays or cancellations 
among major customer projects, as well as the fine balance 
between supply and demand.

What’s next for Forgent?
Besides scaling capacity and meeting utilization milestones 
to convert backlog, we see several additional avenues for 
value creation. Unlike peers, FPS does not have a service 
arm of any meaningful size, and we believe building 
competency in on-site commissioning, start-up and testing 
is a key priority (perhaps coming via acquisitions). From a 
product portfolio perspective, FPS is already highly vertically-
integrated and is not interested in internalizing circuit 
breaker manufacturing. However, areas such as protection, 
controls, and busways would be natural extensions. 
Management noted that entering the European market 
could be an option down the road as well.
From a financial perspective, we will look for consistent 
margin progression as capacity ramps and customer quality 
improves.
From the factory floor to our portfolio
Power and resources targets companies capitalizing on 
bottlenecks in the electrical infrastructure supply chain, 
and we firmly believe value will accrue to participants (like 
Forgent) turning scarce capacity into reliable delivery and 
attractive returns on invested capital.

performance of the EPL owner(s). Including a stock in the EPL constitutes neither a view on its expected, standalone absolute

many other factors. UBS GWM CIO‘s selection methodology enables wealth management clients to invest in a specific 
investment theme or focus on a sector/industry or country/region.

within an EPL. Stocks can be selected for multiple EPLs. For consistency's sake, a stock can only be selected as either Most 
Preferred or Least Preferred, not both simultaneously. As EPL benchmarks differ, stocks do not need to be included on every 
list to which they could theoretically be added.
