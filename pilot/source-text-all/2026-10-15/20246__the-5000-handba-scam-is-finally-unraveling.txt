Hangover
For years, the luxury industry operated under a remarkably simple business model: take a handbag,
raise the price, call it exclusivity, and watch the profits roll in.
Then raise the price again. And again.
Between 2019 and 2025, iconic handbag prices surged by 50–70%. There was just one problem:
eventually, customers stopped buying.
The industry's active customer base has collapsed to 2013 levels, luxury stocks have lost hundreds of
billions in market value, and Bernard Arnault, once the world's richest man, has been kicked out of the
billionaire top ten.
Here are the charts explaining luxury's increasingly expensive hangover.
13 year setback
The luxury industry has a customer problem.
According to Bain data featured in Goldman's report, the number of active luxury consumers surged
from 330 million in 2013 to 400 million in 2022. By 2025, that number was back to 330 million.
Seventy million customers gone in just three years. And while the industry's addressable market
continued growing, actual participation went in the opposite direction.
Bain
China's missing handbag buyers
The Chinese consumer was supposed to be the industry's perpetual growth engine.
Unfortunately, China's young consumers have more immediate concerns than choosing between
Gucci and Louis Vuitton.

Youth unemployment remains elevated, the property market has weakened household wealth, and
consumer confidence is struggling.
The result? One of the world's most important luxury markets is increasingly populated by consumers
who have better things to do with their money than buy another overpriced handbag.
NBS
Lower highs
For years, luxury stocks were treated almost like technology companies: powerful brands, exceptional
margins, global growth and seemingly limitless pricing power.
But the sector's market capitalization tells a very different story today.
Following the post-COVID boom, luxury equities have struggled to regain their former highs. The latest
selloff has only reinforced the downtrend.
As Reuters reported this week, European luxury stocks have lost roughly $350 billion in market value
during 2026.

The billionaire downgrade
Perhaps the most symbolic casualty of luxury's fall from grace is Bernard Arnault.
The LVMH chairman, once the world's richest man, was kicked out of the billionaire top ten in
September after his fortune reportedly shrank by $65 billion this year.
For a man who made his fortune selling exclusivity, being excluded from the world's most exclusive
financial club must sting.
Still, we doubt he'll be shopping at Zara anytime soon.
Greedflation

Goldman argues that aggressive pricing, rather than macroeconomic weakness alone, has been a
major driver of luxury's slowdown.
Between 2019 and 2025, iconic handbag prices surged by 50–70%, often without meaningful
improvements in quality or innovation.
The industry called it brand elevation. Consumers had another word for it: expensive.
Bain
Price pushers
According to McKinsey, roughly 80% of luxury-market growth between 2023 and 2025 came from
higher prices rather than higher volumes. In other words, luxury's growth strategy increasingly
consisted of selling the same things to fewer people for more money.
Less innovation, higher prices
And while prices were soaring, innovation was going in the opposite direction.
According to Goldman, the number of new "hero bag" launches collapsed by 70–80% compared with
pre-COVID levels.
Charge more. Innovate less. What could possibly go wrong?
GIR
Super-spenders
As millions of aspirational buyers disappeared, the industry became increasingly dependent on its
wealthiest customers.
According to Bain, consumers spending more than €20,000 annually now account for 46% of luxury
sales, up from 30% in 2019.
Apparently, the solution to losing customers was to sell more expensive things to the ones who
remained.

Bain
Rich get richer
There is, however, one important reason not to write off the luxury industry entirely.
The wealthy are still wealthy.
Goldman's analysis shows a striking historical relationship between changes in the wealth of America's
richest 1% and luxury sales growth in the region, with a two-quarter lead and a correlation of 0.89.
This makes luxury increasingly dependent on the same asset-price inflation that has powered the
broader K-shaped economy.
When stocks rise, the wealthy feel richer and spend more.
When stocks fall, even the Birkin budget may need reviewing.
GIR
Two super-powers
The luxury industry's global diversification is not quite as diversified as it might appear.
American consumers account for approximately 27% of luxury spending, while Chinese consumers
represent another 22%.
Together, they account for almost half the market.
This leaves the sector exposed to two very different economic realities: America's equity-driven wealth
creation and China's property-driven wealth destruction.
The former has been relatively supportive. The latter decidedly less so.
GIR
Even the Birkin is losing some of its magic
If there is one brand that was supposed to be immune to the luxury slowdown, it was Hermès.
After all, when customers have to wait years for a handbag, demand would seem to be the least of the
company's problems.
Yet Goldman notes that secondhand prices for certain Birkin and Kelly handbags have declined roughly
12–15% since early 2025.

Meanwhile, the bank has initiated coverage of Hermès with a Sell rating, arguing that the company's
historically reliable double-digit growth has slowed materially.
This does not mean the Birkin bubble has burst. Demand for new bags remains strong.
But it does suggest that even the most exclusive corner of luxury is not entirely immune to changing
consumer behavior.

Nobody wants to be seen carrying luxury

Hedge fund long positioning in European luxury has collapsed to multi-year lows, according to Morgan
Stanley Prime Brokerage.
The sector has gone from being one of Europe's favorite compounders to something investors
apparently prefer to avoid.
Luxury brands spent years making their products harder to afford.
Investors have responded by making their shares harder to love.
