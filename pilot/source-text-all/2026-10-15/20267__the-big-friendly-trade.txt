A book at bedtime 
For me, one of the best parts of the day is reading to my children at bedtime: settling into a comfortable chair and taking 
them on adventures into lands dreamed up in the fabulous imaginations of the finest authors. One staple is Roald Dahl, 
whose eccentric tales and kooky characters continue to delight around the world, with one of his books selling every three 
seconds.  
 
A beloved classic and personal favourite is The BFG, the story of the eponymous Big Friendly Giant who teams up with a poor 
orphan girl, Sophie, to take down a troop of man-eating giants with the help of Her Majesty the Queen. Stirring, patriotic 
stuff.  
 
One early scene shows our hero Sophie discovering all the strange details of the BFG’s life, and, with her tummy rumbling, 
the subject turns naturally to food. Here, the BFG shows Sophie the true price of his non-human eating ways: 
"In this sloshfunking Giant Country, happy eats like pineapples and pigwinkles is simply not growing. Nothing is 
growing except for one extremely icky-poo vegetable. It is called the snozzcumber… I squoggle it! I mispise it! I dispunge 
it! But because I is refusing to gobble up human beans like the other giants, I must spend my life guzzling up icky-poo 
snozzcumbers instead” 
Sophie’s initial reaction is denial, proclaiming that such a vegetable simply does not exist. The wise BFG gently reprimands 
her kneejerk scepticism, reminding her that: 
“Yesterday we is not believing in giants, was we? Today we is not believing in snozzcumbers. Just because we happen 
not to have actually seen something with our own two little winkles, we think it is not existing.” 
We have said it before, and we will say it again: all great works of art contain valuable lessons for the thoughtful investor – 
and fantastical children’s stories are no exception. The BFG’s gentle caution is as clear as it is fundamental: a lack of personal 
experience does not mean that something cannot happen, does not exist, or is beyond the realms of possibility.  
 
Indeed, in decision-making theory, this is more formally captured by the availability heuristic, defined as:  
“A judgmental heuristic in which a person evaluates the frequency of classes or the probability of events by availability, i.e., 
by the ease with which relevant instances come to mind”1 
Today, many investors in our view are at risk of falling foul of this dangerous oversight, with one blind spot in particular: the 
continued exceptionalism of American equity returns. 
 
The American dream 
Most professional investors currently peddling their wares – your author humbly included – have lived and worked through 
a virtually uninterrupted period where one single trade dominated: buy America. In the last twenty years, the US equity 
market has resoundingly outperformed other major global markets, whether taken together – represented by the MSCI EAFE 
Index, which captures developed market returns outside of North America - or assessed separately.

As a result, the idea that there could ever be a period during which US equities might underperform their international peers 
is as foreign to many investors as the snozzcumber was to Sophie. With no prior personal experience to think of, and no 
recent data pointing to the contrary, why would this prospect even cross their mind?  
 
Many investors have seen their exposures to US companies balloon over time, rising to historically unprecedented levels. A 
cursory look at the composition of the MSCI World, the most widely used benchmark of global equity markets, will attest to 
that fact: today, the US comprises 71.7% of the global market2, the culmination of a steady upward march over the past 
twenty years.

Whilst it is true that corporate America has delivered strong earnings growth and economic success, this outsized 
representation runs well ahead of their share of global GDP, which currently stands at a mere 25.6%3. Instead, the majority 
of the increase in global equity market weight has been driven by an increase in valuation, rather than earnings: investors 
have been, by and large, willing to pay more and more for each dollar of American earnings than international ones.  
 
Comparing the cumulative growth of earnings for various markets with the growth in their valuations – how much investors 
are willing to pay for those earnings – makes this point even more explicit.

In light of these seismic shifts, driven largely by changes in valuation, we believe that the cautious investor – cognisant of the 
ringing alarm bells in US equity valuations – must consider today whether the future may look different from the recent 
past.  
 
The fall of a giant 
Although it has faded from memory, and certainly from headlines, there have in fact been sustained periods of time when 
US investors suffered years of substantial equity market underperformance, typically following the bursting of a large 
valuation bubble. For instance, at the end of the Nifty-Fifty era in the late 1970’s – when 50 US blue-chip companies such as 
Xerox, Polaroid and Revlon were seen as safe investments at any price – investors caught up in this enthusiasm with US 
companies saw the value of their shares plummet, and would spend the next six years trailing their international peers by 
nearly four hundred percentage points:

Today, we stand at a time in history when the share prices of US corporations have, in aggregate, outperformed their 
international peers for the longest stretch on record: 17.2 years and counting, eclipsing their prior run of 6.1 years, from 
1994 – 2000, memorably a time of a large build-up of frothy valuation excess. As we have previously noted, US equity 
valuations today are abnormally elevated, sitting well above the Nifty Fifty era, and only a touch below the highs experienced 
just before the dotcom bubble burst in early 2000. Both events triggered a subsequent multi—year period of international 
outperformance against the US, with the start of the new millennium a similarly bleak time for US domestic equities.

We are not, of course, suggesting that investors flee from the US in its entirety, nor do we claim to be so prescient as to 
foresee what the next decade holds. However, our observations are merely that, whilst it may seem unconscionable to many, 
the US market can, and has, underperformed its international peers for many years in the past. With valuations as high as 
they are today, the risk of that repeating in the future is, at the very least, a genuine one, one which all investors would be 
prudent to consider. 
 
In sickness, health  
Before we conclude, one final word of caution about the US market is worth considering. The current boom is being notably 
driven by ever fewer companies, a sign of an extraordinarily inefficient market propped up by myopic investors crowding 
into a waning number of trades and pushing the envelope of irrational exuberance. As this is being written, the clearest 
example yet of this phenomenon has presented itself: on Monday, September 21st – when the S&P 500 Index rallied to within 
1% of its all-time high – more companies in the index fell to new lows than new highs5. There have only been two days in 
recorded history with similar dynamics at play: December 21st, 1999, and July 23rd, 1929, both pre-empting devastating and 
historic losses for US equity markets – and marking the start of years of international outperformance.  
 
A positive corollary of this high degree of US dispersion – many companies seeing valuations fall, offset by a few huge winners 
– is that, for the discerning active investor willing to look hard, there is an increasing number of cheap opportunities 
emerging in the US. Indeed, the exposure to North America in our global strategy has increased through the course of 2026

As the BFG cautions Sophie, it is incumbent on investors today to be mindful that, just because many today have never seen 
(with their own two little ‘winkles’) the US underperform for any meaningful stretch of time, it does not mean that this cannot 
come to pass. Indeed, we would argue that current corporate valuations, and increasing reliance on a handful of exceedingly 
popular companies, make that outcome more likely than not. As always, one man’s risk is another’s opportunity – and we 
believe that the opportunity for compelling returns lies, today, in cheap international markets.
