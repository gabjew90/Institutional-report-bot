 
The Next Treasury Put: Citi Expects Bessent To 
Slash Long-End Auctions, Maybe Kill The 20Y As AI 
Steals The Penion Bid 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 13, 2026 - 02:58 AM 
After the buybacks failed, Bessent's "Treasury put" needs a new trick. And according to Citi's 
head of US rates strategy Jason Williams, that trick will be unveiled at the November 4 quarterly 
refunding, when Treasury Secretary Scott "the House" Bessent is likely to cut the auction sizes 
for 20- and 30-year bonds, and could go as far as cancelling the 20-year altogether. Citi's base 
case is a $3 billion reduction per auction in both tenors, funded with even more T-bills, as 
Williams writes in the bank's latest US Rates Weekly, aptly titled "Pension plan for 20s?" 
(available to pro subs). 
The reason, and the part that should be of most interest to regular readers, is who Citi thinks is 
eating Uncle Sam's lunch at the long end: the same culprit we have been pointing the finger at 
for the past year - the hyperscalers. Williams flags this Friday's Treasury dealer survey as a 
possible "bullish catalyst" if the department asks primary dealers whether long-end demand for 
Treasuries is being "partly cannibalized" by high-quality AI issuance. 
Which, as we have been warning since last October, is precisely what happens when the 
biggest companies in the world decide to fund a trillion-dollar capex binge in the bond market. 
Below we walk through Citi's case, why the bank thinks pensions have gone missing from the 
long end, and why shrinking the supply of 20s is a lot easier than finding new buyers for them. 
Strong Auctions, Same Problem 
First, some context. Bond bulls got a rare win last Wednesday when the 10Y auction priced at 
5.300%, the highest 10Y auction yield since November 2000, yet stopped through the When 
Issued by 1.7bps, the biggest stop since April 2025, as foreign buyers took down a whopping 

 
80.3% and dealers were left with just 2.5%, the lowest in 10Y history. At the time, we asked 
whether Bessent had "called in a favor" from one or more "friendly" G7 nations. 
Citi's version is more charitable: the "very low dealer takedown of ~2.5% suggested that foreign 
demand has returned, as it was at the September auction," and the bank has found foreign 
takedown for the 10Y "to be somewhat persistent." 
 
 

 
Still, Williams is not convinced the buyers' strike is over. He remains neutral on duration, "finding 
little evidence that two consecutive strong 10y auctions mark an end of the current mini-buyers' 
strike," and points out that USTs have tended to sell off after two consecutive strong 10Y 
auctions over the past 15 years (though the sample is small). 
 
Meanwhile, the term premium is "starting to break out of the post-tariff range," which Citi blames 
squarely on Chair Warsh, who "pushed on ahead" with a September hike despite the risk of a 
UST buyers' strike. Citi had expected him not to "open Pandora's box." He did. The box is now 
open. 

 
 
And the 20Y specifically has been the problem child. Recall that last month's 20Y auction was a 
disaster: it priced at 5.420%, the highest yield since this maturity was reintroduced in 2020, 
tailed by 2.0bps, and saw Indirects plunge to 52.5%, the lowest on record. This came on the 
same day Bessent bragged to Congress that Treasury had "just had 2 of the most successful 
debt auctions in 20 years," both of which were on days when yields had soared and buyers got 
a fat concession. We put out this chart at the time:

It followed an August 20Y auction which, despite Bessent's same-day panic doubling of

Translation: if you can't buy back enough 20s, just stop selling them. 
Cutting The Long End: $3 Billion At A Time 
Here is Citi's core call: 
We remain neutral on the curve, mostly due to our expectation that Treasury Secretary Bessent 
is likely to reduce the auction sizes for 20s and 30s at the November 4th refunding. Our base 
case is a $3bn reduction per auction which would be funded by additional T-bill issuance. One 
bullish catalyst next week is Friday's dealer survey which could ask a question about such a 
possibility. For instance, Treasury could ask dealers if long-end demand for USTs is being partly 
cannibalized by the increased IG issuance from hyperscalers. We remain in 10s20s spreads 
steepeners to position for a continued reduction, or perhaps cancellation, of the 20y bond. 
Put differently, Citi is positioned for 20Y paper to outperform 10s (via swap spreads) as the 
supply of 20s shrinks. Notably, Citi's own supply forecasts already have the cut baked in: the 

 
November 20Y and 30Y auctions are penciled in at $13BN and $22BN (vs $16BN and $25BN at 
the last refunding-month new issues), with December at $10BN and $19BN. 
How much does it matter? Some napkin math using Citi's auction sizes: at current sizes, the 
20Y and 30Y combine for roughly $444 billion a year in gross issuance. A $3 billion cut per 
auction removes about $72 billion a year, or roughly 1.6% of Citi's $4.4 trillion in projected 2026 
gross coupon issuance. Kill the 20Y on top of that and long-end supply drops by more than 
$200 billion a year, a cut of nearly half. 
 
Of course, those dollars don't disappear: they get funded in bills, which are already piling up. 
With Bills already at almost 23% of total US debt outstanding, the highest since 2010, except for 
the covid crisis period... 

 
 
... Citi expects private T-bill outstanding to reach an all-time high of $6.74 trillion at the end of 
October and top $6.9 trillion by the end of November, which, together with a TGA balance 
temporarily above $1 trillion in the last week of October, "could push SOFR fixes lightly above 
IORB." 
 

 
For now, the Fed is happy to soak up the bills, reinvesting maturing MBS into T-bills, which 
brings to mind something we tweeted last month:

Swap "repurchase" for "stop issuing" and the plumbing looks very similar. It also happens to be 
the same move Bessent spent much of 2024 attacking Janet Yellen for: shortening the debt's 
maturity by leaning on bills. (Turns out it looks a lot better from the inside.) 
Bessent wouldn't be the first to blink, either. Last month the Bank of England said it would stop 
selling 20- and 30-year giltsafter long gilt prices hit their lowest since 1998. When the long end 
won't clear, issuers everywhere reach for the same lever. 
Where Did The Pensions Go? 
This is where Citi's note earns its title. Back in June, the bank argued that corporate defined 
benefit pension demand would return at 5.3% to 5.5% 30Y yields as funding ratios rose. Yields 
got there (and then some), the funded ratio hit ~115%, a post-GFC high and 3pp above the May 
2026 level that triggered substantial buying... and yet: 
There was very little STRIPS demand in September (Figure 14). This is despite the funded ratio 
rising to ~115%, which was a 3pp increase from the local May 2026 high. 

 
 
 
For those unfamiliar, pensions follow a "glide path" that shifts allocations from equities to 
long-duration fixed income as funded ratios rise, and the long STRIPS market has historically 
been the tell. So if the funded ratio is at a post-GFC high and the STRIPS bid is missing, the 
money is going somewhere else. Citi's guess: 

 
We do expect pension demand to pick up somewhat if yields move higher from here, perhaps 
5.75% to 6% in the long bond, but one risk may be that pension funds are buying more 
long-dated IG bonds due to the AI-related issuance backdrop. 
The other problem is that the glide path may be nearly finished. Citi notes that pension equity 
allocations sit around 24% per Milliman (down from 32% at the end of 2020) against a terminal 
target of roughly 20%, which implies only ~$30BN to $50BN of large pension fund demand left 
for USTs, assuming $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion of assets follow a glide path. Rounding error, meet 
rounding error. 
And if pensions do come back at 5.75%-6% on the 30Y, that would be another 15-40bps above 
the 5.60% where the 30Y stood on Oct 8, per Citi. That's not a floor anyone at Treasury wants to 
test. 
The AI Debt Elephant In The Long End 
Here Citi is careful to hedge: "while we do not think such IG supply has impacted the overall 
level of rates, we do think pension funds could be leaning into long-end IG bonds more than 
they typically have." 
Goldman is less shy. In Friday's US Equities Weekly Rundown, which we discussed in detail on 
Saturday, Goldman's futures desk said OpenAI's revenue restatement headlines "prompted the 
rally in bond futures on Thursday," and that this reaction: 
supports the thesis that expectations for more AI-related bond issuance have helped drive the 
rise in longer term UST yields as government and corporate issuers compete for the same 
capital. 
In other words, a hint that the AI capex machine might slow was enough to spark a long-bond 
rally, which is a strange outcome if AI supply has nothing to do with rate levels. The Ultra Long 
future is down more than 5% over the past two months, while asset manager shorts in the long 
bond "had risen sharply since mid-September":

Put simply: a pension fund can pick up a record ~50bps over comparable non-AI IG paper (and 
far more over Treasuries) by buying long-dated hyperscaler bonds, so why buy the 20Y? 
Especially when the preferred debtor is competing with none other than Uncle Sam for the same 
paper! 
"Band-Aid On A Gunshot Wound" 
Not everyone is convinced that shrinking the long end will help. Last week, BNP Paribas warned 
that Bessent should resist calls to axe the 20-year, as doing so could send borrowing costs 
higher: cancelling a tenor may signal that Washington is struggling to fund itself, which could 
push yields higher elsewhere on the curve. In BNP's view, Treasury interventions are a 

 
"band-aid on a gunshot wound" unless inflation and fiscal deficits are addressed, and the bank 
is sticking with its 30Y short, targeting 5.8%. 
That's the same point we made when the buybacks fizzled: technical fixes treat the symptom, 
and the bond market (unlike the stock market) is rarely fooled for long. Besides, US yields aren't 
rising in a vacuum: Japanese repatriation keeps draining foreign demand, and Japan now looms 
over France's bond rout too. 
Bottom Line 
The next catalysts are close. CPI hits this week (Citi expects core at 0.2% MoM, and is 
positioned for the Fed to stay on hold in October), the dealer survey lands Friday, and the 
refunding itself follows on November 4. Williams's final word on why he stays in the trade: 
We remain in 10s20s spreads steepeners to position for a continued reduction, or perhaps 
cancellation, of the 20y bond. While we do not think such IG supply has impacted the overall 
level of rates, we do think pension funds could be leaning into long-end IG bonds more than 
they typically have. 
We think Citi is right on the call and BNP is right on the consequences. Treasury will almost 
certainly trim the long end, and the 20Y, a tenor that was revived in 2020 after a 34-year 
absence and has struggled to find a natural buyer ever since, is the obvious candidate for the 
chop. But fewer 20s won't conjure up new buyers, and the cut itself is an admission that the 
buyers have gone elsewhere, at a time when the AI complex is issuing 40-year paper as fast as 
banks can underwrite it and the funding gets pushed into a bill market already headed for record 
size. 
The Treasury put keeps getting reinvented, and the long end keeps calling its bluff. We'll find out 
on next Friday how much of it the dealers are willing to say out loud, and whether the Treasury 
will go all in on Activist Treasury Issuance on Nov 4, just before the midterms...
