Democrats end up with marginal majorities in the House and Senate, at a minimum. 
Regardless of how many seats they pick up, their challenge for the next two years is to be 
something other than the repository of votes when people are disaffected with Republican 
rule. Since 1995, Democrats have not controlled the House for more than two consecutive 
Congresses, after controlling the House for all but two sessions from 1931 to 1995. Is this the 
year when a more durable base begins to build? There is a generational turn in place among 
voters, and the Reagan Republican model is out of favour (the populism Trump won with). As far 
left and far right converge on many issues, proposing solutions that stretch ever farther from the 
increasingly distanced centre, one fundamental difference between these two ends remains -- the 
left wants to remake, redefine society, the right wants to protect it. One reason why, during 
periods of stress, countries drift “right” rather than “left” (Medicare for all, but no transgender 
athletes on women’s teams). For Republicans in this election, immediate issues are the loss of 
Latino support and poorer turnout whenever Trump is not on the ballot. A lot of money is being 
spent to scare those voters to show up, but they seem uninterested in making the effort. I am not 
sure that more ads impact the result, though election consultants always think so. The Latino 
vote is more problematic long run for Republicans (for whom immigration remains a key hot-
button issue), but here again the Democrats need to tread carefully, Hispanics tend to vote for 
Democrats in the mid-terms, less so in presidential years. By my reckoning, Democrats likely 
end up with 219 seats in the House and 51 in the Senate. Enough seats to deliver the legislative 
stalemate that markets often prefer (Coalition government to solve critical problems, like the

Elections turn on the issue of the day, and its importance is reflected in consumer confidence -
- a number cemented during the summer as far as November voting patterns are concerned. 
When the Conference Board consumer confidence index is <100 in July, the incumbent party in 
the WH (Democrat or Republican) is most often voted out of office (Table 1).  Mid-terms are more 
complex because people vote for local officials rather than a national candidate running on a 
national agenda (Gingrich turned that argument on its head in 1994, but 1994 remains an 
exception). The median change in seats for the party not holding the WH during a mid-term 
election is +2 in the Senate and +14 in the House. When the confidence index is <95 in July, the 
median change is +5 in the Senate and +38 in the House. The July 2026 number was 90.2. As for 
the issues driving voters, most often it is the economy and that, most often, delivers the biggest 
change. 
This year, no surprise, the economy is the biggest issue. The future of democracy ranks second 
among all registered voters, followed by healthcare. Trump and the corruption in his 
Administration, real or perceived, only matters to 36% of voters (Chart 1) -- but it was less than 
10% at the end of 2024.

Those voting for Republican candidates generally have a different order of concerns than 
those voting for Democrats, but this year both sides place the economy first. Democracy is 
second for Democrats with healthcare third. Republicans put immigration second, followed by 
democracy, then crime (Chart 2). Crime is much less of an issue for Democrats (37% vs 64% for 
Republicans). For Democrats, the environment is much more an issue (66% vs 15% for 
Republicans). When the survey moves out to include all registered voters, the environment is only 
41%. Global warming will have to wait until 2028, and candidates seem to have gotten the 
message (based on the non-stop commercials I have seen in the NY metro area) – they are all 
about data farms and personal trading.

the net satisfaction of US citizens about the general direction of the country. Long-standing 
readers of my work know that I have long charted the growing spread between real GDP per 
capita and trend since the 08-09 recession. This widening spread is the problem all 
administrations have been trying to narrow. While the spread is clearly the construct of an

Although Republicans and Democrats are equally exercised about the economy, the 
motivation amongst them to vote has grown for those wanting to vote for Democrats. About 
58% of those who would vote for a Republican were motivated to vote in 2022 (last mid-term), 
only 46% feel so motivated today. In 2022, 56% of Democrats were motivated to vote for their 
candidates, today that number is 63%. (Chart 4) Supporters of the party out of the WH are often 
the more motivated to vote in the mid-terms. It is always more problematic for Republicans when 
Trump is not on the ballot – hence his campaign to make people think he is on the ballot. But that 
tack appears to be falling short, as any number of Trump supporters have become less 
enamoured with his leadership. According to a recent Echelon Insights poll taken for Puck 
News, in January of 2025, “59 percent of Republicans identified more with Trump, while 36 
percent identified with the party. Today only 34 percent of Republicans consider themselves 
primarily a supporter of Trump, versus 56 percent who say they support the G.O.P.”

Looking at the breakdown of who will vote for whom in the election, the turn in the Latino vote 
against Republicans looms large. In 2024, Trump won 48% of the Latino vote (some of that is 
tied to who was running for the Democrats, and I will leave that right there). Today, 51% of 
Hispanics say they will vote for the Democrat (Chart 5). Not good news, especially in Texas where 
redistricting followed the switch of Hispanic voters to Republicans in the last election, thinking 
they would stay there. Never going to happen given the behaviour of ICE in their communities. 
Does the Latino voter switch back in 2028 with a different candidate, etc.? The reason why Rubio 
makes for a stronger candidate in 2028 than Vance.  The Trump / Republican problem has long 
been that the core of their support rests on a narrowing base – white male voters. The Latino vote 
was a way to broaden their appeal, believing that Latinos are more culturally conservative than 
the Democratic agenda. It helped Trump in 2024, but ICE has crushed (splintered?) Latino 
support. Seeing this, Republicans moved to shore up their base by redistricting to eliminate black-
majority districts in red (Southern) states and questioning the citizenship of registered voters 
(Latinos). The coming two years, for Republicans, will include outreach from Republican 
candidates to heal this rift. But the rift, given some of the cultural issues at play, is not a free lunch 
for Democrats. One last point, the redistricting that eliminated black members of the House has 
motivated black voters to turn out and vote against Republican candidates (though the 
Republican running for Governor of Florida is black and should win).  
How does the Fed figure in all this? Not very much, because their tightening cycle is only just 
beginning and continually being downplayed. Nevertheless, ever since the 80-82 recession, the 
funds rate and consumer confidence rose and fell together – which makes sense given the pro-
cyclical nature of Fed policy and confidence rises and falls with the economy. But this was a 
period of growth with a falling inflation trend. While September is only one hike, with more 
presumed to come, we need to go back to the 1970s to find the last time the Fed was hiking with 
sentiment falling. High inflation, cost-of-living concerns, drove confidence more than employment 
and nominal income. True then, true now. This underscores the importance of “economy” as the 
driver of who is voting for whom. Of the three mid-terms during the 70s, only the 1978 election 
had inflation as the major issue (Vietnam in 1970, Watergate in 1974) and in 1978, Republicans 
picked up 3 Senate seats and 27 House seats (Table 1).

What this all adds to are swings to Democratic majorities in the House and Senate, but 
regardless of the size, this majority can be easily reversed in 2028. (Table 2) On the Senate 
side, there are 7 toss-up seats, and only one (Michigan) is currently held by a Democrat (now 
retiring Gary Peters). With Abdul El-Sayed the Democratic candidate, what should be a lean or 
likely Democratic seat has become a toss-up.  Current polling puts the race at a statistical tie. 
Remembering that El-Sayed had a large polling big lead in the primary, only to squeak out a win, I 
am going to guess the Republican (Mike Rogers) will win. If that is the race that keeps the 
Democrats from becoming the majority in the Senate, Schumer won’t be inviting Sanders to any 
holiday parties. That leaves the Democrats with needing to win 4 of the remaining 6 toss-up 
states (AK, IA, KS, ME, OH, TX) to gain a majority – all red states (ME is more purple and Ohio 
used to be). Based on current polling, the Dems could make clean sweep of it – but I doubt it. 
Four of these states (AK, IA, KS, TX) are deep red and have not had a Democrat as Senator in 
years (Kansas last elected one in 1930, when farmers voted for populist Democrats over bank-
supported Republicans). I think two of these states stay true to their Republican core, and my 
guess is Iowa and Alaska. For the record, a 50-50 tie does the Democrats no good since the VP 
(Vance) breaks the tie and keeps Republicans in the majority in terms of committees and, in turn, 
controlling what bills come to a vote. From a Trump agenda standpoint, 50-50 would still be 
almost as unworkable as a Democratic majority. On the House side, races are too numerous to 
break down (100% of House runs for re-election every two years), but it looks like the Democrats 
gain 4 seats and thus end up with the same majority the Republicans have now (419 seats). This 
puts Jeffries at the same mercy to his far-left socialist flank as Johnson has had to deal with his 
far-right flank in the House.

In sum, whether a good night for Democrats or great one (my bet is good), this election is 
about constraining Trump – it is not a precursor to the 2028 results. Fear of a blue tsunami 
amongst Republican supporters is throwing a lot more money at these races than Democrats can 
afford to match (or so they are saying). Given how voters feel, how they have felt since the 
summer, the only thing money can do is impact turnout – and this being an off-year, the marginal 
Republican voter is more likely to stay home (How desperate does Trump’s $5,000 promise 
sound, along with his energy ceasefire that neither Russia nor Ukraine had any idea about). 
Regardless of the size of majority the Democrats end up with in the House and Senate, reversing 
the Trump economic agenda will be impossible. Some of his excesses (the arch!) can be brought 
to heel, and there will be no Republicans leaving the Supreme Court (except maybe Alito during 
the lame duck session) Impeaching Trump is impossible and a waste of time -- there are no 15 to 
17 Republican Senators to get to the needed 67 votes to convict in the Senate. Aside from a 
waste of time, it is a losing election gambit for Democrats in 2028. And if they were successful, 
why put Vance in the president’s chair so he can run with the advantage of incumbency in 2028? 
The objective for Democrats in the next two years should be to become more than a protest vote 
against Republicans. For Republicans, their aim should be to become more than the party of 
disaffected white males without a college education – a shrinking base relative to the total voting 
population – where they were heading before ICE was activated in its present form. I am, to be 
clear, being a bit glib about this, but both parties have work to do to solidify a durable base from 
which to win in two years and beyond. That is the race that begins November 4.
