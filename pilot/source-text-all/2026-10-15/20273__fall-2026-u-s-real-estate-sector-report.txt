Resilience in a higher-for-longer environment
If the global macro landscape feels unusually complex 
and disorderly, that’s because it is. We’re not imagining the 
heightened uncertainty. We’re watching several structural 
transformations unfold simultaneously, interacting with 
one another in ways that create a more volatile and difficult 
investment environment than investors have experienced 
for much of the past three decades.
1.	Inflation has become more complicated. Recent 
inflationary pressures have been driven by factors largely 
outside the Fed’s direct control, including energy prices 
and, increasingly, trade policy. Yet the picture is more 
nuanced than headline CPI suggests. Our decomposition 
estimates indicate that CPI excluding energy and 
estimated tariff effects is running closer to 2.2%--closely 
aligned to the Fed’s broad inflation target, suggesting 
underlying inflation pressures are more contained than 
aggregate measures imply.
2.	Interest rates are rising for structural and geopolitical 
reasons. Higher neutral rates, wider term premia, and 
firmer inflation expectations have all contributed to the 
move higher in yields. Some of this increase is directly 
related to the U.S. war with Iran, which has impacted 
global commodity prices and duration premia, resulting 
in a nearly 100 bps increase in yields since March. 
Additionally, today’s environment resembles other 
periods of transformational infrastructure investment. 
Whether railroads in the nineteenth century, highways in 
the 1950s, telecommunications networks in the 1990s, 
or AI, data centers, and power infrastructure today, a 
common pattern emerges: when demand for investment 
capital grows faster than available savings, real interest 
rates tend to move higher.
3.	Growth remains surprisingly resilient. While some 
traditional economic indicators have softened, headline 
economic performance remains strong. A significant 
driver has been the surge in AI-related investment 
spending, which has become an increasingly important 
contributor to overall economic performance—adding 
nearly a quarter percent to GDP growth on an annual 
basis since 2023.
Against this backdrop, inflation and interest rates are likely 
to remain higher for longer. This has unsettled markets 
and revived concerns about a repeat of the post-pandemic 
repricing in commercial real estate (CRE). What makes this 
time different is that the CRE debt markets remain open, 
liquid, and competitive, and the banking system 
and corporate sector are in stronger condition than they 
were ahead of prior balance-sheet recessions. Credit 
availability does not insulate valuations from higher 
discount rates, but it can reduce the risk of forced sales 
and disorderly repricing.
Our base case is not a repeat of the post-pandemic 
correction, but neither is it a return to the low-rate 
environment that preceded it. Income should remain 
resilient, while durable NOI growth without excessive 
balance-sheet or capital-expenditure risk is likely to 
distinguish the strongest assets. 
This elevates the importance of net operating income 
growth as a driver of total returns, which has been a 
defining attribute of the real estate asset class. Income 
returns have historically accounted for 80% of total returns 
over the past five decades. Moreover, this income return 
based on long-term leases has proved an effective feature, 
often offsetting periods of higher inflation and interest 
rates. In today’s environment, where cap rate compression 
may be limited, NOI growth supports both income 
generation and asset appreciation. Achieving it requires not 
only selecting the right property types and markets but also 
creating value through active asset management and value-
add strategies.
Many properties and markets continue to benefit from an 
early-cycle environment characterized by rising rents and 
values. Others, however, may face a cycle that never fully 
materializes. This ultimately means investors should also 
expect a wider dispersion of returns. 
Dispersion has always existed, but for much of the past 
thirty years it has often mattered less. Broad-based 
recoveries lifted most assets, while downturns weighed 
on nearly everything. The V-shaped recovery following 
the Global Financial Crisis looked much different than 
today’s recovery. For the first time since the aftermath of 
the Savings & Loan crisis, avoiding the losers may be just as 
important as identifying the winners. In a market defined 
by structural change, selectivity matters more, and alpha is 
earned rather than given.

Sector overview
The apartment sector continues to stabilize as the construction pipeline contracts 
and the recent wave of new deliveries begins to recede. Demand remains healthy, 
although near-term rent growth is still modest, particularly in overbuilt Sun Belt 
markets. As supply growth slows, vacancies should gradually decline. Markets 
with limited construction and solid job growth are likely to lead the recovery, 
supporting firmer operating performance over the coming year.
Private equity
Broader apartment fundamentals are gradually improving as the wave of new supply delivered 
over the past several years recedes and development activity remains well below recent peaks. 
Near-term rent growth is expected to remain modest as markets absorb recent deliveries, but 
conditions are becoming increasingly differentiated by region. Coastal markets, particularly along 
the West Coast, are benefiting from limited new supply and strengthening demand, while many 
Sun Belt markets continue to work through elevated construction pipelines. Select Midwest 
markets have also performed well, supported by limited new supply and relative affordability. 
Over the medium term, slower supply growth, challenging development economics, and 
persistent housing affordability pressures should support higher occupancy, stronger rent growth, 
and a more constructive investment environment.
Apartment transaction activity increased modestly during the first half of 2026. Trailing 12-month 
transaction volume totaled $174 billion, an increase of 11.1% from a year earlier. Pricing at a 
discount to replacement cost continues to create selective acquisition opportunities, with investor 
interest gravitating toward high-quality assets in supply-constrained markets. National apartment 
vacancy stood at 4.3%, modestly higher over the trailing 12 months, while net absorption totaled 
approximately 294,600 units during the same period.
Property-level operating trends through the second quarter of 2026 also point to a gradual 
improvement in apartment fundamentals. Occupancy trended higher across a broad group 
of residential assets, while same-property NOI growth remained solid in several markets. 
Performance benefited from lease-up progress at recently delivered properties and continued 
rent growth on renewals. Results varied across residential subtypes, however. Conventional renter-
by-choice apartments generated more moderate tenant retention than alternative residential 
segments, highlighting the diversification benefits of a broader residential allocation.
Alternative and demographically supported residential strategies continued to deliver attractive 
relative performance. Age-restricted properties recorded meaningfully higher tenant retention 
than conventional apartments, supported by favorable demographic trends. Manufactured 
housing also maintains a constructive outlook, reflecting structural supply constraints and 
resilient, affordability-driven demand.
Private debt
Apartment lending remains attractive to many lenders because of the national housing 
shortage, challenging economics for first-time homebuyers, positive year-to-date net 
absorption in nearly all markets, relatively low vacancy rates, and strong market liquidity. 
Although apartment investment sales have remained subdued, strong lender demand has 
sustained robust competition for multifamily loans.
Government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs) generally offer the lowest spreads on stabilized, 
core apartment properties. Pricing can reach the low-100-basis-point (bp) range for loans 
with low to moderate leverage, supported by the GSEs’ low cost of capital. Insurance 
companies and banks typically price core apartment commercial mortgage loans at spreads

REITs
Apartment REITs have lagged the broader all-sector index in 2026, although sentiment has 
improved as investors have grown more confident that supply pressures have peaked and 
fundamentals will strengthen as new deliveries decline through 2027. Despite slower job creation, 
the low-layoff environment continues to support full employment and household formation. 
Apartments’ significant affordability advantage over homeownership, combined with high 
occupancy and solid renewal rent growth, has also sustained demand. Investor attention remains 
focused on new-lease pricing and blended leasing spreads, where recovery has been gradual and 
uneven across several supply-affected Sun Belt markets. Recent earnings reports indicate that 
conditions are stabilizing, but evidence of sustained pricing power remains limited.
Private-market values have remained relatively resilient, with institutional-quality multifamily 
assets generally trading at capitalization rates in the upper-4% to low-5% range. Public apartment 
REITs, however, continue to trade at materially higher implied capitalization rates. This valuation 
gap has encouraged asset dispositions, share repurchases, and other capital-recycling initiatives 
intended to improve shareholder value.
Northern California and New York remain the strongest apartment markets, benefiting from 
limited new supply, high occupancy, and improving pricing power. In contrast, many Sun Belt 
markets are only beginning to stabilize as deliveries moderate and concessions remain elevated. 
Management teams increasingly expect improving supply-and-demand conditions to support 
stronger growth through 2027. Nevertheless, apartment REITs continue to trade at discounts of 
approximately 15 to 20% to net asset value, reflecting investor concerns about the pace of job 
growth, the timing of a meaningful recovery in leasing spreads and revenue growth, and recent 
movements in interest rates.
CMBS
The GSEs remain the dominant lenders in the multifamily sector, with approximately $176 
billion of combined lending capacity for 2026. This represents a 20.5% increase from their 
combined capacity of $146 billion in 2025. Multifamily exposure within conduit and single-asset, 
single-borrower (SASB) commercial mortgage-backed securities remains significant, although 
smaller in scale, with approximately $76.2 billion outstanding. Multifamily has accounted for 
approximately 19% of conduit issuance over the past five years.
Multifamily continues to perform relatively well within the fixed-rate CMBS market. At 
approximately 6.6%, the conduit multifamily delinquency rate remains among the lowest of the 
major property types, with only industrial recording a lower rate.
Some weakness is emerging, however, among floating-rate SASB loans originated at low 
debt yields during the low-interest-rate environment. Higher interest rates have increased 
debt-service burdens, while modest NOI growth and valuation pressure have complicated 
refinancing. Consequently, the SASB multifamily delinquency rate has risen to approximately 
6.4%, driven primarily by maturity defaults.
Multifamily fundamentals are improving as supply pressures ease, but future CMBS credit 
performance will increasingly depend on borrowers’ ability to refinance, local supply conditions, 
and sponsors’ access to capital—not occupancy risk alone.

Sector overview
Hotel fundamentals remain stable, supported by stronger-than-expected 
RevPAR growth, limited new supply, and healthy leisure and group demand. 
Performance in 2026 has benefited from major events, easier comparisons, and 
continued strength among higher-income consumers. Average daily rates remain 
the primary source of RevPAR growth, as occupancy has yet to fully return to 
pre-pandemic levels. Growth is expected to moderate in 2027 as event-related 
demand normalizes and comparisons become more challenging. Nevertheless, 
limited supply growth and continued improvement in business travel should 
support positive RevPAR growth, with luxury and resort properties expected 
to outperform.
Private debt
Hotel debt is actively supplied by debt funds, banks, CMBS SASB, CMBS conduits and a few 
insurance companies today.  Lenders focus heavily on the quality of the property, brand and 
operator, with available debt terms varying widely based on the strength of each of those 
factors.  Available LTV ratios range from lower than 50% for some properties to the more 
common 50 to 65% range, and occasionally as high as 75%, depending on property, brand, 
operator and sponsor.  Spreads over SOFR typically range from the high 200s basis points to 
the mid-400s basis points for most hotel loans with LTVs under 65%.  Recourse requirements 
vary by type of lender, with banks usually reserving non-recourse hotel loans for their best 
customers.  Some banks offer hotel construction loans, although most of those are capped at 
40 to 50% LTV, require recourse, and are priced in the 500 to 700 bps range over SOFR.
REITs
Hotel REITs have materially outperformed the broader REIT market year to date, supported 
by much stronger-than-expected RevPAR growth. Industry RevPAR is on track to increase 
approximately 5% in 2026, well above initial expectations of 1% to 2%. Several factors 
have contributed to this acceleration: demand associated with major events, including the 
World Cup and America 250; easier comparisons following the slowdown in business and 
group travel after the April 2025 tariff announcements; favorable calendar and holiday 
shifts; limited new hotel supply; continued strength among higher-income consumers; 
and a healthy corporate investment cycle. Security concerns in international destinations, 
particularly the Middle East, Africa, and Mexico, have also shifted some leisure demand 
toward the United States. Expense pressure remains a challenge, with hotel REIT wage rates 
increasing approximately 5% in 2026. Even so, efficiency initiatives and healthy revenue 
growth have supported solid profitability gains.
Industry growth is expected to moderate in 2027 as demand normalizes and comparisons 
with major-event periods become more difficult. Nevertheless, the corporate capital-
expenditure cycle remains healthy, even excluding AI-related investment, while hotel supply 
is forecast to grow by less than 1%. These conditions should support RevPAR growth 
near its historical low- to mid-single-digit rate. Following strong year-to-date share-price 
performance, discounts to net asset value have narrowed materially. Valuations remain 
mixed, however, with several lodging REITs approaching net asset value while much of the 
sector continues to trade at modest discounts. Capital-allocation strategies vary based on 
leverage, relative valuation, and available opportunities. Companies trading closer to net 
asset value are better positioned to pursue acquisitions, while those at wider discounts may 
favor asset sales, debt reduction, or share repurchases through year-end and into 
early 2027.

CMBS
Despite moderating from post-pandemic highs, hotel demand and operating performance 
remain solid, supporting an improvement in loan performance. The conduit hotel 
delinquency rate, which peaked near 20% in July 2020, has declined to approximately 6.7%. 
As of August 2026, this was below the overall conduit delinquency rate of 7.6%.
Leisure travel led the initial recovery, particularly in destination markets, while business 
travel has rebounded more gradually. Hotel operators have sustained RevPAR growth 
primarily through increases in average daily rates, even as occupancy remains below pre-
pandemic levels, especially in higher-end segments. Year-over-year RevPAR growth is 
beginning to normalize as consumers have largely depleted the excess savings accumulated 
during the pandemic.
CMBS issuance in 2020 and 2021 included minimal hotel exposure because of underwriting 
challenges and investor caution. As operating fundamentals improved, hotels accounted 
for approximately 11% of conduit issuance in 2024 and 12.3% in 2025. The share has since 
moderated to approximately 8.3% of year-to-date issuance in 2026.
The lodging sector is transitioning from post-pandemic recovery to normalized growth. 
Limited new supply, improving business and group travel, and strong luxury-hotel 
performance continue to support fundamentals. CMBS credit outcomes will increasingly 
depend on asset quality and borrowers’ ability to execute refinancings rather than on broad 
sector recovery trends.

Sector overview
Industrial fundamentals remain stable as the sector absorbs the elevated 
supply delivered over the past several years and tenants navigate uncertainty 
related to trade policy and economic growth. Leasing velocity and large-
format demand have improved, vacancy appears to have peaked, and the 
sharp slowdown in new construction should support a gradual tightening of 
market conditions beginning in late 2026 and continuing into 2027. The sector 
remains strongly supported by equity and debt capital, while e-commerce, 
supply-chain modernization, advanced manufacturing, and data-center-related 
demand provide favorable long-term growth drivers. Well-located, modern 
logistics facilities should continue to outperform older, less-functional assets.
Private equity
Industrial fundamentals remain stable as the sector works through the substantial volume 
of new supply delivered over the past several years. Demand is healthy, supported by 
e-commerce, logistics modernization, supply-chain reconfiguration, and take-up associated 
with data-center development. New construction has slowed substantially from peak 
levels, which should allow the supply-and-demand balance to improve beginning in late 
2026 and continuing into 2027. Rent growth has moderated from the exceptional gains 
recorded during the pandemic, although well-located, modern logistics facilities continue to 
outperform older assets.
Industrial investment activity remains strong, with transaction volume increasing 24% 
year over year in the second quarter and approaching record levels despite modest pricing 
pressure and somewhat higher capitalization rates. National industrial vacancy stood at 9.2%, 
unchanged over the trailing 12 months, while net absorption totaled approximately 190.9 
million square feet nationally during the same period. Although trade-policy uncertainty and 
slower economic growth present near-term risks, industrial properties continue to benefit 
from favorable long-term demand drivers and attract substantial institutional capital.
Industrial operating performance remained healthy through the second quarter, with 
occupancy holding at solid levels and same-property NOI continuing to grow year over year. 
Income growth has been supported by contractual rent escalations, lease renewals executed 
above expiring rents, and the continued lease-up of vacant and recently delivered space. 
Demand for large-format facilities has also increased, with a notable year-to-date pickup 
in leases exceeding 300,000 square feet as pent-up requirements from prior years have 
translated into completed transactions.
Looking ahead, the sector retains substantial mark-to-market potential because rents on 
many leases approaching expiration remain below current market levels. This gap should 
support continued income growth as leases roll through 2026 and 2027, provided underlying 
space-market fundamentals remain favorable. Development and value-add opportunities 
also remain attractive on a selective basis, particularly for modern logistics facilities where 
location, design, and functionality provide meaningful competitive advantages.
Private debt
Lenders continue to actively pursue loans secured by industrial properties. Positive national 
net absorption, the slowest pace of construction completions in a decade, relatively healthy 
vacancy rates, favorable lease-renewal activity, and moderate projected rent growth continue 
to support lender confidence in the sector.

Well-located, high-quality warehouse and logistics properties in major markets can often 
obtain 5- to 10-year, fixed-rate financing from insurance companies and banks. Loan-to-value 
ratios generally range from 50 to 65%, with spreads of approximately 120 to 145 bps over 
U.S. Treasury yields and interest rates typically ranging from 5.75 to 6.25%. Higher leverage 
is often available from CMBS conduits and debt funds, although at wider spreads and higher 
interest rates.
REITs
Industrial REITs have underperformed the broader REIT index year to date, even as 
fundamentals appear to be moving from stabilization into the early stages of recovery. Tenant 
decision-making and leasing velocity have improved as companies make longer-term supply-
chain commitments despite continued macroeconomic uncertainty, elevated energy costs, and 
higher interest rates. Meanwhile, limited new construction appears to have allowed vacancy to 
peak. Industrial REITs identify e-commerce, advanced manufacturing, aerospace and defense, 
and data-center development as important drivers of stronger-than-expected net absorption. 
Data-center-related industrial leasing has become a particularly meaningful and potentially 
durable source of incremental demand. Estimates vary by geographic exposure but generally 
range from 5 to 15% of net absorption.
Looking ahead, management teams are increasingly confident that market conditions have 
reached an inflection point and expect rent growth to accelerate over the next 12 to 18 months. 
Southern California continues to underperform, although several REITs believe fundamentals 
have bottomed as vacancies appear to have peaked and rent declines have moderated following 
three years of pressure. Improving operating conditions have also increased confidence in 
speculative development, prompting several REITs to raise their guidance for 2026 development 
starts meaningfully. Combined with income growth from embedded mark-to-market 
opportunities as below-market leases expire, these trends position industrial REITs for stronger 
growth in the coming years. Macroeconomic concerns continue to weigh on investor sentiment, 
however, leaving the sector trading at an estimated 5% discount to net asset value.
CMBS
Industrial loans continue to record the lowest delinquency rate in the CMBS market at 2.65%, 
demonstrating the sector’s resilience relative to other traditional property types. Although credit 
quality and investor demand remain strong, the implementation of new tariffs has created near-
term headwinds. Trade disruptions have affected supply chains and tenant operations, introducing 
uncertainty around leasing activity and rent growth. Longer-term fundamentals remain solid, 
supported by continued e-commerce adoption and sustained demand for logistics space.
Since 2020, the SASB CMBS market has provided more than $43.2 billion in floating-rate 
financing to industrial owners, serving as an efficient source of capital for large portfolio 
transactions. Issuance was initially concentrated among a small number of large institutional 
borrowers, but participation has broadened since early 2024 as more sponsors have accessed 
the market for portfolio financing.
Industrial allocations within conduit CMBS have normalized to approximately 6 to 7%, consistent 
with pre-pandemic levels, after reaching roughly 10% in recent years. Conduit loans are typically 
smaller than SASB transactions and are often secured by properties in tertiary locations, assets 
with less-functional layouts, or properties occupied by non-investment-grade tenants, including 
those involved in sale-leaseback transactions. Consequently, underwriting standards for conduit 
industrial loans have generally been more conservative than those for SASB financing.
The industrial sector is moving from normalization toward recovery as declining construction 
and improving leasing demand gradually tighten market conditions. Conduit and SASB credit 
performance remains supported by sound asset quality and resilient tenant demand, with 
industrial continuing to record the strongest loan performance among the major commercial 
real estate property types.

Sector overview
Office fundamentals are beginning to improve, although the recovery remains 
uneven. National vacancy has declined to 18.3%, net absorption has turned positive, 
and leasing activity is accelerating across a broader range of markets. Transaction 
activity and debt availability have also improved but remain concentrated among 
high-quality, well-leased properties. Premier buildings are achieving stronger rent 
growth and improved lease economics, while older commodity assets continue to 
face elevated concessions, weak demand, and refinancing pressure. Historically low 
new supply and a backlog of signed leases should support further occupancy gains, 
but performance will remain highly differentiated by asset quality and location.
Private equity
Office fundamentals are showing signs of improvement. National leasing activity has increased, 
net absorption has turned positive, and occupancy is slowly improving, supported by the 
continued flight to quality and increased attendance. National utilization remains below pre-
pandemic levels, however, and slower growth in office-using employment is likely to limit the 
pace of recovery. Capital-markets activity has also improved, with U.S. office transaction volume 
totaling $40.1 billion during the first half of 2026, an increase of 14% from a year earlier. Investor 
demand remains concentrated in high-quality, amenity-rich properties in leading markets, 
while commodity office assets continue to face leasing challenges, elevated capital costs, and 
refinancing pressure.
Pricing indicators suggest that investor sentiment may be reaching an inflection point, with 
values beginning to recover modestly in select central business districts and suburban markets. 
Values remain well below pre-pandemic levels, however, and the recovery continues to vary 
significantly by market and asset quality. National office vacancy stands at 18.3%, down 70 bps 
over the trailing 12 months, while net absorption totaled nearly 40 msf nationally during the 
same period.
Property-level operating performance remains uneven, reflecting the bifurcated nature of the 
office recovery. Higher-quality, well-located properties continue to generate stronger leasing 
activity and tenant retention than older commodity assets. Select opportunities are emerging 
among premier gateway-market properties, well-leased Sun Belt assets, and structured 
or distressed investments. Underperforming properties may require more active lease 
management, targeted capital investment, conversion to alternative uses, or disposition.
Private debt
Despite continued distress across the national office market, select insurance companies have 
shown interest in financing the highest-quality properties with strong leasing profiles and 
experienced sponsorship. This activity remains limited, and spreads are typically 30 to 75 bps 
wider than those on industrial or apartment loans with similar credit risk and tenor. Insurance-
company underwriting remains highly conservative, with a strong emphasis on the durability 
of property cash flow rather than current NOI alone. Loan-to-value ratios are frequently 
capped in the 50% range based on current, lower property valuations.
CMBS lenders also continue to pursue office loans selectively. The CMBS bond market 
generally accepts office exposure of up to 20% within an individual conduit transaction, 
limiting lender capacity. Available financing is therefore concentrated among higher-quality 
properties with strong in-place leasing.

Certain debt funds are willing to finance a broader range of office properties, but their 
required yields often exceed what the properties’ existing economics can support.
Class B and C office buildings, properties with occupancy below approximately 80 to 85%, 
and assets with weighted-average remaining lease terms of less than five to seven years 
generally attract little or no lender interest. Most office financing continues to involve 
incumbent lenders extending or refinancing their existing loans.
REITs
Office REITs have underperformed the broader REIT index year to date despite a meaningful 
recovery since late March. The sector came under pressure in early 2026 amid concerns that 
AI-related disruption could structurally reduce office-using employment and demand. Those 
concerns have since moderated as evidence of near-term displacement remains limited, while 
demand from AI companies has provided a meaningful tailwind in select markets. Higher 
interest rates remain an earnings constraint because of the sector’s above-average leverage, 
partially offsetting recent operating improvements.
Office fundamentals continue to strengthen as leasing activity accelerates and broadens 
across markets. New York remains the strongest major market, while San Francisco is 
recovering rapidly as the availability of high-quality space declines and demand from AI-related 
tenants increases. Several Sun Belt markets are also benefiting from renewed migration and 
business expansion, supporting additional demand for Class A space in cities such as Dallas, 
Charlotte, and Nashville.
The outlook remains constructive. Leading indicators, including property tours and leasing 
pipelines, point to sustained momentum, while the development pipeline remains near 
historical lows. Management commentary has become increasingly optimistic, with several 
experienced executives describing current supply-and-demand conditions as among the most 
favorable of their careers. Premier buildings in stronger submarkets are achieving double-digit 
rent growth and improving leasing spreads. Landlords are also beginning to reduce concession 
packages, strengthening overall lease economics.
Occupancy is rising and should continue to improve over the next 12 to 24 months as a 
substantial backlog of signed leases commences. Earnings growth has turned positive 
following several years of decline, and cash-flow growth should follow as leasing capital 
expenditures appear to have peaked. Despite these improvements, office REITs generally 
continue to trade at discounts of approximately 15% to 30% to net asset value, limiting 
external growth opportunities. Most companies are therefore emphasizing capital recycling 
by selling noncore assets and directing proceeds toward higher-quality properties and largely 
preleased development projects.
CMBS
Office exposure within conduit CMBS remains challenged by persistent secular headwinds. 
Office properties historically represented approximately 30% of conduit collateral, but the

Sector overview
Retail fundamentals remain strong and stable, supported by limited new supply, 
high occupancy, and healthy tenant demand. Grocery-anchored, necessity-based, 
and open-air formats continue to outperform because of defensive demand and 
limited e-commerce exposure. Transaction activity and lender appetite remain

depend more on rent gains, redevelopment, and capital deployment as occupancy 
nears its practical ceiling and consumers become more selective.
Private equity
Retail fundamentals continue to be shaped by a shortage of high-quality space. Availability 
remains near historical lows, while elevated construction costs, financing constraints, and 
limited project feasibility continue to restrict new development. This has created one of the 
most supply-constrained environments in commercial real estate. Tenant demand remains 
healthy despite more selective consumer spending. Vacated space is generally re-leased 
quickly, particularly in grocery-anchored centers, neighborhood retail properties, and high-
traffic locations, supporting occupancy and allowing landlords to capture rent growth 
through renewals and lease rollover.
Capital-markets activity has increased as investors target retail’s durable cash flows, 
attractive yields, and embedded rent-growth potential. Demand remains strongest for 
necessity-based, grocery-anchored, and open-air formats. Trailing 12-month national 
transaction volume totaled $76.5 billion, an increase of 22.5% from a year earlier. National

msf during the same period.
Property-level performance remains strong, with occupancy holding near historical highs 
across high-quality retail formats. Although leasing volume has been modest, this largely 
reflects limited available space rather than weakening tenant demand. Select opportunities 
remain in core-plus acquisitions priced below replacement cost and new development in 
high-growth suburban markets, particularly where supply constraints support durable 
occupancy and rent growth.
Private debt
Lender appetite for necessity-oriented retail properties, particularly grocery-anchored 
centers, has remained strong this year. Neighborhood and community shopping centers with 
strong tenant sales histories and long weighted-average lease terms (WALTs) have attracted 
the greatest interest from insurance companies seeking to diversify their commercial real 
estate exposure. Grocery-anchored and other high-quality retail properties frequently obtain 
lower interest rates than comparable industrial assets. For loans with LTV ratios of 50 to 65%, 
spreads generally range from 115 to 135 bps over U.S. Treasury yields, resulting in interest 
rates of approximately 5.7 to 6.2%.

to 15 bps wider than financing for well-anchored neighborhood and community shopping 
centers, although CMBS lenders remain active in these formats. Financing for regional malls 
remains largely unavailable except for the highest-quality assets operated by experienced 
sponsors. Across retail formats, lenders focus heavily on tenant creditworthiness, historical 
sales performance, remaining WALT, and sponsor quality.
