Scotiabank Forecast: Tighter Financial Conditions Meet 
a Resilient Economy  
• 
The broad growth outlook is little changed, and recent data reinforce the view that 
economic activity remains resilient. Historical revisions show that the U.S. economy 
entered the second half of the year with more momentum than previously estimated, 
while Canadian activity has continued to hold up broadly as expected, recovering 
from the soft patch earlier in the year. Tighter financial conditions provide some offset 
to these positive forces, but the net drag on activity should remain relatively 
contained.  
• 
Canada’s investment outlook has strengthened further. The recently announced 
accelerated depreciation measure provides an additional boost to investment, 
although near-term GDP effects should be muted as part of the demand impulse 
leaks into imports. Over time, stronger capital spending should raise the economy’s 
productive capacity.  
• 
Inflation pressures are proving more persistent in both Canada and the U.S., 
turning the monetary policy outlook more restrictive. Higher oil prices have lifted 
the inflation profile, while the recent surge in diesel prices is expected to feed more 
gradually into a broader range of consumer prices. Together with firm activity, this 
should require further tightening from both the Fed and the BoC. 
Economic activity continues to hold up broadly as expected, but renewed inflation 
pressures give little choice for central banks but to tighten monetary policy. The recent 
data flow suggests that the U.S. economy has been more resilient than previously 
estimated, while Canadian activity continues to hold up. Beyond the incoming data, 
accelerated depreciation measures should provide a substantial lift to Canadian business 
investment and productive capacity in the medium run. However, this is partly offset by 
tighter financial conditions. 
The broad growth outlook remains intact, with the U.S. economy expected to expand at a 
moderate pace and Canadian growth accelerating in 2027. In the U.S., resilient 
consumption and AI-related investment should support activity in the near term, 
although restrictive interest rates and weaker job and real income growth will gradually 
weigh on household demand over the forecast period. In Canada, trade disruptions will 
constrain exports, but stronger business investment, public infrastructure and defence 
spending should provide an important offset and expand productive capacity over time. 
Inflation remains the key risk in both countries, particularly as elevated diesel prices and 
tariffs feed into broader price pressures with a lag. 
Persistent inflation and resilient activity should lead the Fed to raise rates twice more, 
taking the federal funds rate to 4.5% in early 2027, before reversing those hikes in 2028 as 
the economy slows. The BoC should also withdraw stimulus and move into restrictive 
territory, raising its policy rate to 3.25% in 2027. 
RECENT DATA CONFIRM RESILIENT ECONOMIC ACTIVITY 
Historical revisions suggest that the U.S. economy entered the second half of the year 
with more momentum than previously estimated. Second-quarter GDP growth was 
revised up to 2.2%, while earlier data were also adjusted higher. Much of the revision 
reflects stronger business investment—and potential output—and therefore does not 
materially change our assessment of the degree of excess demand in the economy. The 
revisions reinforce the resilience of the expansion but, on their own, have limited 
implications for our inflation or policy rate outlook.

Canadian activity has also evolved broadly in line with our expectations. Real GDP was flat in July, but the comparatively strong flash 
estimate from Statistics Canada for August suggests that third-quarter growth should remain healthy. This is consistent with our view that 
the weakness seen at the beginning of the year was temporary and that the economy carried reasonable momentum into the second half 
of 2026.1  
Financial conditions have tightened more quickly and sharply than we expected, particularly 
in the U.S. (chart 1). We interpret this as a reflection of a stronger economy and more acute 
inflation pressures, which have pushed up short-term rates expectations, alongside heavy 
demand for capital from the AI build-out. The net aggregate macroeconomic drag should 
therefore be relatively contained, although the effects will vary across sectors, with household 
spending being more affected than business investment for instance. In Canada, the 
tightening has been smaller in absolute terms, but the economy’s greater sensitivity to 
interest rates leaves household spending and other rate-sensitive sectors more exposed. 
Overall, tighter financial conditions provide an important, though only partial, offset to the 
recent positive forces supporting the Canadian economy. 
INFLATION PRESSURES ARE PROVING MORE PERSISTENT 
The inflation profile is revised up again in response to higher oil prices (chart 2). Also, the 
surge in diesel prices since the summer adds another layer of inflation pressure, gradually 
feeding into a broader range of consumer prices, often with a substantial lag, as our recent 
analysis shows. For example, we estimate that the effect on food prices will peak roughly 18 
months after the initial shock. On its own, the recent increase in diesel prices raises our core 
inflation forecast by 0.2 percentage points in both countries. This matters for the Fed and the 
BoC: central banks can look through a temporary rise in energy prices, but a shock that 
spreads more broadly across the economy is harder to ignore. 
THE GROWTH OUTLOOK HOLDS FIRM 
United States 
The broad U.S. growth outlook for 2026–28 is little changed, with growth close to potential 
and around 2.1% for the next two years. In the near term, the economy should continue to 
expand at a moderate pace, supported by resilient consumer spending and strong business 
investment. Consumption is expected to slow gradually thereafter as support from buoyant 
equity markets fades, real income slows due to rising inflation, and restrictive interest rates weigh more heavily on demand in 2027. We 
expect some recovery in 2028 as monetary policy starts to normalize. Business investment should remain robust this year, supported by AI-
related capital spending and healthy corporate finances, but should moderate in 2027 and 2028 after several exceptionally strong years 
and as tighter financial conditions constrain further expansion.  
Inflation is still the most troubling part of the U.S. outlook. Price pressures extend beyond energy, and the delayed pass-through of tariffs 
into goods’ prices is becoming visible. Elevated gasoline and diesel prices are also particularly important. We expect this to keep inflation 
from improving materially over the next few quarters. But as household spending weakens in 2027, and the effect of energy prices starts to 
fade, we should see some improvement, with total PCE inflation converging towards 2% in late 2027 and into 2028.

Canada 
Canada’s outlook is characterized by two opposing forces: the lift from the transformation agenda against the drag from the trade war. 
Trade disruptions should visibly weaken exports in the fourth quarter of this year, although fiscal initiatives and stronger business 
investment will provide important offsets.  
We continue to expect growth to accelerate in 2027, as the federal transformation agenda supports stronger business investment and 
public spending, while elevated energy and non-energy commodity prices further reinforce the investment outlook through stronger terms 
of trade and national income.  
A key part of the positive investment outlook is Canada’s recently introduced “Productivity 
Mega-Deduction” measure, which introduces immediate expensing for a wide range of 
depreciable capital and significantly reduces the Marginal Effective Tax Rate on new 
investments (chart 3). We estimate that the measure will lift investment by around 3% by 
2028. The near-term effect on GDP is more modest because part of the increase in capital 
spending will leak into imports, particularly for machinery and equipment. The longer-run 
implications are more meaningful: stronger investment gradually adds to the capital stock, 
lifting productivity and the economy’s productive capacity. By the end of 2028, we estimate 
that the measures will raise the level of Canadian GDP by about 0.3%.   
We forecast GDP growth to remain healthy in 2028 with investment remaining an important 
support, but to moderate slightly to 2.1%, as consumption softens in response to a restrictive 
monetary stance. The labour market should continue to normalize, with the unemployment 
rate settling close to 6%.  
Inflation in Canada remains better contained than in the United States, but the risks are still 
tilted to the upside in our opinion. Elevated oil prices will keep total CPI above 3% for the next 
few quarters. Inflation should then slow in 2027 as oil prices normalize and remain close to 2% in 2028. And like in the U.S., the recent diesel 
price surge is expected to feed through a broader set of inflation pressures in Canada. Along with the impact of retaliatory tariffs, this puts 
upward pressure on core inflation in 2027.  
CENTRAL BANKS REMAIN FOCUSED ON INFLATION  
The resilience of the U.S. economy and persistent inflation pressures should lead the Fed to 
raise rates twice more, taking the federal funds rate to 4.5% in early 2027 (chart 4). With 
monetary policy already restrictive, we think this additional tightening will be sufficient to 
bring the economy into better balance and inflation back toward target. The hikes also reflect 
some form of risk management, helping the Fed preserve its inflation-fighting credibility and 
lean against upside risks to prices. Combined with tight financial conditions that should slow 
the economy, the Fed should reverse these hikes through 2028. 
The BoC faces a similar challenge. Although inflation is better contained than in the U.S., 
persistently high energy and diesel prices are building additional cost pressures in the 
pipeline. Monetary policy cannot address this source of inflation, but the Bank of Canada still 
has a price-stability mandate and must limit the extent to which higher energy costs spill into 
broader prices. Recent growth has also held up well, suggesting that excess supply is being 
absorbed more quickly than expected. Against this backdrop, the BoC has little room to 
maintain an accommodative stance. The policy rate will likely need to move into restrictive 
territory to lean against these inflation risks. We forecast it to reach 3.25% in the second quarter of 2027 and keep it there well into 2028.

RISKS  
Geopolitical tensions and further increases in energy prices. Our baseline assumes that geopolitical tensions gradually ease, and that oil 
supply recovers over time. However, inventories still need to be rebuilt, and the possibility of more prolonged or renewed supply 
disruptions remains significant. Crude prices could stay high for a longer period, and diesel prices could rise even further, adding strains on 
the supply chain. Such an outcome would directly lift headline inflation and raise transportation and production costs throughout the 
economy. This would require a stronger response from central banks. 
A larger-than-expected fiscal and investment impulse in Canada. Governments could introduce additional measures to cushion the 
economic effects of the trade dispute. A larger fiscal package would support Canadian growth in 2027, but it would also add to demand 
and inflation at a time when the economy is already proving resilient. The response to the Productivity Mega-Deduction and the major-
projects agenda could also be stronger than we currently assume. If these measures generate a larger wave of private investment, growth 
and productive capacity would ultimately be higher, but the initial demand effects could push inflation and the Bank of Canada’s policy-
rate path above our baseline. 
A renewed escalation in trade tensions. The latest round of tariffs has already weakened the Canada-U.S. trading relationship, but the 
dispute could escalate further. Beyond the immediate effects on exports and activity, repeated challenges to existing trade arrangements 
could have more lasting consequences for business confidence and capital spending. They could also make the CUSMA review more 
difficult and increase uncertainty around the durability of future agreements. For Canada, this would represent a clear downside risk to 
growth, although additional tariffs or retaliation could simultaneously add to inflation pressures. 
A more disruptive rise in bond yields. Higher long-term yields can shift from being a growth story to a market turbulence story. Pressure 
from persistently elevated term premium and further weakness in treasuries could eventually spread to equity markets, and the resulting 
additional tightening in financial conditions could weigh more heavily on the economy.
