[NECX 2026] Buy One Reactor, Get One Free  
NECX 2026: What financing, contractual and supply-chain structures can turn 
demand into actual projects? 
KB Securities attended NECX 2026 (Nuclear Energy Conference & Expo), held in Dallas, 
Texas, on Aug 25–27. NECX is jointly hosted by two leading U.S. nuclear organizations: 
the American Nuclear Society (ANS) and Nuclear Energy Institute (NEI). Launched in 2025 
by integrating and expanding their respective annual events, the conference marked its 
second year in 2026. While NECX 2025 confirmed “demand for new nuclear plants and 
their commercialization potential,” NECX 2026 moved the discussion one step further:
“what financing, contractual and supply-chain structures are needed to turn that demand 
into actual projects.” 
Key session: Detailed look at DOE’s USD17.5bn supply-chain loan 
The standout among 65 sessions was “Ready to Build: Workforce and Supply Chain 
Development for a Growing Nuclear Future,” held on Aug 26. Participants included 
DOE Energy Dominance Financing, which oversees project financing for the U.S. 
Department of Energy, along with key nuclear supply-chain players. The session 
addressed the supply chains, manufacturing capacity, skilled workforce and 
government financing needed to expand U.S. nuclear construction. Most importantly, 
it laid out in detail the structure and purpose of DOE’s planned USD17.5bn nuclear supply-
chain loan for ten AP1000 reactors. 
The hidden architect of the U.S. nuclear renaissance 
The session carried added weight because of panelist Julie Kozeracki, Acting CIO of 
DOE’s EDF and its nuclear lead. She designed the USD17.5bn nuclear supply-chain
loan and has also played a key role in restarting closed nuclear plants. She is also the 
lead author of Advanced Nuclear: Pathways to Commercial Liftoff, effectively a 
roadmap for the current U.S. nuclear expansion. She has been involved in both shaping 
the overall commercialization pathway for U.S. nuclear power and putting it into practice 
through financing policy. 
Essence of the supply-chain loan: Redesigning the timing and allocation of initial 
risks, not merely providing funds 
When announced in Jun, the supply-chain loan was viewed as USD17.5bn in nuclear 
financing support. Some in the U.S. argued that the amount was modest relative to the 
total scale of nuclear projects. DOE’s on-site explanation, however, made the program’s
intent much clearer. Its goal is not merely to provide funding, but to reallocate risks so 
that private participants take responsibility early without allowing uncertainty at the 
outset to prevent projects from moving forward. The structure is also designed to mobilize 
the supply chain while FID is still being prepared.

Even with a structure that mobilizes the supply chain early, utilities will pursue new 
nuclear plants only if the economics make sense. DOE noted that future U.S. nuclear 
projects will be built around business models and economics that look very different 
from those of the past. Three factors set the new model apart: 1) 30–50% investment 
tax credits (ITCs), 2) hyperscaler participation through long-term PPAs and 3) off-
balance-sheet debt financing that reduces the financial burden on utilities. This does 
not mean nuclear plants have become cheaper to build. Rather, these new 
mechanisms should allow investment, financing and demand risks—previously 
concentrated on utilities—to be shared among multiple participants. The most 
important leading indicator of whether nuclear policy translates into actual orders will 
be which utility is first to make a firm USD500mn commitment by year-end.

On Jun 23, the U.S. government announced a USD17.5bn nuclear supply-chain loan 
program led by the Department of Energy’s (DOE) Office of Energy Dominance 
Financing (EDF), targeting ten Westinghouse AP1000 reactors. A special-purpose 
vehicle (SPV) should be established for each of five sites comprising two reactors, with 
the utility and Westinghouse each contributing USD500mn upfront before drawing 
the loan. U.S. nuclear policy had focused on establishing an industry-wide framework 
through executive orders and Department of Commerce partnerships. This is the first 
initiative to allocate funds to specific sites and operators, marking an important shift 
from policy declarations to individual project execution.  
The structure is primarily designed to shorten project timelines by changing the 
traditional nuclear development sequence. Previously, utilities placed equipment 
orders only after reaching a final investment decision (FID). Under the new model,
long-lead orders can be placed ahead of FID and run in parallel with site-specific 
design and permitting, potentially bringing commercial operation forward by up to 
three years. The flow of funding is also important. A combined USD22.5bn—USD17.5bn 
in loans and USD5.0bn in equity—is expected to fund long-lead equipment first, 
putting capital into a dormant nuclear supply chain. 
DOE had already announced its USD17.5bn AP1000 supply-chain loan program on 
Jun 23. NECX 2026 therefore clarified the rationale behind the program and how DOE 
intends to turn it into actual projects. 
Kozeracki’s explanation showed that DOE is not simply seeking to provide more 
funding for nuclear construction. With much of the AP1000’s first-of-a-kind (FOAK) 
risk considered resolved after Vogtle, the program is designed to require private 
participants to take meaningful responsibility while changing the timing and allocation 
of initial risks so that pre-FID uncertainty does not prevent projects from moving 
forward. 
Kozeracki described many of the challenges at Vogtle as structural FOAK issues arising 
from the AP1000’s first U.S. deployment. By contrast, the AP1000 design is now 
complete, while supply-chain infrastructure and workforce capabilities have been 
built up, making the costs and schedules of follow-on projects far more predictable. 
She put  it more directly: “The first-of-a-kind risk is gone.” 
DOE is not seeking to absorb private-sector project risks. Instead, the phrase 
repeatedly emphasized at the event was “real skin in the game.” To access USD3.5bn 
in DOE funding for each SPV covering two AP1000 reactors, Westinghouse and its 
utility partner must each contribute USD500mn upfront. Kozeracki explained that this 
was designed “to ensure there is real skin in the game for alignment of interest.” 
Rather than a subsidy structure in which the government bears risks, government 
financing is deployed only when private participants commit capital.

However, early private-sector participation does not amount to an irreversible 
commitment to the entire project. A utility can provide a USD500mn letter of credit, 
allowing long-lead items to be ordered before FID. Kozeracki described this as a utility 
commitment to advance the project, but not one that irrevocably locks in its 
participation. The structure requires meaningful early commitment from private 
participants without forcing FID for the entire project when long-lead items are 
ordered. 
Standardization is another prerequisite for early procurement. According to 
Kozeracki, components ordered under the program should be “fully standardized and 
interchangeable across different projects.” This means that if one utility later changes 
course, another can take and use its components. She explained: “If Utility 1 wants to 
walk away, another utility can proceed using the components Utility 1 relinquished.” 
This gives utilities flexibility after early participation while preserving the collateral 
value of preordered equipment for DOE. 
When announced in Jun, the program was largely viewed as USD17.5bn in financing 
support for nuclear projects. Some in the U.S. argued that the amount was modest 
relative to the overall scale of nuclear investment. Kozeracki’s on-site explanation, 
however, made the program’s intent much clearer. DOE’s goal is not to eliminate 
private-sector risk, but to redistribute it—requiring private participants to take 
responsibility upfront while preventing early-stage uncertainty from stalling projects 
before they start. This allows the supply chain to mobilize even before FID is reached.
The program is therefore better understood not simply as a source of low-cost 
financing for individual nuclear plants, but as a mechanism to create the conditions 
for repeat AP1000 builds by leveraging the design and supply-chain experience 
accumulated at Vogtle.

Even with a structure that mobilizes the supply chain early, utilities will pursue new 
nuclear plants only if project economics are viable. Remarks from several utility 
executives at NECX also showed that the economics of new nuclear cannot be 
explained by LCOE alone. As large-scale reactors require billions of dollars in capital 
over extended periods, actual investment decisions must consider not only generation 
costs but also who bears construction and cost-overrun risks, how the project affects 
utilities’ financial health and credit metrics, and how to avoid passing the burden on 
to ratepayers. 
In this context, Kozeracki’s most striking comments during the session were aimed at 
the market. She noted that U.S. equity analysts covering utilities remain overly 
anchored to the financing structure used for Vogtle. In other words, new AP1000 
projects should not be evaluated under the same financing assumptions applied to 
Vogtle. 
Three factors distinguish the current environment from Vogtle: 1) 30–50% investment 
tax credits (ITCs), 2) hyperscaler participation through long-term PPAs and 3) off-
balance-sheet financing that reduces utilities’ financial burden. This does not mean 
absolute nuclear construction costs have fallen. Rather, mechanisms now exist to 
distribute the investment, financing and demand risks previously concentrated on 
utilities among multiple participants. 
At Vogtle, project-related debt was recorded directly on utilities’ balance sheets, 
putting substantial pressure on the credit metrics of project owner Southern 
Company. This helped protect DOE’s loan recovery prospects, but forced the utility to 
bear much of the financing burden for the large-scale nuclear project on its own 
balance sheet. Kozeracki explained that with much of the AP1000’s FOAK risk now 
resolved, DOE can use its lending authority more flexibly. Future projects therefore 
have greater scope to move away from structures in which utilities directly assume 
most of the financing burden toward financing structures that are closer to off-
balance-sheet treatment.

Tax credits provide an additional benefit. Kozeracki explained that DOE loans can be 
combined with a 30–50% investment tax credit (ITC), describing the impact of the 
maximum 50% ITC in highly intuitive terms: 
“A 50 percent investment tax credit is buy one reactor, get one free.” 
This does not literally mean that one reactor is free. Rather, it emphasizes that 
applying tax credits to up to half of eligible investment costs can substantially reduce 
a project’s effective capital burden. Kozeracki also stressed that such powerful tax 
support was unavailable during the Vogtle project. 
Previously, nuclear tax credits were limited to the production tax credit (PTC), which 
is linked to electricity generation from completed plants. However, the 2022 Inflation 
Reduction Act introduced an ITC based on investment costs. The benefit applies only 
to nuclear plants “placed in service” on or after Jan 1, 2025. Vogtle Units 3 and 4, 
which began operations in Jul 2023 and Apr 2024, respectively, were therefore 
ineligible. Some estimates suggest that, had Vogtle qualified for the ITC, at least 
USD10.5bn of its USD35bn total cost could have been recovered through tax credits.
In principle, ITC credits are received upon completion of an investment. However, 
long-duration assets such as nuclear plants can qualify under the Qualified Progress 
Expenditures (QPE) rules. This allows ITCs to be calculated annually based on eligible 
construction expenditures incurred during the construction period, providing tax 
benefits from the project’s early stages and helping recover initial investment more 
quickly.

The final change is hyperscaler participation. Rising data center power demand has 
mainly been cited as a driver of new nuclear demand. However, Kozeracki described 
hyperscalers as more than customers purchasing nuclear-generated electricity. If 
retail customers and hyperscalers jointly purchase power from new reactors, with 
hyperscalers paying relatively higher prices, retail electricity rates can be lowered 
while both groups share the system benefits of large-scale, always-on generation. 
Large-scale, long-term PPAs with hyperscalers can improve revenue visibility for new 
nuclear plants while providing a means of addressing different cost scenarios. This 
suggests that hyperscalers could become participants sharing some of the project’s 
demand and price risks, rather than merely creating future electricity demand. This 
clearly differs from the traditional nuclear business model, in which utilities and retail 
customers bore most project risks. 
The economics of new AP1000 projects cannot be explained by any single support 
measure. DOE financing reduces funding burdens, ITCs lower initial capital costs and 
long-term hyperscaler PPAs distribute demand and price risks. These benefits are 
complemented by the resolution of AP1000 FOAK risks and supply-chain 
standardization discussed above. 
This is where we see the key change. The Vogtle experience clearly remains an 
important benchmark for assessing the economics of new large-scale reactors. 
However, applying Vogtle’s final construction cost and financing structure directly to 
future AP1000 projects may fail to reflect the changes now taking place. At NECX, 
DOE did not argue that absolute nuclear construction costs had fallen significantly. 
Rather, it emphasized the emergence of a structure in which government and multiple 
private participants share the construction, financing and demand risks previously 
concentrated on utilities and retail customers. Taken together, these factors suggest 
that the risk profile of new AP1000 projects could differ substantially from that of 
Vogtle.

We believe 2H26 should mark a critical inflection point for nuclear investment. Actual 
FIDs for large-scale nuclear projects may not be reached until 2H27, but the largely 
declarative U.S. nuclear expansion strategy of the past year should begin taking 
concrete shape at the project level in 2H26. Over the past year, the U.S. has focused 
on building the overall framework for nuclear policy, financing and procurement. The 
next stage is to turn that framework into specific projects with identified operators 
and sites. This should also clarify 1) which Korean nuclear value-chain companies will 
participate, 2) how they will participate and 3) the scale of their involvement. 
Rather than additional policy announcements, investors should now monitor when, 
where and by whom the five projects comprising ten AP1000 reactors will materialize.
Westinghouse stated on Jul 31 that it had already signed LOIs with seven utilities or 
energy companies to participate in DOE’s supply-chain loan program, with each 
prospective partner identifying a specific project site. However, DOE financing can 
support only five projects, each comprising two AP1000 reactors. Five actual projects 
must therefore still be selected from the seven candidates. 
For each project, Westinghouse and the utility must each commit USD500mn in equity 
to access USD3.5bn in DOE financing. Long-lead procurement should then proceed 
in stages, subject to these equity commitments and other conditions. DOE expects 
utility partners to be assigned to the five SPVs by year-end. We therefore see the key 
leading indicator over the coming months as which operator first commits USD500mn. 
That would mark the clearest shift from policy to actual project execution. This is 
consistent with Kozeracki’s timeline at NECX. She said the five two-reactor projects 
should involve different utilities or independent power producers and that “within 
months, we expect greater clarity on where the projects will be located and who will 
lead them.” If the USD17.5bn financing announcement in Jun marked the program’s 
launch, the period from Sep should bring greater clarity on the utilities and sites 
behind the five projects. 
There are already clues as to the potential locations. In a policy document released in 
Mar, NRC identified 23 sites where nuclear construction could begin and outlined the 
licensing status of each. We therefore expect the first candidate site for restarting 
U.S. large-scale nuclear construction to become more concrete by year-end. From 
Sep, investors should monitor three stages: 1) which of the seven LOI partners actually 
commits USD500mn in equity, 2) when the five SPVs and project sites are disclosed 
and 3) whether PPAs and project-development agreements among utilities, 
hyperscalers and EPC contractors ultimately lead to FIDs. At the end of the session, 
Kozeracki likewise pointed to faster progress on project-development agreements 
among utilities, hyperscalers and EPC contractors as the most important next step 
toward starting construction of the ten AP1000 reactors. Ultimately, “who commits 
the first USD500mn” may matter more to investors than the headline policy target of 
“building ten reactors.” Once the first SPV is confirmed and long-lead procurement 
begins, the USD17.5bn program will have moved decisively from policy to project 
execution.
