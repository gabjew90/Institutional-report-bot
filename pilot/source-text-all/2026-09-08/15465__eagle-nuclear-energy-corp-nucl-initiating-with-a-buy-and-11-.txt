stage nuclear company that hosts a large-scale domestic uranium
asset and Small Modular Reactors (SMR) technology under one roof.
Headquartered in Reno, Nevada, the firm’s mission is to strengthen
U.S. energy security by vertically integrating itself into the nuclear fuel
cycle. NUCL's cornerstone asset is the Aurora Uranium Project located
in Malheur County Oregon, three miles from the Nevada border. The site
contains an estimated 32.75M pounds (lbs) of U3O8 in the Measured
and Indicated (M&I) categories and an additional 4.98Mlbs U3O8 in
the Inferred category. Management is advancing Aurora toward a Pre-
Feasibility Study (PFS), with environmental baseline studies already
underway, and a 27,000-foot drill program slated for summer 2026. In
short, Aurora gives Eagle a credible path to becoming a meaningful
domestic uranium developer, with the SMR program adding further
longer-term optionality to its growth story.
Aurora is fundamentally brownfield. We view Eagle's position as
unique in that the firm owns a well-drilled uranium deposit with an M&I
resource and prior engineering and metallurgical base. During past
ownership, the site has seen 617 diamond and reverse-circulation drill
holes, totaling over 200,000 meters. Aurora has also had a PFS from
the late 1970s showing recoveries of 55 – 85%. Importantly, Eagle
also holds option rights to the adjacent Cordex Zone on the same land
package, which is supported by roughly 110 historical drill holes that
have not yet been captured in a resource. In turn, we view this as an
expansion opportunity to Eagle's already impressive asset base. We
emphasize that processing for the asset is likely to happen across the
border in Nevada, which should ease permitting.
We initiate coverage of Eagle Nuclear with a Buy rating and
$11.50 PT. Our valuation is driven by Aurora and its SMR technology.
We apply an estimated value of $10/lb to Indicated and $5/lb to
Inferred resources. In our view, this is a fair premium to typical
uranium developers, reflecting Aurora's safe geopolitical status and past
exploration. We value the adjacent Cordex Claims at 25% of Aurora,
given its comparable size but less drill history. We model $21.7M for
the firm's licensed SMR technology, though we note that it remains in
the design-stage and years from commercial reality. In short, we value
Aurora at $352.3M and Cordex at $88.1M, then add $21.7M for the
firm's SMR technology and cash balance of $28.1M. We then apply our
0.7x NAV multiple to account for Aurora's scale, which yields a value of
$343.1M, or $11.60 per share, which rounds to our final PT of $11.50.
Near-term catalysts. We expect Aurora's PFS in 2H27, representing
the company's largest catalyst to date. In turn, we view any milestones
toward that report, such as updated drilling results, metallurgical work,
or a resource update, as de-risking the timeline to its release. The PFS
would also mark the first set of published economics for Aurora, offering
greater clarity on the project's viability. For now, we expect news flow
related to the firm's drill program and first assays in 4Q26.

Company Introduction 
 
  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Company background. Eagle Nuclear (NUCL) is a development-stage nuclear company that 
hosts a large-scale domestic uranium asset and Small Modular Reactor (SMR) technology under 
one roof. Headquartered in Reno, Nevada, the firm’s mission is to strengthen U.S. energy security 
by vertically integrating itself into the domestic nuclear fuel cycle. NUCL's cornerstone asset is 
the Aurora Uranium Project located in Malheur County, Oregon, three miles from the Nevada 
border. Management is advancing Aurora toward a Pre-Feasibility Study (PFS), with 
environmental baseline studies already underway, and a 27,000-foot drill program slated for 
summer 2026. In short, Aurora gives Eagle a credible path to becoming a meaningful domestic 
uranium developer, with the SMR program adding further longer-term optionality to its growth 
story. 
 
Aurora Project background. The Aurora Project, located in Malheur County, Oregon, is roughly 
three miles from the Nevada border. The site covers 30 km2 on Bureau of Land Management 
(BLM) public lands. The August 2025 Technical Report Summary defined a Measured & Indicated 
Resource of 53.42M tonnes (t) at 278 parts per million (ppm) U3O8, amounting to 32.75Mlbs, 
alongside Inferred resources of 8.96Mt at 252ppm U3O8 for an additional 4.98Mlbs. The study 
anticipated processing costs of $13.00/t, mining costs of $4.00/t, waste costs of $3.00/t, and 
$2.50/t for overburden. In short, the site is likely to offer attractive margins, driven by a U3O8 price 
of $90.00/lb and 85% mill recoveries.

How the Aurora Project was acquired. Eagle acquired the Aurora Project from Aurora Energy 
Metals in late 2024. The firm then went public through a business combination with Spring Valley 
Acquisition Corp. II, a NASDAQ-listed SPAC, which closed on February 24, 2026. The de-SPAC 
was supported by a $30.0M PIPE, purposed to fund near- and intermediate-term permitting work, 
alongside a 2026 drill program meant to drive a PFS in 2H27. We note that the SPAC also 
maintained an additional $5M in a trust following the transaction. 
 
Aurora being advanced at a favorable time for U.S. uranium. The U.S. market continues to 
tighten for domestic uranium supplies given a ban on Russian uranium imports, active Department 
of Energy reserve buying, and utilities scrambling to lock in geopolitically safe supply. In turn, this 
positions a large-scale project like Aurora to command a premium that does not fully get unlocked 
by its historical drilling and resource.  
 
Aurora is positioned on BLM land. The site is therefore subject to a lengthy federal permitting 
process. Importantly, however, we see the current administration's push to fast-track strategic 
resource projects through permitting as a meaningful factor likely to compress the asset’s 
development path.  
 
Oregon permits play a role, but Eagle has structured the project to limit it. With the deposit 
in Malheur County, Oregon, mining at site requires Oregon permits in addition to the federal BLM 
and National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) process. While Oregon has historically been 
cautious about chemical-based uranium extraction, with a 1991 state law written specifically to 
regulate it, Eagle's plan sidesteps that issue by mining in Oregon but moving processing across 
the border to private land in Nevada. We emphasize that Nevada remains one of the most mining-
friendly jurisdictions in the world. Although Aurora being based in Oregon adds some permitting 
steps on the mining side, management’s Oregon-mine/Nevada-process design should ultimately 
avoid Oregon’s tougher rules on chemical processing. 
 
Significant adjacent uranium resource upside. Eagle holds option rights to the Cordex Zone. 
Cordex is adjacent to its main target area and located on the same land package. We note that 
work at Cordex is supported by roughly 110 historical drill holes, though this drilling has not yet 
been captured in any defined resource. Management has been digitizing the existing holes in the 
area, though this work only has limited value as the old core has not been maintained particularly 
well by prior ownership. Management nonetheless expects the site to add a significant amount of 
uranium to the resource once exploration is complete. In short, we view Cordex as a high-value 
embedded expansion target that should eventually blend into the firm’s impressive land package.

supportive policy. President Trump has signed four executive orders on nuclear energy. These

Supply-demand story continues to see significant growth. Federal support comes as no 
surprise. In short, the Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act, which became effective in August

to fill. On January 21, 2026, the World Nuclear Association released its projections for global

unlikely to abate. When taken together, these forces have driven uranium’s spot price to over 
$100 per pound (lb) during January 2026. Following some fluctuations, prices have remained 
fairly consistent around $85/lb over the last few months. In our view, the well-maintained price of

SMR Technology. On June 20, 2025, Eagle entered into an Exclusive Patent License Agreement 
with the University of New Mexico Rainforest Innovations (UNMRI), a nonprofit corporation. This 
agreement granted Eagle exclusive, worldwide rights to use, develop, manufacture, and 
commercialize the SMR technology and associated heat-exchanger and control systems. The 
license also covers one U.S. utility patent application for the Very-Small, Long-Life, Modular 
(VSLLIM) reactor plus three provisional applications regarding VSLLIM and Small, Long-Life, 
Modular (SLLIM) reactors. This gives Eagle the right to grant commercial sublicenses subject to 
UNMRI's review and approval. 
 
Eagle’s VSLLIM and SLLIM technologies are driven by sodium-cooled, fast-spectrum 
(liquid metal-cooled) reactor designs. These reactors use a fast-neutron core fueled by 
enriched uranium nitride and are cooled by natural circulation of liquid sodium. The design is 
marketed as "walk-away safe," meaning it needs no active intervention to remain stable, based 
on its negative feedback and passive cooling. The firm’s VSLLIM reactor design is an ultra-
compact “micro” reactor under roughly 10MWth that can be mounted on a 18-wheeler for 
transport. The reactor is expected to run 5.9 full-power years at its current capacity, with up to 92 
years at 1MWth, before refueling. The larger SLLIM reactor uses the same core technology but 
scales output between 10MWth and 100MWth for grid, industrial, and data-center use. For now, 
however, we stress that this technology remains in the design stage.

Aurora Project Background 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Project overview. The Aurora Project is located in Malheur County, Oregon. The asset, roughly 
three miles from the Nevada border, covers 30 square kilometers of BLM public lands. Aurora’s 
August 2025 Technical Report Summary defined an M&I resource of 53.42M tonnes (t) at 278 
parts per million (ppm) U3O8, amounting to 32.75Mlbs, alongside Inferred resources of 8.96Mt at 
252ppm U3O8 for an additional 4.98Mlbs. With processing costs of $13.00/t, mining costs of 
$4.00/t, waste costs of $3.00/t, and $2.50/t for overburden, Aurora also offers attractive margins, 
based on a $90.00/lb U3O8 price and an 85% mill recovery rate. We emphasize that Eagle holds 
option rights to the adjacent Cordex Zone, located on the same land package. Cordex is 
supported by roughly 110 historical drill holes, which have not yet been captured in any defined 
resource. We highlight that NUCL’s Cordex claims still require additional drilling before they can 
be incorporated into a resource estimate. Overall, however, we already view the area as an 
embedded expansion to Eagle’s already impressive land package.  
 
Geologically sound location. Aurora sits within the McDermitt Caldera, a volcanic system that 
also hosts Lithium Americas’ (LAC; not rated) Thacker Pass lithium project and a cluster of 
historical mercury mines. Within this system, the uranium is located in the volcanic rock formed 
by previous hot-spring activity, a relatively uncommon deposit type, with its closest global 
comparisons being the Dornot deposit in Mongolia, the Michelin deposit in Canada, and the Nopal 
deposit in Mexico.

Production profile grades well. Although Aurora's grade of roughly 250 – 280ppm U₃O₈ is low 
by global standards, we still view the deposit's shallow geometry and large tonnage base as 
supporting an attractive production profile. We believe the asset’s near-surface mineralization 
enables a low-cost, low-risk open-pit mine that can feed a conventional mill, with costs of $4.00/t 
for ore, $3.00/t for waste, and $2.50/t for overburden, per prior studies. Compared with assets 
with similar volcanic-hosted geology, Aurora has the largest tonnage in its peer set. In turn, we 
view Aurora’s scale-for-grade trade-off as ultimately offset by continued resource expansion at 
the adjacent Cordex claims.  
 
High-grade uranium located near-surface. Within its resource, Eagle's own analysis further 
distinguishes moderate-to-low-grade mineralization (below 500ppm eU₃O₈, showing continuity 
across the deposit) from sporadic higher-grade zones exceeding 800ppm eU₃O₈. The latter is 
likely tied to structures that angled drilling has not tested. In our view, this combination of scale, 
shallow depth, and low-cost open-pit mining is what allows Aurora's economics to work despite 
the below-average grade. More notably, assuming pit sequencing prioritizes the near-surface, 
higher-grade zones in Aurora’s earliest production years, initial uranium production could benefit 
from an above-average grade profile relative to the deposit's average. This would likely support a 
faster payback period.

Aurora’s development is its deliberate production and processing that is expected to be split

plans to relocate processing and tailings to Nevada should yield benefits from a mining-friendly 
jurisdiction. We also highlight Oregon’s status as a Nuclear Regulatory Commission “Agreement

SMR Technology Highlights 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Progress of SMR technology. Eagle's SMR technology is currently in the design stage and 
comprises two complementary liquid-sodium-cooled fast reactors, the VSLLIM and SLLIM 
models. Both were originally designed at the University of New Mexico and continue to be 
developed under Dr. Mohamed S. El-Genk, a four-decade nuclear engineering veteran with 
roughly 400 technical papers and over 9,000 citations. The VSLLIM design is smaller than a 
shipping container and is intended for factory mass production and deployment via 18-wheeler 
truck, while the SLLIM model shares the same components but is primed for larger industrial and 
data center needs. With a roughly six-year refueling cycle and a footprint of less than 200 square-
meters (20x10m), the IP includes one utility patent and three provisional patents. 
 
License represents the core asset. Eagle’s executive license agreement with the UNMRI grants 
worldwide rights to design, manufacture, and commercialize its SMR technology and associated 
systems. Specifically, the license covers reactor core design, advanced heat exchangers, 
autonomous control systems, and other critical SMR components. We note that it consists of one 
U.S. utility patent application for the VSLLIM reactor plus three provisional patent applications for 
processes for both the VSLLIM and SLLIM. We also highlight that Eagle holds the right to grant 
commercial sublicenses (subject to UNMRI review), creating a potential licensing/royalty revenue 
stream on top of building the reactors itself.

As shown in Exhibit 22 on Page 19, the VSLLIM and SLLIM share the same underlying

off-grid power source or scaled up to power an industrial facility or small community without re-
engineering the design. In our view, this lowers the development risk of Eagle’s newly invented 
technology and should shorten the path to commercial deployment. We stress that we view the 
dual-format approach as a noteworthy strategy that targets various uses while emphasizing that 
the technology remains in the design stage with no license. 
 
Limited supply amid extensive demand. We note that both enrichment levels fall within the 
High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU) requirements, defined as uranium enriched to 
between 5% and 20% U-235. In short this is the fuel input required by nearly every next-
generation SMR design, where U.S. domestic supply remains limited. Eagle's own disclosure 
states that this uranium nitride fuel is likely to require separate NRC qualification before use, with 
no clarity on the speed of either timeline.  
 
Both the VSLLIM and SLLIM designs are aimed at grid, industrial, and data-center use. In 
short, this strategy benefits from recent U.S. policy, as the current administration has passed 
various Executive Orders to accelerate demand for U.S. uranium supply and the deployment of 
nuclear technology. We also highlight the emphasis on improving the regulatory approval process 
while increasing incentives for developing new technologies. In short, the key theme for the firm 
appears to be its choice to target fast-growing end markets, as demand for resilient clean energy 
across commercial, residential, military, and AI applications remains strong.

Passive safety design. Both the VSLLIM and SLLIM are described by Eagle as "walk-away 
safe.” In short, this implies they have no pumps or other moving parts in their primary cooling 
loop, along with a stated emergency shutdown capability. In turn, the reactor can shut down safely 
through natural processes, such as gravity or convection. SLLIM adds another layer, as it can 
generate its own electricity during a shutdown event, reducing reliance on offsite or backup power 
to maintain safe conditions. We note that more advanced SMR technology firms often use these 
safety principles to argue that their designs deserve an expedited NRC review. For now, the safety 
features remain unverified engineering claims until tested against the design certification process. 
 
Deployment logistics. The VSLLIM is likely to be factory-assembled and delivered by truck. It 
primarily targets remote communities, defense sites, and mine sites with as many as 30 units 
deployable simultaneously. In contrast, the SLLIM would be assembled on site and shipped by 
rail, truck, or barge for below-ground installation at larger areas, industrial sites, and data centers.  
 
Autonomous operations. The SLLIM is expected to be able to be remotely monitored with AI, 
likely resulting in lower on-site staffing costs and requirements when compared to conventional 
reactors. In short, we view this as a meaningful operating cost improvement when pegged against 
current options for the industrial and data-center sites, which the SLLIM is designed to serve.  
 
Regulatory pathway to commercialization. Before either reactor can move from design to 
deployment, Eagle must clear a multi-stage NRC approval process. While this requires more work 
than a single license, we expect the current administration to support its development. The first 
step is design certification, which requires submitting a technical outline proving that the reactor 
design meets NRC safety and performance standards. This is likely to be followed by a combined 
license for each site, covering site-specific safety, security, and environmental review with public 
participation. We emphasize that both the design certification and standard design approval are 
likely tied to future capital deployment. We also note that the uranium nitride fuel requires separate 
NRC fuel qualification before any use, which would add a second, independent approval timeline. 
 
Management acknowledges that its reactor design and fuel qualification remain 
conceptual. Eagle currently discloses no timeline, cost estimate, or pre-application activity for 
design certification, standard design approval, or fuel qualification. While design certification is an 
extensive and necessary step, we do not view it as enough to represent a clear catalyst for the 
firm’s SMR thesis as each deployment site requires its own combined license. We expect Eagle’s 
first disclosed capital budget and timeline to be tied to SMR tech advancements, representing the 
first clear signal of tackling the regulatory process. For now, future per-unit economics, 
deployment timing, and even the delivery methods remain subject to change rather than an 
expected plan until the firm’s design certification is cleared.  
 
In our view, Eagle's upstream uranium business is relevant to the SMR thesis. This is 
notable as successful fuel qualification could create a rare vertically integrated path from domestic 
uranium production to SMR fuel supply. At present, however, this optionality is purely theoretical 
until both the fuel qualification and reactor design certification processes become more definite.

Risks  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Liquidity risk. We emphasize the inherent liquidity risk associated with developing the Aurora 
Project. We base this concern on the capital-intensive nature of exploration and development 
work at the site. Additionally, we highlight the significant amount of capital necessary to develop 
the firm’s SMR technology. To manage its liquidity risk, we continue to monitor its cash and liquid 
assets position. That said, the firm maintains a strong liquidity position. 
 
Operating and technical risk. Uranium mining has operating and technical risks that may impact 
costs and production, including technical issues with and potential changes in geological, mining, 
and metallurgical parameters, as well as equipment, labor, logistics, environmental and safety 
issues. The timing and volume of production may significantly differ from current forecasts, and 
unforeseen events could curtail production or force the closure of a producing asset. In addition, 
Eagle’s SMR technology is quite early-stage and carries numerous risk factors that need to be 
resolved before entering production. 
 
Political risk. Although we view uranium as a bipartisan issue, we note that regulatory decisions 
from other nations have the potential to materially impact future operations and economics. In 
addition, shifts in federal laws and regulations can play that same role. We view uranium as a 
complex global market, meaning Eagle remains exposed to several policy changes from countries 
it has no direct ties to. This, in turn, can impact future profitability. 
 
Commodity price risk. We highlight the inherent risks associated with the company’s 
dependency on uranium, as future cash flow and profitability are highly dependent on its spot 
price. Pricing of uranium remains heavily influenced by export restrictions, geopolitical tension, 
and regulations, which could increase volatility in the future.
