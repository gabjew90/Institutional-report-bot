Key takeaways
• The energy transition is advancing globally, but investment opportunities increasingly
differ by region. Our Barclays Energy Transition Barometer finds momentum strengthening
across every region covered, led by China and Europe (Figure 3). However, differences in
power demand, infrastructure, energy resources and market structure mean that the most-
attractive technologies and sectors vary considerably by market (Figure 2).
• We see the next transition trade as tilted towards transmission, with grids, batteries and
baseload power increasingly emerging as key bottlenecks requiring investment. Growth
in renewables is increasing the need for transmission and distribution networks, battery
storage, interconnectors, demand management and firm generation, such as nuclear and
geothermal. For investors, this broadens the transition opportunity from renewable
generation into regulated utilities, capital goods and electrical equipment.

expanding to the grids, batteries, power equipment, utilities and firm-generation
technologies required to connect new supply and provide reliable electricity.

Introducing the Barclays Energy Transition Barometer
Taking the pulse of the transition at a critical inflection point
We believe we are at an important juncture in the global energy transition. The first phase
was defined by climate ambition, falling clean technology costs and rapid deployment,
supported by a geopolitical environment broadly conducive to global coordination. This phase
supported a lowering of the green premia and establishing clean power as the predominant
source at the margin of the energy system (see Taking stock of the IEA World Energy Outlook
and EI statistical review of world energy - key takeaways).

Energy security and competitiveness are now becoming structural transition drivers
alongside sustainability. Countries are pursuing pathways shaped by their resource
endowments, industrial priorities and energy security needs. Meanwhile, rising power demand
is colliding with constraints in grids, flexibility and equipment supply. Together, these forces are
widening regional differences and reducing the usefulness of a single global transition narrative
(Sustainable Investing 2026 Outlook: Security, Sustainability, Scalability).
This shift leads us to propose a new analytical toolkit. We view the key question being posed
now as whether the wider energy system can absorb that scale of clean technologies reliably
and economically. If renewable capacity grows rapidly while grid investment lags, then clean
power supply could accelerate while electrification struggles to keep pace. Clean generation
would then become cheaper at the margin while system integration costs would rise. These
mismatches matter for investors given implications for capital allocation, in our view.
Rhetoric vs reality: an empirical approach to assessing the transition
The Barclays Energy Transition Barometer provides an empirical view on whether
transition progress is outweighing the headwinds that constrain delivery. It combines
observable physical, economic and market inputs spanning clean supply, demand
electrification, system balance, transition economics and equity market returns (see
Methodology). We have designed this as an input into investment analysis, rather than as an
alignment tool with a prescribed transition pathway.
Our framework is built around three core principles. Separate pace from friction, use hard
data over qualitative policy indicators and prioritise transparency. Our aim is not to optimise a
statistical relationship, but to provide a repeatable framework for measuring transition
momentum and understanding the investment implications across regions.
Our key takeaway from this analysis is that global transition momentum is building, but
bottlenecks are emerging and regional variations are compounding. The opportunity set is
increasingly regional: China’s scale is creating demand for system integration, Europe’s clean
power build-out is increasing the value of flexibility, North America’s load growth is elevating
the importance of speed-to-power, while Asia ex-China and LatAm require more-selective
investment approaches. We examine these Regional investment implications later in the report.

• Demand momentum is strengthening, but lags supply growth. China’s supply-side
expansion is being reinforced by rising electrification, enabling clean power to begin
displacing thermal energy. However, Europe is progressing more slowly. Its transition strategy
is centred on combining clean energy deployment with wholesale electrification, but
electricity accounts for roughly the same share of total useful energy demand as three years
ago.1 North America continues to electrify incrementally, although the emerging energy
challenge is more firmly centred on meeting rising power demand from AI, where speed and
reliability are key (Powering AI: The new space race), rather than simply substituting existing
fossil demand. 
• Balance is strongest where clean capacity, electrification and emissions outcomes are
moving together. China screens strongest because all three ETP pillars are advancing. Clean
capacity is scaling, end-use is becoming more electrified and the power mix is gradually
becoming less emissions-intensive. However, coal’s continued importance in the power
mix (>50% of generation) means this decline comes from a high level; Chinese electricity
generation is still the most carbon-intensive of the regions we track (Figure 7). In
contrast, Europe presents a different picture, with the ramp-up in renewables deployment
and push to decouple from imported natural gas post-Ukraine war having accelerated its
power-sector decarbonisation. North America is more mixed in this dimension, with clean
capacity growing but the emissions intensity improving less decisively as electrification only
occurs incrementally. 
• For investors, regional dynamics are key. China’s deployment supports global equipment
volumes and lower technology costs, but overcapacity may weigh on supplier margins.
Europe’s increasingly decarbonised power mix concentrates the opportunity in power
absorption and flexible assets capable of connecting abundant clean supply with a slower-
moving demand base (What if... EU electrification gets catalysed). In North America,
accelerating load growth shifts the opportunity towards utilities, grid equipment, behind-the-
meter supply and clean firm capacity, such as nuclear (Nuclear progresses from conviction to
construction). We develop these regional conclusions further in the next section.

Transition friction: From generation economics to system integration
We separate our analysis of Energy Transition Friction across Supply, Demand, and Balance. Our
screens in Figure 8 suggest that the economics of generation is no longer the key headwind.
Instead, grid investment and system flexibility are emerging as critical challenges, particularly
as it relates to keeping pace with renewable deployment. This resulting friction is increasingly
visible through congestion as well as negative and volatile power prices.
• Supply friction remains most visible where renewable deployment is running ahead of
grid investment. The grid screen compares renewable capacity growth with the
corresponding growth in grid investment, capturing whether network development is

keeping pace with the clean power being added to the system (Figure 9). China and Asia ex-

transmission, distribution and inter-regional connectivity quickly enough to absorb such
rapid renewable capacity growth. Europe and North America appear comparatively less

• Demand friction is generally easing as clean generation economics become more
competitive. On a generation-only basis, the clean-versus-thermal cost differential is most
supportive in Europe and China, suggesting that the standalone economics of clean power
are no longer the primary constraint on deployment in those regions. Europe’s clean
generation discount has deepened materially (Figure 10), driven both by elevated imported
hydrocarbon costs and a rapidly improving electro-tech stack, which is increasingly geared
towards meeting the continent's critical infrastructure needs beyond just decarbonisation
(Climate Tech: From decarbonisation to critical infrastructure). Demand friction is more
visible across Asia ex-China and parts of LatAm and North America, where financing costs,
fuel price dynamics, technology mix and local market structures can affect relative
competitiveness. We, therefore, interpret this pillar narrowly as a measure of relative
generation-cost friction, rather than as an assessment of whole-system cost or physical
balancing requirements.
• Balance friction is emerging as the most important headwind in higher-penetration
markets. This pillar captures negative wholesale prices and power-price volatility, signals
that clean generation is at times exceeding local demand or available system flexibility.
Europe shows the clearest deterioration, as rapid renewable deployment has increased the
value of storage, interconnection, flexible demand and dispatchable capacity. Balance
pressure is also visible in China and North America, as well as Asia ex-China and LatAm,
although comparisons should be treated cautiously because market structures differ and
regulated tariffs can suppress observable price signals.
• Transition friction is creating investible scarcity beyond renewables. The regional screens
point to different stages of the same system challenge: China and Asia ex-China need
infrastructure to connect and absorb rapid deployment, Europe requires flexibility to manage
an increasingly decarbonised power mix and North America’s rising AI-load growth raises the
value of reliability and grid capacity.

Methodology
FIGURE 12. Barclays Energy Transition Barometer: Framework overview
Source: Barclays Research
The Energy Transition Barometer assesses whether the energy transition is gaining enough pace
to overcome frictions. We construct a quantitative screen that treats transition momentum as
the interaction between the pace of transition progress and the frictions constraining delivery
(Figure 12).
• Energy Transition Pace (ETP): assesses progress in clean supply, end-demand electrification
and the balance between clean power and electricity demand.
• Energy Transition Friction (ETF): measures the extent to which constraints and bottlenecks
are slowing the transition.
The result of these two forces is Net Transition Momentum (NTM), which provides a directional
read on whether transition progress is outweighing the measured headwinds.
ETP and ETF are each assessed across supply, demand and balance. The transition is driven by
the interaction of energy supply and demand, as well as the ability of the system to manage
imbalances. Clean supply needs to scale, final energy demand needs to electrify and the power
system needs to absorb that shift without creating new bottlenecks.
This structure is designed to separate the main moving parts of the transition while preserving a
system-level view. It helps investors see not just whether the transition is progressing, but

Energy Transition Pace
Energy Transition Pace is an equal-weighted average of three pillars:
ETP = ⅓ Supply Pace + ⅓ Demand Pace + ⅓ Supply-Demand Balance
We use equal weights at the pillar level to keep the framework transparent.
The ETP Supply pillar assesses whether the installed power fleet is becoming cleaner and
whether that change is reducing the emissions intensity of electricity generation. It does so by
looking at both renewables and nuclear capacity, with renewables carrying the largest weight
because they represent the principal recurring source of new clean capacity. Renewable
capacity includes wind, solar and renewable hydro. This is then computed by comparing each
region’s current installed renewable capacity with its average 2023 installed renewable capacity
baseline.
We use a stock-based capacity measure because it avoids comparing capacity additions with
prior capacity additions — a “growth of growth” problem. It also reduces quarter-to-quarter
volatility and better captures the structural build-out of the clean power system.
Nuclear is treated separately because it has different economics and commissioning cycles. We
measure total net installed nuclear capacity versus a 2023 baseline, capturing the fleet
trajectory after additions, retirements, restarts and closures.
Power-sector emissions intensity acts as an outcome check. Capacity can grow, but the
transition only matters if the power mix is becoming cleaner. This metric, therefore, measures
whether power-sector emissions intensity is improving versus the 2023 baseline.
The ETP Demand pillar assesses changes in the role of electricity within the overall energy
system. We use an economy-wide electrification proxy calculated as electricity generation
divided by total energy supply. Electricity generation is converted from TWh to EJ using a factor
of 0.0036 before calculating the share, with 2023 used as the baseline year. Higher electricity
share means greater electrification and, therefore, faster demand-side transition pace.

We keep Demand Pace focused on the electrification of end demand, rather than clean
electricity penetration. Clean power supply as a share of electricity demand specifically (as
opposed to broader energy demand) is captured separately in the Balance pillar. This avoids
double-counting clean power generation inside the demand pillar and preserves a clean
distinction between demand shifting to electricity and the power system becoming clean
enough to support that shift.
The Supply/Demand Balance pillar assesses whether clean electricity supply is keeping pace
with demand, while incorporating wider market and economy-level outcomes.
Clean Energy Share of Demand is the core physical balance screen. It measures clean power
generation as a share of total electricity demand. This is placed in the Balance pillar because
the metric has supply in the numerator and demand in the denominator. It is, therefore, not a
pure demand measure; it measures whether the clean power system is scaling fast enough
relative to the size of the power market.
Relative clean-versus-conventional energy equity performance provides a market-facing read
on whether public markets are rewarding the transition theme. We do this by measuring the
relative performance of the S&P Global Clean Energy Transition Index (SPGTCED) with that of a
conventional energy-focused index (MSCI World Energy Sector Index, MXWO0EN).
Emissions intensity of GDP provides a broader economy-wide outcome check, complementing
the framework’s focus on power and electrification. We construct GDP-weighted regional values
using PPP-adjusted output, with lower emissions per unit of GDP indicating a greater
decoupling of economic activity from emissions. This tests whether transition progress is
extending beyond the power sector into the wider economy.
Energy Transition Friction
Energy Transition Friction is an equal-weighted average of three pillars:
ETF = ⅓ Supply Friction + ⅓ Demand Friction + ⅓ Supply-Demand Friction
The logic is the same as for Energy Transition Pace, in that it assesses how bottlenecks to
transition outcomes are progressing across the supply and demand side of the energy system,
as well as accounting for balancing issues. Progress is then measured against a 2023 baseline,
once again to account for the exogenous shocks of 2020-22.
1. ETF Supply
ETF Supply assesses whether grid investment is keeping pace with renewable-capacity growth.
We do this by looking at the grid investment gap, since concerns over network security are
increasingly dictating the extent to which clean capacity can come online and ultimately
displace thermal generation.

Grid gap = grid investment growth — renewable capacity growth
This measures the extent to which grid investment is keeping up with the growth in installed
renewable capacity, given the strain on the grid that variable generation introduces. This is an
important friction element to the energy transition, with grid stability having emerged as an
increasingly important issue for policymakers and investors alike as renewables become a
dominant component in many countries' power matrix (Network security (part 1): Blackouts).
We think looking at the delta between grid investment and renewable capacity growth
represents a cleaner friction metric than grid capex alone, because the transition bottleneck is
not simply whether grid spending is rising, but whether it is rising fast enough relative to the
clean capacity being added to the system. A region with high grid investment may still face
rising friction if renewable capacity is expanding faster, while equally a region with more
moderate grid spend may face less pressure if clean capacity growth is also more measured.
This metric is therefore designed to capture the relative adequacy of grid investment, making it
a more direct proxy for clean-capacity bottlenecks.
2. ETF Demand
The Demand Frictions pillar assesses the significance of hurdles to decarbonising energy
demand. We think the main determinant of this is the relative competitiveness of clean versus
conventional generation costs, measured as the spread between green and thermal LCOE2 :
Green LCOE — thermal LCOE
LCOE is one of the most recognised metrics for comparing the relative economic cost of power
generation across different technologies. We choose this metric given the breadth and depth of
data availability, which allows for cross-regional comparison. LCOE does, however, have
limitations and should not be seen as the full picture of the relative cost of power generation
technologies. The most common critique of LCOE is that it favours renewables by focusing only
on marginal generation costs, ignoring the wider systems cost associated with their variability.
We partially address this by including storage costs while recognising that the measure does not
capture the full network, flexibility and balancing costs of variable generation. Those pressures
are assessed separately in ETF Supply and Balance
3. ETF Supply-Demand Balance
The Balance Frictions pillar measures the extent to which power markets are showing signs of
integration stress. Negative prices capture periods where supply exceeds demand or system
flexibility is insufficient. Wholesale price volatility captures broader instability in power-market
balancing. Both are benchmarked against 2023 because absolute levels are less comparable
across market designs.

in liberalised wholesale markets. In regulated or semi-regulated markets, the absence of

Net Transition Momentum
We estimate net transition momentum by comparing the assessments of Energy Transition Pace
(ETP) and Energy Transition Friction (ETF). Net transition momentum is calculated as the
difference between these two components, designed to give a directional read on whether
transition progress is outweighing the measured headwinds in each region.
