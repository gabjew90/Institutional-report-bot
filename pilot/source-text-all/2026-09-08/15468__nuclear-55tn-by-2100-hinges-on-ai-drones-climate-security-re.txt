Nuclear: $55tn By 2100 Hinges On AI, Drones,
Climate, Security & Replacement
We are launching coverage on 11 nuclear companies. We forecast ~$55tn of
nuclear-related capex through 2100: this is arguably conservative, given the
confluence of data center energy demand, the broader electrification needed for
"physical AI", and pressure from climate stress on aging nuclear power plants
and refineries. For a long cycle, with frequent fly-ups, we favor the integrated
producers (Buy-rated NXE, CCJ, KAP) and global capex (Buy-rated MIR).
The Long Road To Electrification: Governments that want to win in AI, particularly in AVs, robotics
and drones, will want cheap electricity--and that likely entails lowering the ROIC hurdle for new
investment decisions in the nuclear value chain. Even assuming solar and wind capture most of
new energy investment, the investments in nuclear since the 1950s may be only about a tenth of
what a fully electrified economy requires. On a 24 hour clock, it's 1:45 am. Heading to LA from Wall
Street, we're just passing Allentown. Heading to Beijing from Lisbon, Madrid.
#1: 3 Thematic Trades: 1) Geopolitics drives a security premium for conversion and enrichment;
2) Valuation disconnects around the duration of bottlenecks, as all steps of the supply chain
likely experiencing incentive pricing every 7–10 years during the long AI adoption phase, and 3)
Long-dated call options from small modular reactors (SMRs) and new reactor builds with design
approvals, cost validation and secure feedstocks.
#2: The Short Cycle: Utilities face a structural supply shortfall, but new nuclear projects still hinge on
government support. Conversion economics have hit incentive levels; but lags adding conversion
capacity make fly-ups more likely. Heavy tails suggest a 1.4x-2.0x multiplier vs baseline project
NPVs, but equities likely discount this only when spot prices accelerate.
#3: The Long Cycle: Given an aging reactor fleet (avg. ~33 years, ~43 in the US), we estimate ~
$9tn of investment ($6-$7tn reactors) by 2050 and another ~$46tn (~$13tn reactors) by 2100. Full
electrification would imply another ~$30tn in nuclear capex and electricity demand rising 3x-4x. To
the extent SMRs and, eventually, fusion, can lower costs, we believe nuclear, overall, could reach
10%-15% of new power additions in the 2030s, 15%-25% by the 2050s and 20%-30% in 2050-2100.
Our thesis could be amplified by a steepening cost curve for new Western-accessible supply.
#4: Climate Stress: Climate stress motivates decarbonization and fleet turnover. 12%-15% of
reactors have experienced weather outages: ~40% are on rivers and lakes at risk of low flow; coastal
plants can shut for surges or marine heatwaves above intake design. Fossil fuels risk runs >2x
nuclear. Next-generation SMRs and EPC firms are strategic winners in scenarios above +2°C.
#5: Tactics: The Macro Trade, Spot Prices Vs. Interest Rates: WACC is destiny for capital intensive
projects: the nuclear cycle is effectively a function of political appetite and budget capacity to fight
the credit cycle. At the same time, consensus revisions will track swings in price benchmarks for
uranium, conversion and SWU. Given the recent derating, we favor leverage to spot prices.
Risks: 1/The uranium cycle hinges on both regulatory and political volatility, as well as financing
costs and execution: a single incident could reset S/D balances for years; 2/ Climate adaptation,
military spending, or labor dynamics could siphon policy support away from new nuclear projects;
3/ Renewed access to Russian supply would delay the scarcity thesis by a decade.

Policy Sets WACC
The economics of nuclear expansion are not, in our view, primarily about uranium availability.
Navigating bottlenecks within an electricity-constrained system, geopolitical fragmentation and high
capital requirements are the key economic constraints.
Our framework is that WACC is destiny for capital-intensive industries. Nuclear economics depend
heavily on financing structures because new plants require large upfront capital expenditures and
long construction periods before generating revenue. This makes policy support structurally more
important for nuclear than for modular generation technologies with shorter construction cycles and
faster cash flow realization.
Policymakers can support nuclear through multiple mechanisms including;
•
Loan Guarantees that lower financing costs
•
Production Tax Credits that improve operating economics
•
Investment Tax Credits that reduce net capital requirements
•
Regulated Cost Recovery that shifts construction risk to ratepayers
•
Contracts for difference that provide revenue certainty
•
Capacity Payments that value reliability attributes
•
Direct procurement by government entities
•
Fuel-cycle grants for conversion and enrichment infrastructure
•
Strategic Reserve Purchase commitments.
Globally, there have been several tailwinds in support of nuclear over the last 12 months.
•
United States: The Trump Administration issued four nuclear-focused Executive Orders in May
2025, aimed at accelerating reactor deployment, rebuilding the domestic fuel cycle, streamlining
licensing, and positioning the U.S. to compete with China and Russia in nuclear exports. A
central target is to expand U.S. nuclear capacity from ~100 GW today to 400 GW by 2050. The
implementation of the ADVANCE Act and NRC licensing streamlining have also been important.
•
Europe: The European nuclear policy agenda remains heterogeneous, however France, Poland,
the United Kingdom, Czechia and several Eastern European countries have all increased support
for new nuclear capacity.
•
China continues to operate an aggressive energy and nuclear growth strategy, focused on
vertically integrated supply chains and long-term industrial planning.
•
Japan: Japan's 7th Strategic Energy Plan reaffirmed the importance of nuclear power for energy
security, with support for restarts, lifetime extensions and eventual new builds. Government
financing could cover ~30% of new-build project costs, per METI.
 
Our overall thesis on nuclear, however, does not require the US to take the lead in building out
nuclear sites. China has achieved the lowest nuclear levelized cost of electricity globally through
standardized Generation III/IV designs, cheap capital reflecting low weighted average cost of capital,
high capacity factors consistently above 92%, and vertically integrated supply chains. Chinese nuclear
capital expenditure runs ~$6,000–$6,500/kW installed versus $10,000+/kW in the United States. China
is projected to drive 20–25% of global new nuclear builds through 2050, with the fastest construction
timelines in the industry at 5–7 years versus 10–15 years in Western jurisdictions.
 
The European Union, meanwhile, has seen new nuclear build nearly halted outside France, which
may revive a standardized EPR fleet under government support. Offshore wind dominates European
renewable capacity additions, particularly North Sea developments, though capital expenditure has

Decarbonization Policy Vs. Energy Security
Governments pursuing emissions reduction targets increasingly view nuclear generation as a firm low-
carbon resource that can provide baseload power without fossil fuel combustion. Meanwhile, Russia's
invasion of Ukraine in 2022 fundamentally altered fuel-security priorities across North America
and Europe. Governments that previously optimized energy systems primarily for cost increasingly
optimize for supply resilience and geopolitical independence. Western governments recognizing their
dependence on Russian fuel-cycle infrastructure have created strategic premiums for domestic
uranium production, conversion, enrichment, and fuel fabrication capacity. The net result has been that

Debate #1: A Secular Growth Theme
We believe the nuclear industry is well-positioned to deliver secular growth over several cycles, and
possibly for a couple of generations. Our base case is that advances in automation -- using AI to move
and manipulate molecules, objects, people and animals -- will accelerate both electricity demand (for
computing power) and electrification (to reduce robot failure rates). Geopolitics, both security needs
and the desire to secure advantages in new industrial value chains, likely motivate a competition for
lower energy costs. Climate stress likely both increases policymakers' interest in decarbonization and
makes fossil fuel capacity less competitive. The current nuclear power fleet (>40 years old on average
in the West) will need to be replaced. The confluence of these dynamics is that the nuclear industry
likely requires ~$55tn of capex through 2100 (in current dollars).
We recommend a layered approach, even for long-duration thematic investors, given the nuclear
sector's heterogeneity. Each subsector will lead or lag at different points in the cycle and react
differently to sector-specific changes, shifts in policy support and macro factors (e.g., rates, power
prices, geopolitical shocks). Over the past several decades, ROIC profiles have varied across the
nuclear value chain due to differences in regulation, market structure, and related volatility. In particular,
we recommend thematic investors focus on three key themes: 1) Geopolitics drives a security
premium for conversion and enrichment; 2) All steps of the supply chain likely experience incentive
pricing more often over the next decade, and 3) Long-dated call options from small modular reactors
(SMRs) and new reactor builds with design approvals, validation of unit costs, and securing feedstock
as key catalysts.

Across The Cycle Benchmarks: EROI & LCOE
Energy Return on Investment, or EROI, measures the ratio of energy delivered by a power system over
its lifetime relative to the energy required to build, operate, and decommission that system. Nuclear
power exhibits an EROI of 50-70, among the highest of any electricity-generation technology. This high
EROI reflects large, steady outputs over multi-decade lifetimes of 60 to 90 years despite high capital
expenditure and system complexity.
This matters because EROI for high-quality oil fields typically have a 50-100 EROI, whereas the more
recent unconventional oil fields have 5-20. Modern economies typically need a system-wide EROI
of >11 at the extraction stage to grow, which translates to ~3.5 at the economy-wide level given
combustion efficiency losses of 70%-80% and power plant waste heat losses of 50%-60%. Fossil
fuel EROIs depend on applications, at 10-14 for heating, 4.5 for mechanical work and 2.0-2.5 for
transportation. Global energy production runs 6%-10% of GDP, in line with this framework. If system-
wide EROIs deteriorate, the share of GDP allocated to energy production increases
 
Wind-plus-battery systems show an EROI that declines when energy storage is incorporated. Wind
turbines alone achieve EROIs of 20 to 40, but adding batteries, with EROIs of 10 to 20 for manufacturing,
installation, and eventual disposal, reduces the combined system EROI. Solar plus battery exhibits
similar dynamics, with solar photovoltaic panels achieving EROIs of 8-20 depending on insolation
and panel quality, further diluted when coupled to battery storage requiring its own energy inputs for
manufacturing and mineral extraction.
However, EROI provides only one dimension of economic comparison. Capital expenditure per kilowatt
of installed capacity, operating expenditure, build speed, maintenance requirements, and disposal
costs all contribute to the economics of delivered electricity. Nuclear faces capital expenditures
of $6,000-$12,000/kW, depending on jurisdiction and design, with eight to fifteen-year construction
timelines. Wind and solar achieve capital expenditures of $1,300-$2,000/kW for onshore wind, $800-
$1,200/kW for utility-scale solar, with construction timelines of one to three years enabling much faster
capital deployment and cost recovery.
Operating expenditure tells a more nuanced story. Nuclear fuel costs remain low at $7-10/MWh, but
total operating costs, including labor, maintenance, regulatory compliance, and waste management,
reach $30-35/ MWh per year in the United States, lower in jurisdictions with cheaper skilled labor.
Wind and solar have minimal fuel costs but require ongoing maintenance and eventual component

Levelized Cost of Electricity: Regional Comparisons Through 2050
Levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) provides a comprehensive metric incorporating capital expenditure,
operating expenditure, fuel costs, financing costs, and capacity factors into a single dollar-per-
megawatt-hour figure. We acknowledge LCOE's limitations — notably that it ignores dispatchability
and time-of-delivery value, excludes system integration costs (transmission, balancing, etc.), and is
sensitive to assumed discount rates and capacity factors — but we use it here as a transparent, widely
referenced benchmark for technology cost comparisons.
We estimate nuclear power in the United States shows levelized costs of ~$0.20/kWh before subsidies,
compared to restarts at ~$0.08/kWh. By the 2030s, SMRs are expected to be on track to deliver
~$0.12/kWh, with a further learning curve into the 2050s. Nuclear's advantage lies in its 90–92%
capacity factor (vs. wind at ~40% and solar at ~22%) and lower fuel costs (cash costs ~$0.03/kWh,
half of a gas turbine).
Regional cost differences have profound implications for competitive dynamics and market share
evolution. China's cost advantage in both nuclear and renewable technologies creates pressure
on Western developers, who must rely on policy support, import restrictions, or domestic content
requirements to remain economically competitive.
The levelized cost comparison suggests that absent substantial policy intervention, renewable plus
battery systems will continue capturing the majority of new capacity additions in most global markets
based purely on economic optimization. Our base case is that nuclear captures 5%-10% of new power
generation additions through to 2030, reflecting continued construction challenges outside China and
renewable cost advantages. Nuclear's share likely grows to 10%-15% in the 2030s and 15%-20% in
the 2040s as SMRs achieve commercial deployment and policy support intensifies. In absolute terms,
nuclear capacity likely grows substantially through 2100 even while capturing a minority share of total
additions.
We expect the global electricity system to expand 3-4x to accommodate electrification of transport,
industry, and heating while replacing fossil generation. Nuclear growing to 15%-30% of a much
larger total generation base represents significant absolute capacity expansion. We estimate the
likely nuclear capacity addition at 450-900GW by 2050, implying $3-$9tn in potential investment in
reactors and $1-$3tn in the balance of the supply chain (cf. the WNA's recent estimate of ~$5tn in
nuclear projects by 2050). Our base case scenario of long-term electrification scenario would imply
a cumulative new build over the next 75 years of ~1,400 GW of nuclear capacity. This would imply
another ~$7tn in total capital investment in reactors and $40tn in the balance of the supply chain
through 2100, including ~$25tn for the transmission grid. In our full-electrification scenario, the capex
required by 2100 would rise to ~$49tn for nuclear reactors and ~$158tn for the balance of the supply
chain.

A related policy issue is the degree to which investors or policy makers tie the delivered cost of energy
to capital recovery. In the US, for example, the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) financed its grid and
generation buildout in the 1930s-40s primarily through federal appropriations and low-cost federal
bond issuance backed by the US Treasury, allowing it to deploy capital at sub-market interest rates
(effectively 2-3% vs. private utility costs of 6-8% at the time), with cost recovery through regulated
ratepayer tariffs rather than equity returns — a model that compressed the cost of capital for long-
lived infrastructure.
Applied to modern nuclear, a TVA-style financing structure (federal loan guarantees, direct Treasury-
backed bonds, or a public financing authority) could reduce delivered nuclear energy costs by
30-40%, since financing costs typically represent 60-70% of levelized nuclear costs given 10-15 year
construction timelines and 60-80 year asset lives. This would bring US nuclear LCOE estimates down
to $75-110/MWh. This is precisely why TVA remains one of the few US entities actively developing
new nuclear (Clinch River SMR project) and why proposals for a federal "Nuclear Finance Corporation"
have gained traction as the single highest-leverage policy lever for making US nuclear cost-competitive
with Chinese and Korean builds.
There is, however, several decades of institutional momentum against such a policy being effectively
implemented. The shift in TVA financing away from the original appropriation-based model was one
of the first real successes of the rollback of the New Deal, after Taft-Hartley reversed the trajectory of
labor power, and it was successful: the TVA completed only 7 reactors through 1985 and abandoned
10, whereas France's EDF completed 56 reactors in the same time frame. Since then, roughly half
of the New Deal has been dismantled and another third significantly weakened, which contributes
to skepticism on the sustainability of US industrial policy initiatives. Barring such a shift, the United
States faces high labor costs, complex regulatory requirements, and elevated financing costs that slow
construction and increase total project costs. Nuclear in the current context struggles on levelized cost
compared to wind resources in the Midwest and solar in the Southwest.

New Technologies Expand Policy Options
Three potential technology disruptions could materially alter baseline projections by altering
the relative cost arbitrages and enabling more modular and decentralized solutions: 1) SMR
commercialization, 2) Fusion power breakthroughs, and 3) Advanced battery chemistry improvements,
particularly sodium-ion batteries.
Small modular reactors currently sit at late pilot or early commercial demonstration stage, with first-
of-a-kind units under construction or regulatory review as of 2026. Commercial demonstration is
expected in the late 2020s with potential mass adoption from 2035 to 2050 if cost, licensing, and
scaling challenges are successfully resolved. The likelihood of market impact by 2060 is moderate,
with upside potential. However, extreme regulatory and financing complexity in the United States and
European Union creates execution risk, though China may achieve smoother deployment through
state-directed investment.
Fusion power remains in laboratory and pilot stage with no grid-scale demonstration. First grid-scale
pilot projects are targeted for the 2030s by the private sector.
Sodium-ion batteries have reached early commercial stage with pilot grid deployments, particularly
from Chinese suppliers like CATL. These batteries offer potential cost advantages over lithium-
ion by avoiding expensive and geopolitically concentrated lithium, cobalt, and nickel supply chains.
Sodium-ion commercialization is highly likely from 2030 onward, with potential to capture 5% -
10% of grid storage share by 2040. A 30%-50% battery cost reduction through sodium-ion adoption
would materially improve wind and solar-plus-battery economics, potentially capping nuclear market
share, unless nuclear achieves its own disruptive cost curve through small modular reactors or other
innovations.
The cumulative impact of these technology wildcards creates substantial uncertainty in long-term
projections. The base case assumes nuclear grows in absolute terms, while wind and solar plus battery
dominate total new builds based on capital expenditure and construction speed advantages. The
technology pivot scenario sees nuclear potentially capturing 25% - 35% of additions if SMRs succeed
and fusion commercializes, while the continued renewable dominance scenario sees nuclear capped
at 10 - 15% if battery costs collapse and renewable plus storage maintains economic superiority.

The State Of The Cycle
We believe the current state of the cycle can be characterized as follows:
•
Utilities: US & EU utilities are better covered by long-term supply contracts than at any point in
the past 25 years. The uncertainty is how new reactor builds will affect the market.
•
Miners: Supply deficits have narrowed to <10%, vs. ~35% in 2020, and primary production could
exceed demand in 2028-2030, helping utilities build inventory. Supply deficits are likely to expand
in the mid-2030s. Terms on long-term contracts are rising to reflect the supply risks. Both
Cameco and Kazatomprom are pursuing "value over volume" strategies. Prices have reached
incentive levels for new capacity and will likely stop rising once NexGen starts signing longer-term
contracts.
•
Conversion: The world has an ample supply in a globally fungible market; the West saw a fly-up
due to moves to decouple from Russian LEU supply, which at one point satisfied 35%-40% of US
reactor needs. Capacity is coming onstream, so barring project delays, prices are likely to abate
in 2029-2030 and then only revisit incentive levels if the global reactor build-out is mishandled.
•
SWU: The world has ample supply; the US ban on Russian SWU has lifted prices to incentive levels.
Incumbents are adding capacity, and the shift to modular centrifuge designs should make the
SWU conversion step more stable and predictable. Shifts to HALEU fuels will not affect aggregate
uranium needs but could tighten the SWU market.
•
Equities: The sector has sold-off in line with the pullback in spot prices. We expect long term
contract prices to rise over the next 12–18 months, pulling spot in line. The spot discount to term
contract is ~9%, vs a 10-year average of ~12%, with the widest annual spot discount on record
being 2016, at –34%.
We expand on each of these points in the next few sections. From a stock-selection perspective, what
matters, in our view, is how perceptions shift over the next 12-18 months. We expect consensus to
shift to a view that the primary supply deficit will deteriorate into the 2040s, leading to higher contract
prices. Conversion and SWU prices, in contrast, are likely to return to below-incentive price levels
as new capacity comes onstream.

Existing Nuclear Generation: Utility Priorities Drive The Cycle
Existing nuclear plants exhibit fundamentally different economics from new construction projects.
Existing reactors have already absorbed most construction risk and capital costs. Operators must
cover fuel, maintenance, labor, regulatory compliance, waste management, and sustaining capital
costs. This makes existing generation one of the cleaner equity exposures to rising power demand,
geopolitical shifts and capacity scarcity. If data centers, electrification, and industrial load growth raise
power prices or capacity payments, nuclear generators can benefit without taking full new-build risk.

Fuel Fabrication: Narrower Economics, Higher Qualification Barriers
 
Fuel Fabrication: Less Volatility, Deeper Moat: Fuel fabrication represents the final front-end step
before fuel enters the reactor core. Fabricators assemble enriched uranium into reactor-specific fuel
assembly designs, which means qualification, reliability, and regulatory approval matter as much
as manufacturing capacity. Fuel assemblies are designed for particular reactor types and require
extensive testing and regulatory approval.
This means fabrication capacity is not perfectly fungible across reactor fleets and process economics
depend on access to specific reactor fleets, licensing approvals, contract duration, and customer
willingness to diversify vendors.
Fabrication offers lower commodity price convexity than conversion or enrichment but potentially
more stable margins through long-term qualified supply agreements. The segment faces moderate
barriers to entry due to regulatory qualification timelines but lacks the technical scarcity of conversion
or enrichment.
Advanced reactor fuels including, tri-structural isotropic (TRISO) for high-temperature reactors,
metallic fuels for fast reactors, and accident-tolerant fuel designs for light water reactors represent
potential growth opportunities.
These advanced fuel forms require new fabrication capabilities, regulatory qualification pathways,
and customer adoption, creating option value for companies that can navigate the development and
commercialization process.
Equity moves typically react to qualified supplier status for large operating fleets with long-term supply
agreements, reactor-specific qualification status, contract duration, margin stability across contract
vintages, and mix of exposure to large installed reactor fleets.

Spent Fuel Reprocessing Another Bottleneck
 
Spent fuel reprocessing is the chemical separation of used reactor fuel into its reusable components
— recovered uranium and plutonium, which can be re-fabricated into fresh MOX (mixed-oxide) fuel —
and the residual high-level waste bound for final disposal. Stretching finite uranium supply and giving
utilities a route to draw down accumulating spent-fuel inventories could be important in the coming
cycle.
The MOX fuel and spent nuclear fuel (SNF) reprocessing markets are structurally undersupplied
relative to accelerating demand. The MOX market sits at ~$135m in 2025, while the SNF reprocessing
market is ~$1.2bn. The broader recycling ecosystem is a $2.5-$3.0bn market. Capacity is concentrated
in a few players — Orano (France) and JNFL's J-MOX facility (Japan) dominate fabrication, with limited
new entrants due to high capital intensity, long permitting timelines, and jurisdictional regulatory
complexity.
Pricing is bespoke rather than commoditized, driven by bilateral negotiated contracts that reflect
volume, purity, regulatory burden, and policy/subsidy overlays. Demand is being pulled forward by
nuclear's global resurgence, SMR deployment momentum, surplus plutonium management mandates,

At a high level, our approach is to value reactor vendors and small modular reactor developers as
option portfolios rather than utilities with visible earnings, with option delta rising only as project
milestones convert announcements into backlog and ultimately completed projects that demonstrate
repeatability. This leads to four recommendations:
•
First, bottleneck exposure measures whether a company controls capacity that customers
cannot quickly replace. Companies that operate assets with technical scarcity, regulatory
qualification barriers, or geographic concentration are more likely to have rising margins and
ROIC. Conversion and enrichment capacity scores substantially higher than exploration-stage
uranium acreage or unproven reactor designs.
•
Second, contract visibility focuses on revenue backed by long-term binding contracts,
power purchase agreements, regulated cost recovery, or customer-funded capacity additions.
Companies likely sustain higher, and less volatile, multiples when revenue streams extend
multiple years with creditworthy counterparties and limited volume or price risk.
•
Policy alignment hinges on whether government policy directly funds assets, reduces financing
costs through loan guarantees, or protects customer base through import restrictions or strategic
procurement. Companies can see a lower WACC when policy support is enacted through binding
appropriations or regulatory decisions rather than aspirational statements. United States uranium
reserve purchases, Department of Energy HALEU production contracts, Inflation Reduction Act
tax credits, and European Union energy security programs materially shift return profiles for
aligned companies.
•
Fourth, execution burden measures valuation dependence on permitting approvals, first-of-a-
kind engineering, large construction risk, or unproven manufacturing cost curves. This screen
favors fuel-cycle scarcity assets and existing nuclear generation over speculative new-build
stories unless reactor developers demonstrate binding orders, regulatory progress, and credible
financing arrangements.

large infrastructure projects such as nuclear power plants. We expect similar dynamics to play out in
other countries. In our view, the revenue crisis creates incentives for proactive policy. Shifting from
fuel and registration taxes to per-mile or per-ride levies maintains transportation revenue streams.
Robot taxation or elimination of the depreciation subsidy asymmetry could rebalance incentives.
More fundamentally, the consumer spending multiplier effect losses—totaling $3.5-5.5tn annually by
2054 as income concentrates among low-propensity-to-spend households—argue for redistribution
mechanisms that sustain aggregate demand. How quickly governments adapt to these pressures
likely varies by country.
System-Level Productivity Gains Justify Near-Term Disruption
The all-in social value of automation—incorporating quality-of-life savings, real estate repurposing,
reduced accident externalities, and environmental health benefits—reaches 94-105% annual returns
on capital for automation and 65-400% for robotaxis depending on indirect value capture.
These system-level benefits (i.e. ~$790bn/yr) exceed what private investors can capture, yet they
accrue reliably to society. In our base case, which we believe is a good proxy for the economic benefits
policy-makers anticipate, capital efficiency improvements from reduced waste total ~$220bn annually,
non-market time value contributes ~$85bn, and environmental health benefits add ~$485bn.

escalating sea-level-rise exposure.

Appendix 1: The KISS Guide To The Nuclear History
Origins and the First Boom (1940s–1960s)
The nuclear fuel industry was born out of the Manhattan Project in the 1940s, funded almost entirely by
governments (primarily the U.S., U.K., Soviet Union, and later France and Canada). Early uranium mining
was concentrated in the Congo, Canada (Elliot Lake), the U.S. (Colorado Plateau), and the Soviet bloc. In
the 1950s, President Eisenhower's "Atoms for Peace" initiative opened civilian nuclear power, and the
first commercial reactors came online (Calder Hall in the UK in 1956, Shippingport in the U.S. in 1957).
Governments effectively subsidized the entire value chain—mining, milling, conversion, enrichment,
and reactor construction—because the military and civilian programs were intertwined. Estimated
global capital investment during this period is difficult to disaggregate from defense spending but likely
totaled $50–100bn in nominal terms across the 1940s–1960s, overwhelmingly public capital.

The Build-Out Era (1970s–1980s)
The 1973 oil crisis triggered the largest nuclear buildout in history. France launched its Messmer
Plan, building ~56 reactors in roughly two decades; the U.S., Japan, Germany, South Korea, and the
USSR followed suit. Uranium prices spiked to record highs (~ $40/lb in 1978, or ~$180/lb in today's
dollars), triggering a mining boom in Australia, Niger, Namibia, and Saskatchewan (the Athabasca
Basin's high-grade discoveries permanently changed the economics). Downstream, conversion and
enrichment capacity expanded, though largely under state-owned entities (USEC, Eurodif, Urenco,
Tenex/Rosatom). Global capex on nuclear plants during the 1970s–1980s is estimated at $600bn–
$1trn in nominal terms, with an additional ~$30–50bn going into mining and downstream fuel-cycle
facilities. Then came Three Mile Island (1979) and Chernobyl (1986), leading to reduced public support,
cost overruns became endemic, and dozens of planned reactors were canceled. Many U.S. utilities
took write downs.
The Long Winter and Brief Renaissance (1990s–2010s)
The 1990s were characterized by consolidation and cheap uranium. The "Megatons to Megawatts"
program (1993–2013) converted 500 tonnes of Soviet weapons-grade uranium into reactor fuel,
flooding the market and keeping spot prices between $10–20/lb for most of the decade. Mining capex
collapsed; many mines were mothballed. The 2000s brought a "nuclear renaissance" narrative driven
by climate concerns, and uranium spiked to ~$140/lb in 2007 before crashing. Then Fukushima (2011)
triggered Germany's Energiewende, Japan's fleet shutdown, and a second nuclear winter. Cameco,
Kazatomprom, and Orano took major impairments; Westinghouse's bankruptcy in 2017 (dragging
down Toshiba) marked the low point. Aggregate capex for 1990–2015 across new build was ~
$400–600bn nominal, heavily concentrated in China, South Korea, and Russia, with ~$40–60bn in the
upstream fuel cycle.
 
A New Cycle (2020s)
The current cycle was sparked by the combination of energy security, climate concerns, tightening
supply-and-demand balances and Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which altered fuel security priorities
across North America and Europe. Governments optimizing for supply resilience rather than pure
cost economics have renewed interest in domestic fuel cycle infrastructure. This cycle appears more
structural than previous ones given by utility re-contracting needs against accumulated uncovered
requirements, artificial intelligence and data center power demand, and sustained policy support.
However, the cycle remains early with contracting volumes running ~45% below the replacement rate
needed to maintain adequate coverage.

Lessons Shape Dynamics
Companies across the value chain have internalized several hard lessons that shape strategy today.
•
Supply Discipline: Kazatomprom and Cameco explicitly manage production to price signals
rather than chase volume—a stark contrast to the 1970s.
•
Avoid fixed-price EPC contracts: The cost overruns at Vogtle and V.C. Summer, plus Areva's
Olkiluoto losses, mean vendors now push cost-plus or risk-shared structures, and utilities are wary
of first-of-a-kind builds.
•
Standardization and scale: The industry is pivoting toward SMRs (small modular reactors) and
repeat-design fleets (as France and South Korea historically did well) to escape the "every plant
is a prototype" problem.
•
Long-term contracting: Utilities and miners now favor multi-year contracts over spot exposure,
reducing but not eliminating cyclicality.
•
Geopolitical concentration is a risk: ~40% of enrichment services and significant conversion
capacity sit with Rosatom, and post-2022 Western utilities are scrambling to diversify, creating
investment opportunities in Western conversion/enrichment expansion (Urenco, Orano, Centrus,
ConverDyn).
For investors, the baseline assumptions are, in our view, as follows: (1) Upstream miners offer high beta
to uranium prices but are widely viewed as tending to destroy capital across cycles—time the cycle and
embrace volatility; (2) Conversion and enrichment are structurally more attractive with better ROIC so
long as policy focuses on de-Russification of Western supply chains; (3) Reactor construction remains
much higher risk until SMRs prove otherwise; (4) Existing operating reactors (via utility equities) can
generate strong cash flow but face political/regulatory volatility; (5) Quants likely discount a perpetual
risk of a lost decade given how much the Fukushima accident disrupted the industry's supply/demand
balance.

Explanation of Jefferies Ratings
Buy - Describes securities that we expect to provide a total return (price appreciation plus yield) of 15% or more within a 12-month period.
Hold - Describes securities that we expect to provide a total return (price appreciation plus yield) of plus 15% or minus 10% within a 12-month period.
Underperform - Describes securities that we expect to provide a total return (price appreciation plus yield) of minus 10% or less within a 12-month period.
The expected total return (price appreciation plus yield) for Buy rated securities with an average security price consistently below $10 is 20% or more within a 12-
month period as these companies are typically more volatile than the overall stock market. For Hold rated securities with an average security price consistently
below $10, the expected total return (price appreciation plus yield) is plus or minus 20% within a 12-month period. For Underperform rated securities with an
average security price consistently below $10, the expected total return (price appreciation plus yield) is minus 20% or less within a 12-month period.

policies.
CS - Coverage Suspended. Jefferies has suspended coverage of this company.
NC - Not covered. Jefferies does not cover this company.
Restricted - Describes issuers where, in conjunction with Jefferies engagement in certain transactions, company policy or applicable securities regulations
prohibit certain types of communications, including investment recommendations.
Monitor - Describes securities whose company fundamentals and financials are being monitored, and for which no financial projections or opinions on the
investment merits of the company are provided.

fundamental analysis and may take into account other factors such as analyst conviction, differentiated analysis, a favorable risk/reward ratio and investment
themes that Jefferies analysts are recommending. Jefferies Franchise Picks will include only Buy rated stocks and the number can vary depending on analyst
recommendations for inclusion. Stocks will be added as new opportunities arise and removed when the reason for inclusion changes, the stock has met its
desired return, if it is no longer rated Buy and/or if it triggers a stop loss. Stocks having 120 day volatility in the bottom quartile of S&P stocks will continue to
have a 15% stop loss, and the remainder will have a 20% stop. Franchise Picks are not intended to represent a recommended portfolio of stocks and is not sector
