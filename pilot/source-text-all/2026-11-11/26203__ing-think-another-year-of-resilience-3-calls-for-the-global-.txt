Another year of resilience: 3 calls for
the global economy in 2027
Despite a long list of risks, 2027 could deliver a surprisingly benign global
backdrop of resilient growth, easing inflation and steady central banks.
But political disruption in Europe and mounting fiscal pressures could
quickly challenge that outlook
A brighter outlook for the global economy
ING's base call: All will be good
The list of potential downside risks is long, but the global economy will enjoy another
year of resilience. In the US, the Trump administration is riding into the sunset without
disruptive fireworks, sparing the global economy new trade tensions and new wars.
Instead, the investment boom in AI might slow down somewhat, but infrastructure
investments and AI adoption will support growth in many economies as energy prices
return to more normal levels. At the same time, the current inflation wave will lose
momentum, allowing central banks to keep their powder dry.
The mix of modest growth, slowing inflation and policy rates being only mildly
restrictive could surprise many. Admittedly, this is a scenario accompanied by a lot of

Our risky call: Transatlantic divide makes an unexpected turn
Over the last few years, Europe has often been left flabbergasted by political developments in
the US and the increasingly hostile transatlantic relationship. The US midterm results may now
raise hopes that those ties can gradually recover.
Yet just as the US appears to be stepping back from populist and hegemonic policies, Europe
could be moving in the opposite direction. With elections next year in France, Italy, Poland,
Spain, and maybe even Germany, Europe risks ushering in a new period of political disruption,
with stronger eurosceptic forces weighing on sentiment and investment and complicating
further European integration. 
One upside of Donald Trump’s second term in office was that his politics and policies created a
common adversary and brought Europe closer together. The irony of 2027 could be that, just
as Trump enters his lame-duck period, political divisions within Europe begin to do the damage
to European unity that external pressure from Washington never could.
Our bold call: Global fiscal dominance
Our bold call for the global economy sees many governments struggling with the pressures
of higher interest rates, rising debt levels and the need to increase investments, while ensuring
social or societal peace.
We have seen the script before; it’s one that leads to spiralling government debt and even
higher interest rates. There are two main ways out of this situation. The first would be a
globally coordinated debt restructuring, which is even too bold for this call. The second is
imposing direct or indirect pressures on major central banks to restart asset purchase
programmes and keep bond yields under control, with the option to still write off government
bonds once they are on central banks’ balance sheets.
