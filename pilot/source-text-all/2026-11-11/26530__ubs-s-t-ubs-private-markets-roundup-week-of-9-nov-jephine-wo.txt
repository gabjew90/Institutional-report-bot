Jephine Wong

• Frontier AI is putting some very big numbers on the board. OpenAI reached $10B of quarterly revenue, while
Thinking Machines Lab, Mercor and others continue attracting billions of dollars. Capital remains abundant,
but as the numbers get bigger, the evidence needed to support them is getting bigger too.
• Post-midterms, a big question for AI is becoming rather physical: what happens to all the infrastructure it still
needs? Power queues are growing, permitting is getting harder and financing more selective. UBS Karl
Keirstead's industry checks are less bearish than some of the headlines, suggesting the build-out continues,
but increasingly favors companies with the power, capital, customers and credibility to actually deliver it.
• Either way, applied AI is charging ahead. Kalepa is going deep into insurance underwriting, Otter into
workplace workflows and Primordial Labs into controlling military robots. The frontier keeps making
intelligence more general, while startups keep finding increasingly specific places to make money from it.
• And when it comes to healthcare, private markets seem to suggest almost nothing is sacred. UBS Raahul
Ramakrishnan maps $61.8B across 1,107 companies trying to reshape everything from drug discovery and
programmable biology to diagnostics, medtech and care delivery, implying a $500–550B 2030 TAM. Plenty of
technology is getting funded. The question is which of it ultimately produces better outcomes and economics
worth owning.

What caught our eye this week
Frontier AI: The Numbers Are Getting Bigger. The Bar Is Getting Higher.
• OpenAI generated $10B of revenue in the September quarter, nearly 50% more than the prior quarter and
almost three times the year-ago level. It also reportedly completed an initial $11B financing at a $1.25T
valuation, below earlier reported ambitions and short of the full amount originally sought. Demand for frontier
AI is translating into revenue quickly. Investor appetite appears strong too, just demonstrating some
selectiveness around price.
(see here & here)
• That being said, there is still plenty of capital willing to bet on the frontier. Thinking Machines Lab was
reportedly nearing a $5B financing at a $40B pre-money valuation, while Mercor raised $750M at a $20B
valuation supplying expert human data and training infrastructure to AI companies. TSMC also approved a
potential investment of up to $500M in OpenAI preferred shares.(see here, here & here)
• OpenAI's $10B quarter gives investors more evidence to support the scale of the AI opportunity. Its latest
financing also shows that strong growth does not necessarily translate into every valuation or funding target
being met. With the cost of staying competitive still rising across compute, infrastructure, talent and training,
the debate remains open around how much returns all of that capital can ultimately generate. (see here)
AI Infrastructure: Where the AI Build-Out Is Starting to Hit Reality
• AI needs a lot more infrastructure. Whether enough of it can be built quickly and economically is front of mind
for some investors... Norway's Statnett temporarily suspended new data-center grid reservations after
reaching 4.2GW of reserved capacity, with another 9.1GW in the connection queue and 18GW of inquiries. In
Wyoming, Google acquired Crusoe's data-center site and plans to invest $17B into the project after cost and
timing concerns emerged around the development. Power may be scarce, but turning it into finished capacity
is proving difficult too. (see here, here & here)
• And last week’s US midterms adds more questions: UBS Karl Keirstead spoke with more than 15 industry
contacts across the data-center ecosystem and found broad agreement that permitting and community
opposition have become greater risks. But the checks were more sanguine about what happens from here.
Tighter approvals should fall disproportionately on new developments and 2028-plus capacity, while projects
already permitted or under construction appear better protected. Development can also move. Louisiana,
West Virginia and Missouri were among the potential beneficiaries cited if developers face greater resistance
elsewhere. Larger hyperscalers enter that environment with capital, secured customers and established
relationships that many speculative projects lack. (see here)
• Financing is getting more selective too, although UBS's checks suggest the market remains open. Lenders
increasingly want investment-grade offtakers, stronger contracts and greater confidence around execution,
while ING was reportedly marketing roughly $500M of data-center loans last week. The physical risks keep
accumulating as well: permitting, labor, transformers, breakers and utility coordination can all delay go-live
dates, and one UBS industry check suggested six months can materially change a project's IRR. One estimate
in the report puts roughly two-thirds of current US projects as having encountered some opposition. Plenty of
AI infrastructure may still get built. The constraints increasingly favor companies that already have the
customers, capital, relationships and ability to actually deliver it. (see here, here & here)
Applied AI: Intelligence Is General. Making It Useful Is Not.
• And while Frontier labs are spending billions making AI more capable, let us not forget a growing group of
startups deciding that narrower might be the way to go. Kalepa raised $75M at a $470M valuation after
reportedly quadrupling usage of its AI-powered insurance platform. Otter is expanding beyond transcription
into meeting preparation, live coaching and follow-through, while adding support for more than 30 languages.

4
Both are moving deeper into established workflows where domain knowledge, context and integration can
matter as much as access to the underlying model. (see here & here)
• The same idea is moving into the physical world: Primordial Labs raised an $18M Series A for Anura, its
natural-language command interface reportedly operating across more than a dozen crewed and uncrewed
platforms. Europe’s Alta Ares is attacking the hardware side, securing six of 16 lots in a NATO counter-drone
framework and targeting production of more than 1,000 interceptors a month by 2027, although framework
selection does not guarantee those purchase volumes. As the number of autonomous systems grows, the
opportunity expands beyond who builds them to the software used to control and coordinate them. (see here &
here)
• Training those machines has created another market… PitchBook identified $2.1B of investment across 55
physical-AI data deals, with companies collecting the human demonstrations and real-world data robots need
to learn. But successful deployment changes the equation: robots in the field begin producing more of their
own data. The frontier labs are trying to make intelligence increasingly general. Everyone downstream is
finding places where being specific still pays, whether that means understanding an underwriting workflow,
controlling a fleet of robots or owning the data needed to make them better. (see here)
Healthcare: Private Capital Is Betting Almost Nothing Is Off Limits
• Private capital is funding attempts to change almost every layer of healthcare. UBS Raahul Ramakrishnan
initiates coverage of the Private Healthcare space – he estimates more than $130B has been invested across
US and Canadian private healthcare companies in 2026 YTD. UBS then looked at $61.8B invested across
1,107 Series A-C financings since 2024 and mapped them into 20 themes spanning programmable biology,
computational discovery and diagnostics, medtech and AI-native care delivery. Based on the valuations

5
investors have already placed on those companies, UBS calculates an implied $500–550B 2030 TAM using
assumptions around target returns, valuation multiples and private-market share capture. (see here)
• The capital is spread very differently across segments: Pharma and biotech attracted $30.2B across 367
financings and an estimated $200–225B 2030 TAM, including $7.75B invested in AI-enabled therapeutic
discovery and $8.66B in precision immunology and immune reprogramming. Medtech received $11.8B across
317 financings, while HealthTech & Services attracted $14.6B across 426 and represents an estimated
$125–150B 2030 opportunity. Life Sciences Tools & Diagnostics received a much smaller $5.1B despite an
estimated $50–75B TAM. Where the most capital has gone and where the best risk-adjusted opportunity
ultimately sits may turn out to be two different things.
• The harder questions start after the technology works. AI already improves parts of drug discovery, but the
clinical test is whether it repeatedly produces better medicines. Continuous monitoring can generate more
patient data, but somebody still has to act on the signal and improve an outcome. Healthcare automation can
process more transactions, but savings depend on whether it removes work across the system rather than
pushing it somewhere else. Robotics has to turn placements into procedures and recurring economics. UBS
reaches similar questions across very different areas: who owns the workflow, what proprietary data
compounds with use, whether customers get a measurable outcome and how much of the resulting economics
the private company retains.
• Oura's announced $625M acquisition of Counsel Health puts some of this into practice. Oura already collects
longitudinal biometric data from its members. Counsel Health adds AI-powered medical guidance and access
to physicians across preventive, acute and chronic care. Oura reported approximately $1.75B of FY2026
revenue, up 92% YoY, so the move into care is happening from a sizable base. Private capital is betting that
almost every layer of healthcare can be rebuilt, better. The next five years should show which companies can
turn better science, data and technology into outcomes people will pay for and economics worth owning. (see
here & here)
