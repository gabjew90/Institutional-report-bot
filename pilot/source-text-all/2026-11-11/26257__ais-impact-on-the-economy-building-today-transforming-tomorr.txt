•	
Artificial Intelligence (AI) capital spending is supporting U.S. gross domestic product (GDP) 
growth, with estimates ranging from 14 percent of GDP growth to as much as 44 percent. 
•	
The estimated contribution to GDP growth varies from as low as 30 basis points (bps) to as much 
as 120bps is material relative to the 2.5 percent growth in 1Q26, 2.2 percent in 2Q26 and 3.4 percent 
in 3Q26.
•	
The wealth effect has been a factor supporting strong consumption growth that has outpaced 
income growth with an estimated 40bps of additional GDP uplift, approximately, coming mostly 
from strong stock market gains. 
•	
Price pressures from AI add a meaningful 45bps, approximately, to Core personal consumption 
expenditures (PCE) inflation, but not as impactful as the sheer scale of the buildout suggests. 
•	
Near term productivity and employment effects are negligible and longer term impacts are uncertain.

AI’s clearest near-term macroeconomic effects are emerging through the demand that its 
capital investments create and strong equity market gains in a narrow subset of securities 
that has supported consumption growth through positive wealth effects. Spending on 
data centers, computing equipment, and power infrastructure supports activity before any 
widespread productivity improvement is established. The research reviewed here suggests 
that this investment cycle can lift growth and selected prices, even as its net employment 
effects remain uncertain. 
GDP Growth Reflects Domestic Investment and Wealth Effects
AI-related spending has likely contributed 30 to 120bps to GDP growth over the  
year-to-date period.1 The lower estimate reflects direct AI spending, only, while 
the upper threshold throws in spending on software and AI-adjacent categories of 
expenditure. Corporate capital expenditure (capex) announcements cannot be translated 
dollar-for-dollar into U.S. GDP and the AI investment cycle has put upwards pressure  
on nominal prices of many electronics and metals, due to the infrastructure buildout, 
which show up in inflation and must be adjusted for in reporting of real GDP growth. 
The lower end of the AI-contribution-to -GDP-growth calculation isolates the direct effects 
of AI spending and reflects technicalities associated with calculating GDP. The estimate 
ignores net exports and may underreport AI-related growth by up to 30bps, meaning the 
total potential contribution at the low end of estimates can be closer to 60bps. 
Some large chip manufacturing companies produce chips abroad and sell them from 
those foreign locations, effectively underreporting the benefit of selling the chips in 
the domestic GDP figure.2 At the upper end of the estimated 120bps range, broader 
software spending, information processing equipment and non-residential structures 
are entirely included, which may be biased too high, as some of that spend is not directly 
AI-related. Software-related investments have been growing robustly for a decade. 
Another distinction is the contribution that AI is having from AI-related infrastructure 
development for data centers as contrasted with AI-technology-driven adoption. We can 
further attempt to decompose that growth from infrastructure at approximately  
80 percent of that growth and about 20 percent from adoption of the technology.3
AI-related gains in the stock market have also supported consumption growth, which is 
the largest component of GDP at about 67 percent. In fact, about 70 percent of the S&P 
500 Index gains over the past year stem from the outperformance of AI infrastructure 
providers.4 These wealth effects increase the propensity to spend, with contributions to 
real GDP growth ranging from 30bps to 40bps.5 
The wealth effect has supported consumption growth of 3.8 percent in both 2Q26 and 
3Q26 real GDP q/q SAAR (seasonally adjusted annualized rate), which exceeded the  
3.3 percent growth in average hourly earnings over the same period, indicating that the 
upper-end consumer (the upper end of the K in the K-shaped economy) has been driving 
consumption growth. The wealth effect is a reversible channel, and consumers may have a 
higher elasticity to changing consumption on the downside of equity markets than to the 
upside, posing some risk to slower growth if stock market gains reverse.

AI-related inflation pressures on core PCE inflation are concentrated and difficult to 
measure, but are smaller relative to inflationary impacts from the Iran conflict and tariffs. 
AI infrastructure has increased prices for computer memory, computing equipment, 
electricity, and industrial materials. Collectively, these channels currently add about 
45bps to year-over-year (Y/Y) core PCE inflation. Roughly half of the current contribution 
reflects genuine price pressure, with the rest reflecting measurement issues, including 
the weighting of software and accessories and incomplete software quality adjustments.6 
Though estimates vary, some research suggests that the inflationary impacts from the Iran 
conflict and tariffs account for about 100bp of inflation contribution, without which core 
PCE would be nearer the Federal Reserve’s (Fed’s) 2 percent target.7
Employment Gains and Hiring Pressures Coexist
In the near-term, the AI buildout has supported labor demand in construction, while 
AI adoption changes have reduced demand for some types of office and service work. 
Employment in construction categories exposed to data centers rose by roughly 317,000 
additional jobs above the trendline since 2022.8 There has been modest evidence of 
employment pressure in marketing, graphic design, customer service, and some back-office 
technology occupations.9
Additionally, there are hiring headwinds concentrated among entry-level workers and 
occupations at greater risk of displacement. Evidence, to date, supports an uneven change in 
labor demand; but there is no broad support for definitive widespread net job destruction.10 
Productivity Gains Depend on Adoption and Work Reorganization
AI adoption is expanding but remains uneven. AI adoption rates are increasing with 
approximately 24 percent of U.S. establishments utilizing AI in some capacity, with larger 
establishments leading adoption.11 The Fed cautions that adoption surveys do not measure 
how intensively firms use AI technology, limiting what headline adoption rates can tell us 
about either labor substitution or productivity.
Research from the Fed’s July monitoring note12 finds evidence of task-level productivity 
gains, but limited signs of an aggregate transformation. Integration costs and bottlenecks 
can prevent faster task completion from translating into proportional gains in output. 
Potential Long-Term Effects of AI Growth
Longer-term employment effects noted in a May 2026 study13 from the Federal Reserve 
Bank of Chicago described meaningful but not apocalyptic job loss in rapid AI scenarios. 
The study found that workers in routine, codifiable tasks might face higher displacement 
risk, while high-skill workers who can leverage AI may see rising productivity and wages.

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) described a divergence between a 
low‑productivity path, where AI is adopted slowly, mainly as labor‑saving technology 
rather than creating new value, as differentiated from a high‑productivity path, where 
AI enables workers to raise output and living standards.14 The authors see the low-
productivity path yielding higher inequality and weaker overall gains, and the high-
productivity path encouraging higher degrees of work augmentation that generate larger 
advancements and rewards.
In the same study, researchers favored targeted measures like worker retraining and 
transition support. Without strong retraining and safety nets, inequality and regional 
dislocation could rise even if total employment remains high. 
AI could raise GDP and productivity growth, although the timing and size of any gains 
remain uncertain. Under a scenario of rapid AI progress, the Chicago Fed study sees 
longer-term U.S. GDP growth potentially rising to around 4 percent, which is historically 
elevated and above trend.
Views offered by Fed Governor Michael Barr in 2026 held that productivity benefits 
could raise potential growth-of-output to a speed at which the economy can grow 
without generating inflation, while noting that AI-related capital investment could 
pressure rates higher.
Breckinridge Perspective
Breckinridge’s investment process includes a top-down process led by our Investment 
Committee and a bottom-up fundamental research component anchored by our Research 
team. The AI capital-spending boom is likely to require continued focus from both angles.
AI-related spending is certain to influence our top-down outlook, as AI spending influences 
growth, inflation, employment, and productivity. Each of these factors will shape Fed policy 
and inform our decisions about duration, sector allocations, and curve positioning. 
The scale and breadth of the AI buildout is likely to impact many fixed income sectors, to a 
degree, from a credit perspective. Breckinridge anticipates it will selectively participate in 
bond offerings where we have high confidence in the fundamental strength of issuers and 
believe valuations are attractive, on a risk-adjusted basis.
Breckinridge’s High Quality Dividend Strategy may also be well positioned in the event 
of AI-driven stock market correction. The HQD strategy has a structural underweight to 
hyperscalers and the information/technology sectors, more broadly. Stock gains have been 
concentrated amongst a narrow subset of companies, largely in these sectors. HQD should 
exhibit lower volatility and more consistent returns in an AI correction scenario.15
