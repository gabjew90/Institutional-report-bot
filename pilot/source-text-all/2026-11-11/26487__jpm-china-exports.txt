Exports eased from September’s high but were still strong, with mixed tech 
performance and softer auto and consumer goods shipments.

•
Higher commodity and tech prices supported nominal trade values.
•
The trade surplus narrowed, but the full-year total remains on track to exceed 
last year’s level with a resilient outlook. 
China’s export growth eased from September’s high level in October, while 
import growth picked up further, supported by stronger energy and technology 
imports. Price effects remained supportive of both export and import values in 
these sectors. Global commodity prices rallied, while unit prices for integrated 
circuits and mobile phones continued to rise, consistent with the strong October 
PPI, although consumer goods prices remained sluggish.  
Exports moderated, with mixed product performance. Headline nominal 
exports declined 1.0%m/m sa in October following a 6.5% jump in September, 
leaving annual growth at a still-strong 35.2%oya (market consensus: 36.3%; JPM: 
32.4%). The pullback was concentrated in the EU (-3.5%m/m sa), Russia (-5.0%) 
and Africa (-6.5%), partly offset by a solid increase in exports to Latin America 
(+4.2%). Product details were similarly mixed. ADP exports rose a further 5.4%m/
m sa, while integrated circuits (-2.7%) and mobile phones (-2.8%) declined, and 
auto exports edged down 1.1% after September’s 8.0% surge. Low-end consumer-
goods exports fell 4.1%, partly unwinding the previous month’s holiday-related 
restocking boost. 
Imports surged, driven by energy and tech. Imports jumped by 6.5%m/m sa, 
with annual growth reaching 41.6%oya, beating expectations by a wide margin 
(market consensus: 33.9%oya, JPM: 30.1%). The upside was mainly driven by a 
rebound in energy imports volume amid higher global commodity prices during 
the month. Crude oil import volumes rose 4.6%m/m sa, while refined oil product 
imports surged 74.0%. Coal imports also increased a strong 9.9%m/m sa, 
supported by winter restocking demand. Meanwhile, firm domestic AI and tech 
supply-chain demand continued to support imports of high-tech (+2.5%) products, 
which recorded a 12th consecutive month of sequential expansion. By origin, gains 
were led by shipments from Australia, the US and Japan, while imports from the 
EU and Brazil eased following strong readings in September. 
As a result, trade surplus narrowed. The trade surplus moderated to US$105.3bn 
in October after a record-setting September ($133.5bn surplus). Year-to-date 
surplus reached US$1043bn, putting the full-year surplus on track for around 
$1.3tr, versus $1.2tr last year.

Looking ahead, we think auto exports should remain a support to China’s export growth 
through year-end. Chinese automakers may accelerate hybrid EV deliveries to the EU 
before the proposed import restrictions take effect, while growing protectionist pressure in 
other markets could reinforce the incentive to bring forward shipments. The near-term 
support should still come from higher shipment volumes rather than prices, given weak 
domestic demand and excess production. Crude oil import volumes will likely recover 
further as Middle Eastern supply and pricing normalizes and refiners rebuild depleted 
inventories. However, the rebound should remain moderate, with weak domestic fuel 
demand, regulated product pricing and export volume. 
Moreover, global tech prices should remain supportive of both China’s nominal exports 
and imports, supported by sustained AI-related demand. However, since tech prices create 
a two-sided positive value effect, the net impact on the trade balance remains uncertain. 
Meanwhile, a gradual moderation in commodity prices should weigh on further 
headline import growth. The decline in global oil prices, alongside broadly stable prices for 
other major commodities, should weaken the positive price effect on nominal imports, 
suggesting that headline import growth may soften even if underlying import volumes remain 
relatively firm.
The US midterm elections add some uncertainty to China’s trade outlook. Tighter potential 
restrictions on US data-center development could slow AI investment and potentially weigh 
on China’s exports of data-center-related equipment, including power equipment, batteries 
and optical components. However, the impact is likely to be more medium-term, as much of 
the 2027 capex pipeline is already under construction or funded. Moreover, China’s direct 
exposure to US AI demand is relatively limited compared with Korea and Taiwan, which 
should contain the broader impact on Chinese exports.
Overall, China’s trade outlook remains resilient. Supportive global demand, manufacturing 
competitiveness, export-market diversification and the ongoing AI investment cycle should 
continue to underpin exports despite rising trade barriers and CNY appreciation pressure. 
Imports should also remain firm, supported by solid manufacturing production and supply-
chain needs, although this does not necessarily signal a broad recovery in domestic demand.
