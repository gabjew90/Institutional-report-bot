Global Asset Allocation
Risk appetite deteriorated in October amid a raft of disappointing earnings results, ongoing tensions in the Middle East, and an 
explicit tightening bias at global central banks. Notably, some uninspiring earnings results in the megacap technology space stoked 
concerns over the sustainability of the Artificial Intelligence (AI) trade – and particularly regarding whether the revenues generated 
from the massive spending binge will be enough to justify the sky-high valuations. At the same time, escalating hostilities in the 
Middle East pushed oil prices markedly higher and revived inflation concerns – adding to investors’ unease last month. 
Global equity markets (-1.7%) declined in October. The S&P 500 
(-0.9%) retreated – though there was a profound rotation beneath 
the headlines. With losses concentrated in some heavyweight 
megacap stocks, the equal-weighed index gained +5.0%. The 
Nasdaq (-6.7%) posted its worst month since March 2025, while 
the “Magnificent-7”group of stocks declined -4.7%. By contrast, the 
S&P/TSX (+1.1%) managed to post positive results - thanks to solid 
performance in the energy (+8.7%) and financials (+3.2%) sectors 
that offset a challenging month for gold stocks (-13.1%). The MSCI 
EAFE fell -0.8% - while the MSCI gauge of emerging market stocks 
(-8.1%) underperformed by a wide margin – reflecting steep losses 
in technology stocks that pulled South Korea’s Kospi Index (an AI 
bellwether) down 15%.
Fixed income markets staged a rebound in October, with 
deteriorating investor sentiment restoring the safe-haven status 
of bonds and taking priority over the inflationary impulse from 
rising oil prices. Global bond yields pushed broadly lower. While 
the Federal Reserve, European Central Bank, and Bank of Japan all 
left policy unchanged (albeit with a tightening bias) – the Bank of 
Canada raised rates and left the door open to further hikes from 
here. The Bloomberg US Aggregate Bond Index rose 2.4% – while 
the FTSE Canada Bond Universe gained 1.5%. 
The US dollar (DXY) edged modestly (+0.4%) higher even despite 
the latest slide in treasury yields. By contrast, the euro (-1.5%) 
plummeted to a 17-month low amid lingering concerns regarding 
fiscal risks in France. With the euro carrying more than half the 
weighting in the DXY index, that kept the dollar well supported last 
month. The Canadian dollar (+1.8%) was the notable outperformer 
versus its global peers after the Bank of Canada raised its policy rate 
– while the latest spike in oil prices added to the loonie’s strength 
last month.
Finally, WTI crude oil (+15.2%) soared higher amid fresh turbulence 
in the Middle East that stoked fears of continued supply disruptions. 
Gold (-6.2%) failed to provide its usual safe-haven cushion and 
tumbled below $4000/oz as the global tech rout prompted 
liquidation from the precious metal to cover losses elsewhere.

Economic Overview
Canada
The Bank of Canada raised interest rates for the first time in more 
than three years as officials looked to guard against the threat that 
persistently elevated energy prices spread more broadly into inflation. 
While there has been little evidence of spillover thus far, officials are 
clearly envisioning pass-through ramping up in the coming years. 
Notably, the Bank’s inflation projections for both 2026 and 2027 were 
upgraded substantially. Governor Macklem stressed that officials are no 
longer content letting the upside inflation risks build – which leaves less 
scope for policy accommodation. Moreover, the third quarter Business 
Outlook Survey and Canadian Survey of Consumer Expectations showed 
that inflation expectations firmed for both businesses and consumers – 
which likely solidified the case for the hawkish pivot. Regarding the path 
forward, officials noted “there could be a need to raise the policy rate 
further” but will “proceed carefully” given uncertainties stemming from 
the Middle East conflict and US-Canada trade relations.
United States
The Federal Reserve delivered a “hawkish hold” in October and left 
the benchmark rate unchanged in a target range of 3.75% to 4.0%. 
In the accompanying statement, the description of the economy’s 
performance was upgraded to “strong” – reflecting resilient domestic 
demand, strong productivity growth, and robust business spending. 
In the press conference, Chair Warsh argued that underlying economic 
resilience gives officials the leeway to focus squarely on the price 
stability side of the dual mandate – particularly given that inflation 
has been running well-above the 2% target for nearly six years. Indeed, 
the Federal Reserve’s preferred gauge of underlying inflation – the core 
personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index – accelerated to 
3.1% y/y in September. On a three-month annualized basis, the core 
PCE jumped to 2.9%. The key takeaway is that with oil prices remaining 
elevated and demand pressures strong, rates will continue to grind 
higher into 2027. Higher for longer interest rates remains the path of 
least resistance for the Federal Reserve.
China
China’s economy rebounded in the third quarter, with gross domestic 
product (GDP) nudging back into Beijing’s target range at 4.5% y/y (the 
government’s annual goal is 4.5% to 5.0%). The rebound was powered 
by factory production and the artificial intelligence super cycle – while 
there was little evidence of that strength feeding into domestic demand. 
The September activity data highlighted the divergence between a 
struggling consumer and the booming export sector. While industrial 
production expanded at a six-month high of 5.5% y/y, retail sales 
saw a far more modest pickup to 1.1% y/y and the contraction in fixed 
investment accelerated to -7.2% y/y. Meanwhile, survey data showed 
that the economy lost some traction at the start of the fourth quarter. 
Both the manufacturing and non-manufacturing purchasing managers’ 
indices (PMI) fell into contraction terrain (below 50) in October. That 
underscores the challenges facing policymakers as they seek to sustain 
an economic recovery to ensure this year’s growth target is met.

Economic Scenarios
Main Scenario | Sustained Inflation
Probability 45 %
In this high probability scenario, upside risks to inflation and downside risks to growth subside in response to the peace accord between 
the United States and Iran. As the Strait of Hormuz reopens and energy flows through the vital waterway resume, oil prices tumble back 
towards pre-conflict levels. Still, there are some notable forces that risk keeping inflation sustainably elevated – including the potential 
for re-escalation in the Middle East conflict and a subsequent spike in oil prices, the Artificial Intelligence (AI) boom and associated 
supply-side stress, and the prospect for higher tariffs from the US administration. Should long-term inflation expectations remain 
reasonably anchored – central banks would likely lean against tightening aggressively and opt to live with elevated inflation – with 
little appetite to induce a hard economic landing. Still, a higher for longer rate environment suggests economic conditions would shift 
downwards to a trend-like pace.
Scenario 2 | Stagflation
Probability 30 %
Upside inflation risks continue to loom large on the back of an erratic geopolitical and trade environment. However, the trajectory for 
the global economy hinges on the reaction function of the Federal Reserve and other major central banks. In this scenario, a spike in 
long-term inflation expectations would undoubtedly force central banks’ hand and monetary policy would be tightened in response. 
Policymakers prioritize bringing inflation under control, with the economy assuming the collateral damage from an aggressive 
tightening event. That macroeconomic outcome tilts the scales towards an environment of “stagflation” – with upside risks to inflation 
coming up against downside risks to growth.
Scenario 3 | Recession
Probability 15 %
While the worst-case outcomes stemming from a tumultuous geopolitical and trade backdrop have been averted (reducing the risk of 
recession), the potential for flare-ups remain a tail risk. On the geopolitical front, a worst-case outcome involves a protracted conflict 
that leads to sustained disruption to global energy flows. A long-lasting supply shock and a spike in oil prices risks morphing into a 
demand-shock and a contraction in economic growth. Trade dynamics also pose risks to the outlook. While negotiations and trade 
deals brought some relative stability – there’s still little clarity on the policy direction and the long-run tariff regime. The US continues 
its threats to impose new tariffs on its trading peers, the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) negotiations are dragging 
on, and US-China trade relations remain vulnerable. These developments may pave the way for a prolonged period of trade policy 
uncertainty – with the potential to dampen global economic activity.
Scenario 4 | Productivity Boom
Probability 10 %
In this optimistic scenario, massive investment in Artificial Intelligence (AI) boosts productivity (and accordingly, growth) without the 
associated upside pressures on inflation - an environment of “disinflationary growth” that allows the Federal Reserve to resume its 
easing campaign. Indeed, a productivity shock is unanimously dovish for the Federal Reserve. While inflation declines and reinforces the 
case for easier monetary policy, a reduction in labour supply keeps the unemployment rate relatively contained. Moreover, productivity 
gains spread more broadly around the world, with buoyant tech investment and higher stock prices buttressing global growth.
