 
"Still The Best AI Hedge": Goldman Doubles Down 
On The Yen After It Barely Flinched On The 
Nasdaq's Worst Day Since COVID 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, NOV 10, 2026 - 07:25 PM 
What good is insurance that doesn't pay when the house is on fire? 
That, more or less, is what Goldman's FX clients have been asking since last Thursday, when 
the Nasdaq suffered its worst day since Covid, tech hedge funds round-tripped their entire 2026 
gain... and the one currency trade that Goldman (and half of Wall Street) had pitched as the AI 
crash hedge was up all of half a percent. 
Regular readers know the trade well. Back in October we laid it out in "Hedging The AI Bust: 
Goldman FICC Says Look To Tokyo As France Goes "Orbital"" (Oct 22): if AI capex 
expectations crack, stocks and yields fall together, the US-Japan rate gap narrows, and the 
most undervalued currency in the G10 finally catches a bid. Sell dollars, buy yen, sleep at night. 
Or so the thinking went. Then the bust actually showed up, and Tokyo... shrugged.  
So in the bank's latest Global FX Trader note (available to pro subs), Goldman's FX strategy 
team led by Kamakshya Trivedi takes on the doubters directly, under a section title that leaves 
little to the imagination: "JPY: Still the best AI hedge."Bloomberg picked up the note on Monday 
evening, but the wire story skips the best parts, which sit in the full report and in a companion 
piece the same team rushed out in the middle of Thursday's liquidation. 
The Dog That Didn't Bark 
First, the scene of the crime. By midday last Thursday the Nasdaq 100 was down about 5%, 
and according to Goldman's cross-asset sales desk the dollar index was... flat. The euro was 
unchanged, only the Swedish krona and the Aussie had a notable move (down 60bps against 
the greenback), and the yen was the best performer in the G10 with a gain of a whopping 
50bps. 
 

 
How unusual is a dollar that does nothing while tech implodes? As Goldman's Noah Gurney told 
clients while the tape was still bleeding: 
... with the DXY flat (largely driven by an unchanged EUR), this will be only the 3rd time since 
2008 that the broad USD closes within 20bps despite such a down move in risk. If we look back 
to the dot com era this phenomenon was a lot more common (occurring 6 times in 00/01). 
In other words, it would be only the third time since 2008 that the broad dollar finished within 
20bps of unchanged on a risk-off day that size. In 2000 and 2001, by contrast, it happened six 
times. Translation: the last time the dollar routinely ignored a crashing Nasdaq, the Nasdaq was 
in the process of losing three quarters of its value. Nothing says "healthy market" like FX price 
action last seen in the dot com bust. 
The frustration had been building for weeks. In the companion FX Research note published that 
same morning (also available to pro subs), Goldman's Karen Reichgott Fishman concedes that 
investors have had every right to be disappointed. Between October 9 and November 4, the 
Nasdaq dropped roughly 8%, the S&P about 1.5%, 2Y yields fell some 20bps and 10Y yields 
about 35bps. That is the textbook regime for yen strength, and yet USD/JPY went precisely 
nowhere. 
 
Goldman's Defense: Look At The Days, Not The Month 
So is the hedge broken? Goldman says no, and offers three reasons. 
Here is the core of the bank's defense: 
 

 
First, since early October, USD/JPY has fallen on average on the days when the Nasdaq 
underperformed the S&P 500, including November 5. The declines were even bigger on days 
when the underperformance was greater than 1%. USD/JPY’s 1-month rolling correlation with 
daily returns in AI-related stocks is also at its highest of the year. 
Put differently, the monthly picture hides what happens day to day. Since early October, on the 
days when the Nasdaq lagged the S&P, USD/JPY fell by about 0.12% on average, and on the 
days when tech lagged by more than 1%, the average drop more than doubled to roughly 
0.26%. Small numbers, sure, but the sign is right. 
 
The more persuasive exhibit is the correlation. The 1-month rolling correlation between daily 
moves in USD/JPY and Goldman's AI basket (GSTMTAIP) has ripped from around -0.3 in early 
October to roughly +0.6, the highest of the year.The previous peak, around +0.5 in July, ended 
abruptly when Tokyo decided to make its own weather with a record yen intervention. 
 

 
 
Why, then, did spot refuse to move over the full month? Fishman's answer is that the yen had 
already taken its medicine early. During the European-led rates selloff from late September into 
early October (the one we covered in "Sayonara, OATs"), the yen held up far better than its 
usual beta to yields would imply, since European fiscal risk premium rarely matters much for 
JPY. When yields then came back down, partly because that same premium faded, the yen had 
little to give back and little to gain. The chart below, which tracks the cumulative move in 
USD/JPY against what Goldman's model implied, shows the gap opening in late September and 
closing by early November. 
 
 

 
Reasons two and three are really one argument: the selloff so far hasn't been the kind the yen 
hedges. The violence of Thursday's drop, and Friday's relief, looks to Goldman more like 
positioning than a fundamental downgrade to AI expectations (a view shared by the bank's 
delta-one desk, as we discussed in "1998 Or 2000?"). And outside of that one day, the daily 
moves in tech have been muted, FX vol across the majors is still on the floor, and Goldman's 
growth factors for the US, Europe and China say markets haven't really marked down global 
growth. No growth scare, no vol, no yen. 
 
The Hedge Works... Once You've Already Lost 
Here is where it gets a little awkward for anyone who paid up for yen calls three weeks ago. 
Goldman's portfolio strategists believe the AI trade is entering a new phase rather than ending, 
which means a real rerating of aggregate AI expectations, the thing that would actually sink 
USD/JPY, is probably not imminent. Fishman's conclusion deserves to be read in full: 
The aggregate sell-offs have been relatively modest so far, and the Yen often most clearly works 
as a hedge when the global growth outlook is deteriorating rapidly—which should be even more 
true when energy prices are rising and markets are generally pricing in more hawkish Fed 
 

 
policy. But if markets were to more significantly challenge the broader AI story, we see no clear 
evidence in recent price action to doubt that the Yen should outperform. 
Read that again. The yen works best when the growth outlook is falling apart quickly. Today, 
energy prices are elevated, payrolls just printed +165K and the market is pricing a Fed hike in 
December. That's about as far from a yen-friendly backdrop as it gets, and it's why the same 
Goldman report still sees upside risks for the broad dollar in the near term. 
The bank is similarly relaxed about what set off the rout in the first place. Yes, the Democratic 
sweep at the state level will mean tighter data center rules and a slower buildout in key states 
(see "What's Next For Data Centers After The Blue Tsunami"). But Goldman's equity analysts 
see plenty of momentum from projects already under construction, and its policy analysts note 
that data centers rank low among voters' priorities (only 7% rank them as a top issue), so the 
moratoria should fade. For the dollar, that makes AI a longer-term valuation risk and no reason 
to call a change in trend. 
Nevertheless, we note that while the Democratic wins at the state and local level are likely to 
have significant implications for data center regulation, and the pace of development could slow 
in key states, our equity analysts still see strong momentum even based on projects that are 
already under construction, and our policy analysts stress that data centers are low on the list of 
voters’ priorities, and they expect the issue will fade somewhat so the moratoria should be 
relatively short-lived. In short, we still see this more as a longer-term risk to the Dollar given 
current valuations rather than a change in the trend. 
The numbers in the back of the report make the same point with less diplomacy. Goldman has 
USD/JPY at 158 in three months, 155 in six and 150 in twelve, little different from the forwards 
until a year out. Its 12-month return table shows a 5.1% spot gain for yen longs that shrinks to 
2.2% after 2.8% of negative carry. And all of that for a currency the bank's own GSDEER model 
says is 40% undervalued, with fair value at 95. 
 

 
 
So the cheapest currency in the developed world is forecast to earn you 2% a year, unless 
everything breaks. It's a hedge, in other words, in the purest sense: you are paid for being right 
about a crash, and for nothing else. 
Meanwhile, In Tokyo 
There is a domestic angle too, and regular readers will recognize it. Ever since Tokyo began 
nudging its pension giants homeward in July, the market has been waiting for GPIF, the world's 
largest pension fund, to formally cut its foreign allocation, which would be the mother of all 
repatriation flows and the logical next chapter of the story we first told in "Focus Turns To Japan 
As Source Of Relentless Treasury Selling" (Sep 30). 
Last week's quarterly results (through end-September) offered something for everyone. GPIF 
actively bought domestic bonds, lifting their share to 26.5%. It also actively bought foreign 
bonds, keeping that share near 24% when valuation changes alone would have dragged it down 
to roughly 23%. Goldman's read: there's little to learn here. The domestic bond share has risen 
from fiscal Q1 to Q2 in each of the past six years, and the earliest realistic date for an official 
strategy change is still spring 2027. 
 

 
 
The private sector, as usual, is less patient than the bureaucrats. According to Bloomberg, 
Japan's latest balance of payments data showed Japanese investors dumped a net ¥1.65 trillion 
of US sovereign bonds in September, more than triple August's ¥514 billion, and sold the most 
French bonds in six months. The October portfolio flow data, which Goldman flagged as the 
next thing to watch, is due Tuesday. 
And then there is the BOJ. The Summary of Opinions from the October 29-30 meeting, 
published Tuesday morning in Tokyo, showed many board members see the need for a 
near-term hike, with one arguing the bank should move without being overly cautious, 
Bloomberg reports. That puts December firmly in play. Of course, we have seen this movie: the 
last time the BOJ hiked, in September, the yen plunged, because the Fed (and now the ECB 
and BoE) keeps out-hawking it. USD/JPY was last seen right around 158, or exactly where 
Goldman expects it to be three months from now. 
A Hedge That Needs A Bigger Crash 
Goldman's own summary of the trade: 
Overall, we think short USD/JPY should work more clearly as a hedge—it just likely requires a 
bigger and broader shift in underlying expectations than seen so far. 
 

 
Well, that's one way to put it. Another is that the yen will protect you from the AI bust right after 
the AI bust has become a growth scare, a vol event and a Fed pivot all at once, by which point 
your tech book is probably down a lot more than 5%. 
We would add two things.  
●​ First, the market is in no mood to wait: the Nikkei, which fell 6% into a bear market on 
Friday, ripped back above 60,000 on Tuesday after oil plunged on US-Iran diplomacy 
and a new batch of AI models revived the trade, per Bloomberg. Nobody buys fire 
insurance during a parade.  
●​ Second, Goldman is hardly alone in noticing that the dollar itself is now an AI derivative. 
Apollo's Torsten Slok has warned of the dollar's hidden dependence on the AI trade, 
since most of the record foreign money in US stocks is unhedged, and Capital 
Economics made the same point on Monday, per Bloomberg: a bigger AI bust means a 
weaker dollar. 
Which is why we think the doubters have the timing right and the conclusion wrong. With AI now 
a debt bubble too, the next leg lower won't stay a positioning accident for long. When it comes, 
the yen bid will be the easy part. The hard part will be explaining to the Treasury market why its 
largest foreign creditor is suddenly bringing all that money home.
