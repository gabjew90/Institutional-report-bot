FX Daily: Bearish yield curve flattening
lifts the dollar
The dollar is trading close to the year's highs after US inflation came in
hot for the third month in a row and the Fed's Chris Waller threatened to
vote for a 50bp rate hike in December. Bearish yield curve flattening is
typically good for the dollar and bad for procyclical currencies, as the
monetary brakes are applied. Expect the dollar to hold gains today
We're seeing the US yield curve bear flatten, and that's a dollar positive
USD: Big moves in short-dated rates
The Fed's Chris Waller tossed a hawkish grenade into money markets early yesterday with his
threat to vote for a 50bp rate hike at the 9 December FOMC meeting. He conditioned this on
the October CPI data coming in hot for the third month in a row, which it subsequently did. The
comments and the CPI print contributed to a 20bp rise in short-dated Treasury yields and a
7bp flattening in the 2-10 year curve. The case is increasingly building for the Fed to take rates
into restrictive territory to slow aggregate demand and bring inflation back to target. That has
been the stated aim of Fed Chair Kevin Warsh since his first FOMC press conference in June.
While Waller's suggestion of a 50bp hike in December may be too aggressive for many of the
FOMC voters who favour gradual tightening, his comments keep alive the prospect that the Fed
needs to hike three more times.

What does this mean for FX? Bearish flattening of the US yield curve as the Fed takes rates into
restrictive territory is normally good for the dollar against activity currencies. Here, activity
currencies underperform as the world's most important central bank wants to slow aggregate
demand. We did kind of see that price action yesterday, with the Norwegian krone and
Swedish krona underperforming in a strong dollar environment, although the Swiss franc this
year always suffers in a higher interest rate environment. That was the case yesterday.
Given what could be an accelerating US labour market, core inflation well above target and a
Fed ready to pull the trigger again, we expect the dollar to stay strong and explore the top end
of trading ranges over the coming weeks.
Don't expect too much from today's session given that it is the US Veterans Day public holiday.
But Friday's October PPI data could deliver the latest instalment of hot price data and keep the
dollar bid.
DXY should remain near recent highs and could be retesting big resistance at 102.85/103.00
sooner rather than later.
Chris Turner
EUR: Holding support at 1.11
A high technical level at 1.1110 is currently providing the floor for EUR/USD. This marks the
50% retracement of the 25 March to 26 Jan rally and is probably a good level for speculative
accounts to book some profits on short EUR/USD positions. But the case for a bounce from
this support region is weak. Yes, it looks very likely that the ECB will match a Fed hike in
December, but the case for an extended ECB tightening cycle is far weaker than that of the
Fed. And despite seasonal trends normally being bullish for EUR/USD in December, we instead
think a hawkish Fed will be the dominant theme into year-end and EUR/USD will at some stage
want to press the lower end of a 1.10-1.15 range.
The eurozone calendar is quiet today, but a few more ECB speakers will likely prove supportive
of a hike to 2.75% at the 17 December meeting. Elsewhere, today's national strike in Spain will
only remind investors of the political challenges Europe faces later this year and into next. 
Expect EUR/USD to trade well within a 1.1140-1.1200 range today.
Chris Turner
JPY: Manufacturing sector on a roll
The latest Reuters Tankan business sentiment index showed a broad upswing in Japanese
manufacturing sentiment, which hit a five-year high. Semiconductor and data centre demand
drove sentiment and is a reminder of Japan's role as a supplier to the AI data centre build-out.

Locally, Japanese corporate profits have remained strong and, given labour shortages in
Japan, wage increases also continue to come in on the strong side. All of the above can feed
into the Bank of Japan's view that inflation is now stable at 2.00% and its accommodative
monetary policy can be reined in.
The broadly strong dollar means that USD/JPY could easily push through resistance at 159, but
we increasingly think investors will be looking to acquire cheap yen should USD/JPY push above
160 again.

CEE: It’s all about gas
Hungarian inflation remains benign despite headline CPI rising to 2.0% in October. The increase
was largely driven by fuel prices, while core inflation remained subdued at 2.1%, with no
convincing signs of second-round effects. The bigger test will come during the winter repricing
season as higher energy costs feed through. We expect inflation to reach 3.0% in December
and average 3.4% in 2027. For the National Bank of Hungary, inflation already provides room
for easing, but global risks remain the key constraint. We expect rates to stay at 5.50%
through year-end, with further cuts more likely in the second half of 2027.
In the Czech Republic, headline inflation was confirmed at 2.4%, while core inflation remained
unchanged at 3.1%, one-tenth of a percentage point below the Czech National Bank's forecast.
This does not significantly alter the outlook for the CNB. Both our projections and those of the
CNB suggest that headline inflation will continue to rise, heading toward 3% in December and
approaching 4% in January and February due to new-year repricing and base effect.
Subsequently, inflation should begin to decline, and the CNB is expected to maintain a wait-
and-see approach following its November rate hike. However, the balance of risks for inflation
is tilted to the upside, and an upside surprise would, in our view, lead to further tightening.
The rates market has faced heightened volatility in recent days. Yesterday, we began with
lower rates due to a decline in energy prices, but ended higher following a spike in USD rates
after inflation data. The key takeaways are, most notably, a renewed strengthening of the US
dollar and a continued narrowing of rate differentials in the CEE region. Furthermore, the
market should soon begin to differentiate between the relief seen in oil prices and that in gas
prices. While oil has experienced a significant decline, gas has struggled to move lower.
Arguably, gas has greater importance to the CEE region, particularly ahead of winter.
Consequently, despite the temporary relief in the market driven by falling oil prices, we view
the global environment as remaining challenging for the CEE complex, and we maintain our
view of higher levels in EUR-cross pairs, as we have noted in previous days.
Frantisek Taborsky
