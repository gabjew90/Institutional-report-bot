Foreword
It’s become increasingly clear that the economic 
and market environment of the 2020s has 
made hedge fund strategies more additive in 
portfolios. In the 2010s, underlying weakness in 
demand led to a very particular market regime: 
one in which low inflation, low interest rates 
and low correlations made achieving portfolio 
diversification relatively straightforward, while low 
volatility and dispersion left a narrower alpha 
opportunity set for active managers. This was 
a challenging environment for hedge funds. 
Today’s world looks different. Supply constraints, 
geopolitical fragmentation and a succession of 
shocks, now including the historic AI buildout, 
have created bottlenecks across labor, energy 
and materials. The result has been more 
frequent inflation shocks, increasingly stretched 
government fiscal positions and greater 
pressure on monetary policy frameworks.
For investors, these shifts have mattered. 
Stock-bond correlations have become less 
stable, while volatility and dispersion have risen. 
With persistent supply constraints keeping 
inflation elevated and uncertain, long-duration 
government bonds are no longer providing the 
ballast investors had come to expect — increasing 
the diversification value of market-neutral 
strategies such as hedge funds. Greater market 
volatility and dispersion have also expanded the 
opportunity set for long-short investors 
seeking alpha.
This year has reinforced these themes. Although 
the Middle East energy shock has perhaps 
proved less severe than some experts had feared, 
it offered a clear example of a negative supply 
shock at work, raising inflation risks and creating 
meaningful differentiation across countries 
and sectors. Adding to these pressures, 
intensifying competition for capital has created 
another supply bottleneck — that for capital itself. 
With heavy government borrowing competing 
with the enormous capital requirements of the AI 
buildout, a higher cost of capital is favoring 
strong balance sheets while putting greater 
pressure on leveraged borrowers.
AI itself is also reshaping markets, not as a 
single trade but as an increasingly important 
source of dispersion. Beneath resilient headline 
indices, performance has diverged sharply 
across the AI value chain. Beyond its impact on 
markets, AI is transforming how we invest, 
expanding the breadth and accelerating the pace 
of ideas we can generate, test and act upon.
These forces run through the pages that follow 
and inform three themes that help explain 
why we believe hedge funds belong closer to the 
core of portfolios today: dispersion, innovation 
and differentiation.

Foreword
Dispersion is rising as the common macro backdrop of the pre-pandemic 
era gives way to diverging policy paths and market outcomes. Low 
inflation and highly accommodative monetary policy were once shared 
features across most major economies. Today, sticky inflation, diverging 
monetary policy, mounting fiscal pressures and geopolitical fragmentation 
are producing greater variation across countries and asset classes. Tom 
Becker (p. 5) examines the growing competition for capital, showing how 
government and corporate borrowing needs are rising together and why 
widening differences across countries are expanding the macro 
opportunity set. 
Innovation is expanding the toolkit investors can use to identify and 
capture these opportunities. AI, alternative data and systematic techniques 
are changing how investors generate ideas, while derivatives and platform 
capabilities are broadening how they implement them. Raffaele Savi (p. 7) 
explains why, as AI transforms how information is processed, investment 
edge doesn’t disappear but shifts. Following a sharp momentum reversal this 
year, he argues that the breadth of ideas in a portfolio has become 
increasingly important because successful ideas are being absorbed ever 
more quickly into consensus. Rick Rieder and Jacob Caplain (p. 10) show that 
extreme market moves occur far more often than a normal distribution would 
suggest, and that volatility markets can help investors hedge risk and 
generate returns across both turbulent and calmer conditions. Multi-strategy 
platforms, able to combine these capabilities and reallocate capital as 
leadership shifts across asset classes, regions and styles, are particularly well 
positioned to put these tools to work.
Differentiation, meanwhile, is becoming central to the hedge fund value 
proposition. The role of hedge funds in portfolios increasingly rests on 
accessing distinct sources of return rather than adding another form of 
market exposure. That becomes especially valuable when traditional sources 
of diversification are less dependable. If government bonds do not 
consistently offset equity drawdowns, market-neutral and other alpha-
oriented strategies can provide an alternative source of portfolio ballast. Mark 
McKenna (p. 13) describes a “corporate change supercycle,” in which returns 
are driven primarily by the outcomes of individual transactions rather than 
the direction of broader markets. And as demand for skilled managers grows, 
competition for capacity is intensifying. Diana Myint and Albert Matriotti (p. 
16) explain why some of the most valuable sourcing work takes place before a 
fund even exists.
Taken together, these themes point to a common conclusion. Today’s 
environment is not simply broadening the opportunity set for hedge funds 
but heightening the importance of the diversification they can bring. It is also 
increasing the premium on manager skill. Reflecting that, as we look toward 
2027, investment outcomes need to continue to depend less on the direction 
of markets and more on security selection, disciplined risk management and 
genuinely differentiated sources of alpha potential. That is why we believe 
hedge funds increasingly belong closer to the core of portfolios.

Markets ultimately clear at prices that match supply and demand, and the 
challenge for bond markets in 2026 has been finding enough buyers willing 
to finance this rising issuance at prevailing yields. We are positioned for 
this dynamic to continue, particularly since the debt-financed investments 
are directly supporting economic activity and, on a tactical horizon, adding to 
already rising inflation pressures. A combination of resilient growth, sticky 
inflation and abundant bond supply keeps us directionally short bonds. 
We also continue to seek out relative-value opportunities across global rates, 
favoring regions with less acute inflation challenges, more austere fiscal 
policy or more dovish central banks.
Rising IPO supply sends a different investment signal
While rising net debt issuance should continue to pressure fixed income 
markets, the increasing supply of public equities is associated with a 
different dynamic. The initial public offering (IPO) window has reopened as 
private stockholders transfer their ownership into public markets. Private 
companies tend to go public when investment opportunities are plentiful, 
financial conditions are supportive and corporate fundamentals are strong — 
all good descriptions of 2026. Rising IPO activity also tends to coincide with 
an acceleration of economy-wide profit growth, which is supportive of 
equities and can be seen in the visual to the right. We remain long directional 
equities on the back of improving top line fundamentals for companies.
Why cross-regional macro investment opportunities are rising 
The decade before the pandemic was characterized by unusually synchronized 
global markets characterized by low inflation, aggressive monetary 
accommodation and relatively limited fiscal dispersion across countries. 
The post-pandemic environment has looked very different. The last few 
years have seen the rise of persistent and sticky inflation, activist fiscal and 
industrial policies and geopolitical fragmentation. This new macro landscape 
has unsurprisingly increased the cross-country dispersion of stock and 
bond markets and also increased the macro investment opportunity set.

If 2026 has taught investors anything, it has been 
humility.
Geopolitical shocks sent energy prices sharply higher 
and rapidly rewrote expectations for inflation and rates. 
Markets reversed, equities recovered and hedge funds 
rebounded. Then leadership shifted again: crowded AI 
trades began to unwind, momentum weakened and 
fundamentals started to reassert themselves.
If you enjoy stable market narratives, 2026 has 
probably not been your favorite year.
For hedge funds, however, that is precisely what 
makes the outlook interesting.
The opportunity set is getting richer. The competition 
for it is getting better, too.
Different is good
Hedge funds don’t necessarily need markets to go 
up. Or down. Ideally, we need things to move 
differently.
There has been plenty of different in 2026, but 
also a surprising amount of the same. AI, rates 
and geopolitics have become powerful forces 
running through equities, credit, commodities 
and macro portfolios.
That creates an interesting paradox. Dispersion 
within markets can rise even as portfolios that 
look different on paper become exposed to many 
of the same underlying risks.
For us, the search for different means looking 
beyond strategy labels to find genuinely 
differentiated sources of return.
Perhaps the clearest example came from 
momentum. From March through August, 
momentum experienced its third-worst drawdown 
since 2000; an unusually severe reversal in what 
had become consensus positioning.

There is a useful lesson in that: a good idea doesn’t stop being good 
because other investors discover it. But its economics can change rather 
quickly when everybody discovers it. The objective is to be right about 
companies, not accidentally right about momentum. So we seek to hedge 
common style exposures and preserve the company-specific dispersion we 
are trying to capture.
Where are we finding opportunity?
As market leadership has rotated, the opportunities have rotated with it.
Within equities, fundamentals are reasserting themselves. As momentum 
weakened, value and quality signals strengthened. Within technology, the AI 
reversal created opportunities on both sides of the portfolio as investors began 
to distinguish between companies able to support expectations with earnings 
and balance-sheet strength and those more dependent on the broader theme. 
Financials have also offered opportunities as relative-value and sentiment 
signals differentiated among companies.
Geography is providing another dimension of differentiation. Volatility in 
Taiwan and South Korea created opportunities for stock selection, while parts 
of the Hong Kong consumer market benefited as investors rotated away from 
AI-related names.
Macro dispersion is creating opportunities beyond equities. Diverging growth, 
inflation and policy dynamics have produced opportunities across U.S. and 
European rates, currencies and commodities. Energy markets have been 
shaped by an unusual combination of geopolitics, weather, inventories and 
supply constraints.
These opportunities don’t all have to work at the same time. In fact, we’d 
rather they didn’t.
The objective isn’t to predict which source of alpha will lead next. It’s to have 
enough breadth to keep finding something different.
The competition is getting better, too
AI isn’t simply changing what we invest in. It is changing how we invest. When 
I started my career in the 1990s, cash equity trading was one of Wall Street’s 
most coveted jobs. Then algorithms fundamentally changed that business.
Today, trying to beat an algorithm tick-by-tick armed with a mouse isn’t much 
of a contest. Admirable, perhaps. But technology didn’t eliminate the human 
edge. It changed where we had to look for it.
We may be approaching a similar transition in information processing. Agents 
can already read earnings transcripts, economic releases and news almost 
instantaneously and connect that information across thousands of companies 
and markets. That doesn’t mean alpha disappears. It means the edge moves.
Fortunately, we get to use the machines too. Agentic research systems are 
already becoming part of how we work, generating hypotheses, writing code, 
testing implementations and evaluating whether an idea adds something 
differentiated to a portfolio.
A researcher might find three promising signals and focus on developing them. 
An agent has the luxury of asking: what about the fourth? Or the fortieth? It can 
keep looking because it does not get tired or bored.
The opportunity isn’t simply faster research, but broader research, exploring 
more ideas, data and markets than humans could reasonably investigate alone. 
In a world where successful ideas can quickly become consensus, that ability 
to keep searching may become increasingly valuable.

Beyond the signal
Finding an opportunity is only half the job. Good ideas rarely stay lonely for 
long. As more investors discover the same trade, positioning can build, and 
so can the risk of reversal.
That’s why we monitor crowding alongside our investment signals. It’s not 
enough to ask whether we like a trade. We also want to know who else owns it 
and how quickly that positioning could unwind.
The underlying insight may still be attractive even when the position itself has 
become too crowded.
Finding the trade is one problem. Knowing when too many people have found it 
is another.
Judgment is still the job
That brings us to 2027. As AI becomes more capable, the winning model isn’t 
humans or machines. It is knowing what each should do.
Machines can process more information, test more ideas and explore more 
possibilities. Humans still need to determine which questions matter, whether 
the answers make economic sense and when a changing world requires us to 
question what history tells us.
Eventually, everyone will have powerful AI. Simply having the technology won’t 
be the edge. The advantage will come from what surrounds the technology: 
differentiated data, breadth of research, portfolio construction, disciplined risk 
management, efficient implementation and human judgment.
Markets change. Technology changes. Successful trades attract competition. 
There is no permanent source of alpha. There is only the discipline to keep 
searching for something different.

opportunities to hedge 
and generate returns in

When a friend or relative comes back from a long 
trip, one of the first questions we often ask them is 
“how was your flight?” We doubtless value the 
experiences we have on trips — the people we meet, 
the sights we see or the food we eat. Still, a flight 
home filled with turbulence and delays certainly 
impacts our perception of the trip overall. This is all 
to say that the journey matters. It matters in travel 
and it matters in our experience of financial 
markets.
Over time, we have noticed how volatility markets 
can be an effective tool for managing the journey in 
our portfolios, particularly as markets have a 
remarkable ability to surprise us. Under a normal 
distribution, a weekly market decline of three 
standard deviations or more would be expected 
roughly once every 14 years. Since 1987, that 
translates to approximately three weeks. In practice, 
extreme market moves have occurred more 
frequently than this statistical benchmark would 
suggest. However, we saw 13 such weeks in the 
rates market and 23 in the equity market — 
respectively, close to 5x and 10x more than we 
would expect in a normally distributed market! Yet, 
because of markets’ ability to surprise and because 
of investors’ natural desire for a smoother ride, 
volatility markets give us ample opportunities to 
manage our own portfolios’ journey and to generate 
returns in more effective and efficient manners.
Buying or selling volatility is a lot like buying or 
selling insurance. It’s best to buy insurance before a 
hurricane and best to be an insurance salesperson 
at the height of the storm. We look at 10 actual 
spikes in volatility over the past 10 years. 
Proverbially, in 9 of these 10 cases, buying 
insurance 30 days before the storm worked 
for you — realized volatility ended up being higher 
than the cost, or implied volatility, that you paid. The 
inverse holds as well. In 9 of the 10 cases, you sold 
insurance at the peak for a higher level of implied 
volatility than what was realized down the road.

Volatility markets provide opportunities outside of actually volatile markets. 
In the chart below, we explore the opportunities that exist in the quietest 
periods. Here, we look at how often we see various tail shocks compared to 
how often we’d expect to see them in a normally distributed market, but with a 
twist. In periods with low volatility, we definitionally see few tail shocks when 
we measure volatility in the conventional way, by looking at opening and 
closing prices. Let’s dig a little deeper below the surface and measure 
volatility by looking at highs and lows within a trading day. When we zoom 
our lens in a bit, we see more meaningful gyrations in markets; even in these 
periods of seeming calm, we now can see more shock events. Opportunities 
exist to trade around these markets. Options give us the ability to anchor 
trades within a range, harvest premium and generate returns by delta-
hedging positions as markets move. Seemingly calm markets have deep 
undercurrents than create opportunities to generate return.
Volatility markets can help to complete our toolkit as investors. We are able 
to potentially hedge portfolios and generate alpha across asset classes. 
Volatility markets present opportunities to hedge and generate returns in 
stormy markets without needing to make ‘heroic’ calls on market bottoms. 
Volatility works for us across environments, enabling us to generate returns 
even when markets appear placid. In markets like today’s with so many 
crosscurrents across sectors, regions and asset classes, volatility is an 
invaluable tool in our portfolio construction.

M&A activity has broadened
The strength of the current M&A environment is 
evident not only in aggregate volume but also in the 
breadth and scale of announced transactions. North 
American M&A approached $1.7 trillion in the first 
half of 2026, an increase of 56% year-over-year, with 
seven of nine sectors recording double-digit growth. 
Activity has also extended across transaction sizes, 
including 283 deals valued above $1 billion and a 
record 31 mega-deals announced during the period.1
The current cycle may also have room to run. 
Goldman Sachs estimates that “pure M&A” volume 
could reach $3.8 trillion in 2026.2

billion remains particularly elevated, a segment that 
has historically helped lead broader increases in 
dealmaking.2 Taken together, the scale, breadth and 
sector participation of current activity point to a 
more durable opportunity set than headline volume 
alone would suggest. For event-driven investors, 
more transactions across industries and deal 
structures create more opportunities to deploy 
capital and, importantly, more potential for 
mispricing as markets assess the distinct risks and 
outcomes associated with each transaction.
We believe that opportunity is reflected in today’s 
spread environment. Spreads remain attractive, with 
higher base rates supporting higher absolute return 
potential, while strong deal supply relative to a 
smaller dedicated arbitrage capital base than in 
prior cycles can create opportunities for spreads to 
remain wider than underlying deal risk may warrant.
Elevated activity alone does not guarantee attractive 
returns. What matters for event-driven investors is 
the gap between transaction risk and market pricing. 
As deal flow expands across sectors, structures and 
transaction sizes, the market is increasingly required 
to evaluate a diverse set of regulatory, financing 
and execution risks. That complexity can create 
opportunities for spreads to diverge from underlying 
fundamentals, providing fertile ground for security 
selection and transaction underwriting.

The technology clock is accelerating corporate change
The technology clock and the corporate clock are moving at very different 
speeds. Technology is advancing in increasingly compressed cycles, while 
corporate strategies, operating models and business portfolios take much 
longer to change. That widening gap is creating urgency in the boardroom. As 
artificial intelligence, automation, digital infrastructure investment and new 
business models rapidly reshape competitive dynamics, boards and 
management teams are being forced to reassess strategy, capital allocation 
and long-term positioning before the market moves beyond them. The result 
is a broad-based wave of corporate activity that reaches well beyond 
technology, creating opportunities across the entire corporate landscape.
Companies have been more willing and able to act 
Another distinguishing feature of today’s environment is that companies 
have been not only more willing to transact but also better positioned to do 
so. The regulatory backdrop is among the most constructive in recent years, 
with greater clarity around antitrust enforcement, more predictable review 
processes and increased willingness to consider negotiated remedies. 
Together, these conditions have reduced regulatory uncertainty and 
meaningfully improved transaction certainty across many industries.
Capital markets remain open, and we do not believe higher interest rates will 
derail the current M&A expansion. Financing remains accessible across 
public and private markets, while healthy corporate balance sheets and 
significant financial sponsor dry powder provide buyers with ample capacity 
to transact. Although higher rates may affect transaction economics at the 
margin, strategic buyers and sponsors remain well positioned to pursue 
compelling opportunities.
The result is a powerful combination for event-driven investing: companies 
face a growing need to pursue strategic transactions and are better equipped 
with the tools to execute them. Strong deal volumes, improving transaction 
certainty, open financing markets and continued participation from both 
strategic and financial buyers are supporting a robust pipeline of announced 
transactions. For event-driven investors, this translates directly into a larger 
opportunity set and a greater number of return-generating situations across

The corporate change flywheel is creating a pipeline of opportunities
We believe today’s elevated corporate activity is being reinforced by two 
related dynamics. First, the rising cost of inaction is prompting companies 
to consider a broader range of strategic responses. Acquisitions are the 
clearest expression of this imperative to act, but companies are also 
pursuing divestitures, spin-offs, restructurings, recapitalizations, financings 
and capital markets transactions to sharpen strategic focus, fund 
investment, improve balance sheets and adapt to a rapidly changing 
competitive environment.
Second, these actions frequently create follow-on events across the corporate 
lifecycle. An acquisition may require debt or equity financing and lead to 
subsequent divestitures; a portfolio review may result in a spin-off or IPO; 
and a restructuring or recapitalization may create opportunities across 
multiple parts of the capital structure. Strategically and financially powerful 
acquisitions can also generate significant economies of scale and synergy 
opportunities to increase shareholder value. Spin-off activity is near its 
highest level in years, reflecting both companies’ growing willingness to 
pursue alternative forms of strategic change and the follow-on activity 
generated by broader transaction volumes.4
Together, these dynamics create a self-reinforcing corporate change 
flywheel: the pressures forcing companies to act are broadening the types 
of actions they pursue, while each action can generate additional catalysts. 
As corporate change becomes more frequent and varied, the opportunity set 
for event-driven investors expands across investment strategies, security 
types and stages of the corporate lifecycle.
Conclusion
We believe these forces will sustain elevated corporate activity and a lasting 
opportunity set. With the technology clock continuing to move faster than 
the corporate clock, the pressure on companies to adapt is unlikely to ease — 
supporting our view that the corporate change supercycle remains in its early 
stages.
For allocators, the relevance lies in the source of returns. Event-driven 
investing is distinguished by its focus on discrete corporate actions, with 
outcomes primarily influenced by transaction completion, regulatory 
approval, financing execution and security-specific developments. This 
can introduce an additional source of diversification within a hedge fund 
allocation, particularly alongside strategies whose returns may be more 
closely associated with recurring market, style or behavioral factors. As 
corporate activity becomes broader, more frequent and more durable, 
event-driven investing offers allocators access to a persistent and 
differentiated source of return.

Speed should be the 
consequence of

The market environment described throughout this 
Outlook is expanding the opportunity set for hedge 
funds. At the same time, renewed investor demand 
is increasing competition for a finite supply of high-
quality capacity and investment talent.
For allocators, that puts an increasing premium 
not only on selecting among today’s established 
managers, but on identifying the next generation 
of talent before it becomes widely recognized.
The hedge fund industry is continuously renewing 
itself as established businesses evolve, new strategies 
emerge and talented investors build firms of their own. 
History suggests an important lesson: today’s leaders 
cannot be assumed to be those of tomorrow. Of the 10 
largest hedge fund firms by assets 15 years ago, only 
two remain among the 10 largest today.5
Yet identifying the next generation of talent is 
difficult. Many of the most compelling managers are 
not actively seeking external capital and by the time a 
sought-after manager formally comes to market, 
much of the opportunity may already be spoken for.
The most valuable sourcing work therefore takes 
place before there is a fund in which to invest.
This is particularly important as the profile of new 
hedge fund launches has changed. Many emerging 
programs today are founded by experienced 
investors coming from sophisticated hedge fund 
platforms rather than traditional proprietary trading 
desks. These investors may arrive with impressive 
track records, but those results were often 
generated with the support of extensive technology, 
data, financing, research, risk management and 
intellectual capital. The critical question is therefore 
not simply who has performed well, but whose 
investment skill is portable.
Answering this question requires looking beyond 
headline returns. Investors need to understand 
how the alpha was generated, how responsibilities 
have evolved, how decisions were made across 
different environments and which elements of 
prior success can realistically be reproduced in an 
independent setting. 
This process becomes more powerful when it begins 
early. Establishing relationships with talented PMs 
years before they decide to launch their own shop 
can provide insights into an investor’s development, 
decision-making and adaptability that are difficult 
to capture through fund databases. The investors 
closest to a strategy are often best positioned to 
distinguish genuine differentiation from increasingly 
commoditized approaches, while robust systems can 
connect accumulated data, research and qualitative 
observations into an institutional knowledge base.
The objective is not simply to meet more managers. 
It is to build a better-informed view of investment 
talent before that talent becomes widely recognized. 
Being early, however, only creates value when it is 
paired with rigorous underwriting.

Many emerging managers are first-time entrepreneurs even if they have spent 
decades developing their investment capabilities. Building an independent 
firm introduces a new set of challenges around recruiting, operational 
infrastructure, risk management, governance and culture. Experienced 
allocators that have observed successful and unsuccessful investment

early engagement should never substitute independent judgment.
Preparation also matters. Many sought-after programs can be substantially 
allocated before launch. Beginning diligence well in advance allows 
investors to build conviction gradually and move efficiently when an 
opportunity becomes actionable.
Speed should be the consequence of preparation, not a substitute for diligence.
And conviction should not automatically determine allocation size. Strategy 
capacity, organizational risk, the durability of an investment edge and fit 
within the broader portfolio remain separate considerations. In practice, 
sourcing creates the opportunity, underwriting determines whether to 
invest and portfolio construction determines how much to invest.
The most visible benefit is access. Investors that have followed an individual 
over time may be better positioned to secure capacity before an opportunity 
becomes broadly available. But access alone is not the objective. The deeper 
advantage is a better opportunity set. A proprietary sourcing network enables 
allocators to compare existing managers not only with funds raising capital 
today, but with a broader universe of established firms, emerging managers 
and talented investors who may become actionable opportunities in the future.
That broader perspective can support better selection and provide 
differentiated information. Early relationships may also create opportunities 
for enhanced transparency, customized structures, preferential economics 
or future investment rights. These benefits can become more valuable if the 
underlying manager succeeds and capacity grows increasingly scarce.
Ultimately, however, sourcing should be judged by investment outcomes. 
The goal is not the number of managers identified or meetings conducted. It 
is whether an allocator can identify exceptional investors before they become 
widely recognized, develop sufficient conviction to act when the opportunity 
emerges and capture the benefits of being early when that assessment 
proves correct.
For investors, the ability to partner with an established allocator that has 
built these relationships and underwriting capabilities over time may 
become increasingly valuable, providing access to opportunities today while 
creating the potential for differentiated access, economics and investment
