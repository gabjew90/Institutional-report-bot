Affordability and war were key factors in Midterms 
President Trump’s platform for the 2024 election included ending inflation and restoring 
peace in Europe and Middle East and these were top issues for voters heading into the 
Midterm elections. Yet the geopolitical conflicts in Iran and Russia continue to disrupt 
energy flows and drive refined product prices higher. With domestic gasoline and diesel 
prices largely tied to global prices, individual voters this year have felt pressure at the 
pump as well as indirectly as other goods and services try to pass on rising fuels costs 
too. While the US’ positive energy trade balance and data center investments have 
helped drive stocks higher, many voters have not directly benefited. 
State results likely slow down data center buildout… 
We have been discussing AI-nflation for over a year now (see Energy policy favors AI 
over A/C), and data centers were a key factor in last week’s elections. While Federal 
elections are likely to matter for permitting reform, state results are likely more 
consequential for data centers since governors, legislatures and utility commissions 
directly influence large-load policy and commission appointments (see Midterm Election 
Outcomes). Georgia, Ohio, and Texas stood out to us as these 3 states elected governors 
that support a moratorium or pause on data centers and comprise more than 40% of the 
potential data center projects in the US. 
…Congress will need to pass Fed budget and debt ceiling… 
The Federal budget is currently funded via a continuing resolution that lasts until 
December 11 while the US is on pace to reach the debt ceiling in spring or summer 
2027. The timing of must pass legislation like the budget and debt ceiling are critical in 
determining the path of energy policy as Democrats will have much more leverage 
beginning on January 3 when the newly elected Congress convenes. Republicans are 
trying to make progress in the lame duck session on permitting reform for pipelines, 
data centers, and other large infrastructure projects. However, we could see Democrats 
push for items like the resumption of renewable energy subsidies next year when they 
will have more influence on legislation, although the President will have veto power. 
…yet POTUS still has say in geopolitics, and in turn oil   
Although oil seems to be moving out of the Middle East, the pre-war global inventory 
buffer has been shrinking. In addition, refined products are facing threats in Russia and 
the Middle East. Given the long lead time required to build new refineries, the quickest 
way to improve oil and refined product affordability is likely to end these wars, but there 
might not be a quick and easy path to peace (see Oil turns blue). Starting in January, the 
Democrat controlled Congress could pass legislation that restricts military funding or 
requires US forces to withdraw from the Middle East, but President Trump could veto 
either of these paths. In addition, existing appropriations could support US military 
operations in the Middle East. As such, President Trump can likely continue operations in 
the Middle East, which in turn could impact the price of oil and refined products.

Although US is net energy exporter, POTUS’ favorability has been falling… 
The US had one of the largest negative energy trade balances in 2008 but is now a large 
net energy exporter (Exhibit 4). It is quite amazing what the US was able to do with 
shale production during the past 20 years. The US, as a net energy exporter, realizes 
increased revenues when energy prices are higher. However, with domestic refined 
product prices largely tied to global prices, individual voters feel pressure directly at the 
pump during periods of rising fuel prices as well as indirectly as other goods and 
services try to pass on rising fuels costs too. In addition, the technology sector’s 
buildout of data centers for AI (or SI, super intelligence) has fueled domestic 
investments. President Trump’s favorability has declined as many voters have not 
directly benefited from the energy trade balance and data center investments that have 
helped drive stocks higher (Exhibit 5).

Marginal help to consumers from actions like waivers on Jones Act and taxes… 
The Jones Act requires goods moved between US ports to be carried on US flagged and 
owned ships that are constructed in the US and crewed by US citizens. The Trump 
Administration waived the Jones Act in March, and the waivers have been renewed 
through November 15. The Jones Act waivers have led to an increase in shipments of oil 
and refined products from the USGC to the East and West (Exhibit 14). These 
incremental volumes have likely provided at least some relief to consumers. In addition, 
President Trump last month signed the Executive Order “Emergency Tax Relief on Diesel 
Fuel” that allows for the tax-free sale and use of dyed diesel through the end of the 
year. Dyed diesel is regular diesel that is dyed and is typically destined for use in exempt 
activities like farming. However, states have their own dyed diesel laws, and an 
Executive Order cannot override them. For those consumers able to buy dyed diesel, the 
Federal tax savings of ~$0.24/gallon for diesel is helpful, but still only represents ~15% 
of the YoY increase in the US retail diesel prices (Exhibit 15).

passed. Meanwhile, the Trump Administration resorted to using the Defense Production

transport gas from Pennsylvania to New York and New England and was stalled in 2016 
when a water quality permit was declined.
