Resilient growth, evolving risks
As we enter 2027, the global economy remains broadly 
resilient, but the range of possible outcomes is wider 
than before. Elevated energy prices, persistent inflation 
concerns and the risk of further monetary tightening 
pose important challenges. High government debt adds 
another layer of complexity, particularly for bond mar-
kets, for which the balance between yield and risk will 
require careful judgement.
The growing influence of the middle powers will also 
reshape the geopolitical landscape. In this environ-
ment, understanding the drivers of economic growth 
and how policy decisions could influence markets, will 
be increasingly important.
Structural change creates new opportunities
Artificial intelligence (AI) remains one of the most pow-
erful forces shaping the global economy. The scale of 
investment in AI infrastructure is extraordinary, creating 
opportunities across technology, healthcare, cybersecu-
rity and energy. As spending accelerates and financing 
needs grow, investors will need to distinguish between 
companies capable of generating sustainable returns 
and those whose valuations rely on expectations that 
may prove difficult to fulfil.
Energy security will be another defining theme. Recent 
disruptions have reinforced the importance of reli-
able domestic power supplies, while rising electricity 
demand from data centres is driving investment in gen-
eration, grids and storage. 
A disciplined approach to portfolio positioning
These developments have important implications for 
portfolio construction. Private capital can play a key 
role in financing infrastructure and supporting business 
transformation, but higher financing costs and liquid-
ity constraints make manager selection and valuation 
discipline essential. Hedge fund strategies may also 
provide opportunities in a world of divergent corporate 
fundamentals.
Against this backdrop, our message to investors is 
clear: remain invested, but recognise that broad mar-
ket exposure alone may not be sufficient. We favour 
global diversification, with a deliberate emphasis on US 
growth and quality companies, while selectively seeking 
opportunities in other regions and asset classes. Man-
aging interest rates, maintaining portfolio flexibility and 
focusing on long-term objectives will be crucial.
Uncertainty will remain a feature of the investment 
environment in 2027, but for investors prepared to look 
beyond short-term headlines and focus on structural 
change, the year ahead offers meaningful opportuni-
ties to build resilient portfolios and capture long-term 
growth.

Higher energy prices, inflation pressure and

Our core views on global growth remain similar to 2026. 
We see the world economy growing at around 3.5%, 
with the US likely to remain the fastest‑growing major 
developed economy. Eurozone growth should lag the US 
but may pick up relative to 2026 as defence spending 
rises and some countries, such as Germany, adopt a 
more flexible stance on fiscal deficits. The UK is also 
expected to grow slightly faster in 2027 than in 2026, 
although uncertainty remains high because the new 
Prime Minister and his team are untested and fiscal 
headroom is limited. Japanese growth is expected to be 
similar to 2026, with the country continuing its gradual 
normalisation.
Among emerging markets, China dominates. Depend-
ing on the measure used, it is either the world’s first 
or second largest economy. Unlike much of the devel-
oped world, China is expected to grow more slowly 
in 2027 than in 2026, constrained by high debt levels 
and limited proactive policy support. Other emerging 
economies will be shaped by their own fiscal positions, 
reform agendas and exposure to energy prices and 
global demand.
For investors, the message is that growth is still there 
to be captured, but it is more uneven. Where you invest 
matters more than it did in a world of synchronised 
expansion.
3-2-1: The cone of uncertainty
What has changed in the outlook for 2027 is not the 
central growth forecast, but the width of the uncertainty 
around it. The “cone of uncertainty” – the range of plau-
sible outcomes (Figure 1) around our central view – has 
widened, and our confidence in that central view has 
diminished.
We see three main downside risks, two upside scenar-
ios and one wildcard factor that could all influence how 
2027 unfolds.
Theme 1
An investor starting 2026 with perfect foresight about the war  
in the Middle East, the sharp rise in energy prices and  
the dramatic shift in central bank expectations from rate cuts 
to rate rises might reasonably have expected a very difficult 
year for the global economy. Yet growth has been remarkably 
resilient across most major economies, and this resilience 
underpins our central expectation that it will continue into 2027.

Two positive scenarios 
We consider two factors that could contribute positively 
to growth in 2027.
1. A Middle East deal that sticks – A comprehensive and 
credible agreement between Iran and the US could lead 
to a sharp retreat in oil and gas prices, bolstering con-
fidence and freeing up spending for households and 
companies. This would be particularly helpful for coun-
tries that are net energy importers and could be rein-
forced if central banks responded to a more favourable 
inflation outlook by easing policy sooner. 
2. The wealth effect finally shows up – The strength of 
global markets in recent years has surprised many. This 
is reflected in healthy household balance sheets. For 
example, from end-June 2025 to end-June 2026 (the lat-
est official data), US household net worth increased by 
about $20 trillion. Separately, Oliver Wyman estimates 
household financial wealth will increase by $158 trillion 
by 2030 (Figure 3), equivalent to a little over $30 trillion 
per year.3 
There is a boost to consumption associated with such 
an increase – known as the wealth effect – that can 
have a beneficial impact on growth. A relatively mod-
est 3% to 5% wealth effect on consumption would 
imply a boost to the US economy of between $0.6 and 
$1.0 trillion and an uplift of $0.9 to $1.5 trillion per 
annum to the global economy. 
For investors, this is a reminder that staying invested in 
growth assets can have macro consequences, not just 
portfolio ones.

One wildcard: ballots, budgets and bouts of volatility
Next year brings several elections around the world, 
creating conditions for higher market volatility.
One of the crucial elections in 2027 will be in France, 
where Marine Le Pen’s far-right National Rally party 
stands a reasonable chance of winning. It is too early 
to tell what policies the National Rally will follow and 
how they might impact the already-precarious French 
budget situation. However, it is noteworthy that since 
Giorgia Meloni became Prime Minister of Italy in 2022, 
she has adopted a more moderate and conciliatory 
tone than suggested by the pre-election rhetoric. 
Meloni is now the longest-serving Italian Prime Minis-
ter since the Second World War and faces re-election 
in 2027.
Other elections, including in Poland and Argentina, 
could also generate localised uncertainty, with poten-
tial implications for Europe, and, in Argentina’s case, 
for the UK. For diversified investors, the key is to recog-
nise that elections can move markets in the short term 
but rarely change long‑term return drivers. The risk is 
more about the reaction to the event and whether any 
significant policy changes are likely as a result of it.
Three negative scenarios 
1. Energy starts to matter – So far, higher energy prices 
have neither fed through meaningfully to core inflation 
nor significantly affected activity. However, the longer 
the war persists and the higher prices move, the greater 
the risk of a more profound impact. Companies may 
be able to absorb higher energy input costs temporar-
ily, provided they are not too large. If costs rise by too 
much, for too long, companies will be forced to raise 
prices to protect profit margins, increasing the risk that 
higher energy costs spill over into broader inflation.
2. Central banks overshoot – Central banking is always 
difficult, not least because of long and variable lags 
between the enactment of policy and its effects. It may 
only become clear after the event whether policy rates 
were set at the right level. If central banks respond too 
aggressively to headline inflation, they could raise rates 
to a point at which growth slows meaningfully. Higher 
borrowing costs would weigh on consumer spending 
and could further weaken housing markets that are 
already sluggish in parts of the world. This is particu-
larly relevant in economies where many households 
must refinance their mortgages at higher rates. 
For example, it is estimated that roughly 800,000 homes 
in the UK currently facing a mortgage rate of 3% or less 
will need to remortgage in 2027.1 There has also been 
a sharp rise in housing-related expenditures in the EU 
recently, a part of which is related to higher mortgage 
costs.
In contrast, 30-year fixed-rate mortgages are common 
in the US, implying that the impact of higher borrowing 
costs will feed through only slowly for existing home 
owners. Nonetheless, higher mortgage costs on new 
loans have deterred buyers from entering the market 
and reduced transaction volumes, something that may 
drag on activity more broadly in the future.

Our view for 2027 
We believe the global growth outlook for 2027 is much 
the same as for 2026: steady growth globally, led by the 
US, but with heightened risks. 
The negatives could include another rise in energy 
prices, an overtightening of monetary policy and an 
AI-related shock or dislocation. The positives could 
come from a rapid resolution to the Middle East con-
flict and a strong wealth effect from continued gains in 
equity markets. 
Several high-profile elections will take place in 2027, so 
elevated market volatility is likely in the weeks before 
them.
Ultimately, the outlook will depend on how these com-
peting forces unfold, with the balance of risks and 
opportunities likely to drive both market direction and 
volatility through 2027.
For you as an investor, the message from Theme 1 is to 
stay globally diversified with a deliberate tilt towards 
the US. It will be important to manage interest rate and 
energy risk and remain invested while using volatility 
to help position portfolios. The points below translate 
this into concrete portfolio actions for the year ahead.
Action for investors
Stay global, lean to the US. 
Maintain broad global equity 
exposure but consider a modest 
overweight to US equities, which 
are expected to remain the 
growth leader among developed 
markets.
Manage interest rate and 
energy risk. Use a mix of 
shorter‑duration bonds and 
inflation‑linked bonds or quality 
dividend‑paying equities to 
reduce sensitivity to potential 
rate shocks and energy price 
spikes. 
Stay invested, manage risk 
actively. Avoid panic selling on 
headlines, but do not be passive 
about risk. Use volatility to 
rebalance, upgrade quality and 
keep your portfolio aligned with 
your long‑term objectives and 
risk tolerance.

The pattern is strikingly similar across countries. The 
energy shock has raised headline inflation, but eco-
nomic activity has proved surprisingly resilient in most 
major economies. This combination matters for mone-
tary policy. Stronger activity sustains demand, making it 
easier for firms to pass higher energy costs on to con-
sumers and increasing the risk that these costs eventu-
ally spread to wages and other prices. At the same time, 
strong activity reduces the risk that tighter monetary 
policy will trigger a recession.
Have markets over-reacted? 
Markets have responded to the energy shock by sharply 
raising expected policy rates. The question is whether 
they have gone too far. While further tightening may be 
needed, there is still little evidence that higher energy 
prices are spreading to wages and underlying inflation. 
The absence so far of substantial second‑round effects 
suggests that markets may be pricing in more tighten-
ing than central banks will ultimately deliver.
Theme 2
Since tensions in the Gulf erupted at the end of February 2026, 
the global monetary policy outlook has changed. The surge in 
energy prices has pushed headline inflation higher across the 
major economies, while non-energy inflation has been broadly 
stable. The question at the start of 2026 was: when will central 
banks cut rates? Looking towards 2027, the more relevant 
question now is: how much further will rates have to rise and 
is there a risk of markets over-reacting?

Our views on the major central banks are as follows:
Federal Reserve: 
The US starts from the most difficult inflation posi-
tion, with inflation already well above target and the 
economy and labour market still resilient. The Federal 
Reserve (Fed) has raised rates, and a further increase 
seems likely, but recent signs of a softer labour mar-
ket argue against a sustained tightening cycle. Unless 
higher energy costs begin spreading to wages and 
underlying inflation, one or two more increases fol-
lowed by an extended pause seems the most likely 
outcome.
European Central Bank: 
The European Central Bank (ECB) has already raised 
rates twice since the energy shock, but there is still lit-
tle evidence that higher energy prices are spreading 
to underlying inflation and wage growth remains con-
tained. The resilience of the economy makes further 
tightening less costly, but not necessarily more urgent. 
Unless second-round effects emerge, the ECB may have 
little reason to raise rates much further, suggesting that 
markets may be anticipating more tightening than what 
is actually delivered.
A more significant development for the ECB is President 
Lagarde's impending exit on 7 February 2027. At the 
same time, the terms of Chief Economist Philip Lane and 
Executive Board member Isabel Schnabel will come to 
an end. Therefore, the ECB is to face significant changes 
in senior leadership, bringing with it some uncertainty 
regarding the ECB's future reaction function.
Bank of England: 
The UK faces a similar inflation problem, but a softer 
labour market gives the Bank of England more rea-
son to proceed cautiously. Wage growth has slowed, 
unemployment has increased, the Bank Rate is already 
restrictive, and the economy is less resilient than 
those of the US and euro area. Further tightening may 
be needed if second-round effects emerge, but a sus-
tained tightening cycle seems unlikely.
Bank of Japan: 
Japan is at a different stage of the monetary cycle. 
Growth is resilient, the labour market is tight, wages 
are rising rapidly and inflation is expected to remain 
above the Bank of Japan (BoJ) 2% target. With the pol-
icy rate at 1.25%, monetary policy remains accommoda-
tive, leaving room for further normalisation, potentially 
towards 2% in the first half of 2027, although concerns 
about growth could slow the process.
Swiss National Bank: 
Switzerland remains the main exception because infla-
tion is just below the 1% midpoint of the Swiss National 
Bank's (SNB) price stability range and there is little 
evidence of second-round effects. The economy has 
remained healthy, the franc has weakened and mon-
etary policy is accommodative. The SNB can afford to 
wait, but when it eventually moves, an increase looks 
more likely than a cut: zero increasingly looks like the 
trough of the current interest rate cycle.
Reserve Bank of Australia:
The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) raised rates five 
times in 2026, bringing the cash rate to 4.85%. Inflation 
remains stubbornly above target and higher energy 
prices have worsened the outlook. However, further 
tightening now seems unlikely. Rising unemployment, 
subdued consumption and falling house prices suggest 
that monetary policy is already restraining activity. The 
RBA is likely to keep rates unchanged for much of 2027, 
balancing persistent inflation pressures against the risk 
of a further slowdown in economic activity.
For investors, the implication is that policy rates may 
not need to rise as high as current market pricing sug-
gests, especially in the US and euro area, but the path 
will remain uncertain and data dependent.

When the long end starts to worry about debt
Central banks can control short‑term policy rates at the 
front end of the yield curve, but the long end reflects 
changes in policy expectations, risk premia and debt 
supply. The energy shock caused by the intensification 
of the conflict in the Middle East has affected all three.
During 2026, 10-year yields in the UK, US and France 
rose sharply. Higher energy prices have raised inflation 
expectations, and uncertainty over how the conflict 
will end has added to the doubt. This has increased 
the extra yield investors demand for holding longer-
dated bonds, as reflected in the rise in long yields since 
the end of the first quarter of 2026. At the same time, 
elevated government debt and growing concerns over 
fiscal sustainability are adding another layer of uncer-
tainty, particularly at the longer end of the curve.
Fiscal headroom is limited 
Over the last few years, and in previous editions of the 
Outlook, we have warned investors about the implica-
tions of large fiscal deficits in economies such as the 
US, UK and France. Despite a plan to control US fiscal 
spending, progress has been slow. In the UK, the high 
welfare bill means there are limited actions the Trea­
sury can take to reduce spending. In France, discussions 
over the 2027 budget have sparked political divisions 
and social unrest ahead of the elections.
With debt levels already elevated, central banks also 
factor the impact on long-term borrowing into their 
policy decisions. The same three economies therefore 
remain vulnerable to pressure at the long end of the 
curve (Figure 5). Although high long-term yields offer 
attractive returns and a perception of low risk, inves-
tors should be wary of these countries’ fragile fiscal 
positions, which could undermine long-dated bonds.

Where the fiscal risk reward looks better: 
a fresh look at EM
Against this backdrop, some emerging markets (EM) 
may offer more compelling risk-adjusted opportunities 
than parts of the developed world (Figure 6). Economies 
such as India, Mexico, Brazil, South Korea, South Africa 
or Indonesia have lower debt-to-GDP ratios and have 
worked towards strengthening their macro fundamen-
tals. 
However, high rates and a stronger US dollar have 
made EM debt less attractive in hard currency (debt 
issued in US dollars rather than local currency). Spreads 
(the extra yield over safe government bonds) on both 
sovereign and corporate debt continue to look tight, 
close to 25-year lows.
For investors willing to be selective and diversified, 
any resolution to the Iran war could lead to interesting 
opportunities in India, South Korea and Indonesia as 
lower energy prices would reduce the cost of energy 
subsidies. Brazil and Mexico could also be interesting 
as they have continued to manage macro fundamentals 
better. For both opportunities, stability in the US dollar 
or long-term US rates would be a trigger point. 
Our view for 2027
As central banks navigate inflationary pressures, the 
risk is that monetary policy becomes too restrictive, 
weighing on economies in which underlying inflation 
remains contained and labour markets are robust.
At the same time, elevated government debt and 
growing concerns over fiscal sustainability are adding 
another layer of uncertainty, particularly at the longer 
end of the yield curve. Furthermore, the uncertainty is 
being augmented by the large volume of hyperscaler 
bond issuance.
Looking towards 2027, the key question is whether 
major economies can bring inflation under control 
without tightening policy so far, or for so long, that they 
undermine growth and market stability.

The emergence of the 
Middle Powers: The new map  
of risk and opportunity
Key takeaways
The era of easy global 
cooperation is fading; 
geopolitical risk is 
structurally higher than 
during much of the 
post-Cold War period.
The influence of the 
United States and China 
as the “superpowers” 
has polarised the world 
and traditional powers 
are in danger of losing 
their relevance. 
These middle powers 
could be increasingly 
central to how 
geopolitical risks are 
managed – or 
mismanaged. 
Action for investors
Be cautious with long‑dated US, UK and French 
government bonds. The sharp rise in long‑term 
yields reflects not only higher inflation 
uncertainty but also increased risk premia and 
fiscal concerns. Limit exposure to long‑maturity 
bonds in these markets to manage volatility and 
favour short‑to-intermediate‑duration bonds. As 
we suggested in Theme 1, it may pay to be tactical 
when sentiment turns overly negative. 
Use diversified EM bond exposure selectively. 
Some emerging markets offer a better structural 
risk reward, but hard currency EM debt remains 
sensitive to high global rates and a strong US 
dollar. Use diversified EM bond strategies and 
avoid concentrated single country positions.
Introducing the next theme
From rates to geopolitics and middle powers
Interest‑rate decisions do not happen in a vacuum. They are increasingly shaped by fiscal pressures, security con-
cerns and the shifting balance of global power. The next theme, The emergence of the Middle Powers: The new map 
of risk and opportunity, explores how a more fragmented geopolitical landscape and the rise of middle powers 
will reshape trade, energy, technology and, ultimately, where capital feels safest.
Our central view is that:
	▪Markets are currently pricing in more tightening 
than central banks may ultimately deliver in the US 
and euro area;
	▪The front end of yield curves (driven by policy 
expectations) has room to fall if second‑round 
effects remain limited;
	▪The long end will remain sensitive to fiscal concerns 
and risk premia, especially in highly indebted 
countries such as the US, UK and France.
For you as an investor, the message from Theme 2 is to 
be deliberate about where you take duration and sover-
eign risk, being cautious about exposure to long‑dated 
bonds in highly indebted markets and using diversi-
fied emerging‑market strategies selectively. The points 
below translate this into concrete portfolio actions for 
the year ahead.

The emerging middle powers share the following three 
characteristics:
	▪Multi‑alignment: They maintain ties with several 
powers rather than binding themselves exclusively 
to one. Increasingly, the UK and several European 
nations fall into this camp.
	▪Coalition‑building: They form flexible coalitions and 
support international institutions to shape global 
norms.
	▪Industrial and regional strength: They possess 
meaningful industrial capacity and strong regional 
agency. 
Countries such as Australia, Canada, South Korea, Nor-
way, Brazil, Indonesia, India, Turkey and Saudi Arabia fit

Carney captured the mood: “The middle powers must 
act together, because if we are not at the table, we are 
on the menu.” His warning reflects growing economic 
coercion by superpowers and the growing risk that "tra-
ditional powers" could be forced into binary choices 
between China and the US. In this environment, middle 
powers are potential counterweights to coercive rivalry 
rather than passive observers.
Trade as a weapon – and the middle‑power response
Over the last two decades, superpowers have increas-
ingly used economic leverage to achieve political goals. 
Trade, technology and finance have been weaponised 
through sanctions, tariffs, export controls and access 
restrictions.
China has used threats to cut off rare earth exports and 
influence via the Belt and Road Initiative to extend its 
geopolitical reach.
The US has pressured governments and firms to avoid 
Chinese 5G technology and has used tariff threats and 
sanctions to secure more favourable trade and security 
arrangements.
Theme 3

phase in the post‑war geopolitical order, in which geopolitical 
risk has risen above Cold War levels. Military spending is 
higher, populism has spread and the role of multilateral 
institutions has weakened.

Europe: cohesion at risk, middle powers more 
important
Europe’s internal cohesion is also being tested. After 
the recent snap election in Spain, three of the largest 
eurozone economies will go to the polls in 2027: Ger-
many and France will hold presidential elections in Jan-
uary and April respectively, while Italy will hold general 
elections before September. 
Right‑wing parties have risen in the polls by tapping 
into public anger over job prospects and immigra-
tion. The victory of Alternative for Germany (AfD) in 
local elections and rising support for Marine Le Pen 
in France are both signs of this trend. Fragmentation 
among centrist parties and the rise of more extreme 
forces could weaken cohesion among key EU members. 
Parties leading in the polls are campaigning on tighter 
immigration rules, reduced EU powers and lower finan-
cial contributions. 
A less cohesive EU would not only undermine Europe’s 
ability to act as a unified traditional power, but also 
increase the importance of middle powers – both 
within Europe and globally – in sustaining effective 
multilateral cooperation. 
For investors, this means paying close attention to 
European countries that combine strong institutions 
with pragmatic regional engagement, and to how EU 
politics affect the broader balance between superpow-
ers and middle powers.
China: tech supremacy and pressure on middle powers
China will hold the National Congress of the Chinese 
Communist Party (CCP) later in 2027, an event that 
takes place every five years. Analysts expect a signif-
icant leadership reshuffle, with more than two thirds 
of the Central Committee and the Politburo Standing 
Committee being replaced. China will seek to project 
political continuity, social stability and clarity on eco-
nomic policy.
Externally, China is expected to shift some pressure 
away from Taiwan itself and focus more on coercion 
towards countries that support Taiwan, such as Japan, 
the Philippines and Australia. At the same time, China 
sees an opportunity to consolidate its technologi-
cal power. Its drive for tech supremacy will centre on 
boosting production capacity. With domestic demand 
less able to absorb this output, China will continue to 
expand exports and build alliances in other markets.
These relationships will further entangle middle pow-
ers in the global competition for technological lead-
ership. Countries that trade heavily with China in 
advanced technologies will need to balance economic 
gains against security concerns and potential pressure 
from the US. 
For investors, this underscores the importance of 
understanding how middle powers position themselves 
in emerging tech blocs and how they manage the risks 
of being caught between competing regulatory and 
security regimes.
Middle powers and traditional powers have strong 
incentives to cooperate with each other to coun-
ter these pressures, deepen trade links and reduce 
dependence on any single superpower. In recent years, 
they have created new trade frameworks to bypass 
stalled multilateral organisations. Trade between devel-
oping nations (South–South) has more than doubled, 
rising from just over 15% of global goods trade in 2005 
to 28% in 2025, reflecting deeper regional integration 
and lower dependence on traditional partners.
Examples include:
	▪Economic cooperation between Australia and India.
	▪Trade agreements between Southeast Asian 
economies and Gulf states.
	▪Latin American countries strengthening commercial 
ties with Asia and Oceania through the Trans‑Pacific 
Partnership.
Game theory suggests that a fractured global economy, 
where each country acts alone, leads to a non‑coop-
erative equilibrium that leaves everyone poorer and 
more vulnerable. Middle powers such as Canada and 
Australia are instead seeking to de‑risk supply chains 
through diversification and strategic partnerships, turn-
ing reliability into diplomatic leverage.
Their multi‑alignment, industrial capacity and regional 
agency position them uniquely to mitigate geopolitical 
risks. If they can coordinate effectively, renew forms of 
multilateralism and resist economic coercion, they may 
help stabilise a fragmented system and support a more 
coordinated global order.
For investors, the rise of middle powers is not just a 
political theme but a structural lens for assessing risk 
and opportunity in 2027. Understanding how these 
countries navigate superpower rivalry, regional conflicts 
and technological competition will be critical to judging 
their market opportunities and risks.

have shifted their political alignment back towards the

Theme 4
In the year ahead, the debates surrounding artificial 
intelligence (AI) will continue to grow. The topic has fast 
become one of the most significant issues for financial 
markets, not just due to the scale of technological change 
and its potential economic impact, but also because of the 
enormous expenditure associated with the build-out of AI 
infrastructure, which has raised important questions around 
debt issuance. 
GDP growth from the Age of Artificial Intelligence
Most economists agree that AI is already contributing 
meaningfully to GDP growth. The Fed estimates that in 
the first quarter of 2026, contributions from software, 
data centres and IT equipment accounted for around 
one third of US GDP growth.4 This phenomenon is not 
unique to the US. While there are very few estimates 
that directly quantify the impact of AI on GDP growth 
in China, the rise in high-tech investment and exports 
is notable. Perhaps surprisingly, the UK was the fast-
est-growing G7 economy in the first half of 2026, and 
the Office for National Statistics highlighted the role of 
AI-related companies in achieving this.

Many more examples show that AI is already affect-
ing GDP growth, but it is important to recognise the 
channel through which it is doing so. While there may 
be company-specific cases of AI boosting productivity, 
there is currently little evidence that it does this for the 
global economy as a whole.
There are many differing views about the extent to 
which AI will increase GDP growth by lifting productivity, 
and how long it will take for this to happen. Right now, 
AI is clearly still in its infrastructure build-out phase, 
and so investment is the main channel through which 
it is affecting GDP growth.
Rise of the hyperscaler
Key to the infrastructure build-out phase are the hyper-
scalers.5 These are cloud computing providers that 
lease computing capacity to AI companies. As demand 
for their services has increased, the hyperscalers 
have spent significantly, with most of this investment 
directed towards the construction of data centres. The 
scale of capital expenditures (capex) by hyperscalers 
has become so great that it is often used as a proxy for 
aggregate AI infrastructure investment.
However, hyperscaler capex is not a perfect proxy for 
AI infrastructure investment since not all hyperscaler 
capex is AI-related. Furthermore, many of the hyper-
scalers use a mix of operating and financing leases for 
their data centres. In practice there is little difference 
between the two, but in accounting treatment terms 
leases are included in capex while operating leases are 
not. This may result in an understatement of AI-related 
capex by hyperscalers.

The year AI cyber risk became real
AI’s ability to write software increasingly extends to 
finding weaknesses. In April 2026, Anthropic disclosed 
Claude Mythos Preview, a model able to find software 
flaws largely on its own. Rather than release the model 
publicly, Anthropic launched Project Glasswing, which 
gave 50 organisations running some of the world’s most 
critical software early access to fix the flaws. Within 
about a month more than 10,000 high or critical sever-
ity vulnerabilities were found. The bottleneck was no 
longer finding bugs but patching them fast enough. 
Software vulnerabilities are more prevalent than we like 
to believe, and the race is on to fix them before they 
can be exploited.
The risks also extend to AI systems themselves. Dur-
ing internal evaluations in July 2026, OpenAI models 
circumvented containment controls and compromised 
Hugging Face's systems, a leading AI developer plat-
form. They went from a single foothold to adminis-
trator-level access in under 13 hours. Anthropic then 
reviewed its own test runs and found three cases 
where Claude models had not been properly secured 
and accessed the systems of three real organisations. 
In September 2026, Australia’s Prime Minister said an 
OpenAI agent had accessed non-public parts of a Medi-
care portal in June, the first publicly reported AI intru-
sion into a government system.
As well as posing liability questions, these events show 
what AI is now capable of and how defensive measures 
must keep pace with the new threats.
Agents move into consumers’ pockets
Autonomous agents are now being sold to consumers. 
Meta launched Muse in September 2026. OpenAI fol-
lowed with Dots three weeks later and a host of similar 
startups have also launched. The promise is an assis-
tant that acts rather than advises, that is always on, 
working in the background across email, calendars and 
external sites, booking travel, filling in forms and mak-
ing purchases, then checking in for approval. Instinct, 
a popular agentic AI assistant product, needs no app at 
all: users simply text it.
Early demand has been strong; Muse jumped to the top 
of the US App Store, overtaking ChatGPT. But incum-
bents guarding consumer attention pushed back. 
Within two weeks, Amazon blocked Muse from its store, 
citing undisclosed automated browsing and the han-
dling of consumer credentials. Early reviewers also sug-
gest the agents have yet to earn full trust. Agents hold-
ing passwords and payment details widen the attack 
surface for every household, and platforms will defend 
owning the customer relationship rather than handing 
it over to an AI agent.
From security to policy: Washington pulls the plug 
on Mythos
On 12 June 2026, three days after Anthropic released 
Claude Mythos 5 and Fable 5, the US Commerce Depart-
ment ordered Anthropic to block access by any foreign 
national, inside or outside the US. Unable to screen 
users’ nationality, Anthropic removed public access to 
the models worldwide. 
Theme 5

Models found software flaws at industrial scale and, in several 
cases, broke into real systems without being told to do so. In 
2027, we expect AI cyber risk to become increasingly prevalent 
and a top priority for every enterprise. At the same time, 
regulation is beginning to emerge around the world to address 
this and other challenges posed by AI. The next chapter in AI 
will likely be shaped by how safely it can operate and who 
controls access, alongside the heated debate on the economic 
returns of this massive capital spending cycle.

The White House Accord on Super Intelligence
On 29 September 2026, President Trump and the leaders 
of six frontier AI developers (Google, Anthropic, Meta, 
OpenAI, xAI and Nvidia) signed the White House Accord 
on Super Intelligence. The roughly 300-word document 
sets out four layers of oversight: internal monitoring 
of model capabilities, a dedicated function overseeing 
this, independent external audits, and a board-level 
committee above both. The accord is voluntary, though 
the President called it “morally binding”, and it notes 
these steps may eventually be codified into law. 
The endorsement of third-party audits reverses earlier 
scepticism, and it is likely to act as a de facto market 
standard, shaping vendor due diligence, contracts and 
board oversight well before any legislation arrives.

Global attitudes to regulation are varied
China combines strategic support for AI with extensive 
oversight and control. Significant investment across 
local AI, semiconductor and data centre supply chains 
have government support, as shown by its “AI Plus” ini-
tiative to embed AI deeply across industries by 2035. At 
the same time, China maintains tight algorithmic over-
sight and security reviews over what can be released. 
With a more coordinated approach, China is relatively 
less constrained by regulation but faces some techno-
logical challenges due to US-imposed export controls.
Europe is pursuing tighter safeguards while also pro-
moting greater computing capacity. The EU introduced 
one of the first comprehensive laws with the AI Act but 
has since quietly eased regulation, delaying some of 
the strictest rules, due to take effect from August 2026 
to December 2027 and beyond. The focus has shifted 
towards incentivising the construction of AI infrastruc-
ture within Europe. The proposed Cloud and AI Devel-
opment Act (CADA) aims to simplify permitting and to 
triple EU data centre capacity over five to seven years, 
with EU and national financial support alongside pri-
vate investment. If US construction is delayed, Europe 
could attract a share of future investment if developers 
there can secure power and permits sooner.
Our view for 2027 
AI is creating new challenges for businesses and gov-
ernments. As AI tools become increasingly capable 
of identifying software vulnerabilities, companies 
face a growing race to patch flaws before they can be 
exploited. Meanwhile, governments are beginning to 
view frontier AI models – the most advanced systems at 
the leading edge of development – as strategic assets. 
Their potential impact on national security, economic 
competitiveness and critical infrastructure is driving 
efforts to develop domestic capabilities, secure access 
to computing power and support local AI ecosystems, 
while policymakers grapple with how to regulate a tech-
nology evolving faster than existing frameworks can 
accommodate.
These pressures are likely to sustain investment in AI 
infrastructure, computing capacity and data centres, 
while bringing greater regulatory scrutiny as the risks 
become clearer. For investors, this is a fast-moving 
landscape offering significant potential, but one in 
which technological progress, security concerns and 
policy decisions can quickly reshape the outlook. Care-
ful research, selectivity and a degree of cautious opti-
mism will be essential in identifying the opportunities 
that can deliver lasting value.

Two paths for 2027, and the signposts to watch
The upside case rests on proof. If a major “AI-designed” 
drug meets its primary endpoint in a phase three trial, 
which could happen in late 2026 or early 2027, inves-
tors would have the first large-scale evidence that AI 
improves the odds of clinical success. That could prompt 
a significant re-rating of the sector, as valuations move 
away from judging companies on the speed of discovery 
and towards pricing in a higher probability of success. 
The clearest early gauge is how many of the 60+ AI-na-
tive assets now in phase one trials graduate to phase 
two. On the regulatory side, finalisation of the FDA’s 
“competency-based” evaluation model for generative AI 
devices, targeted for late 2026 or early 2027, would give 
developers a clearer route to market.
The downside case is regulatory rather than scientific. 
If individual US states create a “patchwork” of rules, 
for example, Texas requiring conspicuous AI disclosure 
and strict human review that conflicts with federal 
FDA clearance, healthcare providers may fear being 
held legally responsible for autonomous AI care and 
hold back from adopting it. That would limit growth at 
healthcare technology companies and weigh on their 
valuations. A delay to the FDA framework would be an 
early warning of slowing adoption. So would slow pro-
gress by the CMS in replacing legacy claims systems 
with standardised digital workflows by 2027, because 
fragmented systems limit how far administrative auto-
mation can go.
Between the two sits the commercial test, which inves-
tors can track every quarter. Margin sentiment among 
S&P 1500 healthcare companies (a broad index of 
US large, mid and small-cap firms) offers a read on 
whether administrative AI is paying off, and firms that 
mention AI usage currently benefit from better investor 
sentiment than those that do not. If that gap persists 
or widens, the positive case strengthens. If it narrows, 
expectations may be running ahead of results.
By 2027, the investment environment will shift from val-
uing AI potential to recognising the value of AI once 
it has measurably improved a company’s profitability. 
This creates a divergence between large-cap incum-
bents capable of funding massive AI infrastructure and 
small and mid-cap biotechnology companies using AI 
to scale without a proportional rise in headcount.

Energy infrastructure is also increasingly exposed to

Energy security and sovereignty come to the fore 
Disruption to the global energy supply chain raises fun-
damental questions about the current energy sector 
set-up. Energy security and sovereignty are now at the 
centre of the political debate, with sovereignty refer-
ring to a country’s ability to meet its energy needs from 
its own resources. Fossil fuel exporters must invest in 
new infrastructure to guarantee they can fulfil client 
demand without disruption at chokepoints. 
Energy importing parts of the world such as Europe 
must reassess their procurement strategies. In the 
short term, the solution could involve greater diversi-
fication of energy supply chains. In the longer term, it 
requires a structural reduction in dependence on for-
eign supplies.
This does not mean building more refineries in Europe; 
instead, it means reducing demand for diesel. This 
strengthens the case for investment in renewable 
sources of energy and the increased electrification of 
the economy.

Our view for 2027
What does all this mean for Japanese markets and the 
economy in the year ahead? In terms of the headline 
macro picture, we do not expect significant change. Our 
baseline case sees low positive growth similar to that 
experienced in 2026, with inflation drifting back to tar-
get and another two rate hikes spread over the course 
of the year. However, there are clearly some important 
developments taking place within the Japanese econ-
omy that we believe will help support investor senti-
ment for the country’s stock market in 2027. 
The more positive wage and consumption dynamics 
described above provide one important pillar of sup-
port. It is also notable that Japanese corporates have 
been transitioning to much stronger standards of cor-
porate governance over the past decade. Together with 
higher interest rates, this is encouraging a much greater 
degree of capital discipline in Japan. 
With foreigners notably absent from Japan in recent 
years, the combination of an improving macro back-
drop, better corporate governance, a weaker yen and 
attractive valuations – many companies trade on val-
uations below their book value – provides significant 
opportunity for an increase in overseas demand for 
Japanese equities. If this were to happen at the same 
time as locals also increase their exposure to domestic 
stocks, for example because of higher average incomes 
and a shift in perceptions of risk, that could lead to a 
buoyant year for Japanese equities in 2027. 
For you as an investor, the message from the Japan 
theme is to treat Japan as a strategic allocation again, 
increasing exposure through diversified equity strate-
gies and focusing on quality companies that can turn 
better income dynamics, governance and global AI 
demand into durable returns. The points below trans-
late this into concrete portfolio actions for the year 
ahead.
From deflation to a new inflation equilibrium
Several positive developments are worth highlighting. 
In particular, the country appears to be in a new infla-
tion equilibrium. In a very low or even negative inflation 
environment – which Japan experienced for 25 years 
from the mid‑to‑late 1990s – there are limited oppor-
tunities for wage increases, and this feeds through to 
weak consumption. That dynamic can then lead to a 
self‑reinforcing spiral in which a lack of demand is 
associated with reduced pricing power and the cycle 
begins again. 
One of the few positive things emanating from Rus-
sia’s invasion of Ukraine is that the higher energy prices 
that followed appear to have kick‑started more durable 
positive inflation dynamics in Japan. These inflationary 
forces have subsequently been augmented by strong 
AI‑related demand and a weaker exchange rate. 
The headline Japanese consumer price index (CPI) 
increased at an annual average rate of just 0.2% in 
the 30 years to end March 2022, during which time the 
country experienced deflation (negative inflation) in 
more than 40% of the months. Since then, Japanese 
consumer prices have increased by an average of 2.8%. 
Core inflation – excluding fresh food and energy – has 
followed a similar pattern to the headline data. While 
too much inflation can be dangerous, a little bit of 
inflation is not necessarily a bad thing. It can facilitate 
adjustments to the real economy that would otherwise 
be more painful, and it also mitigates against the pos-
sibility that inflation is overestimated. It is for reasons 
such as these that central banks have positive inflation 
targets.
Wages finally responding: income growth and 
domestic demand
However, positive consumer price inflation that is not 
matched by wage inflation of at least the same order 
of magnitude is not good for an economy because it 
reduces individuals’ purchasing power. Another posi-
tive for Japan is that there are growing wage pressures, 
evidenced by the fact wages have grown faster than 
consumer prices in every month this year. Disaggregat-
ing this, there are some parts of the economy for which 
wage inflation is significantly above price inflation. So 
far this year, wage growth has averaged 3.5% compared 
to average headline inflation of just 1.6%. Further-
more, it is encouraging that wage growth appears to be 
most rapid for younger workers who tend to consume 
a higher proportion of their income than for older 
employees, who want to save for their retirements 
(Figure 22).

Beyond beta:  
Where private capital can  
still create real alpha
Recent redemption pressure has put the liquidity offered 
by some private markets funds under scrutiny. Across all 
three themes mentioned above, the entry price, manager 
skill and investment structure will all matter as much as 
the broader market trend.9
Middle-market buyouts and selective growth equity
Cheap debt and rising valuation multiples have sup-
ported private equity returns for much of the past 
decade, but higher financing costs now constrain debt 
capacity, while elevated entry valuations and uncertain 
exit pricing make multiple expansion less reliable. This 
means returns will depend more on earnings growth 
and cash generation. To this end, we favour special-
ist middle-market managers with repeatable sector 
playbooks and the resources to strengthen leadership, 
sharpen commercial execution, improve procurement 
and working capital, and use technology to raise pro-
ductivity. These levers can be especially effective in 
smaller companies with less-developed systems and 
management structures. Buy-and-build remains attrac-
tive where acquisitions add capabilities or market 
reach and integration drives earnings. Strategies reli-
ant mainly on acquisition-multiple arbitrage are less 
compelling.9, 17
Selective growth equity offers a complementary path to 
growth with less reliance on leverage. We favour busi-
nesses with established product-market fit, durable 
unit economics and a credible path to cash generation. 
Technology, including AI, can support the investment 
thesis where it delivers measurable customer value 
or productivity gains, but managers must also assess 
whether it could erode competitive advantages. 
Entry valuation remains critical: revenue quality, cus-
tomer concentration, margin progression and exit 
options should support a realistic downside case. Pre-
qin’s latest survey points to improving expectations for 
private-equity performance, although exits remain a 
major concern.9, 16
Private equity secondaries
The slow return of capital from older private-equity 
funds continues to create demand for liquidity. Sec-
ondaries allow investors to buy interests in exist-
ing funds or assets with more information about the 
underlying portfolio than is available at the start of a 
new fund. They may also shorten the path to distribu-
tions, although neither earlier cash flows nor a reduced 
J-curve is assured.9, 17
The market is now large and competitive: Lazard esti-
mates that Limited Partner-led and General Partner-led 
transactions together reached $124 billion in the first 
half of 2026. For 2027, the opportunity rests on asset 
selection and pricing, not on assuming every seller 
must accept a substantial discount. Buyers should test 
portfolio marks, the remaining upside, concentration, 
unfunded obligations and alignment; General Part-
ner-led deals also require independent price validation 
and clear governance.10

in operational improvement, in solutions to the private-equity 
liquidity backlog and in the significant infrastructure needed 
to meet rising power demand. But private credit investing 
requires a different lens.

Time to choose private credit selectivity over retreat
The central test for private credit in 2027 is simple: can 
managers protect capital when borrowers come under 
pressure and investors seek liquidity? Recent redemp-
tions in semi-liquid funds, elevated payment-in-kind 
interest and closer scrutiny of valuations point to wid-
ening differences between managers. This environment 
argues for selectivity, not retreat: Preqin reports that 
27% of investors intend to commit less over the next 
year, yet almost 85% expect to maintain or increase 
their long-term allocations.9, 11, 12
We favour managers with proprietary or relation-
ship-led sourcing, disciplined underwriting and trans-
parent valuations. Those strengths should be visible in 
the portfolio through resilient cash interest coverage, 
credible free-cash-flow conversion, prudent leverage 
and limited reliance on payment in kind (PIK). PIK can 
provide temporary flexibility, but repeated use may 
defer stress rather than resolving it. Fund terms also 
matter as periodic dealing does not guarantee liquidity, 
and gates can delay exits. 11, 12, 17
Software deserves additional scrutiny because AI could 
reshape competitive advantages. Businesses with 
embedded workflows, proprietary data and strong cus-
tomer retention may be better placed, while less differ-
entiated applications may face lower barriers to entry 
and pricing pressure. 
Credit secondaries and distressed strategies may ben-
efit from market pressure, but only where discounts, 
documentation and workout capabilities justify the 
risk.12, 13
Power and AI bring infrastructure opportunities 
Private infrastructure is evolving from a predominantly 
income-oriented allocation into the physical backbone 
of a more digital and electrified economy. AI adoption, 
broader electrification and energy-security needs are 
increasing demand for reliable electricity and connec-
tivity. The opportunity extends beyond data centres to 
power generation, utilities, grids, storage, power man-
agement, fibre and interconnection capacity. The Inter-
national Energy Agency projects that global data centre 
electricity use will roughly double from 485 terawatt 
hours (TWh) in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030, while J.P. Mor-
gan highlights utilities and power assets as beneficiar-
ies of rising demand.14, 17
Although investment at scale is required, this does not 
guarantee attractive returns. We favour assets with a 
clear role in supplying power or connectivity, credible 
customers and disciplined financing. Power availability, 
grid queues, construction costs and entry valuations 
are especially important in data centre-related invest-
ments. Public funding alone is unlikely to meet these 
needs, creating a role for private capital where pro-
jects can be delivered on acceptable terms. However, 
the wide dispersion in returns reinforces the need for 
careful manager and strategy selection.15, 16

Monetising uncertainty: 
Hedge fund strategies  
for a fragmented cycle
1. Systematic trend-following CTAs
Systematic trend‑following CTAs are well placed to ben-
efit from persistent moves in interest rates, currencies, 
commodities and equity indices, particularly if policy 
paths across major central banks remain divergent and 
geopolitical tensions continue to drive sustained shifts 
in energy and commodity prices. 
Their main strength is the ability to capture extended 
directional trends across asset classes with relatively 
little reliance on the direction of equity markets, offer-
ing diversification if the equity backdrop deteriorates 
or if macro shocks propagate through rates and curren-
cies rather than corporate earnings.
In a best‑case 2027 scenario for CTAs, inflation and 
growth paths diverge meaningfully across regions, 
energy prices remain volatile but trend in identifia-
ble directions, and policy cycles in rates and currency 
evolve in a way that creates clear, sustained trends; in 
that environment, CTAs can monetise cross‑asset moves 
and provide strong returns with genuine diversification. 
2. Global macro
Global macro strategies should find a rich opportunity 
set in 2027, with varied inflation, growth and monetary 
policy paths across major economies, and geopolitics 
driving meaningful dislocations in energy, commodities, 
currencies and rates.

Putting the three strategies into context
Taken together, these three strategies monetise dif-
ferent dimensions of the uncertainty likely in 2027. 
Systematic trend-following CTAs rely on persistence 
in macro and commodity trends, global macro bene-

volatility with direction, macro needs volatility with 
divergence, and market-neutral needs volatility with 
dispersion rather than uniform market-wide moves.
