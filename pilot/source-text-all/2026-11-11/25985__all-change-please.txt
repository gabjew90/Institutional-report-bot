Global Daily 
Weekly Wrap 
What a difference an election makes. And what a difference a day makes! 
Markets initially greeted the Democrats’ landslide victory in the US midterms by enthusiastically 
pricing in a change of direction. Equities rallied, bond yields fell and spreads tightened, while 
markets exposed to geopolitical risk staged spectacular reversals. Oil fell sharply from its recent 
highs and European gas prices tumbled. 
But the risk-on mood did not survive its second full trading day and the blue wave at the polls 
turned into a red one on the bourses. Yesterday, investors revisited what a Democratic sweep 
might mean for the technology sector and, in particular, the enormous capital-spending cycle 
around artificial intelligence. The result was brutal. The S&P 500 fell 4.4%, the Nasdaq lost 5.5% 
and entered correction territory, while the Philadelphia semiconductor index plunged 12%, its 
second-largest decline on record and its worst performance since the Covid sell-off of March 
2020. US two-year Treasury yields fell around 12bp as the curve bull steepened. 
While the first post-election interpretation was that divided government would constrain the 
administration’s legislative agenda and reduce trade and geopolitical uncertainty (which we 
contested), the second was that a Democrat-controlled Congress, reinforced by victories at state 
and local level, could also slow the construction of data centres and associated energy 
infrastructure. A wave of successful ballot initiatives restricting data-centre development added 
substance to those concerns. 
That does not mean the AI investment cycle suddenly ends. But it does mean that it may become 
more expensive, slower, and geographically fragmented. The AI boom is, for now, a very physical 
affair. It requires semiconductors, data centres, electricity generation, grids, cooling systems, and 
large quantities of water. Many projects therefore depend on planning approval, environmental 
regulation, energy availability and local political consent. If some projects cannot proceed in their 
intended locations, they may have to move to other states or even other countries. Others may no 
longer clear a higher political, regulatory or financing hurdle. 
Note that concerns about returns on vast AI investments, strained financing structures and the 
unusually concentrated contribution of technology spending to US growth were present before 
Tuesday. The election just provided a trigger for investors to reassess those risks. Thursday’s sell-
off also spread well beyond technology, suggesting that this was not simply a sector rotation but 
at least a temporary challenge to the wider US growth narrative. The fall in Treasury yields also 
signals that this could mean less pressure on the Fed to tighten further, of course. 
It is too early to know whether yesterday marked the beginning of a more fundamental 
unwinding or a violent correction in a crowded trade. But it illustrates the danger of extrapolating 
political change too quickly.  
The same caution applies outside technology. Democrats will control both chambers of Congress 
from January, substantially increasing their ability to constrain the administration’s legislative 
agenda. But they have not replaced the President. Donald Trump retains the veto, executive-order 
powers and considerable latitude in foreign policy. Divided government is a constraint on Donald 
Trump, not the end of Donald Trump.

For Europe, a peaceful resolution to the Middle East conflict and structurally lower energy prices 
would be a substantial boost to an energy-importing economy struggling with weak growth and 
renewed inflation. Less tariff uncertainty would help too, and the election potentially creates an 
opening for Europe and the US to reconnect. But none of this follows mechanically from a 
Democratic Congress. The White House retains meaningful unilateral levers, while geoeconomic 
fragmentation is bigger than one administration. And if Trump’s focus does shift further to non-
domestic issues, it could even be detrimental. Europe itself is moving towards stronger trade 
defences against China, as we argue here. A more plausible opportunity may therefore be 
renewed transatlantic coordination towards China rather than a return to free-trade liberalism. 
Indeed, an anti-war message from US voters does not itself produce peace as we argued in 
yesterday's Global Daily. The US military build-up around Iran remains in place and Washington’s 
strategic dilemmas have not disappeared. Oil prices also bounced 3% to 4% yesterday after their 
post-election fall, a reminder that geopolitical risk premia can be repriced in both directions. A 
less interventionist America raises an uncomfortable question for Europe: what if America brings 
fewer wars, but also less security? 
Britain's central bank certainly isn't betting on lasting peace just yet, as our own Stefan Koopman 
notes in his post-MPC meeting comment with the singing title “Is this a Mann's world?” (I found it 
hard not to hum the song once I saw it!) 
The Bank of England raised Bank Rate by 25bp to 4% yesterday, its first hike in more than three 
years. The 8-1 vote was strikingly hawkish: Catherine Mann dissented because she preferred a 
50bp increase. In some respects, the Bank and markets are telling opposite stories. Markets say 
the energy shock is disappearing; the Bank says it cannot set policy on the assumption that it will. 
Domestic inflation pressures have stopped easing, economic activity has proved more resilient 
and the labour market appears to be stabilising. Yet broad second-round effects from higher 
energy costs have not emerged so far. That makes this a pre-emptive hike rather than the 
beginning of a conventional tightening cycle. 
Hence, our view remains that another increase becomes likely if Middle East tensions keep energy 
prices elevated, as we expect, or if higher energy costs feed materially into wages and services 
inflation. That would take Bank Rate to 4.25%, after which the MPC's centrists would probably 
prefer to sit at restrictive levels and watch what happens. 
Across the Channel, France provides another reminder that Tuesday did not repeal Europe’s 
domestic problems. The OAT-Bund spread narrowed with the initial risk-on move, but France’s 
fiscal arithmetic remains ugly, parliament fragmented and the government’s budget plans 
contentious. Protesters are communicating the social and political cost of fiscal adjustment; bond 
investors the financial cost of avoiding it. 
That tension also runs through our latest Monthly Outlook, The bill comes due. The danger is not 
necessarily another sudden euro crisis, but a slower fiscal squeeze as rising interest costs compete 
with defence, investment and social spending. The bond vigilantes may not yet be kicking down 
the door, but they have found the address. 
And that captures the broader lesson from an extraordinary week. Markets first priced political 
change as if it were automatically positive policy change, then discovered that the same political 
change could threaten one of the most powerful investment narratives of recent years. A 
Democratic Congress does not make Donald Trump powerless, an anti-war vote does not make 
peace, lower risk premia do not remove geopolitical risks, and political enthusiasm for AI cannot 
be detached indefinitely from its physical, financial and environmental constraints. 
Tuesday changed the constraints. Yesterday showed that investors are still working out who those 
constraints will bind.

Day ahead 
Non-farm payrolls take central billing today with 65,000 jobs expected to have been added in 
October and the unemployment rate to remain at 4.2%.  
German industrial production, released earlier this morning surprised positively, rising 0.7% m/m 
in September, but it followed a 1%-point downward revision for August, leaving overall y/y 
growth considerably lower than the consensus estimate. Spanish industry output rose 0.8%, 
double the consensus estimate. 
French wage growth slowed to 0.2% q/q in the third quarter after 0.7% in the previous quarter – 
highlighting the strain on real purchasing power of French households. Later this morning, we will 
have the Eurozone retail sales data for September 
The latest University of Michigan consumer sentiment report will also be released, and the Bank of 
England’s Huw Pill will be speaking.
