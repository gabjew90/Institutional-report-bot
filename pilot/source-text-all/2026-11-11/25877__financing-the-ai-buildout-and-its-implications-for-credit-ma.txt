Financing the AI buildout and its implications 
for credit markets
In brief
•	Investment in artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure is beginning to 
exceed internally generated cash flow available to fund it. Our analysis 
indicates an external financing requirement of approximately USD 1.1 
trillion for the five major US technology companies (Alphabet, Amazon, 
Microsoft, Meta and Oracle), creating an expanding opportunity for 
credit investors.
•	Computing infrastructure providers appear well positioned to 
capture AI revenues, but attractive returns depend on demand, 
pricing discipline, and the scale and timing of capital investment. 
Strong starting balance sheets provide support, although borrowing 
will increase leverage.
•	Senior unsecured hyperscaler bonds, data centre and AI chip-
backed financing, and utility hybrids offer opportunities to capture 
additional income. Security selection and fundamental research are 
essential to assess whether investors are adequately compensated 
for the risks as financing structures become more complex.
Back to basics: Understanding the AI value chain
The Nobel Prize–winning physicist Richard Feynman famously observed 
that no one really understands quantum mechanics1. The economics of 
artificial intelligence may be heading towards a similar distinction. Contrary 
to popular belief, investors can be a passionate bunch, and few topics in 
recent memory have sparked more debate than financing the AI buildout.
Artificial intelligence has extraordinary potential, but there is considerably 
less agreement about how much revenue it will ultimately generate, who 
will capture the profits, and whether the returns will justify the trillions of 
dollars being invested. For credit investors, these questions are becoming 
increasingly important as the world’s largest technology companies turn to 
debt markets to help finance an unprecedented expansion in computing 
infrastructure, creating new opportunities and risks for bondholders.
The long-term potential applications of AI are almost limitless. Companies 
cite benefits ranging from accelerating drug discovery, to the use of robotics 
in manufacturing and industrial automation, and digital sales assistants 
that tailor recommendations to customers. The clearest commercial 
opportunities today are in software and enterprise productivity. Here, use 
cases include coding assistants for software development, automating 
enterprise tasks such as legal analysis, fraud detection and medical 
diagnostics, as well as handling customer enquiries and sales processes.

Behind these increasingly visible applications lies a 
less visible network of AI infrastructure. The AI industry 
can be thought of as a series of interconnected layers, 
often portrayed as a ‘layer cake’, each with distinct 
competitive dynamics, capital requirements and 
economic characteristics: 
1.	Applications and frontier models: the consumer and 
enterprise-facing models developed by companies 
such as OpenAI, Anthropic and Google. Revenue 
comes from subscriptions, enterprise licences and 
usage-based charges. Competition here is intense, 
including from open-weight models, which could 
constrain pricing power.
2.	Compute: specialised semiconductors, including 
graphics processing units (GPUs), servers and 
networking equipment designed and sold by Nvidia 
and others, plus large cloud providers, known as 
hyperscalers, who assemble these components into 
large computing clusters rented to AI developers to 
operate their models.
3.	Data centres: the physical buildings housing the 
computing equipment, together with cooling and 
electrical infrastructure. 
4.	Energy: the electricity generation and transmission 
infrastructure required to power the data centres.
Revenue enters the AI ecosystem as businesses 
and consumers pay for access to AI applications 
and models through subscriptions or usage-based 
charges, typically measured in tokens, which are 
the units of information processed by the AI models. 
Some of the revenue flows to the hyperscalers, helping 
fund the underlying infrastructure. Demand is also 
supported by internal consumption from companies 
such as Google and Meta deploying AI to enhance the 
revenues and profitability of their existing businesses.
Who captures the economic value of AI?
While AI adoption is accelerating, there is considerable 
uncertainty about how the resulting revenues and 
profits will be distributed across the industry. We believe 
computing infrastructure providers are particularly well 
positioned to capture a substantial share of AI-related 
spending, for two important reasons.
First, computing equipment accounts for the majority of 
the investment required to deliver AI services. Building 
and equipping a 1 gigawatt (GW) data centre can 
cost approximately USD 50 billion: around 70% goes 
towards GPUs, servers and networking equipment, 
around 20% towards the data-centre shell (including 
cooling and electrical systems), and 10% towards power 
infrastructure. Meeting US demand could require 
around 100 GW over the coming decade, implying a 
multitrillion-dollar investment. This level of investment 
illustrates both the capital-intensive nature of AI 
infrastructure and the significant revenues required 
by its providers to support the investment. While high 
capital requirements do not necessarily translate into 
attractive returns, early indications are promising.
Amazon chief executive Andy Jassy highlighted the 
different investment cycles in a May 2026 CNBC 
interview: “On the networking and the hardware side, 
it’s about six years. On the data centre side, it’s 30-plus-
year useful life assets.” Data centres can take two years 
to build, whereas equipment is purchased closer to 
deployment. Jassy has also indicated that Amazon 
recovers its investment in computing equipment 
in less than three years on average, with most AI 
capacity contracted for at least five years. This recovery 
period excludes the initial investment in physical 
infrastructure, but provides some early evidence that 
the economics of supplying compute are attractive.
Second, the competitive dynamics differ considerably 
across the AI ecosystem. Competition between frontier 
model developers is intensifying, with advances in 
open-weight models (AI models whose underlying 
parameters are publicly available, allowing others to 
download, modify and run them independently) and 
relatively low switching costs potentially constraining 
their pricing power. By contrast, providing computing 
capacity at scale requires enormous financial 
resources, technical expertise and access to scarce 
infrastructure, including advanced semiconductors, 
data centre capacity and electricity.
In a recent discussion, the management team of a 
leading AI infrastructure provider characterised to us 
the provision of compute as effectively monopolistic, 
reflecting the substantial barriers to entry and the 
limited number of companies capable of delivering 
computing capacity at scale. These barriers to entry 
have concentrated the market among a relatively small 
number of hyperscalers (with neoclouds – specialist 
cloud providers focused on GPU-powered AI computing 
– growing but still dwarfed by hyperscalers), creating 
oligopolistic characteristics that may provide greater 
pricing power than exists at the model layer. 
The durability of these advantages will ultimately depend 
on whether the industry maintains sufficient pricing 
discipline as computing capacity expands.

Can the AI economy justify the investment?
We estimate that AI data centre capital expenditure 
(capex) will accelerate significantly in 2027 before 
beginning to level off from 2028, as capacity 
approaches around 100 GW of compute. This equates 
to additional spending from the major hyperscalers of 
approximately USD 6 trillion over the period from 2026 to 
2030 (Exhibit 1).
The scale of the investment raises a central question for 
investors: will AI profitability eventually justify the capital 
being deployed? The compass for navigating this 
uncertainty is return on invested capital (ROIC), which 
measures the profits a company generates relative to 
the capital invested in its business. 
ROIC provides a common yardstick for company 
management teams, shareholders and bondholders. 
For management, the objective should be to invest 
where returns exceed the cost of capital, creating 
economic value. Equity investors benefit from this value 
creation, while bondholders benefit from additional 
earnings and cash flow that support a company’s 
ability to service and repay its debt. Conversely, 
projects that fail to earn their cost of capital can 
destroy shareholder value and weaken a company’s 
creditworthiness. Management teams therefore have 
an incentive to exercise capital discipline, keeping 
investment proportionate to expected demand and the 
profitability of providing computing capacity. 
Recent market events have highlighted investor 
concern about these risks. While the focus of this 
paper is the investment grade credit landscape, the 
significant pressure on companies such as CoreWeave, 
a high yield rated neocloud company specialising in AI-
related cloud infrastructure, illustrates the challenges 
facing more leveraged participants in the AI ecosystem. 
Investors are increasingly questioning not only whether 
the eventual returns on these investments will exceed 
their cost of capital, but also how much leverage 
companies can prudently assume while waiting 
for those returns to materialise. Even economically 
attractive investments can create financial stress if 
the cash flows needed to service debt take too long to 
materialise, or if the investment is too heavily financed 
with borrowing.

Hyperscalers have the capacity to invest
However, we draw an important distinction between 
these smaller AI-related companies and the largest 
hyperscalers, whose substantial earnings and balance 
sheet strength provide considerable capacity to finance 
the buildout. We estimate that for every 1 dollar entering 
the AI ecosystem as external demand for frontier model 
providers, the hyperscalers will capture 75 cents of 
revenue for providing computing infrastructure. 
We use a 15% ROIC as a benchmark for assessing the 
potential economics of this investment. While the extent 
of value creation will depend on individual companies’ 
cost of capital, we believe returns at this level would 
be consistent with a financially sustainable AI 
investment cycle.
Assuming USD 5 trillion of nominal capex over the 
next five years, rather than the USD 6 trillion implied by 
current projections, to allow for some moderation in 
the pace of the AI buildout, external AI revenues would 
need to be approximately USD 2.3 trillion a year by 2030 
to support our investment assumptions, equating to 
USD 1.7 trillion in revenue to the hyperscalers. These 
revenues must cover substantial operating costs, 
including depreciation of the computing equipment 
and supporting infrastructure. For our analysis, we 
assume a 40% earnings before interest and tax (EBIT) 
margin, with profits subsequently adjusted for taxation 
when calculating ROIC.
These are demanding assumptions, but not 
necessarily unrealistic ones. The estimated USD 
2.3 trillion in annual revenue is comparable to the 
revenues generated by some of the largest sectors in 
the US economy. However, the ultimate addressable 
market could be considerably larger, particularly if 
AI increasingly substitutes for existing software and 
labour-intensive services. The question may therefore 
be less about whether sufficient demand exists, and 
more about how quickly that demand can be monetised 
and whether hyperscalers can capture enough of the 
resulting revenue at attractive margins. 
The total addressable market (TAM) represents the 
annual revenue opportunity available to an industry.
Our analysis suggests that the potential AI TAM could 
ultimately exceed USD 5.8 trillion (Exhibit 2), drawing 
on spending currently directed towards software, 
information technology (IT) services, labour intensive 
business processes and consumer applications. 
While this revenue opportunity is considerably larger 
than the USD 2.3 trillion of annual revenues required 
to support our investment assumptions, economic 
potential does not guarantee realised revenue. AI may 
deliver substantial productivity benefits without all of 
them being captured by AI providers, while the timing 
of adoption and the distribution of profits across the 
industry remain highly uncertain.
Bringing these three concepts together – capex, 
ROIC and TAM – highlights the importance of capital 
discipline for companies financing the AI buildout. 
Overspending on infrastructure risks materially diluting 
future returns, with our analysis suggesting that each 
additional USD 1 trillion of capex could reduce ROIC 
by several percentage points for a given revenue 
opportunity. Equally important is the pace and scale of 
AI monetisation.

Financing the AI buildout
For credit investors, the implications of disappointing returns will depend not only on the scale of capital deployed, 
but also on how that investment is financed.
Even for the world’s largest and most profitable technology companies, the scale of investment is beginning to 
exceed internally generated cash flow. Historically, hyperscalers have largely funded their expansion through 
operating cash flow, maintaining exceptionally strong balance sheets and relatively modest borrowing 
requirements. That model is now changing. Our analysis, drawing upon S&P Global Ratings’ forecasts, suggests 
that Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle could face a combined residual financing requirement of 
approximately USD 1.1 trillion between 2027 and 2029, after accounting for operating cash flow and investment 
commitments (Exhibit 4). This level of financing represents a substantial increase relative to their existing debt 
obligations and is driving greater reliance on capital markets.

Financing the AI buildout and its implications for credit markets
For 2026 alone, we have identified approximately USD 
560 billion of actual and expected funding through 
corporate debt, equity and structured financing markets 
for investment grade rated hyperscalers (Exhibit 5). 
This excludes other sources of financing, including bank 
lending and leases with third-party developers. 
A defining feature of this year’s capital markets has 
been the variety of instruments used to access different 
pools of investor demand. The funding mix already 
spans corporate debt, equity, investment grade data 
centre special purpose vehicles (SPVs) and chip-related 
SPVs. The latter two structures can provide financing 
outside the technology companies’ conventional 
corporate debt programmes, although associated 
leases, guarantees and contractual commitments may 
create significant financial obligations that require 
careful assessment.
While corporate investment grade debt will remain 
important, it is only one component, with bank lending, 
private credit, SPV and project bonds, securitisation, 
and equity all likely to play a role. Multicurrency issuance 
has also accelerated, with Amazon and Alphabet issuing 
record-sized bond deals in EUR, CAD and GBP markets, 
including Google-parent Alphabet’s landmark 100-year 
GBP tranche, and among the largest on record in JPY 
and CHF.
The result is that investors are now able to choose 
between liquid senior unsecured bonds and structured 
securities, whose credit outcomes depend on 
construction progress, lease protections, tenant quality 
and refinancing assumptions. This diversification in 
funding sources reduces reliance on any single market 
and creates a more varied investible universe.

The implications for credit quality
Importantly, debt financing forecasts of this nature 
assume infrastructure investment begins to pay off 
and capex can again be funded by internally generated 
cash flow in the near future. For example, within our 
forecast framework, the period covering 2028 and 
2029 is shaping up to be key, with aggregate free 
cash flow expected to recover significantly, with those 
hyperscalers currently experiencing negative free 
cash flow returning towards positive territory as new 
computing capacity becomes operational (Exhibit 6), 
reducing the financing burden on debt markets.
Until then, the additional debt raised by this cohort of 
companies will continue to push leverage closer to 
thresholds associated with downgrades. For the most 
part, though, these are companies with exceptionally 
strong starting balance sheets. Even one or two 
notches of rating agency downgrades would leave 
the likes of Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta 
comfortably in the A/AA categories. Oracle, though, 
has less flexibility than the rest of the investment grade 
rated hyperscalers, given its BBB rating.

Investment grade bonds are the engine financing the AI buildout
The investment grade bond market will play a central 
role in financing the AI buildout. Hyperscaler bond 
issuance has accelerated dramatically from USD 17 
billion in 2024 to USD 109 billion in 2025, and we expect 
around USD 280 billion in issuance globally in 2026. 
In terms of capacity, if we apply a soft cap of 2.5% 
per issuer, in line with the largest issuers in the 
investment grade index today, the US investment 
grade bond market could absorb approximately USD 
500 billion to USD 600 billion of additional supply, or 
approximately half the funding gap we estimate over 
the next three years. While this level of supply sounds 
like a substantial amount for one market to absorb, it is 
important to remember the investment grade corporate 
bond market has particularly broad shoulders. It is 
a USD 14 trillion market, with USD 10 trillion in USD-
denominated debt. 
Gross US investment grade corporate bond issuance is 
expected to exceed USD 2 trillion this year, reflecting the 
depth and breadth of demand for the asset class. 
Nevertheless, hyperscaler debt spreads have widened 
this year, which we attribute partly to indigestion as 
new supply prices, and partly to weaker fundamentals 
as leverage rises (Exhibit 7). Issuers do appear to be 
learning, and have begun providing clearer guidance 
on issuance cadence to prevent surprises. Alphabet is 
a good example: the company has indicated it intends 
to issue in US dollars twice a year, and its USD 25 billion 
August transaction was 4.4x oversubscribed. Demand 
for new issues remains generally strong, and we expect 
that to continue to be the case as companies disclose 
more about their funding needs, investment plans and 
the economics of building AI infrastructure.

Financing the AI buildout and its implications for credit markets
An expanding opportunity set for active credit investors
For active credit investors, the AI investment cycle is creating opportunities across both conventional corporate 
bonds and more specialised financing structures. We see three principal areas where security selection and 
fundamental research can add value:
1. Tactical opportunities in senior unsecured bonds: 
The substantial increase in hyperscaler issuance is 
creating tactical opportunities in senior unsecured 
bonds (Exhibit 8). New issues have generally offered 
attractive concessions to encourage investor 
participation, with bonds frequently outperforming 
in secondary markets following issuance. As supply 
continues to expand, we expect opportunities to 
emerge across issuers and maturities, particularly 
where spread widening reflects technical pressures 
rather than a material deterioration in credit 
fundamentals.
2. Additional yield capture from more complex structures: 
The growing market for data centre and AI chip-
backed financing offers opportunities to capture 
additional yield through more complex structures. 
These securities provide exposure to contractual 
commitments from technology companies, but 
require careful assessment of construction and 
execution risks, security arrangements, tenant 
guarantees and residual asset values. Chip-backed 
financing introduces additional risks associated with 
technological obsolescence and rapid depreciation 
of computing equipment. The substantial spread 
premium of data centre financing over conventional 
senior unsecured bonds can be attractive (Exhibit 9), 
although the risks are not directly comparable. 
While most of these deals are not yet index eligible, 
we expect these vehicles to become a growing source 
of AI buildout funding, with issuance of chip-backed 
financing in particular likely to increase over the 
coming year. We prefer deals with strong contractual 
protections and credit support, limited construction 
and execution risk, and sufficient spread compensation 
for structural complexity and residual asset risk. This 
area is where deep fundamental research can help 
determine whether the extra spread justifies the 
outsized risk.

3. Financing opportunities in the utilities sector via 
hybrids: Investment in electricity supply to data 
centres is creating financing opportunities across the 
utilities sector, including through hybrid securities. 
These instruments can offer attractive additional 
yield relative to senior unsecured bonds, while 
helping issuers manage their credit metrics through 
partial equity recognition from rating agencies. 
US energy and utility hybrids, for example, offer an 
option-adjusted spread premium of almost 100 basis 
points (bps) over the broader US investment grade 
corporate index. The key to investing in this sector is 
conviction in the issuer. Where we have confidence in 
a strong investment grade credit, the subordination 
can be a risk worth taking. However, subordination, 
coupon-deferral provisions and extension risk make 
careful security selection essential.

Conclusion: Navigating the impact of the AI buildout on credit markets
The AI revolution may ultimately prove as transformative as its proponents anticipate, but technological 
success does not guarantee attractive financial returns for every participant. The extraordinary scale of 
investment required will test the economics of the industry and the ability of companies involved to balance 
ambitious growth plans with financial discipline. 
The financing of this investment cycle will have equally significant implications for credit markets. While we 
believe the market has the capacity to absorb the substantial increase in borrowing, the scale and diversity of 
financing requirements will reshape the investment grade universe. Alongside traditional corporate debt, we 
expect a growing role for project-specific financing, asset-backed structures and other forms of capital that 
distribute risks across different investors. These structures will expand the investment grade opportunity set, 
but also make it more complex to establish where the risks ultimately reside. 
The result is that the AI buildout is likely to be as consequential for the evolution of credit markets as it is 
for the technology sector itself. The challenge for investors is to determine which securities offer adequate 
compensation for the risks being assumed. This is an environment where active management is essential. 
As the opportunity set expands, fundamental credit research will become increasingly important in 
distinguishing between issuers and financing structures, understanding where risks ultimately reside and 
identifying those securities that offer the most attractive compensation for the risks assumed.
