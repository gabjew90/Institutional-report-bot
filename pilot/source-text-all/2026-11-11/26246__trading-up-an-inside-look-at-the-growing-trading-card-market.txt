Trading up: An inside look at
the growing trading card
market
David Bellinger
Senior Analyst, Consumer
Hardlines & Consumer Internet
Investor interest in alternative asset classes continues to rise, and the
trading card market is one we see undergoing a major transition from a
hobbyist hideout to a multibillion-dollar opportunity. This transformation has
garnered notice from companies across industries.
At Mizuho, we estimate the U.S. trading card market represents a $10 billion
to $20 billion opportunity, with annual sales exceeding $1 billion at Target
and Walmart. At Costco, management has indicated that trading cards
generate some of the highest gross margins across its product assortment.
Interest has also expanded beyond traditional categories like baseball and
other sports-related cards into Pokémon, Magic: The Gathering, Star Wars,
and Disney character cards. At Hasbro, management described trading cards
as the “hottest category in all of toys and games.” At eBay, management
has referenced a “long runway for secular growth” against the backdrop of
accelerating sales across several categories.
Like many investments that attract outsized fan attention, the risk of a
boom-and-bust cycle for trading cards is real, but we don’t believe the

dynamics that felled Labubus or squishy dumplings, among other recent
fads, are in play for trading cards at this time.
A growing collector base and multigenerational appeal gives this category
an evergreen growth aspect, in our view, and we believe three key features
of the trading card market – nostalgia, innovation and a diverse ecosystem –
make this is a unique opportunity for enthusiasts, hobbyists, and investors
alike.
The appeal of nostalgia
For many collectors, trading cards are a connection to childhood. Millennials
are among the collectors drawn by nostalgia and, as they’ve moved into
their higher-earning years, they have more disposable income to spend on
the hobbies from their younger years. A case in point: The average Magic:
The Gathering player is 35 years old, according to Hasbro.
As longstanding franchises continue to attract established collectors, they
are also introducing new audiences to the hobby. Pokémon, for example,
appeals to adults who grew up with the franchise as well as children
discovering it for the first time. Sports cards benefit from a similar dynamic,
with new athletes and memorable performances creating fresh reasons to
collect.
This multigenerational appeal is one reason we believe trading cards have
more staying power than many collectibles driven primarily by short-lived
trends. New collectors enter the market even as established enthusiasts
deepen their participation.
Innovation: Reinventing the collector experience
Innovative products, platforms, and real-time events have transformed
collecting.
On platforms such as Whatnot and eBay, collectors can participate in
livestreamed breaks, where hosts open sealed boxes of cards while viewers
purchase spots or shares in the contents. The format combines shopping,
entertainment, and the thrill of the chase. Collectors can also participate

without necessarily buying an entire box themselves. To fund its expansion,
Whatnot raised $545 million at a reported $20 billion valuation earlier this
year.
Manufacturers are finding new ways to extend engagement beyond the
initial sale. Fanatics, which acquired Topps in 2022, has helped introduce
programs connecting cards to real-world sporting achievements. Through
Topps' MVP Buyback program, for example, collectors can exchange eligible
cards of award-winning players for credit toward new purchases.
Live experiences are another engagement engine. Fanatics Fest, which
brings together cards, brands and athletes, drew more than 200,000
attendees in 2026, up from 125,000 in 2025.
Supporting industries such as grading have also soared. PSA is one of the
industry’s leading grading services. In 2025, the company graded more than
19 million cards, up from just 2 million five years earlier. In 2026, PSA
announced a $200 million investment to expand its operations, technology
and workforce to meet demand.
Innovation has also expanded the retail experience, with new ways to buy
cards and pursue scarcity. Hanger packs, which are larger than regular
packs, are typically sold as an entry-level product, with no guarantee of
autograph or relic cards. At the higher end, there are hobby boxes geared to
dedicated collectors. These boxes, which usually come with a guarantee of
autograph or relic cards – some with one per box or multiple per box – have
premium price points of $100-$1,000 or more.  
Manufacturers have also learned from the past how to better manage
scarcity. During the junk wax era of the late 1980s and early 1990s,
overproduction undermined scarcity and retail value.
Today, manufacturers offer accessible products alongside limited parallels,
individually numbered editions and one-of-one cards. This allows casual
collectors to participate at accessible price points while creating the
possibility of discovering something a product that is exclusive or rare.
A diverse, expanding ecosystem

Perhaps the clearest indication of the market's evolution is the range of
businesses now participating in it. For investors, there’s opportunity across
industries and multiple entry points. Retailers are benefiting from product
launches and repeat purchases; manufacturers and rights holders from
licensing and product design; marketplaces from transactions and related
services, and authentication firms from grading and verification.
Even cryptocurrency platforms are taking an interest. Coinbase signaled
plans in 2026 to let users open trading-card packs on their phones, with
each pull backed by a physical card that could be stored or shipped. The
concept points to another potential convergence of digital commerce,
physical assets, and collecting.
A market with staying power
For trading cards, we expect momentum to continue into 2027 and beyond,
supported by new releases, digital innovation, and in-person experiences.
Industry watchers are now considering trading cards as an alternative asset
class.
Still, risks remain. Speculative pricing, counterfeit cards, oversupply and
changing consumer tastes could all undermine parts of the market, and
participation doesn’t guarantee success for every business. We have seen
some froth at the high end of the market - in 2026, a PSA-graded 10 Pikachu
Illustrator card sold for $16.5 million, setting a trading card auction record –
but we don’t believe it’s a pervasive issue.
For investors, the opportunity isn’t only that people are willing to pay
extraordinary prices for rare cards, but rather than an increasingly
sophisticated ecosystem that supports the business of collecting continues
to create new pathways for growth and participation.
