Nuclear Energy
Initiating Coverage of Advanced Nuclear Energy Sector
  
The Wedbush View. We initiate coverage on the advanced nuclear energy sector
with a strongly positive view. Advanced nuclear shares fell 41-65% year to date
(YTD) on AI-buildout concerns and a 10-year Treasury yield above 5%, creating an
attractive entry point. Our five fission names trade at enterprise values (EV) of just
$6m-228m per GW of their largest disclosed pipelines, or 0.1-3% of the Department
of Energy's (DOE) first-of-a-kind (FOAK) build cost estimate. A higher cost of capital
cannot explain a discount this deep; the stocks discount very few reactors ever
reaching operation.
AI and electrification have created a potential multi-decade investment
supercycle in nuclear energy. DOE projects ~100 GW of new U.S. peak demand
by 2030, half from data centers, and Amazon, Google, Meta and Microsoft have
announced over 20 GW of nuclear deals. Govt. targets call for 400 GW of U.S.
nuclear capacity by 2050, up from ~97 GW today. We size U.S. new-build capital
spending at $350b-1.05t through 2050, with a $1.8t upside case at FOAK costs.
New designs change the economics and the risk profile of nuclear power. Small
modular reactors (SMRs) and microreactors shift construction from megaprojects
to factory-built modules, which cuts upfront capital, speeds deployment and
widens siting options. Passive safety systems that rely on physics rather than pumps
or operators sharply reduce accident risk. In the U.S., the Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) finalized its technology-inclusive Part 53 rule in March 2026 and
has issued five advanced reactor and SMR construction permits since 2023.
We frame the sector around six debates and side with the bulls on most. We see
the midterm risk as indirect. Bipartisan support (393-13 for the ADVANCE Act) limits
policy risk; the exposure is sentiment toward high-beta, AI-linked stocks if a sweep
by Democrats raises data center fears. Tax credits under Sections 45Y and 48E pay
in full for nuclear projects that start construction by the end of 2033. High-assay
low-enriched uranium (HALEU) supply and FOAK project financing remain the two
largest constraints, and DOE's $2.7b enrichment awards and loan program help
address both.
We rate Oklo (OKLO), X-energy (XE) and NANO Nuclear (NNE) OUTPERFORM.
OKLO ($36 price target, PT) built Groves to criticality in 229 days; XE ($17 PT) pairs
a pending Long Mott construction permit with the first HALEU fuel fabrication
license; NNE ($18 PT) trades near cash with a CPA under NRC review. We rate
Terrestrial Energy (IMSR, $4 PT) and General Fusion (GFUZ, $7 PT) NEUTRAL, and
NuScale (SMR, $5 PT) UNDERPERFORM on its lack of a binding customer.

Executive Summary 
We see a rare entry point. We initiate coverage of the advanced nuclear sector at what we believe 
marks a cyclical low for the group and a structural inflection for the industry. Three forces drive our 
positive view: rising demand for clean, firm electricity from AI data centers and electrification; 
improving economics as designs move from megaprojects to factory-built modules; and the most 
supportive regulatory and policy environment for nuclear power in the U.S. in five decades. 
Together, these forces create what we view as a potential multi-decade investment supercycle in 
nuclear energy. 
Two powerful stories converge. The sector sits at the crossroads of a policy-backed nuclear revival 
and an AI boom starving for clean, around-the-clock baseload power. Economic growth increasingly 
depends on energy supply: the PJM Interconnection's 2027/28 capacity auction cleared at its 
$333.44/MW-day cap and still fell 6,623 MW short of its reliability requirement. Over the past year, 
markets and policymakers have broadly recognized that only a massive expansion of nuclear 
capacity can meet demand for clean, firm and low-cost power, and that the winning plants will be 
smaller, modular and capable of supplying high-temperature heat. 
The selloff creates an attractive entry point. Our six names fell 41-65% YTD and 60-88% from their 
52-week highs, with an additional 17-32% decline over the past month. Concern over AI capital 
spending, a September Federal Reserve rate hike and a 10-year Treasury yield above 5% explain 
part of the move. However, a 200bp increase in the discount rate cuts the present value of a cash 
flow ten years out by only ~16%. The rest of the decline, in our view, reflects a market that now 
prices in very few reactors getting built. 
Valuations embed almost no pipeline conversion. On our analysis, the market assigns OKLO 
~$111m of EV per GW of disclosed pipeline, XE ~$228m and NNE ~$6m, against DOE's FOAK 
overnight cost estimate of $6-10b per GW. NNE and IMSR trade at market caps near the cash on 
their balance sheets. We believe investors with a three- to five-year horizon can buy regulatory 
progress, fuel supply progress and hyperscaler offtake at a deep discount today. 
We favor OKLO, XE and NNE. OKLO offers the best demonstrated execution in the group, having 
taken Groves from groundbreaking to criticality in 229 days, and a three-segment platform 
spanning power, fuel recycling and isotopes. XE holds pole position among high-temperature gas 
reactor developers: the NRC completed the Long Mott final safety evaluation on October 30, 2026, 
and its TRISO-X subsidiary holds the first NRC Category II fuel fabrication license. NNE trades at a 
~$45m EV despite a construction permit application under NRC review and ~$581m of cash and 
investments. 
We stand aside on IMSR and GFUZ and sit below consensus on SMR. IMSR has the strongest 
regulatory record among Generation IV designs and avoids HALEU, but none of its ~7.8 GW pipeline 
is binding. GFUZ offers the only public pure-play exposure to fusion, the “holy grail” of energy 
abundance, though the hardest physics milestones lie ahead. NuScale holds the only NRC-certified 
SMR design, yet it has never signed a binding, financed module order, and its commercialization 
depends on a single partner, ENTRA1 Energy.

The prize through 2050 could be enormous. We size U.S. nuclear new-build capital spending at 
$350b in our low case, $700b in our medium case and $1.05t in our high case through 2050, or up 
to $1.8t at FOAK-level costs, before fuel and services. Government targets of 400 GW of U.S. 
capacity by 2050 and global forecasts of 561-992 GWe by 2050 frame a multi-decade opportunity. 
Fuel alone represents a large adjacent market: TRISO fuel demand could reach ~112 MTU per year 
by 2035 on Wood Mackenzie's mid-case forecast. 
Catalysts cluster in the next 6-12 months. We expect the Long Mott (Dow/XE) construction permit 
and the Part 57 microreactor rule in early 2027. The midterm elections on November 3 could 
remove a sentiment overhang. Binding offtake agreements, fuel contracts and DOE financing 
announcements offer additional upside, in our view. Our five fission names trade at an average of 
~$90m of EV per GW of disclosed pipeline, against ~$2.2b per GW of operating capacity for 
Constellation (CEG), ~$1.5b for Vistra (VST) and the ~$730m per GW Vistra agreed to pay for the 
Cogentrix gas fleet. Even allowing for the gap between pipeline and operating megawatts, we 
believe each milestone can drive a sharp re-rating.

Four drivers explain the decline. We attribute the selloff to macro pressure layered on top of 
sector-specific supply and timing issues: 
1. Higher rates: The Federal Reserve raised its policy rate 25bp to 3.75-4.00% on September 16, 
2026, its first hike since 2023, and the 10-year Treasury yield rose above 5.2% after a hot 
September consumer price report on October 14. OKLO, SMR and NNE each fell 9-10% that 
day. 
2. AI capex anxiety: Alphabet raised its 2026 capital spending guidance to $195-205b in July, 
reviving questions over how hyperscalers fund the buildout, and semiconductor stocks 
suffered an eight-session losing streak in October. 
3. Equity supply: OKLO launched a new $1b at-the-market (ATM) program on September 11, SMR 
filed a $750m ATM on August 11, and XE released its initial public offering (IPO) lock-up early 
on September 1. 
4. Slipping commercial milestones: The ENTRA1/Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) agreement for 
up to 6 GW of NuScale modules remains non-binding, and XE pushed the first Amazon-linked 
groundbreaking into 2027.

from 14% to 16% lowers the present value of a cash flow ten years out by ~16%, and of one fifteen 
years out by ~23%. Our coverage fell 60-88% from 52-week highs. In our view, the gap between 
those figures shows that investors now assume most announced projects never reach commercial

Debate #1: Does an AI Capex Slowdown Break the Nuclear Demand Case? 
We side with the bulls. Bears argue that slowing AI capital spending would strand the nuclear 
buildout before the first SMR fleet reaches operation. We disagree. U.S. power demand now 
outruns supply in nearly every forecast, the shortfall extends well beyond data centers, and nuclear 
offers the only clean, firm source that can scale. In our view, investors conflate the timing risk of 
individual data center projects with the far longer arc of electricity demand growth. 
Demand Forecasts Point to a Structural Break After Two Flat Decades 
U.S. electricity demand grew less than 1% annually for a decade after the 2008 financial crisis. That 
era has ended. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) estimates data centers consumed 
176 TWh in 2023, or 4.4% of U.S. electricity, and forecasts 325-580 TWh by 2028. Our March 2026 
Power 30 report projected 470 TWh by 2030, a 16% compound annual growth rate from 2025. U.S. 
electricity consumption set a record in 2025, and the Energy Information Administration (EIA) 
expects new records in 2026 and 2027.

Economic growth now depends on energy supply. Interconnection queues held ~2,061 GW of 
projects at the end of 2025, and gas turbine delivery slots now extend toward 2031. Power scarcity 
already shows up in prices: PJM's last two capacity auctions cleared at or near the cap and still 
missed reliability targets. The AI buildout adds to, rather than drives, this shortage, as reshoring, 
electrification of industry and transportation, and grid retirements of ~104 GW by 2030 (per DOE) 
all require new firm capacity. 
Physics favors nuclear for AI loads. AI training clusters run near continuously and draw 10-50x the 
power density of conventional cloud infrastructure. Nuclear plants operate at capacity factors 
above 90%, with no fuel price exposure and no carbon emissions. Solar and wind need costly 
storage to match that profile, and natural gas adds emissions that conflict with hyperscalers' 
carbon-free commitments. In our view, gas serves as a bridge through ~2030, while nuclear 
supplies the long-duration baseload that data center owners want under 15- to 20-year contracts.

Industrial heat adds a second demand pillar. High-temperature reactors can supply process steam 
and heat directly to chemical plants, refineries and hydrogen production, a market that light water 
reactors cannot serve well. Dow and XE submitted the first construction permit application for a 
commercial advanced reactor at an industrial site, a four-unit, 320 MWe Xe-100 plant at Dow's 
Seadrift, Texas complex. XE estimates its serviceable industrial market at ~$500b by 2040 and ~$1t 
by 2050, and the NRC completed its environmental review of the project with a finding of no 
significant impact in May 2026. 
Ratepayer politics favor on-site generation. The House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act (H.R. 
9340) 417-3 on September 16, 2026. The bill directs state regulators to consider standards that 
make data centers of 100 MW or more cover the full incremental cost of the grid upgrades they 
require. XE rose ~12% the next day, though the Senate blocked a cloture vote 57-43 on September 
30. Even without passage, we believe the policy direction raises the relative appeal of co-located 
and behind-the-meter nuclear, a structural positive for SMR and microreactor developers. 
Sizing the Opportunity Through 2040 and 2050 
The U.S. operates the world's largest nuclear fleet but relies on it far less than other leading nuclear 
nations. Its 94 reactors and ~97 GW of capacity produce ~30% of global nuclear generation, yet 
nuclear supplied only ~18% of U.S. electricity in 2025, less than half the ~41% from natural gas and 
roughly level with coal at ~17% (Figure 8). Measured against total primary energy, which also 
counts oil for transport and gas for heating, nuclear supplied ~8.5% of U.S. energy, versus ~40% in 
France, ~26% in Sweden, ~23% in Finland and ~15% in South Korea (Figure 9). The gap reflects four 
decades with almost no new construction rather than any limit of the technology: France built most

of its fleet in 15 years, while U.S. capacity has barely changed since 1990. Raising nuclear to ~25% 
of U.S. electricity, still far below France's ~68% in 2025, would require ~40 GW of new capacity at 
today's demand and a ~92% capacity factor, before any load growth from data centers. The 400 
GW target for 2050 implies roughly quadrupling the fleet, which we view as the scale of catch-up 
the new build cycle must deliver.

Debate #2: Do Uprates, Gas and Large Reactors Crowd Out SMRs? 
We see near-term competition, not long-term substitution. Bears argue that uprates and restarts 
at existing plants, new gas turbines and Westinghouse's AP1000 will capture the demand before 
SMRs reach operation. We agree that these options win the race to 2030 because they offer faster

capital or process heat. We expect all four to grow together through 2050.

Debate #5: Is HALEU the Binding Constraint? 
HALEU constrains growth through ~2030, but we view the problem as solvable. Most Generation 
IV designs require high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU), enriched to 5-20% U-235, versus less 
than 5% for the existing fleet. Russia supplied nearly all commercial HALEU until the U.S. banned 
Russian uranium imports in 2024, with waivers ending no later than January 1, 2028. DOE projects 
HALEU demand of more than 40 metric tons (MT) by 2030. Domestic supply today amounts to 
Centrus's demonstration cascade in Piketon, Ohio, which has produced more than 1,900 kg 
cumulatively, enough for only a handful of first cores. 
Licensing gives Centrus an early-mover advantage. Centrus and Urenco hold the only two NRC 
licenses for commercial uranium enrichment in the U.S., although only Urenco's Eunice, New 
Mexico plant produces commercial low-enriched uranium (LEU) today. Centrus holds the only NRC 
license to enrich up to 20%, the HALEU range. Urenco received authorization to enrich up to 10% 
in December 2024, which covers LEU+ designs such as NNE's 9.9% KRONOS fuel. 
New American entrants target the market. General Matter, backed by Peter Thiel's Founders Fund, 
plans a ~$1.5b HALEU plant on a DOE-leased site at Paducah, Kentucky, and won a $900m DOE task 
order in January 2026. Global Laser Enrichment (GLE), owned 51% by Silex Systems and 49% by 
Cameco, applied for an NRC license for a laser enrichment plant at Paducah, received a draft 
environmental impact statement in March 2026 and targets completion by 2030. Orano plans a 
~$5b LEU plant in Oak Ridge, Tennessee. LIS Technologies, a laser enrichment developer led by NNE

Industry History: Lessons From Eight Decades of Nuclear Power 
1942-1957: The Science Proves Itself 
The story begins not with a power plant but with a controlled experiment. On December 2, 1942, 
Enrico Fermi and his team achieved the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction in Chicago Pile-
1, beneath the University of Chicago's Stagg Field. That experiment showed that scientists could 
release the energy locked inside atomic nuclei in a controlled, measurable and reproducible way. 
The U.S. Navy, not civilian engineers, forged the path from physics to commercial power. Admiral 
Hyman Rickover chose the pressurized water reactor (PWR) for naval propulsion after the sodium-
cooled USS Seawolf proved problematic relative to the PWR-powered USS Nautilus, which put to 
sea in January 1954. President Eisenhower's Atoms for Peace initiative then carried the same 
technology into civilian use. The Shippingport Atomic Power Station, the first full-scale commercial 
nuclear plant in the U.S., began operating in December 1957 as a direct adaptation of the naval 
design. 
The industry defaulted to the light water reactor for pragmatic rather than optimal reasons. The 
Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) evaluated as many as 80 reactor concepts in 1953, and many 
experts favored the liquid metal fast breeder for long-term economics. However, the PWR was the 
most ready to build and had an institutional anchor in the Navy. Seventy years later, every licensed 
commercial reactor in the U.S. still uses a light water design, and the NRC's decades of light water 
precedent remain an asset that non-light water developers cannot easily replicate. 
1958-1979: The Build Cycle and the French Lesson 
The 1960s and 1970s brought rapid scaling. The AEC projected more than 1,000 U.S. reactors by 
2000, and construction permits averaged more than 12 per year between 1967 and 1978. Utilities 
ordered plants across the country on the promise of stable fuel costs, high capacity factors and 
energy independence. 
France provides the most instructive case study. The 1973 oil crisis hit a country that relied on 
imported oil for ~70% of its energy needs. On March 6, 1974, Prime Minister Pierre Messmer 
announced a plan to install 50 GW of nuclear capacity, building six to seven reactors annually. 
France then built 56 standardized PWRs between 1974 and 1989, each from a Westinghouse-
licensed design built by a dedicated industrial ecosystem with a common manufacturing base. By 
the 1990s, nuclear supplied ~75% of French electricity, and it still supplied ~68% in 2025. 
Construction costs per kWe doubled over the program's life as designs grew in complexity, but U.S. 
costs quadrupled over the same period. The lesson for the current cycle concerns standardization 
rather than state mandates: the first reactor in a series bears almost all of the design, licensing and 
learning cost, while later identical units become cheaper and faster to build. That insight underpins 
the modern SMR and microreactor business model. 
1979-2023: Accidents, Stagnation and Regulatory Accretion 
The U.S. experience turned darker. The 1979 Three Mile Island accident caused no deaths and 
released little radiation, but its regulatory and psychological impact stopped new orders. Utilities 
canceled more than 100 ordered reactors, and the NRC approved no new commercial reactor 
construction from 1978 to 2012. Chernobyl in 1986, a Soviet RBMK design with no containment 
 
www.wedbush.com
Page 24

 
 
structure, pushed U.S. public support for new nuclear construction from 69% in 1977 to 34%. 
Fukushima in 2011 reversed a nascent recovery. 
Reactor technology kept improving through the stagnation. Generation III+ designs such as the 
Westinghouse AP1000 added passive safety, digital controls and simplified engineering, and China 
brought four AP1000s online ahead of the U.S. The bottleneck lay in the licensing and financial 
framework, which accumulated requirements for 40 years and priced new nuclear out of 
competition with shale gas. Vogtle Units 3 and 4 cost ~$35b and finished seven years behind 
schedule. The project showed that the megaproject model cannot finance the next generation of 
capacity. 
The stagnation also eroded the supply chain. Specialized fabricators, nuclear-grade component 
suppliers, qualified welders and experienced operators aged out of the workforce. The U.S. has no 
domestic capacity for the ultra-large forgings that large reactors require, and Japan Steel Works 
dominates that global market. Small, modular designs sidestep part of that constraint because they 
use smaller components that a broader supplier base can produce. 
2024-Present: The Reset 
The ADVANCE Act, passed in July 2024 with overwhelming bipartisan support, marked the first 
systemic attempt to reverse regulatory accretion since the Energy Reorganization Act of 1974. Four 
executive orders in May 2025 turned that reform into hard deadlines and targets, and hyperscalers 
shifted from passive power buyers to direct funders of new reactors. In our view, regulatory reform, 
firm policy targets and private capital together mark the start of a new build cycle. 
The executive orders set four priorities. First, they direct the NRC to decide on new reactor 
applications within 18 months and to overhaul its rules. Second, they created DOE's Reactor Pilot 
Program, which targeted three test reactors reaching criticality by July 4, 2026. Third, they call for 
a reactor at a domestic military installation by September 30, 2028 and for advanced reactors at 
DOE sites to power AI data centers. Fourth, they target 400 GW of U.S. capacity by 2050, 10 large 
reactors under construction by 2030 and 5 GW of uprates. Together, the orders give developers 
firm timelines and federal anchor demand.

Fusion: Long-Dated Optionality With Accelerating Public Support 
Fusion offers optionality, not near-term revenue. Fusion is the “holy grail” of energy abundance: 
its main fuel, deuterium, can be drawn from seawater in near-limitless supply, and it produces no 
long-lived high-level waste and carries no risk of a runaway chain reaction. If commercialized, it 
could provide virtually unlimited clean, firm power. That promise remains unproven, however, as 
no machine has yet produced net electricity. The investment case differs from fission: longer 
timelines, higher binary risk and asymmetric upside if the physics works. GFUZ offers the only public 
pure-play exposure today, and TAE Technologies plans to list through a pending all-stock merger 
with Trump Media & Technology Group (DJT), for which the companies filed an amended S-4 on 
October 26, 2026.

The Case for Our OUTPERFORM Names 
Oklo (OKLO, OUTPERFORM, $36 PT) leads the race to criticality. OKLO took its privately funded, 
privately sited Groves isotope test reactor in Texas from groundbreaking to first criticality on August 
5, 2026 in 229 days, the fastest build of its kind on record. Meta anchors the 1.2 GW Aurora-Ohio 
campus with a prepayment, and DOE selected Groves 2 for its Nuclear Energy Launch Pad in 
October. OKLO holds ~$3.0b of liquidity, though we expect more than $10b of capital spending 
through 2036 and ongoing dilution. At ~$111m of EV per pipeline GW, we see a compelling entry 
point. 
X-energy (XE, OUTPERFORM, $17 PT) holds pole position in high-temperature gas reactors. XE 
pairs an Xe-100 design in active NRC construction permit review with the first new NRC-licensed 
commercial fuel fabrication facility in more than 50 years. The NRC completed its Long Mott safety 
evaluation on October 30, with a construction permit guided for early 2027. Amazon acts as both 
equity investor and customer, with a 5 GW target, an Energy Northwest project and a new 960 MW 
Indiana project with AEP. XE holds ~$1.9b of cash and no debt, and DOE covers ~50% of its ARDP 
scope. 
NANO Nuclear (NNE, OUTPERFORM, $18 PT) offers a licensing lead at a cash-backed price. At 
$11.65, NNE trades near its ~$10.81 per share of cash, leaving only ~$45m of EV for a microreactor 
program that leads its peers on licensing. The NRC accepted the KRONOS construction permit

Why We Stand Aside on IMSR and GFUZ and Rate SMR UNDERPERFORM 
Terrestrial Energy (IMSR, NEUTRAL, $4 PT) needs binding contracts. IMSR's design became the 
first Generation IV reactor to clear Phase 2 of the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission's Vendor

its $2.68 of cash per share, and the Company announced a $25m buyback on October 27. However, 
none of the ~7.8 GW pipeline, led by a 4 GW Riot Platforms memorandum of understanding, is 
binding. Conversion of any memorandum to a contract would make us more constructive. 
General Fusion (GFUZ, NEUTRAL, $7 PT) faces its hardest physics ahead. GFUZ offers the only 
public pure-play exposure to fusion and reached the first of LM26's three technical milestones, 1+ 
keV, with the UK Atomic Energy Authority in October 2026. Its liquid lithium wall avoids large 
superconducting magnets and lasers. However, the 10 keV and Lawson milestones remain ahead, 
~92% SPAC redemptions left the Company with ~$150m of cash, and a January 2027 lock-up expiry 
releases ~47m shares. 
NuScale (SMR, UNDERPERFORM, $5 PT) still lacks a first customer. NuScale holds the only NRC 
design certification for an SMR, yet it has never signed a binding, financed module order in its 19-
year history. Its commercialization depends on ENTRA1 Energy, whose non-binding framework with 
TVA covers up to 6 GW, and Romania's RoPower project has stalled with more than 80% of 
conditions unmet. A $495m milestone payment to ENTRA1 on a non-binding agreement underlies 
pending securities litigation. We forecast later module deliveries and lower margins than 
consensus.

Key Risks to Our Sector View 
• FOAK cost and schedule overruns: Nuclear construction has a long record of overruns, and 
early SMR units could cost more than developers target, as NuScale's Carbon Free Power 
Project showed when its target price rose from $58/MWh to $89/MWh before cancellation. 
• Dilution and funding risk: Every covered company remains pre-revenue or near pre-revenue, 
and most will need additional equity to fund FOAK projects; ATM programs at OKLO and SMR 
already weigh on the shares. 
• HALEU and fuel supply: Delays at Centrus, General Matter or other enrichers could push back 
first cores for HALEU-dependent designs. 
• Regulatory and litigation risk: NRC staff constraints, first-of-a-kind reviews or legal challenges 
to DOE's pilot program could slow approvals. 
• Policy risk: A future Congress could alter tax credits, FEOC rules or DOE funding, and the 2033 
construction-start deadline creates a cliff for delayed projects. 
• Demand risk: A sharp slowdown in AI investment could delay hyperscaler offtake 
commitments, even if it does not reverse long-term power demand growth. 
• Safety and public acceptance: An accident anywhere in the world could reverse public and 
political support for nuclear power. 
• Governance and listing mechanics: Several companies listed through SPACs, with 
redemptions, warrant overhangs, lock-up expiries and related-party arrangements; the 
pending TAE/DJT merger carries additional disclosure considerations.
