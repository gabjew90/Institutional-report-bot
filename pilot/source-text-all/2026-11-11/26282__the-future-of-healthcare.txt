Future of healthcare: 
Extinction or accelerated 
evolution?
Shapers of healthcare’s future must evolve under relentless economic 
pressure. To avoid extinction, they must harness AI and biopharma 
breakthroughs to innovate clinical and business models.
by Shubham Singhal
November 2026

Key Insights
—
Innovating with AI presents the opportunity to simultaneously enhance access, affordability, 
experience, and quality of care.
—
Payers can transform from transaction processors to enablers of personalized, efficient 
care.
—
A tenfold higher access to high-quality primary care is possible.
—
Consumer-focused and outcomes-based models can expand access to breakthrough 
therapies.
—
Health services and technology companies can accelerate value by transforming critical 
workflows with AI.
—
Health systems can see higher productivity and asset utilization.
—
The challenge is in navigating the exponential speed of AI advancement, the linear speed of 
core technology modernization, and the uneven speed of organizational change.
More than 500 million years ago, temperatures rose by up to 6 degrees Celsius, making Earth 
inhospitable to many forms of life. Still, leaps in genetic developments occurred and oxygen 
levels doubled. This Cambrian period of Earth’s history saw an accelerated evolution of the most 
disparate life forms ever in our planet’s history. All species today descended from life forms that 
originated in this explosion.1
The healthcare industry2 stands at a similar evolutionary inflection point. The industry is well into 
its sixth year of a harsh operating environment—costs are rising and reimbursement is falling. All 
indicators suggest the temperature will stay elevated for the foreseeable future.
At the same time, the AI and biopharma revolutions are providing once-in-a-generation 
breakthroughs. Healthcare consumers, clinicians, and industry stakeholders are rapidly adopting 
AI, with more than 40 percent of physicians using OpenEvidence daily3 (Exhibit 1). And drug 
development is accelerating.4 Advances in life-saving treatments, such as those for metabolic 
disorders, are redefining chronic condition management,5 while programmable medicine through 
cell, gene, and mRNA platforms, as well as AI-enabled personalized diagnoses and treatments, 
are scaling rapidly. McKinsey Global Institute research shows that emerging technology arenas 
(such as AI, robotics, and bio-frontiers) are capturing a majority of value creation across 
industries, and healthcare should be no exception.
Additionally, healthcare is among the top three destinations for private capital, with an estimated 
$330 billion to $440 billion available to deploy.6 The industry is also a top destination for

So, are we on the cusp of an explosion of healthcare innovation that will spawn great new 
business models that define success for the future? It’s important to acknowledge that change 
in healthcare is tough—appropriately so given that people’s lives are at stake. The healthcare 
industry employs more than 20 million people, and innovation will require most of them to do 
their jobs differently and learn new skills. Concerns about AI-driven job loss abound, but 
demand for healthcare services is rising and access remains inadequate. Therefore, we expect 
healthcare employment is poised to grow rather than contract. Managing this transition requires 
care. Clinical safeguards and regulations are essential to ensuring the safety of new treatments 
and the use of AI. Equally important is the ability of healthcare organizations to implement

In this article, we offer five provocations for practical avenues toward a diverse set of business 
models. This is not meant to be a comprehensive list of all potential innovations, but rather a 
source of inspiration for leaders.
Reimagine the healthcare payer
Healthcare payers create networks, define and enforce rules, set prices, and process payments. 
AI can do many of the tasks embedded in those functions, or prompt their complete 
reorganization or even obviation. The impact will look different across three categories of payer 
work: transaction-oriented work, knowledge-oriented work, and relationship or management 
work.
Start with transaction-oriented work. At a typical payer, 65 to 80 percent of jobs are transaction 
oriented, such as claims processing; AI could fully automate all or almost all of these jobs, based 
on our analysis.7
The bigger question is: Do we need claims at all in a world where universal electronic medical 
records exist and AI can review that data? Medical records are a far closer representation of 
patient needs and services rendered than a claim, which is ultimately a billing artifact derived 
from clinical documentation. AI agents could efficiently analyze the appropriateness of 
treatment and diagnosis coding, as well as assess clinician performance, safety, quality, and 
more by evaluating longitudinal medical records against medical policies, reimbursement 
policies, and health insurance benefits and then make accurate payments.
In such a world, a payer is still necessary—not merely as a claims processor but as the platform 
that integrates data with intelligence to enable better health and care delivery. A good analogy 
comes from modern-day stock exchanges, which have gone beyond simply completing trades. 
Stock exchanges create indexes that can be used to develop investment products and provide a 
variety of security solutions to enable asset managers and traders to operate more effectively. 
Such a business model for payers would derive its value from pushing down transaction costs to 
a de minimis level and generating revenue from new data-driven, value-added products and 
services to enable better health and improved care delivery.
Next, consider knowledge-oriented work. Another 10 to 25 percent of jobs at a payer are 
knowledge oriented, such as pricing actuaries and medical management nurses.8 AI could 
deliver, in our estimation, as much as 50 percent greater efficiency in these jobs by handling 
tasks such as looking up information, cleaning and collating data, and taking notes. More 
important, these knowledge-oriented jobs, reimagined, could massively improve healthcare by 
helping payers live up to their role as orchestrators of the healthcare system.

The disaggregation and reallocation of medical risk to create risk-aligned funding models 
and sophisticated payment models.9 For example, in cancer care, a payer could carve out 
cancer risk from an employer-sponsored plan and create a shared-risk model across the 
value chain: payers for population and severity risk, the oncologist for treatment pathway 
selection risk, related clinicians for whole-person care and complications risk, the health 
system for procedural risk, and the pharmaceutical company for therapeutic safety and 
efficacy risk. Similarly, for patients with chronic diseases, a payer could create a risk-
impaired annuity model—one in which funding is calibrated to the patient’s chronic-
condition risk and spread over time—with outcome risk shared across patients (for example, 
lifestyle behavior and treatment adherence), primary care physicians and the broader care 
team, relevant specialists, the pharma company, and the payer.
—
The enablement of “n of 1” healthcare delivery. Payers are uniquely placed to create a full 
view of the consumer. The opportunity is to extend beyond formal medical care to having a 
fuller view of each consumer by becoming the engagement platform through which they 
take care of their health. This would go far beyond medical care alone and encompass 
fitness, sleep, nutrition, mental health, spiritual health, and more. Through such an 
engagement platform, consumers could access a variety of health and well-being solutions, 
access formal medical care when needed, and connect with appropriate clinicians and 
treatments, confident that they are achieving the best possible care. The platform would 
need consent, privacy and data governance, and boundaries on data use.
Finally, the remaining jobs at a payer, including sales, are relationship oriented or related to 
management. AI could also improve the efficiency of individuals in these roles by up to 50 
percent, in our estimation.
Taken together, AI has the potential to reshape the payer workforce and enhance the focus on 
value-added activities that improve care and experience.
These ideas are not new, but they can be implemented now. AI absorbs the complexity and 
lowers the cost of intelligently personalizing healthcare for each individual, allowing such models 
to flourish.
Empower consumers through an always-on care model
In this century, while the average American’s lifespan has increased by two years, their health 
span (years lived in good health) has increased by only eight months.10 Improving health span 
requires clinicians to enable preventive care and individuals to actively manage their health in 
daily life. However, the current model of primary care cannot deliver that level of support given 
the shortage of primary care physicians (PCPs).

The conditions are now in place to provide continuous access to high-quality primary care to all 
individuals on demand with an AI-enhanced direct primary care (DPC) model.
AI can transform DPC, a membership fee-based model, to deliver scalable and financially 
accessible primary care. Because DPC physicians are not paid per visit, AI-enabled productivity 
gains are economically attractive rather than being seen as a reduction in visits and therefore 
revenue. Roughly 60 percent of primary care visits involve chronic disease management and 
preventive care.11 Such care is protocol driven, involving evidence-based pathways that AI 
agents can largely execute with the physician’s overall guidance. A DPC physician augmented 
by clinical AI could potentially serve more than 3,000 patients, according to our research,12 
rather than today’s average of about 400,13 while delivering more continuous and personalized 
attention than traditional primary care provides.
Utah’s 2026 Doctronic pilot has extended this logic to prescribing, demonstrating that AI could 
autonomously renew 192 commonly prescribed chronic medications and had a 99.2 percent 
match rate to physician decisions.14 An important barrier—patients’ ability to pay with tax-
advantaged dollars—has now been addressed by H.R. 1,15 which allows the more than 59 million 
Americans covered by health savings accounts (HSAs) to use those pre-tax dollars to fund DPC 
membership fees. A DPC membership combined with an HSA-eligible high-deductible health 
plan has the potential to provide superior access to continuous care compared to today’s model.
Such a model can also be enhanced with a variety of additions, such as personalized agents for 
health improvement assistance, access to health improvement solutions, direct-to-consumer 
(DTC) diagnostics, and DTC pharmacy services.
Rethink acute care delivery
The healthcare delivery system’s inability to improve productivity is legendary, as is the fact that 
waves of technology have added to, rather than reduced, costs. The productivity growth gap 
between healthcare delivery and the overall US economy from 2019 to 2025 was about three 
percentage points,16 a gap large enough that closing it would double the standard of living from 
one generation to the next, in our estimation.
The signal to rethink acute care is clear: Medicare dominates volume growth and reimbursement 
continues to be pressured, site neutral payment lurks, and legislative proposals like Medicare for 
All would tip the scales further. Profitability at Medicare rates is imperative. In our experience, 
clinical-care organizations have achieved meaningful transformation in the past few years but 
not enough.
Perhaps the best analog for such a business-model rethink comes from the airline industry, 
which, like healthcare, has substantial fixed capital assets and a large specialized workforce. In 
the face of relentless price pressure after deregulation, the airline industry reduced cost per 
passenger mile by half.

Halving costs in US acute care would be a game changer. Of course, the shift to more efficient 
sites of care has been a positive trend for many years and continues with meaningful growth in 
ambulatory settings. But more will be necessary. For instance, a multifold increase in hospital 
capital asset utilization is important. On average, the hospital capital cycle is 30 to 40 years long, 
in our experience. Over the next 30 years, we anticipate that acute procedure demand might 
decline in many geographies due to demographics and the impact of new therapies such as 
glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists.17 Markets vary meaningfully along three 
important dimensions: the size of the over-65 population is a major driver of current demand; 
the ratio of working-age adults to seniors is an indicator of growth in demand over the long run; 
and capacity utilization is an indicator of whether existing infrastructure can absorb future 
demand (Exhibit 2).
Growth markets with constrained capacity are good candidates for investment—but deploying 
capital toward ambulatory sites with a higher growth outlook and ROIC should be the focus.
In growth-constrained markets, particularly where capacity is also constrained, clinical-care 
organizations should redouble their focus on length-of-stay improvements to free up capacity. 
More important, they must innovate to increase utilization of current fixed assets rather than 
building new capacity. The airline industry substantially reduced the cost per passenger seat 
mile by increasing the amount of time planes are flying—an example hospitals would do well to 
replicate. For example, if an operating room was utilized like major US airlines utilize their 
aircraft, we estimate that it would see 2.5 times its current weekly utilization.
Unlock affordable access to the biopharma revolution
The pharmacy dispensing, management, and services value pools continue to grow, spurred by 
the biopharma revolution. At the same time, the economic model is entering a period of material 
upheaval due to multiple regulatory changes. The competing objectives—providing access to 
amazing possibilities for extending healthy life or ensuring the affordability of 
treatments—scream out for the need for innovation.
A few potential models exist based on two dimensions: whether they align with the secular 
growth of new treatments and whether they are zero-sum or focused on positive value creation. 
Where innovation is needed—and can thrive—is in models that ensure access to new treatments 
while triggering positive value creation. Consider a few avenues that could be combined in 
various permutations to form creative models:
—
Consumer empowerment. Many aspects of health management are more directly in the 
hands of the individual than the healthcare ecosystem. For example, addressing obesity and 
several chronic conditions requires individuals to make lifestyle and behavioral 
modifications. Individuals, however, also require assistance in achieving these goals

Develop an AI-centric business model for the healthcare services and 
technology sector
The healthcare services and technology (HST) sector has been the fastest growing and most 
resilient profit pool for several years. Behind this growth, however, hides a continual cycle of 
obsolescence at one end and new business model creation at the other end. And AI is 
accelerating this creative destruction. When considering whether and how to reinvent the 
business model, leaders should consider opportunities along two axes: first, the level of value 
added by AI in the solutions offered, and second, how central the solution is to the customer’s 
value creation or operational workflow (Exhibit 3).
Generating a measurable benefit either by reducing total cost of care or improving operating 
efficiency will be table stakes, as payer and health system customers’ internal capabilities are 
improving rapidly because of AI. Successful business models will lean more heavily into 
technology and be less dependent on services-centric strategies. In addition, the moat for HST 
businesses lies in being deeply integrated into clinician workflows, patient interactions, and the 
care encounter. This can be achieved either through high-adoption, mission-critical solutions or 
by providing the enabling platforms on which other solutions depend. Individual businesses can 
adopt specific strategies for success, though. Examples include using AI to disrupt traditional 
workflows that are often services-centric (for instance, revenue cycle management) or 
integrating AI solutions into proprietary and interconnected data (for example, to connect 
patient-level clinical information with broader claims, population health, and patient engagement 
information).

Master innovation in a three-speed world
The opportunity for leaders to improve healthcare has perhaps never been greater. A failure to 
innovate could prompt a further decline in public trust in the healthcare system and intensify 
calls for major government action. The latter would only exacerbate the downward economic 
trajectory that the healthcare industry has experienced since the COVID-19 pandemic. Clearly, 
the required future models will not naturally emerge; healthcare leaders will need to actively 
incite, motivate, and nurture them.
So how must leaders move forward? While we do not provide a full playbook in this article, we 
urge leaders to focus on speed, specifically the speed to value, as the primary source of 
advantage. In today’s world, this requires mastering the art of managing across three speeds
—the exponential trajectory of AI, the linear progression of modernizing the core technology 
infrastructure, and the sublinear trajectory of organizational change.
AI capabilities are improving at an exponential pace. In such an environment, healthcare leaders 
need to move beyond the AI hype. Equally important is not falling behind. Leaders need to have 
a clear window into AI’s progress and where it is gaining traction. They will need to forge 
connections not just with AI innovators but also with leaders in industries that may be further 
ahead in adoption than healthcare. New entrants in healthcare are still stymied by complexity 
and regulations, preserving the competitive moat for the industry, but the AI era does raise the

possibility of disintermediation by industry-adjacent participants or new entrants. Healthcare 
leaders therefore should be continually scanning the landscape, identifying emerging models, 
and creating approaches for partnering—and exiting partnerships—with entrants.
AI may be improving exponentially, but changes to legacy technology infrastructures are slower, 
moving in a linear way. To account for this mismatch, healthcare leaders will need to accept the 
constraints for their core systems while simultaneously reducing the centrality of those systems 
to value creation. It will be critical for them to create a semantic layer within their technology 
infrastructure that delinks development of new products, services, solutions, and capabilities 
from core systems. In this way, they can maintain speed to market without replacing entire 
systems.
Perhaps the biggest constraint to unlocking value from AI is the nature and speed of change 
itself: As every leader knows, organizational change is hard, and a majority of change efforts fail 
to reach their objectives, in our experience. Worse yet, organizations also demonstrate 
recidivism in their change efforts. To improve the odds of success, CEOs and senior leaders 
must lead from the front. Perhaps most critical to change in healthcare is articulating the 
reasons for change and elucidating the value for all stakeholders, including patients, consumers, 
employees, and communities.
Innovation is not limited to new products; it includes the ability to conceive, develop, deliver, and 
scale new services, processes, and business models that create net new growth. That broader 
definition matters here: Healthcare leaders will need to make innovation an operating discipline 
embedded across all parts of the organization, not an episodic initiative or an isolated 
department.
Healthcare is a purpose-driven industry. This Cambrian moment provides the opportunity to 
bring the industry closer to the aspiration of providing what every individual deserves: access to 
high-quality care, delivered safely, with a great experience, and at an affordable price. The 
original Cambrian explosion unfolded over millions of years; healthcare leaders do not have that 
luxury. The organizations that evolve fastest—reshaping clinical models, business models, and 
operating models together—will define the next era of healthcare.
