Key takeaways
Growth: Growth remains resilient but is losing momentum. The U.S. continues to outperform,
supported by  iscal spending and arti icial intelligence (AI)-related investment. However, this
impact is fading, and the growth boost from the AI buildout is expected to peak around late
2026 or early 2027. The U.S. midterm election results could also reshape the growth outlook.
Developed markets face weaker underlying growth and labor market conditions, while China
remains the outlier, pursuing countercyclical easing amid persistent de lationary pressures.
In lation: In lation is reaccelerating, but pressures remain predominantly supply-driven.
Favorable base eﬀects should provide some cushion through the  irst half of 2027, although
higher energy prices represent an important upside risk. Potential tariﬀ relief following the U.S.
midterm elections increases uncertainty around  iscal and regulatory policy. This leaves central
banks balancing weaker activity against in lation that may stay above target for longer.
Rates: The consequences of large  iscal de icits, coupled with AI-linked borrowing and central
bank hikes, are starting to increasingly push longer-term interest rates higher. However, the U.S.
midterm results could tilt the balance of  iscal spending away from tax bene its toward other
sectors. With growth losing momentum and  inancial conditions already restrictive, we believe
the current market pricing for rates is particularly vulnerable to a re-rating. Ultimately, we
believe central banks should deliver fewer hikes than markets are currently pricing.
Geopolitics:  Geopolitics remain one of the most important drivers of markets. Beyond the
immediate impact of con licts, geopolitical developments continue to shape commodity prices,
market expectations, supply chain decisions, and  iscal spending priorities. In the U.S., the
midterm election results introduce another policy variable, with potential implications for  iscal
policy, AI investment, health care,  inancials, tariﬀs, and housing.
Recent market moves
In our view, the recent sell-oﬀ in U.S. equities was as much about an exceptionally poor
technical setup and forced re-hedging by market participants as it was about a deterioration in
fundamentals. The near-term tail risks remain elevated, with more positioning to work through.
However, the Democratic sweep changes the distribution of policy risks and may drive
sentiment.
The massive investment in AI and the return on that investment have been under scrutiny for
months at this point. The fundamentals argue for greater scrutiny of large technology platforms,
data centers, and infrastructure supporting AI, particularly around power consumption,
permitting, data use, and local infrastructure. This could slow the buildout and add regulatory or

tax burdens, but we believe it is unlikely to stop it entirely. The relatively large market moves in
recent days appear to be more technically driven at this stage. Typical proxies and broader
parts of the equity market have been relatively resilient, and neither suggests that the recent
moves are indicative of a broader "growth scare" or "in lation scare."
We believe it is becoming increasingly clear that market participants had reduced hedges
following the midterms, only to reestablish them yesterday, helping drive these moves.
Thursday saw the highest put option volume in history, with “Tariﬀ Day” (April 4, 2025) a close
second. While the technical backdrop matters tremendously in the short term, we argue for
patience over a longer horizon. Excess liquidity has been shrinking on both the  iscal and
monetary sides; the yield curve has  lattened, and  inancial conditions have tightened over
2026. In the same vein, positioning in the AI theme has been crowded, and we have not yet
seen the kind of broad capitulation that would normally give us greater con idence that the
unwind is complete.
Our economic outlook
In our view, the global economy is showing increasing signs of being late in the economic cycle.
In the U.S., growth remains above trend but is gradually losing momentum, with a softer labor
market evident in weaker participation, volatile payrolls, and slowing wage growth. In lation is
beginning to reaccelerate, largely re lecting supply-side pressures, although favorable base
eﬀects should provide some cushion through the  irst half of 2027. At the same time,  inancial
conditions remain restrictive, with large  iscal de icits, AI-related capital spending, and tighter
monetary policy keeping sustained pressure on longer-term interest rates. The AI investment
cycle remains an important support for activity, but its contribution to growth is likely to peak in
late 2026 or early 2027.
The U.S. midterm elections shifted control of both chambers of Congress, resulting in a divided
government beginning in January. This adds another dimension to our outlook. We expect to
see a higher level of scrutiny of capital expenditure depreciation, tariﬀs, and defense spending.
Higher taxes appear unlikely in the near term, though Democrats may begin laying the
groundwork for potential changes in 2028. A narrower  iscal de icit could be supportive for
bond investors.
The election results could also reshape the investment and sector backdrop. Critical
examination of AI investment incentives and data center development could raise costs and
slow the pace of the AI buildout while potentially improving capital discipline and credit pro iles
among hyperscalers and other AI providers. Health care and  inancials may face greater policy
scrutiny, while consumer-oriented sectors could bene it from potential tariﬀ relief. Taken
together, these shifts reinforce the case for greater selectivity across markets as investors
assess the implications of a changing  iscal, regulatory, and monetary policy environment.
Elsewhere, other developed economies face a similar but more challenging mix of weaker
growth, softer labor markets, and persistent in lation pressures, without the same degree of
growth support that the U.S. has bene ited from. Geopolitical risk remains prominent across the
major economies, with higher energy prices representing the primary transmission channel to
in lation, growth, and ultimately monetary policy. China remains the notable exception, with
policymakers pursuing countercyclical easing in response to persistent de lationary pressures.
All together, we see a global economy that is slowing rather than contracting, but with
narrowing sources of growth and less room for policy error. We believe that despite near-term
in lation risks, softer activity and restrictive  inancial conditions should ultimately allow central
banks to deliver fewer hikes than markets currently price.
Prospective changes to asset allocation

Equities
We maintain a neutral view on equities, balancing resilient earnings against a more uncertain
macroeconomic and geopolitical backdrop. Elevated real yields remain a consideration,
while expectations around the AI investment cycle continue to in luence the broader
technology sector. Investor attention is increasingly shifting from the scale of AI-related
capital expenditure to the returns generated by that investment, including its contribution to
revenues, margins, and earnings per share. This suggests expectations are becoming more
demanding, even as the underlying investment cycle remains robust.
Fixed income
We remain neutral overall on  ixed income but see greater relative value opportunities within
the asset class. We have used recent spread widening to add selectively to high yield,
funded from investment-grade credit and sovereign bonds. Within sovereigns, we remain
neutral on overall duration, preferring to express views through cross-country positioning.
Global real yields remain attractive, but persistent in lation pressures and diverging growth
trajectories argue for greater selectivity. We are favoring sovereign debt from Japan,
Australia, and Canada, albeit for diﬀerent reasons. Japan is supported primarily by technical
factors, despite the country’s normalizing monetary policy. In Australia, the rate-hiking cycle
is more advanced and the outlook is increasingly driven by growth. In Canada, softer growth
and comparatively contained in lation pressures provide a diﬀerent source of opportunity.
Commodities and foreign exchange
We remain neutral on commodities while broadening our opportunity set within the asset
class. In energy, persistent geopolitical uncertainty leads us to favor relative value
opportunities over outright directional exposure. We also favor industrial metals and retain a
modest overweight to agricultural commodities, despite some softening in underlying
fundamentals. Within currencies, we remain positive on select economies where favorable
growth dynamics, orthodox monetary policy, and attractive starting rates provide support.

Carry  refers to the net return earned from holding an asset over time, independent of price
movements, after accounting for the costs of  inancing or maintaining that position.
Curve steepener refers to a change in the yield curve where the diﬀerence between long-term
interest rates and short-term interest rates increases, making the yield curve steeper.
Duration is a measurement of the sensitivity of a bond’s price to changes in Treasury yields. A
fund’s duration is the weighted average of duration of the bonds in the portfolio. Duration

should be interpreted as the approximate change in a bond’s (or fund’s) price for a 100-basis-
point change in Treasury yields. Duration is based on historical performance and does not
represent future results.
Hedging a security means using a separate investment to oﬀset the potential risks of that given
security.
