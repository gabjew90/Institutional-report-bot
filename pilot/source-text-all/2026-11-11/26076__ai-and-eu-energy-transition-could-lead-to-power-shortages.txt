 
Lights Out Math: AI Data Centers And EVs Push 
Europe Toward A Power Shortfall From 2027 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
SUNDAY, NOV 08, 2026 - 01:30 PM 
For sixteen years, Europe's power grid had one thing going for it: nobody wanted more 
electricity.  
That is about to change, and according to a new deep dive from China's Huatai Securities, the 
continent is nowhere near ready for it. In "AI and EU Energy Transition Could Lead to Power 
Shortages" (available to pro subs), Huatai's power generation team builds supply-demand 
balance tables for the EU, the UK and Germany and reaches a conclusion that should make 
anyone running a data center in Frankfurt (or a heat pump in Hamburg) a little nervous: 
Building the EU power system supply-demand balance table on this basis, we estimate the 
system to fall into power shortage from 2027... we find that, excluding new-type energy storage, 
the EU power system's reserve margin falls below 25% from 2027 and declines from 20% from 
2027 to 2035, while the supply-demand ratio exceeds 80% from 2027 and rises from 83% to 
94%. In other words, the EU power system faces a power shortfall from 2027, and this shortfall 
could keep widening. 
In other words, Europe's grid has about a year left before its peaking capacity falls below what 
Huatai considers safe, and by 2035 the cushion between available firm power and peak 
demand shrinks to just 6%. And that is the base case. 
 

 
 
Regular readers know we have spent much of the past two years on the American version of 
this problem, most recently in "The 70 Gigawatt Hole" on Sunday, where Morgan Stanley's net 
US shortfall more than tripled in a year. Europe, it turns out, has the same disease with different 
symptoms: less AI demand, but a grid that has spent a decade retiring exactly the kind of power 
plants you need when the wind stops blowing on a January evening. 
Twice The Power Plants, Same Electricity 
Start with the history, because it explains everything that follows. Huatai calls 2008 "a watershed 
for the EU power system." From 1991 to 2008 EU end-use consumption grew at a 1.6% CAGR 
to a peak of 2,622TWh. Then it stopped. By 2024 it was down to 2,457.7TWh, and industrial 
demand alone fell from 1,003TWh to 825TWh, a drop equal to 108% of the entire decline (yes, 
more than all of it: households and transport grew a little). 
Who stopped using power? Steel (128TWh to 88TWh), chemicals (180TWh to 149TWh) and 
non-ferrous metals (68TWh to 39TWh), which together account for 56% of the industrial decline. 
Huatai blames the post-GFC slowdown, efficiency gains and "the relocation of energy-intensive 
production out of the EU," with industrial power prices compounding at 4.8% a year since 2008. 
Probably not a coincidence. 
Meanwhile, the supply side went in the opposite direction. EU installed capacity more than 
doubled, from 506.6GW in 1990 to 1,150GW in 2024, while generation rose just 25%, from 
2,275TWh to 2,839TWh. As a result, average system utilization collapsed 55%, from 4,471 
 

 
hours to 2,490 hours a year. Wind and solar now make up 46.8% of installed capacity but just 
28% of generation; solar alone is 26.6% of capacity and a whopping 10.7% of output. Put 
differently, Europe more than doubled its power plants to produce a quarter more electricity. 
 
None of this mattered much while demand was falling. As Huatai puts it, "stagnant demand 
eased the pressure of traditional exits and wind-solar entry happening at the same time." 
Thermal and nuclear output fell by 897.5TWh from 2009 to 2024; wind and solar added 
673.3TWh; and the 164.7TWh drop in consumption quietly filled the gap. Europe's green 
transition was subsidized, in large part, by its deindustrialization. Good news for the emissions 
targets. Steelworkers in the Ruhr might see it differently. 
Then Came The Data Centers... And The Cars 
That cushion is now gone. Huatai sees EU demand returning to growth from 2026, driven by 
two sources that have one thing in common: they are rigid and they show up at peak. 
The first is AI. Using the European Commission's own plan to triple data center capacity, Huatai 
has EU AI data center capacity rising from 12GW to 36GW by 2035, with power use climbing 
from 75TWh to 185TWh (a 9.5% CAGR), or 15% of all incremental demand. And unlike your 
dishwasher, an AI cluster does not run at off-peak hours: "AIDC electricity consumption is rigid 
demand for the power system and affects peak load more directly than other load types." 
 

 
 
If anything, the Huatai figure looks conservative. Goldman's European utilities team led by 
Alberto Gandolfi wrote last month, in "Powering Up Europe: Datacenter Economics and the Rise 
of DC Infrastructure Services" (also available to pro subs), that it projects c.60-80GW of data 
centers installed in Europe by 2035, roughly double Huatai's EU-only number even allowing for 
the UK and Norway. Goldman also warned that to keep up, utilities would need to pour 
€100-150 billion into new dedicated renewables: 
This would ensure security of supply, and avoid price spikes. Failure to accelerate RES 
investments could lead to rising power prices, which would boost returns on legacy assets. 
Translation: if Europe doesn't build fast enough, the owners of the old power plants get paid. 
Which is precisely what Huatai's model ends up implying (more on that below). And Goldman 
thinks the demand risk skews higher still: back in March it estimated that every 10% increase in 
agentic AI queries vs baseline could boost data center needs by c.25% in both the US and 
Europe. 
Of course, wanting data centers and getting them are two different things. Morgan Stanley's 
latest global forecast, which we covered in "Splash Zone" over the weekend, has global capacity 
growing by 180GW from 2026 to 2030, with the US adding 88GW, China 49GW and Europe... 
12GW. Which is something we noted back in January: 
The reason Europe lags is the grid itself. Per Ember data cited by Huatai, data centers wait on 
average 7 years for a grid connection in Germany, 9 in the UK and 10 in Ireland and the 
 

 
Netherlands (where there are "effective bans"). ENTSO-E's own data center report from May 
found that 76% of operators cite electricity access as their biggest challenge, and in Denmark 
connection requests topped 60GW against a national peak of 7.3GW. Copenhagen's answer, 
according to Goldman's October 8 utilities note: an emergency law that moves large data 
centers to the back of the grid queue. 
 
Which is why Huatai expects the new buildout to drift north. Nordic power prices were about 
51% of the EU average in 2Q25, and by Huatai's math electricity in Stockholm, Oslo and 
Helsinki is 52%, 59% and 63% cheaper than the FLAP-D average (Frankfurt, London, 
Amsterdam, Paris, Dublin). Since training workloads don't care where they sit, Huatai sees 
Nordic AI capacity rising from 2.19GW to 10.7GW by 2035, a 17.2% CAGR vs 11.6% for the 
EU. 
 
 

 
The second, and much bigger, source of new demand is transport. Huatai projects EU transport 
power use rising from 85TWh to 492TWh by 2035, a 19.2% CAGR that accounts for 55.4% of 
all EU consumption growth over the decade, as the zero-emission passenger car fleet goes 
from 7.61 million to 92.44 million. That is, of course, assuming Brussels doesn't keep softening 
the combustion engine ban, which France and Germany were lobbying to do as recently as last 
month. Even so, EVs plug in when people get home from work, which is right around when the 
evening peak hits. 
 
The Coal Exit Nobody Will Actually Finish 
On the supply side, the plan is simple: more wind and solar, a lot more. Per ENTSO-E's ERAA, 
combined wind and solar capacity rises from 591GW in 2025 to 1,442GW in 2035, taking their 
share of the fleet from 48.7% to 70.9%. Hydro and nuclear stay broadly flat (Germany's nuclear 
row in Huatai's table is a neat line of zeros through 2035), and coal goes away. 
Or at least, that's the plan. The consensus has EU coal capacity falling from 107GW to 15GW 
by 2035, a 16.2% annual decline. Huatai doesn't buy it, and this is where the report gets 
interesting: 
The market expects the structural transition within the EU's thermal power fleet to proceed 
quickly from 2026 to 2035, but we see it as difficult to complete.... we think that the exit of 
coal-fired power units in the EU could be slower than the forecast of the European Network of 
 

 
Transmission System Operators for Electricity (ENTSO-E), with EU coal-fired power units falling 
from 107GW to 30GW from 2025 to 2035, at an average annual rate of -10.8%. 
In other words, Huatai sees the coal exit running at about two-thirds of the consensus pace, 
because Poland and Czechia lean conservative on security of supply (Poland's and Czechia's 
2035 coal fleets could be 7.7GW and 3.7GW above ERAA's forecast) and Italy already pushed 
its full exit from 2028 to 2038. Germany, which has written its coal exit into law, takes its fleet 
from 40GW to around 5GW. Huatai also has EU gas capacity rising from 189GW to 232GW 
instead of peaking in 2030 as the market expects, and roughly 17GW of hydrogen-fired units 
arriving by 2035 (good luck with that: last year we noted that Germany's Hydrogen Dream 
Became A $9 Billion Yearly Black Hole). 
 
And still, total thermal capacity shrinks 1.8% a year through 2035. The plants that can be 
switched on at 6pm in a windless January are going away faster than anything that can replace 
them, and we already got a preview of what a grid heavy on inverters and light on spinning 
mass looks like: 
 

 
Short From 2027 
Put the two sides together and you get the core of the report. Huatai has EU peak load rising 
from about 424GW in 2026 to 559GW in 2035, a 3.1% CAGR, as new rigid loads pile on top of 
the old ones. 
 
Against that, it sets two reliability tests: a reserve margin of at least 25% for the EU (20% for 
individual countries) and a supply-demand ratio below 80%. Huatai borrowed its reliability 
coefficients from the Chinese grid (whose planners, it should be noted, have a habit of building 
power plants before they need them), and raised the EU threshold from 20% to 25% because, 
as the team points out, "a 20% reserve margin for the EU as a whole does not necessarily mean 
every member state can maintain 20%." 
Excluding new battery storage, the EU fails both tests from 2027. In the base case, the reserve 
margin slides from 20% in 2027 to 6% in 2035 and the supply-demand ratio climbs from 83% to 
94%. In Huatai's "bullish" case (bullish for demand, that is: it lifts the 2025-2035 demand 
increment by 50%), peak load hits 638GW, the reserve margin goes negative after 2030 and 
ends at -8%, and the supply-demand ratio hits 108%. For those keeping score at home, a 
supply-demand ratio above 100% means peak demand exceeds firm supply. 
 

 
 
Even the bearish case, which cuts the transport and manufacturing demand increments to 90% 
of the original forecast, has the EU short from 2027, with the reserve margin at 13% in 2035. 
Here is how 2035 looks across all three scenarios and all three grids: 
 
The UK is the worst of the bunch. Using NESO's demand forecast, which has UK consumption 
rising from 278TWh to 382TWh by 2035, Huatai sees peak demand climbing from 44GW to 
 

 
74GW. Excluding new storage, Britain is already short in 2026, its reserve margin turns negative 
in 2033 and reaches -12% by 2035, while the supply-demand ratio hits 113%. Even in the bear 
case it ends the decade at -1%. 
 
Germany looks better on paper, mostly because it starts with more slack: the reserve margin 
falls from 23% in 2026 to 12% in 2035 as peak demand rises from 74GW to 116GW and the 
coal fleet heads to 6GW. In the bull case, it falls to 0%. Not bad for the country that switched off 
its last nuclear reactor in 2023. 
 
Batteries Included (143 Gigawatts Of Them) 
So how does Huatai close the gap? Batteries, lots of them. To keep the EU reserve margin at 
25% and the supply-demand ratio at 80%, and assuming average storage duration rises from 
two to three hours, the EU needs to add 143GW / 467GWh of electrochemical storage from 
2026 to 2035, 56GW more than ERAA projects. That takes the EU fleet from 39GW in 2025 to 
181GW, more than 4.5x in a decade. The bull case needs 323GW; the bear case 91GW. The 
UK needs to go from 7GW to 39GW and Germany from 16GW to 56GW. 
 

 
 
Huatai's poster child for the buildout is RWE, which had 1.7GW of batteries in operation at 
end-2025, 2.4GW under construction, and a 6GW target for 2030. RWE's real edge, per Huatai, 
is something Europe has plenty of: retired coal plants with grid connections attached (it is 
building 0.6GW/1.2GWh of storage on the site of the Westfalen coal plant). Goldman made the 
same point about the sector as a whole in its October report: European utilities own c.25GW of 
grid connection points, which it values at c.€20 billion, and an integrated "land, grid access and 
energy" offering could create €1-1.5 billion of value per GW of data center. 
Put differently, in a grid where the bottleneck is the connection, the connection is the asset. 
Well, that... and the gas plants Huatai thinks Europe will be forced to keep building. 
There is one small problem with the battery fix: a two- or three-hour battery smooths the evening 
peak, but it doesn't do much during a week-long Dunkelflaute, when the wind is still, the sun is 
gone by 4pm and the batteries need something to charge from. Which is why, buried in the risk 
section, Huatai lists the one variable that could make all of this much worse: "a further 
escalation of the US-Iran war — pardon, an escalation of the US-Iran conflict." We appreciate 
the candor. 
Winter Is Coming (And So Is 2027) 
The timing of this report could hardly be better. Last week, Europe's benchmark TTF gas 
contract crossed the 100-euro "psychological mark" and closed at €104/MWh for the first time 
since the 2022 energy crisis, and this week we reported that Germany missed its November 
storage target, entering the heating season just 61.2% full vs a 70% requirement and a 15-year 
average of around 91%. European gas now trades at roughly 11 times the US price. So the fuel 
 

 
for the gas plants Huatai expects Europe to keep building is getting more expensive, and the 
fuel for the batteries depends on the weather. Meanwhile, back in June, the European 
Commission was already asking households to cut their electricity use during peak hours to 
make room for AI data centers. Nothing says "energy security" quite like asking grandma to skip 
the kettle so a GPU cluster in Frankfurt can keep training. 
Huatai's conclusion is that the gap can be closed with enough batteries, a slower coal exit and 
more gas units. Maybe. But that requires Brussels to admit coal is staying longer, Berlin to make 
peace with gas at €100, and grid operators to connect 143GW of storage in a system where 
data centers already wait a decade for a plug. Our bet is that the shortfall arrives on schedule 
(or earlier, if Goldman's much higher 60-80GW data center number is closer to the truth), and 
that the real fix is the one we have been pounding the table on in the US: if hyperscalers want to 
plug a city's worth of load into a strained grid, make them bring their own power plant, behind 
the meter, and ideally one with a microreactor in it. 
Until then, Europe will keep doing what it has done for 16 years: balancing the grid by making 
sure nobody uses more electricity. It worked fine while Germany's factories were leaving, put out 
of business by cheap Chinese imports. It will work a lot less well once the data centers and the 
EVs arrive.
