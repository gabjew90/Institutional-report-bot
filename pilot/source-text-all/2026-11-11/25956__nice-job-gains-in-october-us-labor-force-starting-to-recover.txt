Nice Job Gains in October, US Labor Force Starting to Recover?
By Michael J. Bazdarich, PhD
Private-sector payroll jobs rose by 159,000 in October, on top of a +85,000 revision to the September jobs estimate,
according to data released today by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Upon first seeing the October job gains, our
inclination was to say that the data have been following a sawtooth pattern, with strong gains reported for August and
October, but soft gains for July and September. However, the upward revisions to September were so significant that
that month’s performance can no longer be described as soft.
Exhibit 1 provides a good snapshot of two aspects of recent labor market performance. First, as we just stated, the last
three months all now show good job gains. Second, those healthy gains have recently been far more diffuse across
industries than was the case over the previous three years. 
Exhibit 1: Private-Sector Job Growth—Health Care vs. Other Sectors 
 
Source: Bureau of Labor Statistics. As of 31 Oct 26. 
As we have remarked repeatedly in previous posts over 2023-2025, job gains were concentrated in health care and
social services, a sector comprising only 20% of the labor force, but accounting for 80% of job gains at the time.
Throughout 2026, job growth within health and social services has slowed while job growth elsewhere has picked up
nicely. This has been the case especially over August-October. Yes, health and social jobs bounced in October, but the
bounces elsewhere were even larger.
The strong recent job gains outside of health and social occurred in professional and business services, transportation
and warehousing (logistics), and manufacturing. These are all sectors offering good jobs and relatively high wages.

(Indeed, the manufacturing gains were concentrated in relatively heavy industry durable goods sectors, such as metals,
machinery and electronics.) 
This may be what is behind the simultaneous rebound in labor force participation and the “employment rate” (the ratio
of civilian employment to the population). As shown in Exhibit 2, both labor force participation and employment rates
had been trending down since the onset of Covid. While there was a brief rebound in both immediately after the end of
the pandemic lockdown, the underlying trend has been lower ever since. Apparently, post-Covid job prospects were not
sufficient to induce older adults to delay retirement or younger adults to look for work. 
Exhibit 2: Labor Force Participation and Employment Rates—All Adults 
 
Source: Bureau of Labor Statistics. As of 31 Oct 26. 
The recent rebound in participation and employment rates is not quite prolonged enough to constitute a change in the
trend, but it provides at least a preliminary hope of an awakening from a post-Covid hibernation of the labor force. What
makes this all the more intriguing is its coincidence with three months of stronger job growth in well-paying
quasi-industrial goods- and service-producing sectors. 
Will the Federal Reserve (Fed) be troubled by this possible reawakening of the labor force? It shouldn’t be. The relative
decline in the labor force over 2023-2025 (marked by declining participation rates) was an inflationary force, not a
disinflationary one, and it’s possible the recent reversal is good news for inflation, even if it also means stronger job
gains. 
Meanwhile, yes, unemployment has declined from 4.5% in late-2025 to 4.1% as of October, but that rate is not
particularly low, especially with a mature labor force. Note also from Exhibit 2 that the US economy had higher
employment rates over 2016-2019 than what we see presently, without any inflationary pressures emerging. 
Finally, wage gains also bounced in October, but that followed seven months of much softer gains in hourly wages.
Wage growth over the last six months is still well below the 3.5% rate consistent with the Fed’s 2% inflation target. 
So, today’s release definitely provides good news for economic growth prospects, and we believe its import for inflation
is favorable as well.
