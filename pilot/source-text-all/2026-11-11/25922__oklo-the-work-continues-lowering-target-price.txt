The work continues
The winds have certainly shifted. Nuclear stocks, once lauded, have retreated; the
intense investor appetite of a year ago has cooled. Much, not all, of this fatigue likely
stems from a crowded stampede of upstarts, all scrambling to claim a throne in the
West's tentative nuclear re-emergence. And in a field this congested, clarity is currency.
And now, investors are second-guessing the infinite appetite of data centers, a
hesitation our “Something’s got to give” readers understood. So, what now? Our
conviction runs deeper than the AI build. Long before data centers gripped the public
imagination, the imperative was simple and stark, at least to us: America needs clean,
reliable baseload power. That foundational truth remains unshaken.
That first pillar brings us to the second: our belief that Oklo is poised to endure as a
long-term winner in the emerging Small Modular Reactor (SMR) space.
Through sheer vertical integration, management is hoping to take an axe to the Gordian
knot of industry complexity. They are steering the nuclear re-renaissance, determined
to unleash what they call the "awesome fundamental competitive advantage that
nuclear technologies have," where, to put it in perspective, a single golf ball of uranium
holds enough energy to power a human being's entire lifetime.
We see Oklo rising not merely as a manufacturer, but as a vertically integrated, globally
distributed energy enterprise breaking down barriers by closing the loop.
It is a synchronized, 3-pronged engine: Power, anchoring the model via Aurora
powerhouses; Fuel, driving deployment through fabrication and recycling; and Isotopes,
branching into high-value markets.
And in a market where capital has suddenly grown tight and cautious, Oklo stands
fortified with a formidable ~$3.1B in cash. In this climate, that war chest is more than a
cushion; it is a decisive strategic advantage.
So what did we learn new from the earnings report? That the work continues.
From a regulatory perspective, CEO Jake DeWitte mentioned that “the NRC has finalized
a rule that allows applicants to use relevant technical evidence from DOE authorized
reactor projects to support commercial licensing. For Oklo that reinforces the strategic
value of advancing Aurora, Idaho under DOE oversight. The safety analysis, testing
and operating experience we develop can support subsequent NRC applications where
applicable.” That should help fast track potential future NRC approvals.
Congressional action could unlock access to the $50B+ Nuclear Waste Fund for eligible
Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus (NLIC) infrastructure supporting Oklo's closed-loop
vision of fast reactors generating clean power while recycling used nuclear fuel.
In Idaho, construction on the Aurora powerhouse is advancing rapidly. Site excavation is
complete, safety-related rebar work is underway, and structural steel is going vertical,
supported by a Preliminary Documented Safety Analysis (PDSA) approval from the
Department of Energy (DOE) that functions similarly to an NRC construction permit
to reduce regulatory risk. Parallel to construction, Oklo is advancing its procurement
process by releasing requests for proposals and selecting vendors, alongside developing
the Aurora Fuel Fabrication Facility to qualify and fabricate its first core using allocated
recycled material by 2027.

Our recent October visit to Groves
Near Lockhart, Texas, the Groves Isotope Test Reactor is Oklo’s small, low-power
nuclear reactor that sits in a pool of water and uses standard fuel. The facility skips grid
electricity entirely. Instead, it prioritizes rapid deployment and domestic radioisotope
production.
Domestic production of these high-value materials is essential. Oklo is securing a
resilient domestic supply chain to replace fragile foreign sources. This effort supports
cancer therapies, semiconductor manufacturing, materials testing, scientific research,
deep-space exploration, and defense systems.
Oklo executes this strategy through 3 distinct pathways. The company recovers
isotopes from existing stockpiles. It builds dedicated reactors like Groves for active
production. Finally, it extracts high-value elements during advanced fuel recycling.
International and domestic partnerships feed used nuclear fuel into this ecosystem,
creating a closed-loop processing model.

How does Groves fit in? Oklo is not running 3 separate businesses: the team is
building a single, coherent nuclear enterprise across power, fuel, and isotopes—an
architecture by deliberate design.
• Power is the anchor. The Aurora powerhouses are designed to deliver steady, clean,
electricity and heat.
• Fuel. But even the most elegant reactor is merely steel and hope without
fuel. Customer demand cannot will uranium into existence, and no nuclear
renaissance can happen on an empty tank. That is why Oklo is not treating fuel as
an afterthought outsourced to strangers. From sourcing and fabrication to recycling,
Oklo is attempting to secure the lifeblood of its reactors itself.
• Isotopes. Then come isotopes, which take the very same materials, licenses,
and operational know-how and open the door to high-margin frontiers: medicine,
defense, deep space, and industry.

stays in-house.
The old nuclear playbook is linear, sometimes disjointed, and often wasteful. One
company mines, another enriches, a third fabricates, a utility generates, and the
remnants sit idle, their residual energy stranded. Advanced nuclear cannot afford that
kind of fragmentation. A reactor without a sovereign fuel strategy is likely vulnerable.

Near term: government supply. Oklo has been awarded and anticipates receiving
government-allocated fuel sources - including 5MTs of HALEU recovered and
reprocessed from EBR-II spent fuel and cold-war era weapons-grade plutonium.
• 5 MTs from Idaho National Laboratory: filling in the near-term HALEU
supply gap. In December 2019, Idaho National Laboratory (INL) – through a
cooperative agreement - awarded 5 MTs of downblended HALEU to Oklo, produced
through the recovery and treatment of SNF generated by the now-decommissioned
EBR-II. Importantly, Oklo’s Aurora reactor/ downblended INL fuel validation project
has completed site characterization; Oklo selected Kiewit Nuclear Solutions as its
lead constructor and is on track for demonstrated operations in late 2027/ early
2028.
Mid-term: conventionally produced HALEU fuel. Over the mid-term, Oklo plans to
leverage anticipated commercial supply - from both conventional (i.e., centrifugation)
and advanced (i.e., lasers) enrichment processes.
• Conventionally enriched HALEU supplied by Centrus. In late-August 2023,
Oklo signed an MOU with Centrus to supply HALEU - produced at Centrus’
production facility in Piketon, Ohio; currently the only NRC-licensed, domestic
HALEU enrichment facility - to fuel Aurora Powerhouses. Importantly, Centrus
recently announced planned plant HALEU production capacity expansion; the exact
scope and size of capacity expansion, however, will depend on pending federal
funding decisions. The companies also plan to collaborate on Aurora Powerhouse
development at the Piketon Plant.
Long term: recycled fuel. Over the long term, Oklo plans to reprocess SNF
to produce HALEU fuel. Oklo's anticipated Tennessee Advanced Fuel Center - in
partnership with TVA - enables recycling and fabrication at scale. Management is
targeting a reprocessing ramp-up at the facility in the early 2030s. According to Oklo,
uranium/ transuranic byproducts (i.e., spent fuel and railings) could produce ~96,000
MTs of used nuclear fuel (UNF) - capable of refueling reactors. Moreover, ~2,000 MTs of
UNF could be produced annually from operating reactors.
• The Aurora Fuel Fabrication Facility (A3F). A3F is a specialized nuclear facility
being developed at INL. Currently, the company is installing and operating the fuel
fabrication equipment. Importantly, the DOE has approved the NSDA and PSDA for
A3F - allowing Oklo to move forward with final design and construction.
• Notably, during 2025, Oklo completed fast-spectrum plutonium criticality
experiments - validating and supporting the use of plutonium fuel in Aurora
Powerhouse reactors.
