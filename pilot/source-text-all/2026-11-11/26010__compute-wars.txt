◼ Models are commoditising: value moves to distribution and cheap compute 
◼ China can't own Western distribution, but can be compute's low-cost producer 
◼ A blue wave in Washington and Nvidia's curbs push compute East 
A few news items landed at the start of the week that set the shape of one story: Democrats took 
Congress partly on a backlash against data centres, Nvidia told customers to keep Chinese firms 
to a minority of their compute, and Revolut moved its engineers onto a Chinese model at a tenth 
of the cost. As models become commodities, AI's lasting value goes to whoever owns distribution 
and whoever supplies compute most cheaply. China can't own Western distribution, but it can be 
the cheapest supplier of compute, as it was for solar and batteries. The West is currently taking 
action to hurry it along. The opportunity is the PRC’s tenacious prowess in value chain integration. 
Markets have noticed: in the AI sell-off most Chinese AI baskets rose while their US peers 
plummeted 
Compute is the hottest commodity and it is getting pushed into China 
Washington shuts the door. Democrats took both chambers, and data centres were on the ballot: 
governors-elect in Texas and Georgia, two of the biggest build-outs, campaigned partly on 
moratoria. Expect slower permitting and hyperscalers footing the bill for grid upgrades. But a 
slower US build does not reduce demand for compute, it reallocates it, and the cheapest marginal 
supplier with spare power is China (Chart 2).

Nvidia is closing the side door. It has told data-centre customers worldwide that at least 51% of 
their services must go to non-Chinese firms, narrowing the route Chinese labs used to rent its 
chips in Southeast Asia. The aim is to keep the US stack out of Chinese hands, and these are the 
pressures under which China thrives. The effect is to push China's biggest buyers onto Huawei, 
Cambricon and T-Head sooner. Each door Nvidia closes makes the domestic market bigger, and 
in solar and batteries it was scale that brought the PRC's costs down. 
Beijing is closing the front door. State-funded data centres must use domestic chips, and 
customs blocked Nvidia's H200 for two months this year. Nvidia's share of China's accelerator 
shipments fell to 34% in H1 2026, with Huawei now close behind at 28% (Chart 3).

Compute is now a commodity, and China is the low-cost producer 
Defining the commodity: By compute we mean the raw ability to provision fast, capable compute 
at a low price. It already trades like one: H100 rental prices have more than halved since late 
2023, and CME now lists GPU rental futures. Once compute is priced as a raw resource, it is a 
market China can rapidly come to supply, with a regional software layer of open models running 
on Chinese hardware. 
The node gap is real but narrowing in importance. Taiwan has 2nm in volume; China has been 
stuck at 7nm-class since 2023, and its headline breakthroughs rarely materialise (Chart 6). But as 
gates approach atomic scale, packaging, chip-interconnect and power matter more than the 
reticle, and China can innovate around the lithography it lacks. ASML's EUV tools are golden 
spokes on a bicycle and is less binding to progress than it was (at least less binding for high-
throughput GPUs than it is Node devices/CPUs). China can compete on brawn: Huawei's 
CloudMatrix 384 beats Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 by 1.7x on compute and 3.6x on memory, albeit 
currently at roughly four times the power (Chart 7). That is bad economics where power is 
expensive, but a nothingburger for the PRC.

The first win: Running costs. Data-centre hubs in Inner Mongolia pay about 4.7 US cents per 
kWh, around half the US industrial average, and China added ten times as much generating 
capacity as the US last year. On price per TFLOP, Chinese chips now match Nvidia (Charts 8 and 
9). 
The second win: Accelerator costs. A Chinese chip only has to undercut Nvidia's ~75% gross 
margin, not its manufacturing cost (Chart 10). China has done this before: solar module prices fell 
95% and battery packs 90% as it took more than 80% of manufacturing in both (Chart 11). 
The pathway is already here. Chinese open models are the default in price-sensitive channels 
and increasingly built for Chinese chips: DeepSeek V4 launched with day-zero support on 
Huawei's Ascend, and GLM-5 was reportedly trained on it.

Addressing security concerns: China could buy the West's trust with trustless infrastructure. 
The broad concern with Chinese compute is privacy, and that is largely a solved engineering 
problem. The tools are confidential computing and remote attestation. Data and model weights 
are decrypted only inside a hardware-isolated enclave on the processor and, since Nvidia's H100, 
on the GPU too, with the traffic between them encrypted. Before sending anything, the customer 
checks a signed hardware report that proves what code is running, then releases keys it holds 
itself. The operator cannot see even if it wished to. 
Alibaba is a leader in confidential compute and both sides already sell it. Azure has rented 
confidential H100s since 2024, Google has followed, and specialists such as Tinfoil and Phala run 
whole inference services this way. China has the same plumbing: Alibaba Cloud uses Nvidia 
GPUs inside Intel TDX enclaves, with a guide to secure DeepSeek inference, and Hygon and 
Huawei have built home-grown equivalents. So the PRC doesn't need Western customers to trust 
Alibaba or Huawei more than AWS or Microsoft, which have to answer to the US CLOUD Act. It 
can offer cheap compute where trust isn't needed by design. Our audit of providers is in the 
appendix.s 
 
What it means for investors 
It is not a winner-takes-all market. Microsoft owns the workflow layer, Amazon much of the 
development layer, Meta is betting on personal use, and the labs chase the panacea. Up the 
supply chain, compute is still up for grabs. A likely outcome: National owners of distribution, 
Various models in the middle (We believe open and closed source/weight models are likely to co-
exist, with volumes scaling on the open-source/weight side), and compute supplied from China. 
Risk falls on the margins of the western hardware names as well as Taiwan and Korea. 
This is part of the China Tech Shock, a topic TSL began talking about a few years ago. The 
“shock” is just getting started, we think the PRC is unbeatable in manufacturing and highly 
competitive in software. The macro fallout? Shrinking profits for Western tech and a structurally 
stronger RMB.
