October jobs report very strong, but may not change 
much for the Fed 
◼ 
The October jobs report was a very strong one across the board, with all 
of the major indicators coming in better than expected. This included a 
+165k jump in NFP that topped even the highest estimate submitted to 
BBG, solid upward revisions of +84k for the past two months, stronger 
AHE for October on top of an upward revision to September, and an 
unemployment rate that ticked lower despite a bump in participation. 
◼ 
While we always hesitate to read too much into any single jobs report 
given the volatility of some of the metrics, NFP being the prime example, 
there is little in today’s data that would raise any clear red flags. As such, 
the report should only strengthen the widely held view at the Fed that the 
labour market is in a good spot, remaining close to balance, with the 
downside risks that drove the late 2025 cuts having diminished. 
◼ 
That said, while at the margin today’s data would support a December 
hike, we are not convinced it will really make all that much of a difference. 
For one, some may see it as more backward looking given rising 
concerns over the AI buildout in the wake of midterms, but even leaving 
that aside we maintain our view that the Fed’s reaction function with 
regards to the employment side of the mandate is an asymmetric one. 
While weaker jobs data can drive the Fed towards easing policy, as we 
saw in late 2025, strong jobs data does not automatically drive the Fed 
to hike, it simply leaves the focus on the inflation side of the mandate. 
◼ 
In that vein, we will get the next update on inflation on Tuesday when the 
October CPI report comes out, and while a downside surprise could raise 
some questions, our base case continues to see the Fed hiking a bit 
further, though at a gradual pace. This includes the FOMC coming back 
with another 25bp hike in December following the skip in October, which 
is priced at about a 75% chance following the jobs data. 
◼ 
For more information on the labour market, we have recently released 
our US Labour Market Monitor and NFP Explorer at Red Mount Analytics, 
which covers everything you need to know about the jobs report, from a 
360-degree overview on the data release to a deep-dive into the weeds of 
the report to our in-house indicators.

The October jobs report was a very strong one across the board, with all of 
the major indicators coming in better than expected. While we always hesitate 
to reach too much into any single jobs report given the volatility of some of the 
metrics, there is little in today’s data that would raise any clear red flags. As such, 
the report should only strengthen the widely held view at the Fed that the labour 
market is in a good spot, remaining close to balance, with the downside risks that 
drove the late 2025 cuts having diminished. 
In detail, headline NFP jumped by +165k, more than doubling consensus of 
+70k while also topping even the highest forecast submitted to BBG. On top 
of this, the prior two months were also revised up by a solid total of +84k, with 
September now at +60k and August now at +186k. 
Taking into account the October data along with prior revisions, the three-
month moving average surged to +137k from +79k (see Figure 1). This is now 
very close to the highest level of the year (+142k in May) and given the current 
environment in which the breakeven has dropped sharply, this level sits solidly 
above basically any estimate of breakeven (see Figure 2). 
On a sectoral basis, private payroll growth also posted a strong acceleration 
to +159k from +78k as government NFP rose a modest +6k on the month led by 
state government education. Within private payrolls, goods-producing payrolls rose 
by +15k in October to slow a bit compared to a +27k increase in September. The 
advance was led by a strong manufacturing print (+19k), while construction (-3k) 
and mining (-1k) each edged lower. 
Private service-providing payrolls drove the advance in jumping +144k on 
the heels of a softer +51k last month. The gains here were led by professional 
& business services (+70k) and private education & health services (+53k), with 
trade, transportation & utilities (+22k), financial activities (+7k), and other services 
(+6k) adding more modestly. These were partially offset by losses in leisure & 
hospitality (-13k) and information (-1k).

Outside of NFP, the unemployment rate ticked back down to 4.1% from 4.2%, 
despite an increase in participation. This matches the July and August trough 
and remains at a low level overall, consistent with the view that recent job gains 
have been above the breakeven rate. 
The dip in the unemployment rate was driven by strong employment growth 
in the household survey, which topped NFP for a third straight month as 
household survey employment jumped by +463k (see Figure 3). This was 
enough to offset a further recovery in the participation rate, which edged up to 
61.9% from 61.8% and has now more than fully retraced the early summer dip (see 
Figure 4) after the labour force increased by +421k in October. As a result, the 
number of unemployed fell by -41k to 7.068m, or roughly 4.14% of the labour force. 
Overall, the unemployment rate has been largely rangebound in the low-4% 
range throughout 2026, despite the swings in participation from the summer

We would highlight that the U6 underemployment rate rose to 7.9% from 7.6% 
as those working part-time for economic reasons (people that would like to 
work full-time but cannot find full-time work) surged by +339k (see Figure 6). 
This was one of the few soft spots in the October data, but is not enough to 
outweigh the strength elsewhere, especially as the U6 rate can be somewhat 
volatile and the October print of 7.9% is still well below the peak of 8.7% hit just 
after last year’s government shutdown. Thus, we will monitor the evolution of part-
time employment and the U6 in the coming months but are not overly concerned 
for now.

Turning to wages, average hourly earnings also topped expectations in 
rising 0.4% MoM in October, firming even compared to an upwardly revised 
0.3% MoM print in September, though base effects still saw the YoY pace 
edge back down to 3.1% from 3.2%. The YoY rate is now back in the pre-Covid 
range and should back the Fed’s view that the labour market is not a major source 
of inflationary pressure at the moment, despite the upside surprise this month. 
For the Fed, while at the margin today’s data would support a December hike, 
we are not convinced it will really make all that much of a difference. For one, 
some may see today’s report as backward looking given rising concerns over the 
AI buildout in the wage of midterms, but even leaving that aside we maintain our 
view that the Fed’s reaction function with regards to the employment side of the 
mandate is an asymmetric one. Here, while weaker jobs data can drive the Fed 
towards easing policy, as we saw in late 2026, strong jobs data does not 
automatically drive the Fed to hike, it simply leaves the focus on the inflation side 
of the mandate. 
In that vein, we will get the next update on inflation on Tuesday when the 
October CPI report comes out, and while a downside surprise could raise 
some questions, our base case continues to see the Fed hiking a bit further, 
though at a gradual pace. This includes the FOMC coming back with another 
25bp hike in December following the skip in October, which is priced at around a 
75% chance following the jobs data.
