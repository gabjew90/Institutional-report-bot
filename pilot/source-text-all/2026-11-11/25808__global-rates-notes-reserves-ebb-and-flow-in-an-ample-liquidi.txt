Fed policy: A stabilizing force for funding  
Fed policy should remain a source of stability for funding conditions in 2027. We expect 
the Fed to restart reserve management purchases (RMPs) this month and maintain a 
modestly positive purchase pace of around $10bn/month through at least 1Q27. 
Thereafter, our baseline is that RMPs will rise to around $25bn/month, which we 
estimate would keep reserves stable as a share of bank assets. Our steady state 
assumption is that reserves stabilize around 11% of bank assets, though risks to that 
view are skewed to the downside on greater evidence of a shift in reserve demand (more 
below). 
While the potential for pockets of easier funding conditions due to debt limit dynamics 
could temporarily allow for a period with RMPs close to zero next year, we think the 
more prudent approach for the Fed would be to look through any resulting easiness. In 
the very near-term, we think tighter funding conditions are likely to bring a resumption 
of RMPs at $10bn/month but would not be surprised by a higher pace out of precaution 
ahead of year-end. Additionally, we think the Fed is unlikely to restart QT in response to 
easier funding conditions, especially those temporarily triggered by debt limit. Instead, 
the Fed would more likely view these effects as a short-term boost to the supply of 
liquidity, rather than a structural shift in funding demand, and the subsequent rapid 
increase in TGA following a resolution could reverse the liquidity boost and increase 
upside risks to funding costs.

The Fed’s balance sheet task force’s strategy review is another key consideration for 
funding outlook. We expect the results to reinforce an ample reserves framework, rather 
than recommend a broader change in approach. Fed officials have voiced support for 
the ample reserve framework this year, while acknowledging that evidence of a 
reduction in reserve demand would warrant an adjustment in the supply of reserves. 
Governor Waller, Dallas Fed President Logan, and SOMA Manager Perli have all 
commented on the ample reserves framework and potential balance sheet policy 
adjustments. President Logan, specifically, has noted that the “ample reserves 
framework is efficient and effective” and has proven to “keep money market rates in the 
FOMC’s target range,” and she favors an “approach that reduces banks’ need for 
reserves and shifts the demand curve inward.” 
Collectively, their assessment of the benefits of the current framework and the wider 
support within the FOMC suggest that an imminent change is unlikely. But the level of 
reserves that constitutes ample can evolve. We have previously discussed policies that 
could encourage a leftward shift in the demand curve, namely those that reduce both 
regulatory-induced demand for reserves and stigma associated with using the Fed’s 
liquidity facilities. Potential policies include allowing banks to include pre-pledged 
collateral at the Fed’s discount window and FHLB borrowing capacity in liquidity 
calculations, such as the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and internal liquidity stress tests 
(ILSTS); and/or revising Treasury’s cash management policy to allow some portion to be 
lent in repo when short-term rates exceed IORB. 
Regulatory changes this year, such as the amendment of banks’ supplementary leverage

Treasury supply and demand: A more difficult balance  
The supply-demand backdrop for USTs should remain relatively supportive for funding 
conditions in the year ahead, though changes to coupon supply, continued reliance on 
bill issuance, and slower money fund AUM growth could make that balance 
incrementally more challenging. 
Total net supply is poised to increase only slightly year-over-year, from $2.1tn to $2.2tn, 
though the underlying mix of issuance is likely to shift more noticeably towards bills 
(Exhibit 6). We expect increases in coupon auction sizes beginning in August 2027. 
Treasury’s attentiveness to pressures on long-end yields will probably limit auction size 
increases to the 2-to-10-year part of the curve, while we expect long-end auction sizes 
to remain unchanged (with risks skewed towards modest reductions). Larger coupon 
auction sizes could put upward pressure on repo rates to the extent that marginal 
absorption falls on the shoulders of levered investors. That said, net coupon supply is 
likely to shrink in 2027 based on our forecasts, despite larger auction sizes, which could 
help limit tightening pressure on funding conditions. 
 
In contrast, we expect net bill supply to grow by about $200bn to $1.1tn in 2027, of 
which we expect non-Fed buyers to absorb about $600bn versus $480bn in 2026.

Recently, larger bill issuance has intersected with slower MMF asset growth and shorter 
WAMs to generate tighter funding conditions and bill cheapening versus OIS (Exhibit 7). 
At the latest QRA, Treasury noted that it continues to see strong demand for bill 
securities from a broad range of market participants, highlighting growing demand from 
the private sector and SOMA purchases and pointing to ongoing comfort with bill 
absorption. That said, recent evidence points towards risks of more price sensitive bill 
absorption moving forward. 
Firm risk asset returns and higher yields further out the curve have attracted larger 
inflows into long-term bond funds and likely contributed to softer money fund AUM 
growth this year (Exhibit 8). The recent increase in risk asset volatility has not translated 
into outsized demand for money funds. Instead, it appears that investors have favored 
bond funds further out the curve, potentially reflecting the rediscovered hedge value of 
duration. Fed policy uncertainty has also pressured money fund WAMs shorter, 
suggesting caution about exposure to hike risk (Exhibit 9). This has also incrementally 
contributed to the cheapening in bills versus OIS. In 2027, we think less pronounced risk 
asset outperformance and a more benign inflation picture should support slightly 
stronger money fund demand and longer WAMs. This could help reverse some of the 
recent widening in bills-OIS, but we do not expect the strong pace of money fund 
growth seen in 2024-25 to repeat; instead, more moderate AUM growth looks like the 
likelier outcome. 
 
TGA & the debt limit: A meaningful source of volatility  
Debt limit dynamics present a potentially significant source of reserve and bill supply 
volatility in 2027, assuming the debt limit is not addressed preemptively. Our current 
funding path implies the debt ceiling will likely bind in Q1 2027 (most likely in February). 
Over the last three episodes, the TGA has declined by about $400bn in the period 
between when the debt limit binds and there is an eventual agreement, though a higher 
starting cash balance would leave scope for a larger decline. We estimate that a $400bn 
decline in the TGA (and corresponding shift in bill issuance) would imply about 4bps of 
downward pressure on SOFR versus the counterfactual.

should reverse the downward pressure on SOFR and could generate a period of greater 
volatility, as in 2025. Increased willingness to use the Fed’s standing repo program (SRP)

The starting point for reserves matters for funding volatility. When reserves are high and 
banks are operating on a flatter part of the demand curve, TGA fluctuations as a result 
of the debt limit should have more limited impact on funding conditions, as in 2021 and 
2023. Meanwhile, when reserves are lower and banks are on the steeper part of the 
demand curve, the post-debt limit TGA rebuild can trigger greater funding volatility, 
such as in 2019 and 2025 (Exhibit 10). Reserves are currently at more of a cusp on the 
demand curve, as shown in Exhibit 5 above, which means volatility could be more 
two-way: the initial decline and then subsequent build in TGA could cause volatility in 
funding both on the easier and tighter sides of the distribution. 
 
Looking out into 2027, underlying fundamentals point to a slightly tighter baseline for 
funding than this year, and a clearer case for more repo rate volatility. The debt limit and 
Treasury’s continued reliance on bill issuance and larger coupon auction sizes are likely 
to add incremental pressure on funding costs, especially against a backdrop of slow 
money fund AUM growth. That said, Fed policy should remain a stabilizing force for 
funding, both through RMPs and any balance sheet policy changes that reduce demand 
for reserves. In our view, the market reasonably discounts this slightly tighter baseline, 
but the path is likely to be bumpy, with various stumbling blocks along the way and risks 
of tighter funding conditions likely to build later in the year.
