In our October/November PPA Tracker, we cover the deals announced since our September
publication and take a deeper look at some of the notable transactions shaping the evolving
AI-power landscape.
Total disclosed power demand increased to ~90 GW (Sep 2026: 72 GW), while binding
contracted capacity rose to 29 GW (Sep 2026: 21 GW), driving an improvement in the
binding-to-announced ratio and reducing the tracker’s implied "ghost ratio."
We have added Bitcoin miners to our tracker, recognizing their increasing participation
in AI and high-performance computing ecosystems, as well as their potential role as both
power consumers and infrastructure partners.
Google's agreement with Constellation Energy was the largest transaction announced
during the period, adding approximately 3.6 GW of contracted capacity and further
reinforcing nuclear power's role in supporting hyperscaler demand.
Hyperscalers accounted for approximately 33% of the volume signed over the last month,
led by continued procurement activity from Google and other large technology customers.
Recent agreements suggest buyers remain willing to secure long-dated power supply
despite elevated power prices, with reliability, scalability, and speed to market increasingly
outweighing pure cost considerations in procurement decisions.
Deal to watch: A Memorandum of Understanding signed between Bloom Energy (BE) and
California Resources Corporation (CRC) contemplates the development of more than 1
GW of clean, firm power. The partnership combines CRC’s land, infrastructure, natural gas
supply, and carbon storage capabilities with Bloom’s fuel-cell technology, representing one
of the more innovative approaches emerging to serve AI-driven power demand.

Two principles matter. First, disclosed MW are not forecasts of delivered MW. They are capacity referenced in public
company disclosures and are retained because those disclosures influence market expectations and capital allocation. Second,
Tier 1 contracts can still face ordinary execution risk — permitting, construction, interconnection, fuel supply, or plant
restart risk. The Tier label asks whether the buyer and seller have a current contractual obligation, not whether physical delivery
is risk-free.
RECENT MAJOR DEALS: SIMILAR CAPACITY HEADLINES, DIFFERENT COMMITMENT LEVELS
Recent deals highlight growing demand for reliable power, but differ materially in commitment levels. Some secure customer
purchases, others establish development pathways, while some only identify potential capacity.
CEG / GOOGLE: INCREMENTAL NUCLEAR OUTPUT + EXISTING FLEET SUPPLY
CEG’s Oct. 6 agreements with Google cover 3,590 MW, but only 890 MW represents incremental generation. The remaining
2,700 MW is supply from CEG’s existing PJM fleet.
Key takeaways
• 890 MW, 20-year PPA supports >$4.3B of investment across 11 reactors at six sites, increasing output from existing
nuclear assets rather than building new plants.
• Separate 15-year, 2,700 MW agreement secures demand for CEG’s existing multi-fuel PJM fleet, improving revenue
visibility but adding no new capacity.
• First uprate is expected in 2028, with remaining projects extending through 2032-33, making earnings contingent on
phased delivery.
• Both agreements are Tier 1 and count 3,590 MW binding/disclosed, but should remain separate as one reflects
incremental nuclear output and the other existing-fleet supply.
BE / CRC: FUEL CELLS + CARBON CAPTURE DEVELOPMENT PATHWAY
Bloom Energy’s Nov. 2 collaboration with CRC and Carbon TerraVault evaluates ≥1 GW of fuel-cell generation paired with
carbon capture and storage. It creates a development channel but lacks committed customer offtake. We also discussed the
CCs opportunity for Bloom in detail in October 2026 (click here to refer).

RATIO TERMINOLOGY
Binding Ratio (per company): Tier 1 (binding) capacity ÷ Total disclosed capacity, expressed as a percentage. 100% means
every disclosed deal in our tracker is a fully executed, binding PPA.
Ghost Ratio (portfolio and per-company): Total disclosed capacity ÷ Tier 1 (binding) capacity, expressed as a multiple (e.g.,
600/ 200 ≈ 3.00x). A higher Ghost Ratio indicates a larger amount of disclosed capacity in MOUs, frameworks etc. relative to
contractually binding capacity.

Covered companies: Contract visibility improving, but earnings timing remains uneven
The six covered companies have disclosed around 56.0 GW of capacity, of which 22.9 GW is binding (41% binding ratio;
2.4x ghost ratio). CEG and VST have largely contracted portfolios, but a meaningful portion of value remains tied to restarts,
uprates and phased deliveries, meaning contracted MW does not immediately translate into earnings. NEE has the highest
development exposure, with a 4.8x ghost ratio, reflecting large-scale opportunities in Japan and Korea that remain dependent
on ownership, customer commitments and project execution. BE, FRVO and ORA sit between these extremes, with stronger
contract conversion but varying levels of project readiness. Overall, investors should focus on contract conversion, delivery
readiness and earnings attribution rather than headline MW.
Others: Large pipelines, limited contractual certainty
The "Others" group has disclosed 31.7 GW of capacity but only 5.1 GW is binding, implying a 6.2x ghost ratio. Much of the
capacity is tied to SMR frameworks, development agreements and financing arrangements rather than firm power-purchase
commitments. While companies such as TLN, WMB and AEP provide a stronger contractual foundation, many announced
agreements lack minimum purchase obligations or identifiable revenue streams. The key distinction is between development
optionality and enforceable demand, as not all signed agreements provide the same level of revenue protection or cash-flow
visibility.
Bitcoin miners: Power access matters more than PPA count
Bitcoin miners have disclosed 1.6 GW of capacity, with 1.3 GW binding (82% binding ratio), but the category includes utility
service agreements, powered-site access, fixed-price procurement and amended contracts rather than comparable generator

KEY DEAL INSIGHTS
CONSTELLATION ENERGY
CEG's contract portfolio increasingly converts hyperscaler, government and institutional demand into long-term support for
nuclear restarts, uprates and existing-fleet retention, improving revenue visibility and supporting future investment across the
fleet. However, signed MW and cash-flowing MW are not equivalent: a meaningful portion of contracted capacity, including
the 835 MW Crane restart, 890 MW Google uprates, 190 MW AWS uprate, and 744 MW residual nuclear tranche, is not
expected to contribute materially until 2029-2032 and remains subject to construction, regulatory and execution milestones.

Key takeaway
Microsoft
Microsoft is the sole 20-year demand anchor for the 835 MW Crane restart, making it foundational to CEG's contracted-growth thesis and supporting the 
economics of returning retired nuclear capacity to service. While the contract is signed, revenue generation depends on achieving commercial operation in H2 
2027, with restart execution, fuel licensing and interconnection-transfer milestones remaining key gating factors. Investor reaction was highly positive: CEG 
surged roughly 22% intraday on the announcement, the strongest market response across the PPA portfolio, reflecting the strategic significance of the nuclear 
restart.
Meta
CEG's 1,121 MW agreement, including a 30 MW uprate, protects Clinton's economics beyond Illinois' zero-emission-credit program and extends the plant's 
operating visibility without ratepayer support. The virtual-PPA structure enables faster contracting by avoiding physical-delivery constraints, though it introduces 
settlement and basis risk and makes Meta's credit quality a direct economic consideration. CEG gained 13-14% in premarket trading on the announcement, 
although the stock closed lower the following session as investors weighed valuation and execution considerations.
Walmart
Walmart expands CEG's nuclear customer base beyond hyperscalers and government buyers while supporting a 30 MW Dresden uprate. The two sequential 
15-year contract terms provide substantial cash-flow visibility, although the arrangement should not be viewed as a fully locked-in 30-year contract because 
the second term remains subject to future re-contracting risk.
CyrusOne
CyrusOne represents CEG's largest disclosed data-center relationship, with the partnership expanding beyond the initial 380 MW commitment to more than 1.1 
GW. The key uncertainty is economics, as ERCOT lacks a capacity market and pricing terms have not been disclosed, leaving margin visibility less certain than 
for CEG's nuclear-PPA portfolio.
GSA (US Gov't)
The federal government provides CEG's highest-quality credit counterparty and a stable 10-year revenue stream, while validating nuclear energy procurement 
within the public sector. However, the contract's shorter duration makes it more of a near-term credit benefit than a long-term fleet-retention agreement; the 
reported ~114 MW is derived from annual energy volumes rather than a dedicated capacity block. Investors responded favorably, with shares rising about 6% 
intraday and gaining another 4.0% the next session, reflecting the value of sovereign-backed revenue.

KEY TRENDS ACROSS THE PPA MARKET
Global corporate PPA additions reached approximately 36.4 GW through September 2026, taking cumulative procurement
to 380.1 GW. Activity remains ahead of prior years through September, although matching 2025’s full-year volume requires
another strong fourth quarter. US cumulative procurement reached around 229 GW - approximately 60% of the global total.
Solar remains the largest component of US annual contracting, but nuclear and co-located projects are increasingly
important. Buyers are diversifying supply rather than abandoning renewables. However, co-location alone does not establish
firm power; reliability depends on the underlying technology and contractual commitments.
Buyer concentration makes individual transactions consequential. Meta leads with approximately 7.6 GW, including 2.6 GW
of nuclear, followed by Google at approximately 3.7 GW.
Supplier rankings reinforce this: Vistra leads with 2.6 GW entirely from nuclear, while Engie’s approximately 2.4 GW is
predominantly wind. Xcel Energy and Tenaga Nasional feature prominently through co-located projects.

FROM ANNOUNCED DEMAND TO MONETIZABLE OPPORTUNITY
Since our September publication, the AI power market has shown early signs of progressing from announced ambition
toward executable demand. While total disclosed capacity continued to expand, a disproportionate share of new additions
came from binding agreements, improving overall contract quality and reducing the gap between announced and contracted
volumes. Importantly, several of the largest transactions announced during the period were tied to firm, dispatchable
resources such as nuclear, natural gas, and fuel-cell-based generation, highlighting a growing preference for reliability
alongside clean-energy objectives.
We continue to believe investors should focus less on headline megawatt figures and more on contract structure, delivery
timing, and monetization pathways. The key development this month is not simply that more capacity was announced, but
that a larger portion of announced capacity appears positioned to convert into earnings. As AI-driven electricity demand
accelerates, the distinction between pipeline and monetizable opportunity is becoming increasingly important to valuation.
