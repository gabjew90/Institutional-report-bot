Key takeaways 
• The biggest mistake in major investment booms is often 
underestimating duration, rather than focusing too narrowly on 
whether revenues can immediately justify investment. 
• China's industrial expansion shows how long-duration investment 
cycles can survive repeated doubts about returns, profitability and 
excess capacity. 
• Artificial intelligence (AI) may prove no different: leadership is likely 
to evolve as value migrates through the ecosystem and supply 
constraints shift. 
 
Is the revenue debate the right starting point? 
Investors are increasingly asking whether future AI revenues can justify the scale of 
investment now taking place. Spending on data centres, compute infrastructure, 
power and related technologies has risen rapidly, while uncertainty remains over 
when, and how widely, AI’s economic benefits will translate into revenue and 
profits. Equity valuations are part of that debate, but they are not unusually 
elevated across the market. The more immediate question is whether demand and 
monetisation can expand sufficiently to support the build-out. 
Many of the most significant investment booms of the past attracted similar 
scepticism. Railway investors worried that construction was outrunning demand, 
electrification raised doubts about whether capital spending would translate into 
productivity gains, and China’s industrial expansion prompted persistent

Capital Group research on China’s industrialisation suggests AI may be best 
understood as an S-curve investment cycle: one in which adoption starts slowly, 
accelerates as use cases expand, and eventually matures. As portfolio manager 
Nick Grace puts it, “both are S-curve demand stories,” both are “dogged by the 
same doubts about return on investment,” both produced “violent stock-price 
volatility,” and in both cases “the genuinely hard question is duration.” That 
framework shifts the focus away from whether near-term revenues already justify 
the investment. Instead, the key questions are whether demand is still 
expanding, monetisation is broadening, constraints are moving through the 
system, and supply has yet to catch up.

Every major investment boom has its own causes, beneficiaries and excesses; AI 
will not replicate railways, electrification or China’s industrial rise. But viewed as 
investment cycles, several recurring patterns stand out. 
Major investment booms differ in their causes and outcomes, but they  
often share a common pattern: 
1. Demand broadens over time. 
2. Questions about ROI never disappear. 
3. Volatility accompanies the opportunity. 
4. Duration matters more than investors expect. 
5. Value shifts as bottlenecks move.

Five questions for investors looking at AI 
Question 1: Is demand still broadening? 
Long investment cycles rarely move in a straight line. Adoption usually begins 
slowly, accelerates as use cases expand and eventually moderates as markets 
mature. The challenge is distinguishing a temporary pause from the natural 
unevenness of a longer structural trend. 
China offers a useful comparison. What looked like unsustainably strong 
demand was, in many cases, an economy moving up the steep part of an 
industrialisation S-curve. The equivalent question for AI is whether adoption is 
moving beyond early experimentation into broader enterprise use cases. 
Within this, three questions matter most: 
1. Is AI usage broadening beyond early adopters? 
2. Are new use cases emerging across industries and business functions? 
3. Is demand shifting from training and experimentation towards inference, 
automation and day-to-day deployment, where AI is used in live  
business processes? 
If the answer to these questions remains yes, the trajectory of adoption may be 
more informative than short-term fluctuations in spending or sentiment.

Question 2: Are returns beginning to justify the investment? 
No major investment boom escapes questions about return on investment. As 
capital spending accelerates, investors inevitably ask whether demand will 
ultimately support the scale of investment taking place. Those concerns surfaced 
during the railway era, reappeared during electrification and became a defining 
feature of China’s industrial expansion. 
Capital Group analysts’ research into the canal and railway manias highlights an 
important lesson: The technology may prove transformational, but history 
suggests that the financial realisation of such revolutions rarely moves in a 
straight line. That distinction is important for AI. A technology can be adopted 
rapidly while the financial benefits remain unevenly distributed across 
companies, sectors and time. 
The more useful test is whether evidence of adoption and monetisation is 
becoming more widespread. If productivity gains remain confined to a narrow 
set of applications, the debate becomes more challenging. If they become 
increasingly visible across industries and workflows, near-term ROI concerns may 
prove to be a feature of the cycle rather than evidence that it is ending. 
 
Question 3: Is volatility a warning sign, or a feature of the cycle? 
Investors often remember the long-term winners of major investment booms 
while forgetting how difficult the journey was along the way. Grace’s work on 
China is a useful reminder: “The winning stocks compounded roughly 8-10 
times, but with wicked volatility along the way.” 
For AI investors, market volatility does not necessarily invalidate the structural 
opportunity. Economist Bobby Esnard’s work makes the distinction clearly: 
“Technology cycles and financial cycles are not the same, though they can 
coincide and there is spillover.” In other words, adoption, infrastructure 
investment and productivity gains can continue to advance even when markets 
are unsettled by concerns about liquidity, valuation, earnings expectations  
or confidence. 
For investors, the key question is whether volatility reflects a change in the 
underlying drivers of the cycle. If adoption is expanding, investment remains 
robust and constraints persist, volatility may simply be part of the cycle. If those 
drivers weaken at the same time, the signal becomes more concerning. 
 
Question 4: How long can the cycle last? 
Perhaps the most important lesson from past investment booms is that near-term 
revenue and return expectations can be a poor guide to the duration of a 
structural cycle. Valuation still matters, but the larger mistake is often 
underestimating how long demand can continue and how monetisation can 
develop over time. 
China illustrates the point. Investors repeatedly questioned whether demand 
could support the scale of infrastructure spending and industrial capacity being 
built. Yet the cycle persisted because demand continued to absorb new supply.  
For AI, the implication is not that valuation should be ignored. It is that investors 
should also monitor the conditions that determine duration: whether adoption is 
still broadening, whether new sources of demand are emerging, whether 
constraints are easing or shifting, and whether supply is beginning to catch up. 
 
Grace’s conclusion from China is striking: “Valuation was not a useful guidepost.

Question 5: Where are supply constraints emerging? 
One of the most consistent features of major investment booms is that supply 
rarely expands smoothly enough to meet demand. Instead, constraints tend to 
emerge in different parts of the system as adoption accelerates.

China’s industrialisation followed this pattern, with value moving from 
commodities into equipment, logistics and eventually financial institutions linked 
to the build-out. AI appears to be following a similar path. The earliest focus was 
on compute, but attention has broadened into memory, networking, data-centre 
capacity, cooling, power and grid infrastructure. 
Copper illustrates how bottlenecks can migrate beyond the technology sector. 
AI infrastructure adds to demand already being generated by electrification, 
power-grid investment and other strategic priorities. At the same time, long 
development timelines and supply constraints can prevent production from 
responding quickly. The investment implications of the AI cycle may therefore 
extend well beyond semiconductor and software companies to the physical 
resources supporting the build-out. 
For investors, bottlenecks are useful because they can indicate both where value 
may migrate and where the cycle stands. If one constraint eases only for another 
to emerge, the build-out may still have further to run. If supply catches up across 
several parts of the value chain, the cycle may be becoming more mature. 
 
Where the analogy has limits 
Historical comparisons can provide a useful framework for thinking about AI, but 
they should not be treated as a template. Three differences matter most. 
1. AI can evolve faster than earlier infrastructure cycles. Railways, power grids 
and industrial capacity took years to build and replicate. AI models can

improve rapidly as computing power increases, algorithms evolve and new 
entrants emerge. 
2. The economics of AI models have no clean historical analogue. In previous 
investment booms, investors could generally identify the infrastructure being 
built and the services it would provide. In AI, the long-term economics of 
models and applications remain less certain. 
3. Markets may recognise and price bottlenecks faster than before. During 
China’s industrialisation, investors often had time to observe emerging 
constraints and reassess opportunities. Today’s markets process information 
and direct capital much more quickly, which could compress  
some opportunities. 
A final difference is the interaction between the technology cycle and the 
financial cycle. Esnard’s work highlights that financial cycles are vulnerable to 
liquidity, confidence, leverage and disappointment over earnings or revenue 
expectations, even when valuations are not unusually high. 
History is therefore best used as a guide, not a forecast. It helps identify the 
forces that often shape long investment cycles, but it does not eliminate the 
need to assess competition, regulation, capital intensity and model economics 
as the AI cycle evolves. 
For investors, value migration has both sector and geographic implications. 
China’s industrial expansion created opportunities not only among the 
companies building the original infrastructure, but also among equipment 
suppliers, logistics providers, commodity producers and financial institutions 
across multiple regions. AI may follow a similar pattern. As bottlenecks move 
from compute into memory, networking, cooling, power and materials, the 
opportunity set may broaden beyond today’s largest technology companies. 
The signposts we are watching 
History cannot tell us exactly how the AI cycle will unfold. But it suggests 
investors should focus on a small number of indicators rather than trying to 
predict every turn.

Conclusion 
The similarities to earlier booms do not guarantee a particular outcome, and 
they do not remove the risks associated with competition, regulation or revenues 
failing to keep pace with investment. But they do provide a useful framework: 
demand must broaden, monetisation and returns must emerge, volatility should 
be interpreted carefully, bottlenecks should be monitored and supply will 
eventually matter. 
The objective for investors is not to predict every turning point, but to judge 
whether the cycle is still expanding or beginning to mature. The answer is 
unlikely to come from any single valuation metric, company or technology, but 
from how adoption, revenues, productivity, constraints and supply evolve over 
the years ahead.
