1  2026 Gold Outlook
The story for gold continues
We remain constructive on gold heading into the final months of 2026 and into 2027. While 
volatility is likely to persist, we believe the forces supporting gold are increasingly structural.
Elevated government debt, persistent fiscal deficits, geopolitical fragmentation and concerns 
around currency debasement continue to reinforce gold’s role as a strategic asset. Central-bank 
reserve diversification remains an important pillar of demand, with purchases having increased 
significantly versus pre-2022 levels, particularly from emerging economies. Investor concerns 
over preserving purchasing power could provide an additional source of demand, particularly in 
an environment of falling real rates or financial repression.
Gold equities also remain attractive, in our view. Higher gold prices have supported margins and 
free cash flow, while miners have generally maintained greater capital discipline than in previous 
cycles. We expect stock selection to remain important, as operating execution, cost control and 
capital allocation increasingly differentiate shareholder returns.
2026 has reinforced the investment case
Gold has experienced an eventful and volatile year. After surging to record highs in January, the 
gold price corrected sharply through the first half of 2026. Escalating conflict in the Middle East 
pushed energy prices higher, reigniting inflation concerns and contributing to a higher-for-
longer repricing of interest rates. A stronger US dollar added further pressure. Gold has since 
rebounded strongly, rising sharply in August and returning to positive territory for the year.
Gold responds differently depending on the macro scenario. Historic drivers of interest rates and 
the US dollar remain influential in the short term, but gold is no longer behaving simply as an 
inverse real-yield trade. Since 2022, concerns around sovereign risk and more recently, fiscal 
sustainability and the long-term purchasing power of fiat currencies, have become important 
drivers of demand1.
Central banks purchased 345 tonnes of gold in H1 2026 2, continuing the step-change in 
official-sector demand seen since 2022. Investor demand has also begun to recover following 
significant selling from physically backed gold ETFs earlier in the year.
The path has not been linear, but 2026 has reinforced our view of gold as a strategic asset in 
a world of elevated debt, geopolitical fragmentation and growing concerns over currency 
debasement.

Rising government debt and persistent fiscal deficits are 
increasing concerns around the long-term purchasing power of 
fiat currencies. As debt-servicing costs rise and fiscal flexibility 
declines, we believe investors may increasingly look to non-
sovereign stores of value, supporting gold’s role as a strategic 
asset.
•
Rising debt increases the risk of financial repression:
Government debt and debt-servicing costs continue to rise 
across major developed economies. In the U.S., net interest 
costs are projected at around $1 trillion in 2026 3, already
exceeding defence spending, and CBO expects them to rise 
further as debt accumulates. Faster economic growth or fiscal 
consolidation could help stabilise debt, but policymakers could 
also turn to measures that suppress borrowing costs, including 
forms of financial repression such as quantitative easing or 
yield controls. By holding interest rates below where they might 
otherwise be, particularly alongside inflation, such policies 
could erode the real value of government debt — but also the 
purchasing power of paper currencies, potentially 
strengthening gold’s appeal as a scarce, non-sovereign asset.
•
Gold’s relationship with real yields has changed: Higher real 
rates have historically been a headwind for gold by increasing 
the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset. That 
relationship has become less reliable since 2022, with gold at 
times rising alongside elevated real yields as central-bank 
buying and concerns around fiscal and geopolitical risk have 
become more important. This suggests the reason yields are 
high increasingly matters — particularly when they reflect fiscal 
or inflation concerns rather than stronger economic growth. 
•
Gold has no sovereign issuer: Unlike government bonds, 
physical gold is not another country’s liability. The freezing of 
Russian central-bank reserves in 2022 highlighted the 
jurisdictional risk embedded in foreign reserve assets, helping 
strengthen the case for gold as a reserve asset that can be held 
outside another country’s financial system.

Central-bank buying stepped up structurally since 2022
Gold’s buyer base continues to broaden. Central banks 
remain significant purchasers, while new buyers such as 
Tether are emerging. Investor demand is also beginning to 
recover, although physically-backed gold ETF holdings 
remain relatively moderate. We believe this increasingly 
diversified demand base strengthens the structural case for 
gold.
Since 2022, there has been a shift in how central banks view their 
foreign reserves. Western sanctions on Russia may have 
reinforced demand for reserve diversification and assets that can 
be held domestically, supporting gold given its scarcity, store-of-
value characteristics and role as a geopolitical hedge. Central 
banks purchased 345 tonnes in H1 2026, while 89% expect 
global official holdings to increase over the next year.
The PBoC has continued to add gold to its reserves, with 
purchases accelerating in recent months. Yet gold represents 
just 9% of China’s foreign-exchange reserves 4, suggesting 
significant scope for further diversification over time. 
Gold price outpaced recovery in physically-backed 
ETF holdings
China has ramped its gold purchases: PBoC monthly 
purchase estimates
Tether has become a significant physical gold holder, with 
more than 146 tonnes at the end of Q2 2026, including 14 
tonnes added during the quarter. Its purchases highlight 
the emergence of new sources of demand, particularly as 
the tokenisation of gold gains traction.
Flows into physically-backed gold ETFs have improved 
following selling earlier in the year. However, holdings have 
risen only modestly relative to the strong move in the gold 
price, suggesting investor participation is not yet 
stretched.

While the structural forces supporting gold remain firmly in 
place, gold miners are operating businesses whose returns 
depend on costs, operational execution and capital allocation. 
We believe strong cash generation, improved balance sheets 
and attractive valuations create a compelling backdrop for 
selective gold equity investors.
Strong cash generation: Higher gold prices are supporting record 
margins and substantial free cash flow, while returns on invested 
capital have improved. These characteristics compare favourably 
with more capital-intensive areas of the equity market.
Seeing the assets on the ground: Mining is ultimately a geology 
business. Tom Holl recently visited five gold producers in Ontario, 
meeting the teams operating the assets. Site visits provide insight 
into factors including ore quality, mine life, infrastructure, labour 
availability and operating standards, as well as how companies are 
managing labour and energy costs.
Capital allocation will determine the winners, in our view: Through 
ongoing company engagement, we assess how management teams 
deploy excess cash across dividends, buybacks, reinvestment and 
growth. We favour companies that maintain capital discipline, 
protect balance sheets and prioritise shareholder returns over 
expensive acquisitions or value-destructive projects.
Valuations remain attractive: Despite stronger cash generation and 
improved financial characteristics, gold miners continue to trade at 
relatively modest valuations. A re-rating could provide an additional 
source of shareholder returns if investor sentiment towards the 
sector improves.
