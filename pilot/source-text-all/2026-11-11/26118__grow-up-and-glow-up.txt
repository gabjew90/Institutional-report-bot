Executive 
Summary
In March 2025, we argued that investors who want digital assets should put them 
in the benchmark, size them by risk contribution rather than by notional weight 
and keep allocations below 20% of total portfolio risk. We also wrote that 
contribution to risk would vary over time as the asset class evolved. Eighteen 
months later, we now return to the data to ask how much it has evolved and what 
that means for allocators. This time, we include a greater focus on institutional 
portfolios that also incorporate illiquid private and liquid alternative asset classes.
Key Findings
•	 Bitcoin’s return distribution has compressed. Annualized daily volatility has fallen from ~84% in 
2017–2019 to ~46% since mid-2024, and the average loss on the worst 5% of days has halved, from 
-9.9% to -5.0%. This is consistent with the institutionalization we anticipated: deeper derivatives and 
exchange-traded fund (ETF) infrastructure and a larger base of long-horizon holders. That said, 
bitcoin remains volatile and prone to large drawdowns; the current era still includes a 53% peak-to-
trough decline.
•	 Correlations with traditional assets have retreated from their 2022–2024 highs, but they are 
regime-dependent. Bitcoin’s correlation to global equities is highest when equities are in a negative 
regime (roughly 0.4–0.5 since 2020) and lowest when equities are strong (0.1–0.3). For prudent 
dynamic allocators this implies that funding for digital assets should continue to come from other 
high-risk assets but also points to their increasingly diversifying quality.
•	 The same 20% risk ceiling now accommodates a 7.5% allocation. In a diversified institutional 
portfolio (45% global equity, 25% US bonds, 12% private equity, 10% real estate, 8% hedge funds), a 7.5% 
bitcoin allocation funded pro rata contributes 20% of total portfolio risk. In our 2025 paper, 3% in a 
60/40 portfolio contributed 11.4%; today, with volatility bands continuing to shrink, 3% in the same 
60/40 portfolio contributes just 6.5%. 
•	 Across 12 macro and crypto-specific shocks since 2020, bitcoin’s first-week behavior has been 
mixed and its two-month recovery has been consistently stronger than equities. In the one true 
liquidity shock in our sample (COVID-19), bitcoin fell twice as far as equities in the first week and its 
equity correlation doubled. During the 2023 banking crisis, the 2025 tariff shock and the 2026 US-Iran 
conflict, bitcoin outperformed equities in the first week as well as over a 60-day period. The direction 
of each correlation change is explainable by the nature of the event, which implies that allocators can 
make informed Dynamic Asset Allocation (DAA) decisions for crypto assets based on their macro 
forecasts.
•	 Risk budgets should be actively monitored. While peak risk contributions have fallen in each 
successive cycle, the asset class remains prone to volatility spikes. We recommend risk-contribution 
ceilings as rebalancing triggers, reviewed at least quarterly. The same discipline applies to private and 
alternative allocations whose risk is understated by smoothed valuations.
How This Paper Is Organized
We follow the logic of an allocator approaching a new asset class. Section 1 asks what bitcoin represents 
in a portfolio, because sizing depends on the role you expect it to play. Section 2 shows how the asset’s 
statistical character has evolved, because those figures are the core inputs into allocation formulas. 
Section 3 applies our risk-contribution framework to a diversified institutional portfolio and reconciles the 
result with our 2025 paper. Section 4 tests the framework against real shocks. Section 5 covers monitoring 
and rebalancing. A sidebar outlines further ideas for the digital asset sleeve itself. The Notes and Index 
Definitions section at the end covers the data points mentioned throughout this paper.

1. What Is Bitcoin’s Role in a Portfolio?
Allocators tend to frame bitcoin in one of three ways: a 
leveraged equity proxy, a store of value (“digital gold”) or a 
high-volatility speculative position. As with many stereotypes, 
these carry some truth yet fail to be validated with data. The 
nuanced reality is that bitcoin’s relationship with traditional 
assets has been evolving and has clear regime dependency, 
further modified by the nature of the shock.
Exhibit 1 summarizes rolling 36-month correlations of bitcoin 
with major asset classes across two periods. Correlations rose 
broadly after 2020 and peaked in 2023-2024, when bitcoin 
traded as a liquidity-sensitive risk asset alongside equities 
and high yield. Over 2025-2026 that linkage has loosened: 
Equity correlation has fallen toward 0.3, high-yield toward 0.1–
0.2, aggregate bonds toward zero, and commodities have 
turned modestly negative. The precious metals relationship 
has never been persistent in either direction, which argues 
against a simple “digital gold” framing.
The same picture holds against private and alternative assets. 
On the recent-weighted covariance, we use for portfolio 
construction (Section 3), bitcoin’s correlation is 0.32 with 
global equities, -0.01 with aggregate bonds, 0.28 with private 
equity, 0.22 with real estate and 0.18 with hedge funds. By 
comparison, private equity and hedge funds correlate 0.72 
and 0.84 with global equities. Thus, from that perspective, 
bitcoin is more diversifying and liquid than the alternatives 
with which it is often compared. Section 4 sharpens the 
picture by regime and by event. 
To answer this section’s question, we consider bitcoin a long-
duration, high-volatility asset with moderate and time-varying 
equity beta, whose idiosyncratic drivers (adoption, regulation, 
and market structure) are large enough that it does not neatly 
reduce to any one traditional analogue. That is why we size it 
by its risk contribution rather than by analogy and consider 
cryptocurrencies to be a fundamentally new asset class.

2. How Has This New Asset Class Changed?
In 2025 we noted that contribution to risk “will vary over time 
due to the evolving volatility of financial assets.” For bitcoin, 
the evolution has been directional. We divide the history since 
2017 into four eras defined by who held the asset and how it 
could be accessed (Exhibit 2 and Exhibit 3). Each era adds 
infrastructure that widens the holder base and lengthens its 
horizon: regulated futures and options; institutional custody 
and corporate treasury buying; the 2022 deleveraging and 
the transition to spot ETFs; and, since mid-2024, broad access 
through ETFs, ETF options and wealth platforms.
Three points stand out. First, annualized daily volatility has 
reduced by roughly 40% from 90% to 50%. This is an apples-
to-apples comparison; the decline is in the data, not in how 
we measure it and supported by the aforementioned growth 
in institutional infrastructure.
Second, the tails have shrunk in severity. The average loss on 
the worst 5% of days has moved from –10.3% to -5.3%, and the 
upside tail has moderated in step (the average gain on the 
best 5% of days fell from 11.9% to 6.2%). Relative tail heaviness 
has not declined uniformly: excess kurtosis rose from 2.56 to 
4.42 across the first two eras; peaking in the 2019Q3–2021Q4 
institutional adoption before falling to 2.68 in the current era. 
Skewness has stayed near zero throughout but has slightly 
shown positive value of 2.68 in the more recent era of spot 
ETFs and board institutional access. The maturation story is 
about compression of the whole distribution, not a change in 
its asymmetry.
Third, drawdowns remain large. A 53% peak-to-trough decline 
in the “mature” era is still several times what a diversified 
portfolio experiences, and the 2022 episode, driven by 
crypto-native leverage rather than macro, produced a 7% 
deeper drawdown than the era before it. Lower day-to-day 
volatility and persistent cyclical drawdowns both matter for 
sizing—the former sets the steady-state risk contribution and 
the benchmark allocation; the latter sets the dynamic 
rebalancing discipline (Section 5).
Why has volatility fallen? Our view, which we articulated in 
2025 and in client conversations before that, is that 
institutional participation centralizes holdings in “diamond 
hands” (long-term investors who tend to hold their assets 
through periods of market volatility rather than actively trading 
or selling) that trade less. Spot ETFs, corporate treasuries and 
staked or long-term holdings reduce the share of supply that 
turns over on any given day. We cannot attribute the 
improvement exclusively to adoption, and the same forces 
may compress returns as well as risk. This paper makes no 
return projection; all return figures shown are historical.

3. How Should Bitcoin Be Sized?
The same framework, with updated inputs
Our approach has three steps. Put digital assets in the 
benchmark, so that its risk is managed as policy rather than 
as an off-benchmark position that swamps the active risk 
budget (in 2025 we showed a 4% off-benchmark position 
accounting for 103% of a 60/40 portfolio’s active risk). Size 
the benchmark weight by its contribution to total portfolio 
risk, with a guideline that no single position should contribute 
more than about 20%, and a range of roughly 5%–20% 
depending on the portfolio’s overall risk profile. Then refine 
the funding source and offsetting tilts.
A diversified institutional portfolio
Our 2025 examples used a 60/40 portfolio and a 100% equity 
portfolio. Here we extend the analysis to a portfolio that 
reflects the growing weight of private and alternative assets in 
institutional allocations: 45% global equities, 25% US 
aggregate bonds, 12% private equity, 10% real estate and 8% 
hedge funds. This is not a like-for-like update of the 2025 
exhibits: We have less granularity within public equities and 
fixed income, and more breadth across asset classes. 
Our methodology: We fund the bitcoin allocation pro rata 
from all five asset classes. We do this for simplicity and for 
conservatism. Funding pro rata, rather than from the highest-
risk and fattest-tailed sleeves, overstates bitcoin’s incremental 
risk contribution relative to a more refined funding program, 
which is what allocators should continue to do in practice. 
Volatility, tracking error and risk contributions use a covariance 
matrix estimated on the most recent 45 months (October 
2022–June 2026) with a 24-month half-life exponential 
weighting, because the purpose of those statistics is to 
describe the near-term risk structure, and because bitcoin’s 
volatility has fallen enough that a long equal-weighted history 
would misstate it. Historical returns, ratios and tail metrics use 
120 months of monthly returns, because 45 observations are 
too few to estimate a 95% tail. For private equity we use a 
de-smoothed return series, so that its risk contribution is not 
artificially understated by appraisal-based valuations; real 
estate and hedge fund series are used as reported and 
should be read as conservative on risk.

Three features of Exhibit 4 drive our conclusion. Bitcoin’s 
share of total risk rises supra-linearly: 2% of capital is 4% of 
portfolio risk, 3% is 7.5% risk, 6% is 15%, and 10% is more than a 
quarter. The diversification of the rest of the portfolio absorbs 
the first increments almost entirely; volatility rises 0.1 
percentage points at 2% and 0.7 points at 6%, while the 
10-year maximum drawdown widens by 2.4 points and the 
number of months below the risk-free rate rises from 41 to 44. 
Over the past decade in this study risk-adjusted returns 
improved at every allocation shown, but we should be mindful 
that past performance doesn’t forecast future returns.
Applying our 20% guideline, the implied allocation for 
this portfolio today is 7.5%. The result is not sensitive to the 
covariance window—on a 120-month exponentially weighted 
covariance, which gives earlier and more volatile years some 
weight, 6% contributes 17.9% of risk and 8% contributes 25.2%. 
Allocators with a benchmark that has riskier assets to use 
as funding sources may reasonably lift the ceiling. Our 
guideline is that single position risk concentration should be 
under a fifth of the total, thus the benchmark’s other asset 
classes will affect the final prudent weight for digital assets 
in the portfolio.

Reconciling With 60/40 and 2025
In our 2025 paper, a 3% bitcoin allocation in a 60/40 portfolio 
contributed 11.4% of total risk, and 6% in an all-equity portfolio 
contributed 16.4% (February 2015–February 2025). Re-running 
the same 60/40 portfolio today, with bitcoin funded from 
equities as before, 4% contributes 9.6% of risk and 6% 
contributes 16.6% (Exhibit 5). In other words, an allocation 
one-third larger than our 2025 example now carries less risk 
than that example did. The rule has not changed; the inputs 
have. As we expected, further adoption and centralization of 
digital assets have lowered their risk contribution per unit of 
capital even as the asset class remains volatile and subject to 
large, sudden drawdowns. This is precisely why allocators 
cannot take a set-it-and-forget-it approach but should instead 
take a risk-forward stance that allocates based on current 
realities and revisits them as the asset evolves. That is not a 
crypto-specific point; it applies equally to private and 
alternative assets whose smoothed valuations understate their 
true contribution to risk.
Refining the Allocation
Our 2025 guidance still applies: Fund digital assets from the 
sleeves with the highest volatility or fattest tails, such as 
emerging market equities and high-yield credit, and consider 
offsetting part of the added risk with safe-haven exposure 
such as US Treasuries. Funding from public equities alone, the 
other construction we tested, produces near-identical risk 
shares (15.3% at 6%) in this portfolio, because equities 
dominate its risk to begin with. Allocators should also decide 
explicitly whether bitcoin sits inside the alternatives budget or 
alongside public equities, because that decision determines 
which sleeve is trimmed and therefore the net risk effect.

4. How Does Bitcoin Behave in a Shock?
Averages hide the behavior allocators care about. We examine 
12 events since 2020: six macro and geopolitical shocks, and 
six crypto-specific milestones. For each, we compare the 200 
trading days before and after the event—bitcoin and equity 
volatility, and bitcoin–equity correlation. We also show returns 
over the first week and the first 60 days after the event. 
Correlations are computed on traditional-market trading days, 
with bitcoin’s weekend returns rolled into the next trading day 
so that non-trading days do not bias the estimates; bitcoin 
volatility uses the full seven-day series and is annualized on 
365 days, equity volatility on 252.
Two patterns stand out, and they pull in different directions, 
which is why we show both. First, bitcoin’s first-week behavior 
is not uniformly worse than equities’ as some investors have 
feared. It was only the case in the one true liquidity shock in 
the sample, COVID-19, when bitcoin fell twice as far as 
equities in seven days and its equity correlation doubled. But 
in the 2023 banking crisis, the 2025 tariff shock and the 2026 
US–Iran conflict, bitcoin outperformed equities in the first 
week, and in the latter two its volatility fell after the event 
while equity volatility rose. Second, bitcoin’s 60-day recovery 
has been stronger than equities’ in all six episodes. The 
direction of the correlation change tracks the nature of the 
shock: Correlation rose when the shock was about liquidity 
and rates (COVID-19, Russia–Ukraine war), fell when the shock 
implicated the banking system itself or when bitcoin-specific 
drivers dominated, and was unchanged in the tariff episode. 
Correlations with the full portfolio excluding crypto move the 
same way: 0.27 to 0.51 around COVID-19, 0.41 to 0.64 around 
the Russia–Ukraine war, 0.57 to 0.16 around the banking crisis, 
0.43 to 0.49 around tariffs.

Crypto-specific events show the complementary behavior: 
Regulatory and market-structure milestones moved bitcoin 
without moving equities, and correlations in the following 
months were typically low. Several milestones were followed 
by a short-term reversal (the ETF approval, the Strategic 
Bitcoin Reserve), which is a reminder that anticipated catalysts 
are priced before they arrive. Where correlation rose after a 
crypto milestone (the 2024 halving and ether ETF launches), 
the post-event window coincided with macro episodes that 
dominated both markets. The two tables together describe an 
asset whose diversification value is real but conditional, and 
whose conditionality is legible.
This is an important finding because it implies that bitcoin has 
consistent loadings to macro and idiosyncratic factors, rather 
than being an erratic speculative asset, and thus dynamic 
investors can make informed predictions about its path, same 
as with traditional asset classes.
Regime View
To generalize beyond individual events, we classify global 
equity markets into positive, neutral and negative regimes 
using five trend and momentum indicators, which yields about 
18 regime changes per year and a transition matrix with 91%–
95% persistence along the diagonal. Before 2020, bitcoin–
equity correlation was near zero in all three regimes. Since 
2020 it has been highest in negative equity regimes (0.3–0.5, 
most recently 0.43) and lowest in positive regimes (0.1–0.3, 
most recently 0.29). The gap has narrowed somewhat but 
remains. The practical reading is the same as the event 
studies: Expect bitcoin to behave more like equity when risk 
assets sell off, and stress test your portfolios for that scenario.

5. How Should the Allocation be Governed?
Risk contribution is not a constant to be set in stone. Exhibit 9 
tracks bitcoin’s share of total portfolio risk through time for 
allocations of 2% to 10% in the institutional portfolio, using 
weekly returns, a rolling 24-week window and a 12-week half-
life so that the estimate reflects prevailing conditions rather 
than a long-run average.
Four observations follow. The relationship between weight and 
risk share is non-linear and time-varying, because the 
denominator, total portfolio variance, changes as bitcoin’s own 
variance and covariances change. There are distinct high-risk 
regimes: A 10% allocation contributed as much as 90%–95% of 
total portfolio risk in 2017–2018, and even a 4% allocation has 
reached roughly 40%–50% in the worst episodes. Peak 
contributions have fallen in each successive cycle, from 
roughly 90% in 2017–2018 to 75%–80% in 2021 and 40%–45% 
in 2024–2025 for the 10% allocation, which at the end of the 
series (June 2026) contributes about 25%. And at small 
weights the contribution can briefly turn negative, meaning 
bitcoin’s covariance with the rest of the portfolio was negative 
enough to reduce total risk at the margin.
The shrinking envelope is the quantitative version of our 
central message: Lower volatility has made a given risk budget 
go further. The width of the envelope within any era is the 
warning that accompanies it. We therefore recommend:
•	 Risk-contribution ceilings as rebalancing triggers. 
Define the maximum share of total risk the digital asset 
sleeve may represent (we use 20%, but each client should 
make this an explicit policy in line with their objectives and 
horizon), estimate it on a rolling basis and trim when it is 
breached rather than waiting for a calendar date. While 
quarterly reviews should be the minimum, as we 
recommended in 2025; the rolling analysis suggests the 
binding events may arrive faster.
•	 Symmetry. A ceiling implies a floor. When bitcoin’s risk 
share falls well below target in a quiet period, the same 
discipline argues for adding back toward the policy weight, 
which is how a risk-forward allocation harvests the volatility 
it tolerates.
•	 The same discipline for alternatives. Once private 
equity returns are de-smoothed, that sleeve carries 
roughly a fifth of this portfolio’s ex-ante risk at a 12% 
weight, more than the 10% bitcoin allocation does. Real 
estate and hedge fund series in Exhibit 4 are as reported 
and almost certainly understate risk for the same reason. 
Allocators who adopt rolling risk governance for digital 
assets should apply it to their desmoothed private sleeves 
as well; the 2022 experience, in which public and private 
marks diverged for several quarters, is the cautionary case.

Conclusion
Our framework continues to hold up: Put digital assets in the 
benchmark, size them by their contribution to total risk, keep 
any single position below about a fifth of that risk, and fund 
them thoughtfully. What has changed are the inputs: Bitcoin’s 
volatility has roughly halved as the asset has institutionalized, 
and its correlation with traditional assets has retreated from 
the 2022–2024 highs. The result is that the same risk budget 
now accommodates roughly 6% in a diversified institutional 
portfolio, and 4% in a 60/40 portfolio carries less risk today 
than 3% did when we last wrote.

high-beta asset in a true liquidity shock, its drawdowns are 
still measured in halves, and its risk contribution still swings 
by a factor of several within a cycle. The right response to an 
asset that is changing is a process that changes with it: rolling 
measurement of risk contribution, explicit ceilings that trigger 
action and a willingness to revisit the policy weight as the 
data move. That is a risk-forward stance, and it is good 
practice well beyond digital assets.

Proprietary Pilot
This paper treats the digital asset allocation as a single spot-bitcoin position because 
bitcoin has the longest history and the deepest institutional market. Several 
extensions are available to allocators who want more from the sleeve.
Diversification within digital assets. Our 2025 institutional guide described three 
complementary approaches to capital market assumptions for digital assets: statistical 
models (broadest application), cash-flow-based valuation for fee-generating 
platforms, and network-based valuation for ecosystems whose value tracks usage. 
The same taxonomy can shape a multi-token sleeve with targeted exposures across 
cryptocurrencies, smart-contract platforms, decentralized finance, consumer and 
utility tokens, sized to a client’s risk budget using the framework above.
Systematic strategies as a partial complement to spot. Franklin Templeton 
Digital Assets runs a systematic trend-following strategy on ether that combines 
a daily trend signal with a regime-aware machine-learning overlay. Over its live history 
since October 2022, its monthly return correlation has been 0.61 with spot bitcoin, 
0.18 with global equities and -0.03 with US aggregate bonds, compared with 0.32 and 
-0.01 for spot bitcoin against equities and bonds. Replacing a fifth of a spot-bitcoin
sleeve with such a strategy reduces the sleeve’s dependence on bitcoin-specific
directional risk. We have excluded it from the core analysis because 45 months of
history is too short to support tail-risk estimates with confidence.
Dynamic risk-based scaling. The rolling risk-contribution analysis in Section 5 
implies a natural extension: a sleeve that scales exposure down as ex-ante volatility 
and correlation with the rest of the portfolio rise, and back up as they fall. We expect 
to publish on this separately.
