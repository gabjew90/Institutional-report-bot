EUR inflation analysis 
Massive drop in energy prices and update of our forecast 
Leaving aside the November roll effect, energy prices have significantly dropped 
since last Tuesday. This is due to geopolitical de-escalation in Iran, fuelled by the 
Democrats’ victory in the midterm elections and the ongoing discussions with 
Qatar. The price of Brent crude oil has gone down by EUR25/bl within a 
week and gas TTF by EUR12/MWh. Keep in mind that until the situation is 
settled in the Strait of Hormuz, a new surge in energy wholesale prices remains 
possible. 
If persistent, this drop in energy prices will have a direct impact on headline MoM 
inflation through motor fuel prices in November and December, and on natural 
gas and electricity prices in the coming months. We now have headline HICP at 
3.71% YoY in November, vs 3.95% in our last weekly.  
Moreover, we assume that lower gas prices would ease pressure on production 
costs and hence on spillover effects from the energy crisis to overall inflation in 
2027. The June 2027 TTF future contract went down by around EUR5/MWh last 
week. Referring to our usual pass-through, we lowered our EZ Core HICP 
overall by around 10bp in 2027, now at 2.9% on a yearly average, and 3-4bp 
in 2028, at 2.3%. 
Last look back at October flash HICP 
October flash HICP for the Eurozone came out at 4.00% YoY, 3bp above our 
take from the Inflation-Linked Weekly. Core inflation came out at 2.54%, 6bp 
above our take. The upside surprise in headline inflation comes from minor 
adjustments in the Big 4 countries as well as strong prints in Portugal and 
Greece, especially concerning tourism-related services. 
Core HICP came out stronger than expected in Spain, for which INE (Spanish 
statistics institute) provided very little data in its press release. As in most 
European economies, tourism-related services (eg, package holidays, mentioned 
in the INE release, airfares, accommodation) were likely stronger than we initially 
expected. Spanish core goods also contributed to the upside surprise, but we 
assume this was mainly linked to clothing after a weak September print.

What is trend, what is noise? 
Last week, we mentioned the difficulty to disentangle noise from trend in 
this October release. Part of the October upside surprise came from energy 
prices, which we said could totally reverse the following month. What happened 
last week on energy markets gives us firsthand proof. 
The core HICP YoY slightly increased in October whereas we expected a 
slight decrease. The strong print in volatile services might be partially offset next 
month. However, we think that airfares could remain high for several months as 
airline companies’ jet fuel hedges gradually expire. Core goods inflation 
increased by only 2bp, illustrating the lack of spillover effects from the energy 
prices so far. 
What do we learn from a granular analysis of PPI? 
Manufacturing Producer Price Index (PPIs) kept growing in terms of YoY in 
September, but it has little to do with consumer goods so far. 
As a reminder, PPIs are defined as the revenue perceived by the producers of 
industrial goods. Thus, we generally expect PPIs to pass on to consumer prices 
with a three- to six-month delay. Keep in mind that there is no obvious pass-
through from PPIs to HICP for several reasons: 
⚫ 
PPIs encompass a mix of consumer and intermediate goods, the latter 
affecting consumer prices but with more lag. However, they do not include 
directly consumed imported goods, which count in the HICP basket. 
⚫ 
PPIs elude (1) transportation costs; (2) part of the subsidies and taxes as 
well as sales seasons; and (3) the distribution margins. 
⚫ 
The categories used by PPIs differ from those used by HICP, making it 
difficult to match series. 
Strikingly, while manufacturing PPIs have increased from 0.3% YoY in February 
2026 to 7.0% YoY in September, consumer goods PPIs settled at 0.4% YoY in 
September.  
This is due to the contraction in the YoY of PPIs of non-durable goods, 
particularly food products (-1.9% YoY in September). This is surprising with 
prices of the means of agricultural production (fertiliser, fuels, machines) that 
increased in most of European economies in Q226. The lack of transmission 
might be explained by producer margin contraction, hedges and government 
subsidies. 
As for durable goods, PPI rose by 3.4% YoY in September, up from 2.5% in 
February. Notably, the steady rise in the PPI of furnishings is already visible in 
some countries’ HICP figures, and we assume it will continue to pass on in the 
months to come. 
All in all, PPIs offer little evidence of strong spillover effects from the 
energy crisis to consumer prices in the months to come. We assume that 
food inflation will be stronger than suggested by PPIs because of the surge in 
input prices. Core goods PPIs have already started to increase and should be 
visible on the HICP data in the months to come.

What about Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s programme? 
Last Saturday, French presidential candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon presented his 
programme for the 2027 election. On inflation-related topics, we mainly identified 
that he intends to (1) raise the minimum wage; (2) index wages to inflation; 
and (3) freeze prices on essential goods. 
Mélenchon proposed increasing the minimum wage by roughly 15%, from 
EUR1,500 in December 2026 to EUR1,700 if he is elected. Economic literature 
tells us that a 1% increase in minimum wage triggers a rise in the average wage 
by 0.11-0.25% in France within one year. Taking the upper estimation, we would 
have a 3.75% increase in the average wage. Considering 33% price elasticity 
to wages – according to the same economic literature – this measure would 
add 125bp to headline inflation spread over three years. 
Bear in mind that, according to current polling, Mélenchon has a very low 
probability of winning the 2027 presidential election.

EUR inflation strategy 
Quick Q&A on the legislative debate over ending OATei/i supply 
What was the recent news about it? 
On Wednesday 4 November, LFI2 members of France’s National Assembly 
proposed an amendment to the 2027 budget so that French bonds “should 
not be linked to inflation”. The amendment was initially adopted3, but shortly 
after the vote, the National Assembly unanimously rejected the entire revenue 
section of the 2027 budget including this amendment, something that already 
looked likely at the time. Bear in mind that the RN party4 has also long been 
opposed to inflation-linked bonds. 
At the current juncture, the 2027 Budget will very likely pass with Article 49.3 or 
Article 47, meaning that the usual issuance pattern – including OATei and 
OATi – will almost certainly repeat in 2027. 
What is the rationale for opposition to OATei/i issuance? How valid is it? 
The LFI and RN lawmakers’ rationale is that with rising inflation, inflation-linked 
bonds have become far too costly for France – see Figure 7, which shows a 
translation of the (very) beginning of LFI MP Aurélien Lecoq’s speech on the 
matter at the National Assembly on 4 November. As mentioned above, the RN 
shares a relatively similar view. 
Fig 7. A (very short) excerpt of the discussion on ending OATei/i issuance 
 
Source: French National Assembly, transcript of the 4 November 2026 session 
How valid is the argument? Well it depends. In the 2000s and 2010s, many OATi 
were launched with a breakeven inflation rate significantly above what 
inflation turned out to be over the life of the linker, meaning that the French 
state made savings on this.  
More recently, the opposite was true, as inflation increased.  For example, the 
OATi Mar-2028 was launched in February 2017 with a breakeven inflation rate 
around 1.60% (see our 9 March 2017 Inflation-Linked Weekly, p11), while French 
inflation ex-tobacco has run around 2.15%5 YoY on average since then, implying 
that a standard OAT would have been a cheaper option – by around 0.5% – 
for France in this specific case. 
Needless to say, it is easy to say, in hindsight, that it was a ‘good’ or a ‘bad’ idea 
to launch linkers, once inflation – whether low or high – is a known variable. 
From a DMO (Treasury) perspective, issuing inflation-linked bonds is 
perfectly logical: the revenues of a state are overall linked to inflation, as the 
higher nominal GDP growth, the higher the tax base and the higher the revenue 
for a state. Therefore, for a Treasury, issuing linkers makes total sense from 
an ALM perspective.

Also, there are investor types who need inflation protection, therefore they will 
accept to pay a moderate inflation premium, which implies that a DMO may issue 
linkers at slightly better conditions than regular bonds, and in addition a DMO 
may have access to other investor categories. This is why many countries – 
eg, the US, the UK, Australia, Italy, Spain and Brazil – issue inflation-linked 
bonds despite the fact that, sometimes, paying inflation is indeed costly, as has 
been the case since 2022. 
However, from a political standpoint, inflation-linked bonds have a 
weakness: poor timing. Indeed, their cost for public finances (inflation 
indexation) will increase after a phase of rising inflation, which generally 
coincides with social unrest (eg, in 2022 and 2026 in France) or higher interest 
rates (2026). Simply put, their higher cost may well appear at the worst 
moment.  
Didn’t Germany stop issuing linkers? They must have had a reason… 
As we mentioned above, there are pros and cons to issuing linkers. Among the 
cons is the risk that – if inflation is very elevated for a long time – it would become 
costly for the issuing country. 
In Germany, the linker programme had never been central – it issued around 
EUR10bn per year in the 2010s, around EUR6bn per year in the early 2020s 
(Figure 8), four times less than in France in recent years – and the linker 
programme was also under heavy criticism from the AfD party. In the end, 
following the inflation spike of 2022, Germany preferred ending the programme 
Bear in mind that even if Germany has stopped issuing linkers, the Bundei -30, 
-33 and -46 will of course have to be all repaid, with inflation accrual of course.

Could the German case – the end of linker supply – apply to France? 
Never say never, but in our view, it is unlikely that linker issuance will stop in 
France next year or in the foreseeable future. 
1. The French linker programme is much bigger in France – around 
EUR25bn on average since 2020 – than it was in Germany, when the Bundei 
programme was stopped.  
2. If it ended a EUR25bn annual linker programme, France would immediately 
have to supply roughly EUR30bn more of OATs (or anything else), as linkers 
have a higher market value than nominals (because of inflation accrual). In 
our view, this would not be feasible in the current context. 
3. The French linker programme has always been very predictable, well built, 
with two curves, and with smoothly rising size, for three decades. Breaking 
this predictability would not be a good signal, as investors like stability 
and predictability.

4. Last but not least, in France, there is the Livret A (EUR600bn size), which 
pays French inflation. Without OATi, it would be more difficult for French 
banks – which distribute the Livret A – to hedge for the inflation risk. 
Creating new risks for French banks right now would also be 
ill-advised, in our view. 
In our view, linker supply would only run the risk of being ended (1) if the next 
French president is from RN or LFI; (2) if the National Assembly supports this 
president’s policy; (3) if the new government sticks to the promise of ending linker 
supply; and (4) if the French fiscal situation allows it. However, these are many 
hypotheticals that would need to coincide – and the last one in particular is a big 
if. 
Livret A update 
On Tuesday 3 November, French PM Sébastien Lecornu announced6 that the 
Livret A ceiling will be raised from EUR22,950 to between EUR25,000 and 
EUR30,000. 
We had envisaged this possibility two months ago, after the Minister for the 
Green Transition Monique Barbut mentioned the same idea. We even had 
recommended being long French inflation (5YF5Y) vs Eurozone inflation, as such 
an increase in the Livret A would likely trigger significant inflows into the Livret A, 
and in turn need for French inflation hedging. The trade idea went well at first. 
Then, later in October, the French National Assembly rejected the increase in the 
Livret A ceiling and we therefore closed our trade recommendation. 
In the end, it looks like (1) the Livret A ceiling will indeed be increased by 10-30% 
and (2) the Livret A rate will be increased early next year. Regarding the latter, 
the formula suggests a Livret A rate raised possibly to around 2.6% in 
February, then possibly to 2.8% in August (see Figure 8). These possible 
levels for the Livret A rate are slightly lower than our estimates last week, 
courtesy of the recent decline in energy prices.

Today: the US CPI preview 
All eyes on the US CPI numbers for October, which are due a bit later today. 
Essentially, we expect US headline CPI to come out at 0.29% MoM SA in 
October. We cannot provide a YoY number, as the October 2025 index was 
never published by the BLS, due to last year’s government shutdown. There is no 
YoY consensus either of course.  
The US fixings market – using an arbitrary level of 325.604pt for the index in 
October 2025 – currently has a YoY mark of 3.54% for October 2026. With this 
same reference, we are now 4bp above the market. 
We expect core at 0.26% MoM SA in October, vs 0.29% in August (a number 
boosted by noise in telephone services) and 0.33% in September. Our take is 
4bp above the 0.24% MoM SA Bloomberg average consensus, as of 
9 November.  
The 3mma annualised core would come out at 3.53% in October, the highest 
level since January 2025. Simply put, we expect a relatively benign report with 
core back around 0.25% MoM SA, so a bit softer than August and September. 
For more details, see our US CPI preview.

Back to the midterms 
Our US Chief Economist published a quick reaction piece – Democrats ride a 
blue wave – following the results of the midterm election, followed by his usual 
US Macro Weekly last Friday. 
Regarding the inflation outlook, three topics matter in particular. We answer with 
our US Chief Economist’s views: 
1. Conflict with Iran: resolution or not? 
“Democrats will certainly attempt to put an end to the conflict, but it is not 
clear they will be able to do so on their own given the President’s greater 
ability to conduct foreign policy without Congress. Trump’s initial rhetoric has 
sounded more conciliatory, but there is always a risk he could respond to the 
defeat by increasing his assertiveness with re-escalation still a possibility.”

2. AI: some blockage? 
“On the AI front, we still do not think that many new regulations will emerge 
from Washington given the spectre of Trump’s veto, though we would note 
that the blue wave carried on down ballot with key governorships changing 
hands in states like Texas and Ohio. With the new Democratic governors 
more hostile to data centre construction, local politics may offer some 
additional roadblocks that could delay, though we do not expect it to entirely 
derail, the buildout” 
3. Fiscal impulse in 2027-28: less than expected? 
“Despite the sweeping victory, however, we are not convinced it really 
changes much about the base case outlook for policy gridlock […]. Thus, 
while there may be some room for compromise on certain smaller issues […] 
any major fiscal package, for example, may be a step too far”. 
Bottom line, this is not a U-turn for US inflation dynamics. The prospect of a 
ceasefire with Iran looks a bit more probable than before, which is already 
reflected in crude oil prices – down from around USD110/bl to USD90/bl in two 
weeks – and included in our energy CPI path. 
US CPI: updated path 
As usual, we have updated our CPI path, namely taking stock of the recent 
decline in crude oil and gasoline prices. Not so long ago – back in September 
and October – our path regularly stood around 20-30bp below market 
pricing for H227 and 2028. 
With the midterms and the decline in crude oil prices, the market has converged 
to our CPI path, which we cut at the same time. We are now well in line with 
fixings for the rest of 2026 and 2027, only standing slightly below market pricing 
at the Q328 horizon. 
As a reminder, core CPI will look a bit stronger than ‘normal’ in YoY terms from 
November 2026 to February 2026, due to base effects in OER and rent. Once 
this has passed, we have core CPI around 2.5-2.7% YoY for the rest of 2027.
