Summary
Robust growth and underlying corporate fundamentals support a continuation of our “risk-on” 
approach, despite recent market volatility driven by concerns about rich valuations for global 
technology stocks.
Besides solid fundamentals, our analysis of artificial intelligence (AI) demand shows consistent 
adoption, despite concerns around financing and commoditization. This research supports 
an AI tilt within our equity portfolios, with a preference for the United States, emerging markets 
(EMs) and Japan. 
Inflation remains a primary driver of bond markets and has influenced our fixed income positioning 
moving into November. A reflationary environment in the United States means we have curtailed 
our optimism toward US Treasuries, as we see greater relative value in international bonds issued 
by countries with weaker macro backdrops, such as the United Kingdom and Canada.

Strong Growth

corporate earnings power.

•	 Leading economic indicators and business activity appear 
healthy, but we are monitoring the impact of higher rates

Inflation Uncertainty

•	 We expect limited second-order effects from the energy

Tighter Monetary Policy

a tightening bias in all major regions, including the 
United States.
•	 The market now expects a more hawkish Federal Open 
Market Committee (FOMC) as the US Federal Reserve 
(Fed) focuses on reinforcing its credibility.
•	 US midterm elections should have little impact on fiscal 
policy or bond yields, although debates on budgets and 
the debt ceiling may cause some volatility in bond markets.

Growth Is Fine. Inflation Is the Question.
Global growth looks healthy, with most major economies 
showing positive annual growth rates1 in line with or better 
than six-month averages. Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI) 
data also shows that activity in the services and 
manufacturing sectors is expanding almost everywhere, 
barring a few exceptions.2
Growth is particularly robust in the United States, where the 
Atlanta Fed estimates a current annualized quarterly growth 
rate of 3.7%,3 driven by strong consumer spending and capital 
expenditure. 
Inflation has been more problematic, remaining persistently 
above target in most economies. In the United States, the 
Fed’s preferred measure of personal consumption 
expenditure (PCE) inflation rose to 3.6% in September, with 
core PCE (less energy and food) reaching 3.1%, driven by 
persistent services inflation.4 Euro area annual inflation rose 
to 3.8% in September, up from 3.2% in August, on higher 
energy and services prices.5 Perhaps more importantly, 
inflation expectations have also risen recently in most major 
economies, feeding concerns that inflationary pressures may 
be becoming entrenched.
Even so, our research suggests that energy price shocks are 
not translating into persistent inflation. Prices for broad goods 
and services appear contained, as do wages. US wage growth 
has ticked up in recent months but isn’t yet inflationary and is 
likely influenced by labor constraints linked to immigration 
policy. 
This conclusion drives our belief that longer-term market 
expectations for interest-rate hikes are broadly overdone, 
which we will discuss later in the piece.

Earnings Strength Supports Equities
Concerns about rich valuations for technology stocks caused 
market volatility in October. This can be explained by 
uncertainties around market concentration and the viability 
of the AI trade, as rapid adoption changes competitive 
dynamics within a nascent industry.
Set against this uncertainty are very strong corporate 
fundamentals that provide a solid argument to stay positive 
on equities. Forward estimates of global corporate earnings 
have eased slightly but earnings are still forecast to grow by 
almost 20% over the next 12 months. This figure is influenced 
by the technology sector, particularly semiconductor 
companies, whose heavy weighting in EM ex-China indexes 
helps explain EM earnings expectations of close to 30%.6
As we have noted in previous editions of Allocation Views, 
forward price/earnings ratios have fallen below their 10-year 
median as strong earnings forecasts have outpaced price 
gains for the technology sector so far in 2026. We would 
argue that the recent drawdown has moderated sentiment 
and positioning, keeping equities attractive from a valuation 
perspective.

AI Is Not One Trade: Who Wins as Adoption 
Accelerates?
Fundamentals aside, a proper assessment of the AI trade 
requires us to “look under the hood” and analyze the forces 
driving adoption and commoditization.
Demand for AI is strong, as evidenced by ambitious capital 
expenditure (capex) plans from “hyperscalers” and AI model 
providers. Broad adoption across industries and sectors is 
driving demand for hardware and infrastructure, meaning 
firms involved in memory chip manufacturing, data-center 
construction and specialized energy networks have strong 
order pipelines.
Recent investor uncertainty has centered on monetization 
assumptions for proprietary AI models from companies such 
as Google, OpenAI and Anthropic.
An increasing number of AI adopters prefer publicly available 
open-weight models that can be customized for specific uses 
and often offer cheaper tokens. Faster adoption is therefore 
impacting free cash flow (FCF) by fueling higher demand for 
computing power without necessarily increasing revenues 
for model providers.
Accelerated adoption also raises costs for model providers 
as demand for access to graphics processing units (GPUs) 
grows, meaning data-center owners such as Google (Cloud), 
Microsoft (Azure) and Amazon (AWS) can increase rental 
rates. It should be noted here that rising infrastructure 
revenues add to the resilience of “hyperscalers,” offsetting 
FCF concerns.
We conclude that investors should not treat AI as one trade 
but rather seek to understand how this technological 
paradigm shift might be impacting participant companies 
differently. The upcoming initial public offering (IPO) of 
Anthropic should provide a good test case for this theory.

Growth and Inflation Shape Duration
The assumption that strong AI demand will continue to 
support capex is a key factor in our fixed income positioning 
heading into November.
Global AI-related capital expenditure is expected to 
approach US$1 trillion in 2026, rising to US$1.7 trillion in 2027. 
The five largest US “hyperscalers” are set to spend US$800 
billion in 2026 alone.7
Such spending has contributed to above-trend growth and 
inflation in the United States, creating conditions for tighter 
Federal Reserve monetary policy. Against this background, 
we believe near-term market expectations for increases to the 
federal funds rate are broadly correct, reducing the relative 
value of US Treasuries in comparison to international bonds.
As a result, we hold a preference for duration in countries 
with weaker macroeconomic conditions, notably Canada 
and the United Kingdom.
Markets are pricing in at least three more interest-rate hikes 
from both the Bank of Canada and the Bank of England 
during the next 12 months, which we think is excessive given 
anemic growth, fragile labor markets and weak domestic 
demand. 
The one caveat to our cautious US duration view is the 
potential for US Treasuries to become a haven for international 
investors in the event of rising AI credit risk or heightened 
geopolitical tensions. Momentum has also influenced our 
improved view of Japanese government bonds, although we 
remain underweight here amid elevated inflation expectations.
Elsewhere, we have downgraded our view on Australian 
bonds to neutral. Resilient domestic demand, a tight labor 
market and higher energy prices are contributing to 
persistent inflation, which should mean further policy 
tightening is required.

When AI Borrows, Who Gets Crowded Out?
As capital expenditure continues apace, AI companies now 
make up 12% of the US investment-grade bond market, with 
AI-related borrowing potentially rising close to US$1 trillion 
in 2027.8
This sharp increase in the supply of high-quality investment-
grade credit has not yet affected government bond sales. 
However, it could begin to test demand when the US 
Treasury issues more longer-dated bonds next year. 
Instead, the AI debt boom may be crowding out high-yield 
issuers, with companies struggling to refinance lower-quality 
debt. This may be contributing to greater differentiation 
within the high-yield sector, including spread widening 
among lower-quality issuers.
Moving underweight credit may be one way to hedge overall 
AI exposure, should new issuance meaningfully outstrip 
demand. However, we stay neutral into November, retaining 
our preference for equity risk amid strong earnings and 
relatively tight spreads.

WHAT ARE THE RISKS?
All investments involve risks, including possible loss of principal.
Equity securities are subject to price fluctuation and possible loss of principal. Large-capitalization companies may fall out 
of favor with investors based on market and economic conditions. Small- and mid-cap stocks involve greater risks and 
volatility than large-cap stocks.
Fixed income securities involve interest rate, credit, inflation and reinvestment risks, and possible loss of principal. As interest 
rates rise, the value of fixed income securities falls. Low-rated, high-yield bonds are subject to greater price volatility, illiquidity 
and possibility of default.
The allocation of assets among different strategies, asset classes and investments may not prove beneficial or produce the 
desired results. To the extent a strategy invests in companies in a specific country or region, it may experience greater 
volatility than a strategy that is more broadly diversified geographically.
Commodity-related investments are subject to additional risks such as commodity index volatility, investor speculation, 
interest rates, weather, tax and regulatory developments.
International investments are subject to special risks, including currency fluctuations and social, economic and political 
uncertainties, which could increase volatility. These risks are magnified in emerging markets. The government’s participation 
in the economy is still high and, therefore, investments in China will be subject to larger regulatory risk levels compared to 
many other countries.
Investing in privately held companies presents certain challenges and involves incremental risks as opposed to investments 
in public companies, such as dealing with the lack of available information about these companies as well as their general lack 
of liquidity.
Active management does not ensure gains or protect against market declines. Diversification does not guarantee a profit 
or protect against a loss.
