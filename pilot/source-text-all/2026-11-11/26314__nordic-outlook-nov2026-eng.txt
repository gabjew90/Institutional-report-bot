International overview 
Growth challenged by rising 
rates and energy prices 
Despite global uncertainty, high energy prices and rising 
interest rates, global GDP is growing by just over 3 per 
cent a year. We marginally raise our growth forecast. 
Investment in AI, defence and energy, an improving 
manufacturing cycle and stable labour markets provide 
support. Yet downbeat households face rising 
borrowing costs, higher inflation and stock market 
volatility, casting doubt on the expansion’s strength. 
High energy prices drive inflation, with stronger indirect 
effects the longer prices stay elevated. Inflation 
concerns will prompt central banks, including the ECB, 
the Fed and the Riksbank, to further hike once or twice. 
Fed and ECB then cut again later in the forecast period. 
 
Growth continues to show resilience despite a long list 
of challenges. Wars, tariffs and energy prices increase 
uncertainty and raise costs for businesses and 
households, while higher interest rates challenge both 
fiscal stability and the lofty valuations of AI companies. 
Yet businesses and households continue to demonstrate 
considerable adaptability, while a structural investment 
cycle in defence, energy and AI is supporting near-term 
growth and could boost productivity and growth further 
ahead. Overall, we see a favourable outlook for the 
global economy, but risks are tilted to the downside for 
growth and to the upside for inflation.  
 
Industry and investment in an expansion phase. 
Investments in areas such as AI, defence, infrastructure 
and energy are key drivers of growth, and 
manufacturing sentiment has improved. How effectively 
private and government investment translates into 
higher productivity and profits will be crucial to the 
outlook. Question marks about AI companies’ business 
models and future profitability have unsettled stock 
markets. Competition from China’s low-cost AI, political 
demands for temporary pauses in the AI expansion, 
particularly from US Democrats, and rising interest 
rates are among the concerns, but quarterly earnings 
reports have generally been strong. It is nevertheless 
reassuring that interest rates are partly rising for the 
“right reasons”: resilient economies with strong demand 
for capital linked, among other things, to the investment 
cycle. There are, however, good reasons to monitor the 
impact of interest rate movements on investment, 
consumption and asset prices, including both equities 
and housing.  
 
Energy prices will be the key factor for growth and 
inflation amid persistent geopolitical tensions and 
conflicts, and thus for financial markets. Europe’s 
security situation is strained by factors including 
escalating Russian hybrid attacks and the ongoing war 
in Ukraine. This also affects relations between the US, 
NATO and China. The US–China trade and technology 
wars have global repercussions across many areas. 
Meanwhile, the EU has an imbalance in goods trade with 
China that is destabilising over the longer term. It is now 
seeking to address this through restrictions on imports 
of Chinese products in selected sectors. Global trade 
has continued to expand, but its structure is changing. 
Although supply chains are under strain, the situation is 
not alarming. The pattern is repeating itself: some 
countries are exploiting dependencies for both political 
and economic purposes, creating a substantial risk of 
further disruptions.  
The risk of renewed US attacks keeps energy prices 
elevated. More crude oil is flowing out of the countries 
around the Persian Gulf, and an unusually large volume 
of oil is currently on tankers en route to consumers. 
Even so, prices for oil, natural gas and various distillates 
remain high. This appears to reflect considerable

concern about renewed US attacks after the midterm 
elections, with a risk of lasting effects if production 
facilities are damaged or transport routes blocked. 
Transportation costs are also rising due to factors 
including conflicts and longer shipping routes. A 
shortage of refining capacity continues to generate wide 
price differentials across regions and distillates.  
 
Rising inflation risks extend beyond energy. Inflation 
has picked up, so far mainly due to higher energy prices. 
We see increasing, but still limited, indirect effects. 
There are as yet no signs of second-round effects 
through higher wage growth or changes in corporate 
pricing behaviour. Long-term inflation expectations also 
remain stable. However, repeated cost shocks from 
tariffs and energy, strong demand for IT components, 
extreme weather and seasonal events create 
uncertainty. Countries whose currencies have 
depreciated against the dollar face an additional 
inflation risk. We have revised our inflation forecasts 
higher compared with August but still expect core 
inflation to move back towards the target during 2028. 
Slower wage growth in both the US and the euro area 
supports our view that the rise in inflation is temporary, 
while companies’ pricing plans have so far been 
relatively stable. Headline inflation is expected to fall 
below target in many countries in 2028, provided 
energy prices decline in line with our expectations. 
Policy rates set to rise a little further Higher actual 
and expected near-term inflation, along with relatively 
solid growth in economies such as the US and the euro 
area, has contributed to hawkish market pricing of 
central bank policy, although this has eased somewhat 
recently. Pricing has fluctuated markedly in line with 
energy prices. Meanwhile, rising long-term yields have 
tightened financial conditions. We forecast one more 
Fed rate hike, in December this year. The ECB has two 
more hikes ahead: in December and in March 2027. The 
ECB is concerned about indirect inflation effects from 
energy, but the final hike may not materialise if energy 
prices start to decline. Once energy prices, and thus 
inflation, fall, lower policy rates are expected. But this 
will not happen until the latter part of 2027 (Fed) or 
early 2028 (ECB).  
Growing public protests. In several countries, including 
France, Italy, Serbia, India and Indonesia, we see protest 
movements against incumbent politicians as well as the 
emergence of new parties seeking a break with the 
past. The reasons differ across countries, but in several 
cases they reflect a response to high living costs, labour 
markets being reshaped by increased AI use, corruption 
and government sector cutbacks. This is happening as 
more funds are being directed towards areas that 
benefit households less directly, such as defence and 
support for other countries, not least Ukraine. The 
political landscape is changing and becoming less 
predictable, potentially leading to greater 
fragmentation, particularly in Europe. Foreign influence 
operations are also becoming more prevalent. It is 
important to take popular expressions seriously, while 
also understanding their causes and underlying drivers.  
 
Political crises amplify fiscal risks. Higher long-term 
yields challenge more than just the investment cycle. At 
present, yield movements do not appear to be driven by 
a marked increase in inflation or term premiums, but the 
drivers vary. Elections in countries including the US and 
France add to uncertainty about governments’ ability to 
act. Countries with large fiscal deficits and concerning 
debt dynamics, currently France in particular but also 
the US and the UK, have experienced greater market 
unease and face higher borrowing costs.  
France faces a particularly challenging combination 
of a budget deficit of almost 5.5 per cent of GDP, public 
debt of almost 120 per cent of GDP, a presidential 
election in April 2027, weak productivity and wide-
spread public protests. Widening spreads between 
French and German government bonds are concerning, 
but also a natural development as markets demand 
compensation for differing fiscal risks. Competition for

Slightly higher growth, but risks remain. We are again 
revising our global growth forecast higher, albeit 
marginally. Risks remain tilted to the downside, 
however, and increase the longer various disruptions 
persist. There is considerable uncertainty about the 
developments facing the world economy and the effects 
of structural and political changes. The outcome will 
determine whether the coming years bring a success 
story of investment- and productivity-led growth, lower 
inflation and falling interest rates, or whether new 
conflicts, political problems and other disruptions result 
in cost shocks, falling stock markets and stagflation.  
 
Tailwinds and headwinds of varying strength. The AI 
boom and high energy prices create winners and losers. 
AI is driving investment and stock markets mainly in the 
US, but also in parts of Asia, including China, that are 
major suppliers of inputs. Defence spending is 
increasing globally, with a particular emphasis on 
Europe. In the short-term, high energy prices benefit oil 
producers such as the US and countries in the Middle 
East, while hurting importers such as Europe. A greater 
focus on energy security will put pressure on fossil fuel 
producers over the longer term. Increased political 
uncertainty surrounding global cooperation is helping to 
reshape global trade and international relations.  
Major setbacks for Republicans in the US midterm 
elections. Democrats have taken control of both 
chambers of Congress. This forces the Trump 
administration to negotiate with Democrats on fiscal 
policy. In other areas, such as immigration, tariffs and 
foreign and security policy, the president nevertheless 
has considerable room for manoeuvre. During his first 
two years Donald Trump has largely exercised his 
powers through means other than congressional 
legislation. A key question is how the major election 
setbacks affect Trump’s grip on the Republican Party, 
and to what extent Republican lawmakers may join 
forces with Democrats to halt the war with Iran, for 
example, or increase support for Ukraine defence 
against Russia. Overriding a presidential veto requires a 
two-thirds majority in both chambers. Despite 
unexpectedly large gains, Democrats still need support 
from a significant share of Republicans, particularly in 
the Senate.  
Slightly stronger growth after all 
We are marginally raising our global growth forecast 
compared with the August issue of Nordic Outlook, by 
0.1 percentage points for both 2026 and 2027. The 
manufacturing cycle is improving, and global PMI is 
comfortably above the 50 mark despite global tensions, 
tariff disputes and high energy prices. Investment is 
benefiting from both cyclical and structural tailwinds. 
One question for the economic outlook is whether 
households can withstand the headwinds from higher 
inflation, high energy prices and rising interest rates. 
The same applies to residential construction, which was 
weak in many countries even before interest rates rose. 
Consumer confidence is low and has barely recovered 
from the inflation shock of 2021–2022.  
 
US growth is broadening. AI-related investment is 
supporting growth, but its high import content reduces 
its contribution. Businesses, particularly in 
manufacturing, are becoming more optimistic, and 
growth is broadening. The labour market is stable, and 
positive wealth effects are supporting consumption, 
which has been strong recently. However, weak

consumer confidence, slower wage growth, waning 
fiscal support and a low savings ratio limit the scope for 
upside surprises in consumption.  
 
The euro area is growing at a reasonable pace, amid 
both political tailwinds and headwinds. Growth has 
surprised on the upside so far this year. Consumption 
and investment are driving the expansion, but exports 
face challenges from Chinese competition and higher 
tariffs. Higher energy prices and France’s weak growth 
and political uncertainty are weighing on the outlook. 
German fiscal initiatives targeting defence and 
investment are providing support but are progressing 
somewhat more slowly than would be desirable. The 
region faces major structural challenges, but the hope is 
that investment, defence spending, a better-functioning 
common capital market and reduced reliance on fossil 
fuels will support growth while reducing dependence on 
the US and China.  
China’s economy continues to be driven by exports, 
with AI-related products playing an important role. 
Domestic demand remains subdued, with a persistently 
weak housing market and high household savings. Large 
trade surpluses are increasing tensions with the US and 
Europe. This growth model will be difficult to sustain 
over time, and rebalancing the economy is key to 
continued growth. Without successful steps in that 
direction, which are not part of our main scenario, 
growth will decline towards the lower end of the 4.5–
5.0 per cent target range.  
India’s growth has surprised on the upside. Industry 
has performed well despite high energy prices, and 
fiscal policy has cushioned the effects of the war in Iran.  
Energy: Awaiting US and Iran’s next moves 
Oil prices remain high despite increased flows out of the 
Persian Gulf. Prices reflect a combination of concern 
about renewed escalation and damage to production 
facilities that could push up energy prices even over the 
longer term and hopes of a resolution. It is difficult to 
see how a new wave of attacks would bring Iran closer 
to a negotiated settlement, but uncertainty about such a 
development is enough to keep energy prices elevated.  
Wide price differentials persist across distillates and 
regions. We continue to see considerable variation in 
prices for oil, gas and different distillates. Although 
supply has changed, the shortage of refining capacity 
persists, keeping prices high for certain distillates, such 
as diesel and jet fuel. As a result, prices differ across 
regions and products. For example, Brent crude 
(Europe) currently costs just under USD 100 per barrel, 
while US WTI crude costs around USD 90. European gas 
prices are very high due to both tight supply and low 
inventories ahead of winter. Compared with levels 
before the US attacked Iran, Brent crude has risen by 
around 40 per cent, while European natural gas prices 
(TTF) are almost three times as high. 
 
A wide range of uncertainty. Once the risk premium 
disappears, oil prices could fall rapidly as the supply 
surplus grows. The timing depends on decisions in the 
hands of just a few politicians. Our forecast is broadly in 
line with futures prices and implies an annual average 
Brent crude price of USD 80 per barrel in 2027 and USD 
70 per barrel in 2028.

Fiscal policy under pressure on many fronts 
Public finances are weak or very weak in many 
countries, including the US, the UK, Japan and France. 
Countries’ ability to manage their debt differs, 
depending on factors such as whether deficits can be 
financed domestically through private savings, the 
scope for raising taxes, how much government debt 
central banks are willing to hold, interest rate levels and 
growth prospects.  
Heavy spending demands put pressure on budget 
balances. At the same time, policymakers must manage 
expectations from both voters and markets, alongside 
numerous demands on spending and revenue. The need 
to invest in defence, security policy, energy security, 
new technology and the green transition, together with 
cost pressures from ageing populations, is placing 
significant strain on public finances. Scope for fiscal 
initiatives is limited to a few countries with greater room 
for manoeuvre, including Germany and the Nordic 
countries.  
Markets increasingly impose discipline on policy-
makers. The recent rise in interest rates further reduces 
the scope for managing high debt levels. It also creates 
growing monetary policy challenges for central banks 
and, in some cases, may challenge monetary policy 
independence. In recent years, weak government 
finances combined with political uncertainty have 
caused bond yields to surge. The French yield spread 
over Germany remains narrower than at the time of 
previous ECB interventions. We believe that an even 
larger rise in French yields, potentially accompanied by 
spillovers to other countries and banking systems, 
would be needed for the ECB to act. 
 
Downside risks continue to dominate 
The resilience of growth is reassuring, but major 
questions remain about how much the economy and 
financial markets can withstand before something gives 
way. The risk picture resembles that presented in the 
August issue of Nordic Outlook. A weaker scenario is 
more likely than a stronger one, and the potential 
deviation from our main scenario is greater on the 
downside than on the upside. Downside risks are 
numerous. If we underestimate inflation spillovers while 
markets question AI companies’ ability to generate 
profits, we could face a stagflation-like scenario in 
which already heavily constrained fiscal policy struggles 
to provide support.  
AI, productivity and investment. Although a stronger 
scenario is less likely, we may instead be under-
estimating the strength of growth. Most of the AI 
revolution’s effects still lie ahead, and once 
technological shifts take hold, developments can 
surprise. Higher productivity supports growth, positive 
wealth effects and lower inflation and interest rates. 
Investment in other areas, such as defence and 
infrastructure in Europe, could have similar effects and 
reduce the headwinds from ageing populations.  
 
Inflation: Increasing upside risks 
The drivers of inflation have not changed significantly in 
recent months, but the rise in energy prices, and their 
volatility, has intensified. High energy prices are pushing 
up headline inflation. Indirect effects dominate the risk 
picture, with risks tilted to the upside. However, we 
expect the pass-through to core inflation to be limited, 
with the main impact on headline inflation. Core inflation 
will decline towards 2 per cent, although not until 2028.

second-round effects are major uncertainties in the

inflation forecast. Experience suggests that such effects 
take time and depend more on the duration of the price 
shock than on its intensity. Historically, the correlation 
between energy price increases and their effects on 
core inflation has been weak. The years 2021–2022 
were an exception in this respect (see Theme: How big 
will the effects of rising energy prices be? Nordic Outlook

We currently see only moderate effects of this kind in 
US and European inflation data. Companies’ pricing 
plans have risen, but not to a worrying extent. Wage 
growth is slowing in the US and Europe. Strong AI-
related demand is providing some support to goods 
prices but has only a limited impact on the CPI. Food 
prices are affected by weather phenomena such as 
drought and El Niño, but historical experience suggests 
that the effects are often more regional than global.  
 
Central banks concerned about inflation  
Uncertainty about energy prices and inflation is 
troubling central banks, and market expectations have 
fluctuated sharply over the summer and autumn. 
Central banks have also shifted their interest rate 
rhetoric in a more hawkish direction, although the tone 
has softened somewhat in recent weeks. Overall, 
however, we regard market expectations as somewhat 
too aggressive, including for the Fed, the ECB, the Bank 
of England and the Riksbank. Market pricing has 
nevertheless eased recently as higher long-term yields 
have tightened financial conditions and energy prices 
have fallen. Inflation and growth are cited as reasons for 
raising policy rates, but in some cases, we see signs of 
central banks tightening pre-emptively to contain 
inflation risks. For the Riksbank, the difficulty of 
diverging too far from the ECB may also be a factor.  
The Fed will raise its policy rate once more. As 
expected, the Fed left its policy rate unchanged at its 
October meeting, but two members favoured a hike, 
citing inflation. The rise in long-term yields has tightened 
financial conditions, but the Fed’s models and lending 
surveys indicate that conditions remain accommodative, 
supporting the case for a little more tightening. Fed 
Chair Warsh has emphasised that the aim is not to 
impede growth, but to make monetary policy more 
neutral.  
 
The ECB will hike in December and March. The ECB is 
concerned about direct, but above all indirect inflation 
effects and second-round effects. The risk that rate 
hikes undermine the recovery should not be under-
estimated. We regard the December hike as fairly 
certain, but if energy prices fall faster than expected, 
the March hike may not materialise.  
 
The Riksbank has now raised its policy rate once and 
is signalling further tightening. We expect one more hike 
over the next six months, taking the policy rate to 2.25 
per cent. It is then expected to remain at that level for 
the rest of the forecast horizon.  Norway, by contrast, is 
judged to have already reached the peak of its rate 
cycle. Norges Bank is expected to keep its policy rate at 
4.50 per cent for an extended period before beginning 
cautious cuts towards the end of 2027 and during 
2028.

interest rate levels of recent years, we expect rates to 
remain higher for longer. Over the coming years, 
however, yields in countries such as the US, Germany 
and Sweden will settle somewhat below current levels.  
Policy rate movements drive long-term yields. The 
near-term path is shaped by high energy prices, which 
contribute to higher short-term rates and, in turn, higher 
yields at the long end of the curve. Meanwhile, our main 
scenario remains that inflation will decline over the 
coming years, allowing policy rate cuts in 2027–2028. 
If inflation expectations were to become de-anchored 
from central bank targets, however, the bond market 
outlook would change. US and European long-term 
yields are expected to move sideways or slightly higher 
in the near term, before gradually declining as markets 
price in policy rate cuts and these subsequently begin. 
Structural factors contribute to higher long-term 
yields. The decline is expected to be limited compared 
with previous cycles. Structural factors such as large 
government borrowing needs and investment in AI, 
defence and energy point to a persistently higher 
interest rate environment than in the years following the 
financial crisis. Alongside increased bond supply from 
both the public and private sectors, shrinking central 
bank balance sheets also play a role. As large, less 
price-sensitive participants withdraw from the bond 
market, more price-sensitive investors need to absorb 
growing financing needs. This keeps the price of capital, 
the interest rate, elevated. Market distrust of countries 
with weak public finances may push some yields higher 
than others, as is currently the case in France.  
 
Energy, politics & rates weigh on EUR & SEK 
Currency markets are dominated by the interaction 
between energy prices, central banks and growth 
prospects. In the short term, currencies with high 
interest rates and safe-haven status continue to benefit, 
particularly the dollar. However, as markets eventually 
begin to price in US rate cuts in the latter part of 2027 
and in 2028, the dollar is expected to weaken gradually. 
As uncertainty surrounding the Persian Gulf and energy 
prices recedes, the forces currently supporting dollar 
strength will work in reverse. 
Energy prices and political uncertainty weigh on the 
euro. The euro has moved in the opposite direction to 
the dollar during the autumn. High energy prices and 
concerns about France’s public finances are weighing on 
the currency, and the narrow range in which EUR/USD 
had traded for the past 18 months was broken in 
October. Meanwhile, we expect the ECB to raise its 
policy rate to 3.00 per cent within the next six months, 
which could provide some support for the euro. As 
energy prices subsequently normalise and attention 
shifts towards forthcoming rate cuts around the world, 
the euro will gradually strengthen against the dollar. 
 
The Swedish krona regains lost ground in 2027 and 
strengthens significantly. The krona is currently 
disadvantaged by higher interest rates abroad and 
investors’ tendency to avoid smaller currencies during 
periods of heightened uncertainty. As markets turn their 
attention to forthcoming Fed and ECB rate cuts, the 
krona is expected to strengthen gradually against both 
the euro and the dollar. It will also receive some support 
from continued relatively strong growth, sound public 
finances and further Riksbank tightening during the 
winter, although global factors play the largest role. We 
expect the krona to be stronger than today at the end of 
2028, but a sustained appreciation depends on 
declining global inflation and lower interest rates 
abroad. 
The Norwegian krone continues to benefit from high 
energy prices and an already high policy rate. 
However, with Norges Bank expected to have reached 
its peak policy rate, the scope for further appreciation is 
limited. Instead, we see a significant weakening of the 
Norwegian krone against both the Swedish krona and 
the euro as likely during 2027. Sterling benefits from 
high UK interest rates in the short term but faces 
weaker growth prospects further ahead. The Japanese

Equities: Strong fundamentals, AI angst 
Up until last week, global stock markets had been 
trapped in a narrow range since the last issue of Nordic 
Outlook despite a favourable macro and earnings 
backdrop as tech stocks were hit by doubts about future 
AI profitability. The Q3 reporting season was strong and 
earnings estimates have continued rising. However, the 
sharp technology sell-off last week pushed the whole 
market down. Our base case is that AI investment 
growth slows in 2027 without turning negative, 
allowing tech shares to stabilise while lower oil prices, 
easing interest rate pressures and improving earnings 
estimates support the broader market. However, 
Anthropic’s planned IPO is a key near-term test for the 
AI boom.
