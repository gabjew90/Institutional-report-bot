The Blueprint
Our view: BP will host a CMD under new CEO Meg O’Neill on 15th
December. Meg inherits a company trading at a steep discount to peers,
with investor trust having deteriorated. But we believe the setup has never
been better. The macro is highly supportive and a balance sheet repair story
is nearly complete. BP’s current valuation prices continued disappointment
and leaves the potential for a re-rating. In this note, we outline what we
think would be a sensible blueprint into 2027.
Key points:
De-leveraging, de-leveraging and more de-leveraging...but there is more
to life than de-leveraging. BP’s balance sheet should be ‘fixed’ by
mid-2027. We see total financial obligations hitting ~$25bn or 1x net debt/
normalised CFFO, a level sufficient to restart buybacks. Over 2027-30E,
we see BP returning more of its market cap to shareholders than peers.
A clean balance sheet alone doesn’t make BP investible, it’s table stakes.
We believe BP needs to convey a balanced investment case, incorporating
upstream growth, cost reductions, downstream improvements and
competitive distributions. Given its relative valuation, the aim is to force
investors to view it as a comparable investment case vs peers, rather than
helping investors find reasons why it should trade at a discount.
Diving into the upstream portfolio. Alongside balance sheet repair and
competitive distributions, BP will likely need to show it can deliver modest
growth. Under new leadership, the focus is on the core businesses, while
unwinding much of the Looney era. BP has had decent exploration success
and we anticipate a focus on the hopper required to supplement the 2030s.
Our refreshed reserve life modelling to 2030 points to a modest decline,
although we note that Bumerangue, Azule and BPX remain key levers.
Ultimately, BP’s upstream portfolio could be supplemented with bolt-on
M&A, however this spend needs to be incorporated into capex guidance.
This year’s performance masked by Board drama and lack of clarity. BP’s
share price has underperformed across all relevant timeframes, and has
persisted even in 2026 through exceptional macro tailwinds. We examine
BP’s enterprise value progression through 2026 and observe that the EV
has grown by <$10bn, with the increase in market cap effectively offset
by debt reduction. This is a stark contrast to peers. The market is not yet
capitalising any operational improvements or earnings upgrades. This de-
rating presents a compelling opportunity for investors, in our view, in the
wake of greater clarity on strategy and continued macro support.
Our view. The combination of improving operational performance, a
supportive macro environment and the potential for greater strategic and
financial clarity leave us with a positive view on BP. This is a rebuilding trust
story and the upcoming CMD will be an opportunity to set out strategic
priorities beyond de-leveraging. We maintain our Outperform rating and
720p Price Target. BP remains on RBC’s Global Energy Best Ideas list.

BP – The Blueprint 
BP will host a CMD under new CEO Meg O’Neill on 15th December. The company has seen a 
tremendous amount of change over recent years, with the strategy whipsawing alongside 
broader shifts in the market. This pro-cyclical approach has led to weak share price 
performance, while also raising Board level governance concerns. Is this time different? We 
don’t know. Investor trust levels have deteriorated. But the macro environment and relative 
valuation provide the best set up possible for a chance at redemption. In this note, we 
outline what we think would be a sensible blueprint for BP into 2027.  
Context on performance 
They say a picture can paint a thousand words. In this instance, a chart can do the same. The 
context for this CMD is that on any timeline we look at BP versus its closest peers, it has 
underperformed. We won’t review all the reasons why that is the case, but it is notable that 
in both high and low price environments over recent years, the company has not been able to 
string together a consistent period of outperformance. Even in 2026, with the macro tailwinds 
driving material earnings uplift for BP (absolute and relative to peers), drama in the Boardroom 
as well as concerns on strategy have left BP lagging behind peers.

This is the context going into BP’s CMD on 15th December. With the shares trading on a 
discounted valuation, BP needs to convince the market that it should be considered 
alongside the peer group, rather than find reason after reason that it should trade at a 
discount. We discuss the investment case and key parameters heading into the CMD, 
starting with the most hotly debated topic (which should fix itself), the balance sheet. 
De-leveraging, de-leveraging, de-leveraging…there is more to life than de-leveraging 
BP has historically run with higher leverage than peers, and the focus most recently has been 
utilising high commodity prices to pay down debt. This trend has been well documented in our 
recent research. Ultimately, the energy crisis has afforded BP a significant opportunity to “fix” 
the balance sheet. As yet, BP has not communicated what it sees as an appropriate financial 
framework, and what level of debt would trigger a restart of buybacks. In the last cycle, BP 
restarted buybacks earlier than peers despite having a weaker balance sheet, and ultimately 
this was not rewarded by investors over time because of the lack of sustainability. It is natural

unhelpful aspects of recent commentary has been the focus on its relative balance sheet vs 
peers. In a rising commodity price environment this has effectively left an open ended de-
leveraging narrative. BP will need to communicate what is the appropriate balance sheet for 
the company, rather than simply communicating relative to peers. 
In our minds, the de-leveraging of BP’s balance sheet is a part of the investment case but not 
the only thing. A clean balance sheet is not likely to garner interest with investors. BP needs a 
well-rounded investment case, with the balance sheet there to facilitate the strategy through 
the cycle. Because BP is likely to offer only modest growth at a headline level over the coming 
years, we think there needs to be a clear line of sight on when the company is set to restart 
buybacks, particularly given where BP’s valuation is relative to peers, with it trading at a steep 
discount. We discuss the financial framework later in the note. 
On the balance sheet front, we see BP’s overall financial obligations (debt + leases + hybrids + 
Macondo) at around $30bn by year end, at the lower end of management’s guidance, and at 
$21bn by mid-2027. We do not think BP needs to provide guidance on a one quarter ahead 
basis for net debt trends. This figure also equates to roughly 1x ND/CFFO (pre-leases & hybrids 
which are incorporated in the net debt figure).

Aspects of financial framework specific to BP – Macondo & Hybrids 
There are two incremental aspects of BP’s investment case that have muddied up the financial 
framework relative to peers. The first is Macondo and the second are its outstanding hybrids. 
On the Macondo front, BP has a fixed liability of around $5bn remaining, however most 
recently agreed to pay an early settlement to Louisiana for part of this. It received a small 
discount to pay in one bullet rather than over seven years. We wonder whether this is a 
precursor to more of the same, and we think the investment case would benefit from this. At 
the moment, BP’s EV is overlaid with this liability, while investors sometimes look at FCF each 
year post the Macondo payment. This ‘double whammy’ is not helpful. We wonder whether 
BP could take a leaf out of Eni’s book and copy the ‘satellite approach’, creating a SPV for the 
liabilities, while funding it with its excess cash on the balance sheet. Once set up, the liability 
could be de-consolidated and it would clean up BP’s financial statements.

On the hybrid front, we expect BP to move from ~$11bn in hybrids outstanding to closer to 
$9bn over time, however at this point, going below this would risk the rating agencies 
removing the equity credit for the instruments. With a sufficiently strong balance sheet, we 
do not think the hybrids itself are an issue – ultimately they provide a diversified source of 
non-callable funding which is helpful for BP. However, BP should run with a more conservative 
balance sheet as it may need to replace them with vanilla debt over time subject to interest 
rates and corporate debt at the time of the call dates. For now, there is a modest arbitrage 
between the hybrids yield to call and BP issuing a five year bond, but the delta is fairly 
immaterial in our view, meaning an immediate action does not seem to be required.

Liquidity is not a problem for the majors in general, and so the ‘debt issue’ that the market has 
been concerned about for some time is more about the FCF leakage away from shareholders 
rather than concerns in an absolute sense. In 2024-25, when looking at the proportion of CFFO 
impacted by these costs, we see that hybrids and other minority outflows added ~$7-8/bbl to 
BP’s FCF breakeven after dividends (~$60-70/bbl). This was compounded by some poor 
operational performance. Our analysis suggests that BP should be able to reduce its breakeven 
to between $40-50/bbl even after these payments by 2029E, which brings it more in line with 
peers. Much of the investment case for BP is about bringing metrics in line with peers, which 
should then shine a light on its discounted valuation and whether it is still appropriate.

Shareholder returns framework – our $0.02 
The significant de-leveraging we are seeing come through in the numbers should drive a re-
start in buybacks. We think buybacks are an important part of the investment case for BP, as 
the company should provide a modestly growing yield and relatively low headline growth. The 
buybacks effectively help support growth and can alter the dividend per share growth 
trajectory. As yet, the company has been hesitant to provide guideposts on this issue, and we 
think investors are expecting clarity at the CMD. 
We believe an overall debt like obligation level of ~$25bn is sufficiently conservative enough 
to restart buybacks and we expect BP to kick things off in a modest fashion, starting with 
$1.5bn per quarter in 2H27, rising to $2bn per quarter in 2028. On an annualized basis, this 
would equate to a 40% payout ratio on headline CFFO. We think BP should put forward an 
intention to pay out around this range through the cycle, but we do not anticipate the 
company to be overly formulaic about it. Overall, we’ve seen some majors move away from 
formulaic approaches to payout, as these become more challenging as the macro gets more 
volatile. For example, this year Dated Brent has ranged between $60/bbl and $145/bbl – and 
adjusting share buybacks to quarterly volatility in this environment is a recipe for disaster.

Thinking about the period 2027-30E, at current share prices, we see BP delivering ~45% to 
shareholders via dividends and buybacks, relative to Shell at 40%, TotalEnergies at 36% and 
Eni at 33%. While some investors may not be willing to pay up for this without clearer line of 
sight, given the rapid pace of de-leveraging amid a very supportive macro environment, we 
think BP has room to positively surprise into 2027 on both timing and magnitude of buybacks.  
Providing more visibility would help, but management may be hesitant  
We think it would serve BP to provide some visibility on buybacks under reasonable macro 
conditions, perhaps guiding to payouts twice a year rather than quarterly, thus allowing 
investors to focus more on the business. Management, however, may be hesitant to provide 
multi-quarter guidance in such a volatile environment. Recall in February 2024, BP announced 
a multi quarter buyback at $1.75bn per quarter, but refining margins subsequently took a 
negative turn and this left BP exposed to paying a fixed outflow on declining underlying cash 
flow. We think this problem is solved by simply having a larger buffer on the balance sheet 
than BP has historically had. 
A return to “slow growth” mode 
Alongside de-leveraging and the restart of more competitive shareholder returns, BP needs to 
present a well-rounded investment case which incorporates modest growth. Following the 
misadventures in the low carbon space under Bernard Looney, much of what Meg has and is 
expected to do is unwinding of these moves – whether it be divesting assets or simply pausing 
projects. She has been clear that the focus of the company is set to be the upstream, 
downstream and its trading organisation. 
While the emphasis from investors has mostly been on the balance sheet and financial 
position, BP has had a number of exploration successes which look set to support its upstream 
hopper into 2030 and beyond. The highest profile of this is Bumerangue in Brazil, where two 
more appraisal wells are ongoing. Our model currently assumes an early production system in 
2030, however there remains uncertainty around the commercial viability of the discovery, 
and we expect early 2027 to be a period of heavy news flow for the prospect. Elsewhere, BP 
has made discoveries in Egypt, multiple discoveries in Namibia and in Angola through the Azule 
JV, while also picking up more blocks in the Gulf of Mexico to supplement its current asset 
base. In addition, BP signed a contract for the Kirkuk re-development in Iraq, although this 
should contribute more meaningfully to production further out.

Reserve life – forward looking analysis 
A few years ago, we published a thematic note on the sector on reserve lives across the sector. 
These numbers are typically utilised on a backward looking basis, with annual changes driven 
by FIDs, start-ups, as well as acquisitions and divestments. 1P, or proved reserves, are typically 
used to assess the depth of upstream portfolios over time. It is a limited measure, but as all 
companies are required to disclose it on an annual basis, it becomes an easy, but crude tool to 
compare across the sector. Low reserve lives typically indicate weaker portfolios or the risk of 
M&A as companies seek to acquire resources. High reserve lives typically indicate confidence 
in the upstream portfolio and could drive premium multiples.  
Ultimately there is some use for these metrics, but they represent a snapshot at a point in 
time. In our prior thematic note, we used some rules of thumb to build a forward looking 
picture on reserve lives across the sector. While there is a lot of uncertainty around the figures 
booked for each project, we assume a third of reserves are booked at FID, a third at start-up 
and a third through the life of the field over time. For unconventional assets, we base our 
reserves addition assumptions on rig counts and EURs for each basin. We then take away 
production each year which depletes the reserve base, to build up a picture over 2025-30E. 
For full methodology and our prior analysis, see “European & US Integrated Energy – A 
distinctive approach to reserve life analysis”. 
For BP, the company’s starting position is lower than peers, with 7.9 years for liquids and 6.4 
years for gas. Our analysis suggests the overall reserve base could remain consistent with 
current levels, although with growing production we see overall reserve life declining from 7.2 
years in 2025 to ~6.5 years by 2030. Note that some of the key moving parts are likely to be 
whether Bumerangue is commercial (we assume an early production system FID’d late 2027, 
but start-up after 2030), as well as a positive development in Namibia FID’d in 2029 through 
the Azule JV. Other material additions include the Kirkuk development, where there is plenty 
of uncertainty on reserve bookings. Another key moving part is BPX, where our calculations 
suggest a steady decline in reserves bookings, which may prove overly cautious.

The bottom line is that we do think BP’s upstream portfolio could be supplemented with M&A

Downstream performance has been improving, but has been masked by macro 
In the downstream, we’ve noted a marked improvement in underlying performance in BP’s 
downstream assets, which may have been missed amid the rise in refinery margins we have 
seen more broadly this year in the industry. Refining availability has been well above BP’s 96% 
target this year, while utilisation has been close to 100%, marking the second successive year 
of yoy increases. This also marks the highest refining utilisation in recent years, and 
importantly means BP is able to take advantage of the exceptional environment we are seeing 
this year.

Valuation
We value BP based on a 50/50 weighting between SOTP
and normalized EV/DACF using a multiple of 5.5x – a wider
discount to its historical average than we use for some peers,
reflecting some uncertainty on the outcome of its longer-term
strategy. This leads to a price target of 720p, which supports
our Outperform rating.
Upside scenario
Our upside case is based on a $100/bbl scenario in 2027, as
well as base case refining & chemicals, which assumes a small
recovery versus 2019. We use average multiples vs history. On
this basis our valuation would be 860p.
Downside scenario
Our downside scenario assumes $40/bbl Brent in 2027, as well
as weaker refining, chemicals and marketing earnings nearer
to recession levels in the downstream, using earnings in ’08-09
as reference points. On this basis our downside case would be
460p.
Investment summary
We rate BP with an Outperform rating and 720p price target.
Given BP’s balance sheet and higher debt levels than peers,
our positive stance is predicated on our bullish commodity
view, as this should drive material debt reduction through
2026-27E. We believe BP’s leverage will be closer to peers
within 12-18 months, with the company having already
reached its year-end 2027 net debt target ($14-18bn reported
net debt) at 3Q26, and we see reported net debt on a
trajectory to go below zero in 1H27.
We still see BP in the early stages of its turnaround journey,
and its potential FCF generation leaves its valuation multiples
looking undemanding relative to peers. Given BP's balance
sheet, outside of macro conditions, the key thing to watch is
progress on asset sales, with the LightsourceBP farm-down
one of the potential divestments not currently announced as
far.
Beyond de-leveraging, with a new CEO in place, we believe
BP is set to accelerate its cost reduction and efficiency drive,
and expect positive news flow on this front through 2026, with
greater emphasis on its core oil and gas business, and less on
the transition businesses, where BP has not developed a track
record for shareholder value creation.
Risks to rating and price target
Risks to our price target and rating that are specific
to BP include: risk on oil and gas prices. In general,
all international integrated oil companies are exposed to
resource price fluctuations, political/security risk, execution
risk, and environmental/permitting risks. Given the operating
and financial leverage, lower commodity prices would be
more negative for BP than peers. Issues around governance
and the Board could remain an overhang if investors are
unconvinced of the quality of Board personnel.

Key sustainability questions
This section is intended to highlight key sustainability discussion points relevant to this company, as well as our views on the outlook. Both the
questions we highlight and our responses will evolve over time as the dialogue between management, analysts and investors continues to
advance. We welcome any feedback on the topics.
Our view
What are the most material
sustainability issues facing this
company?
With the Energy sector, much of the focus is on the environment given the business
is highly carbon intensive. BP previously put forward some of the more aggressive
low carbon ambitions, and previously committed to reducing oil and gas production
by 40% by 2030, although the company has since moderated these targets amid the
shifting geopolitical and macro landscape.
Does the company integrate
sustainability considerations into its
strategy?
BP issues a sustainability report which helps outline plans around sustainability,
including progress on net-zero ambitions, methane intensity and other key metrics.
As an oil and gas producer, the company aims to produce hydrocarbons at the lowest
carbon intensity possible, while also maintaining safety considerations.
What is diversity like at board /
management level?
BP’s board of directors was previously Chaired by Albert Manifold, who was recently
removed from the board. There is an ongoing search for a new Chair. The board
consists of ten members, four of whom are women, and three are ethnic minorities.
BP’s CEO Meg O’Neill and CFO Kate Thomson are also on the board.
How does the company’s
sustainability strategy and stance
differentiate versus its peers?
BP’s updated strategy is more in line with the in-sector peers, with modest capital
being put towards key energy transition themes, and many of these investments
being put off balance sheet.
How is the management team
compensated, and how does this
differ versus peers?
BP’s compensation structure includes a combination of base salary, short-term and
long-term incentives compensation, similar to its peer group. BP’s LTIP is judged on
a three-year period with a further three-year holding period thereafter. Progress
on its energy transition targets as well as safety are key considerations for the
management team’s scorecards.
Are there any other considerations
at the company-specific level to
highlight?
BP has a mixed history and track record for safety, the highest profile of which was
the Gulf of Mexico oil spill over a decade ago. Multi-billion dollar fines have been
paid to date, and we expect $1.1bn in payments per annum over the next few years.
Further, BP, similar to peers, operates in many geographies and can be exposed to
volatile supply chains, potential corruption and challenges in dealing with native
populations.

Company description
BP plc is one of the four largest publicly traded Western oil companies with its headquarters in London and a primary listing on
the London Stock Exchange. BP has a presence in more than 80 countries worldwide.
Following the Macondo tragedy in April 2010, BP embarked on an aggressive divestment programme, revised the company’s
strategic direction, and overhauled the senior management team. BP’s new strategy places an increased focus on safety and
operational risk management, rebuilding trust with its global stakeholders, investing for growth in its upstream business, and
reshaping its downstream portfolio.
