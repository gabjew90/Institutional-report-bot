The Wedbush View
We are assuming coverage of OKLO at OUTPERFORM with a $36 target price (from
$110). OKLO is the furthest along of any U.S. advanced nuclear developer: it has
taken a privately funded, privately sited reactor from groundbreaking to criticality
in under 11 months at its Groves isotope test reactor. That record, paired with a
three-segment platform spanning power, fuel recycling, and isotopes, separates
OKLO from peers still in first-of-a-kind regulatory review with no operating asset.
Groves is the proof point. On August 5, 2026, Groves became the first DOE Reactor
Pilot Program reactor to reach criticality on private land, showing OKLO can move
a greenfield site through DOE authorization to an operating reactor using private
capital and commercial suppliers. Groves is a test and isotope asset, not an Aurora-
scale powerhouse, but its authorization pathway, supplier network, and operating
team carry forward to Aurora-INL, Aurora-Ohio, and the broader fleet.
A three-segment platform, still pre-revenue. OKLO reports Power (Aurora
powerhouses), Fuel (recycling and fabrication), and Isotopes (Groves and the
Idaho Radiochemistry Laboratory). Its first revenue, $1.21m in 2Q26, came
entirely from ARMEC and Creative Engineers, Inc., two engineering businesses
acquired in June 2026. The Company targets first isotope revenue from the Idaho
Radiochemistry Laboratory in early 2027. Aurora-INL targets commercial operation
in late 2027/2028; DOE approved its Preliminary Documented Safety Analysis
(PDSA) in June 2026, and the final Documented Safety Analysis (DSA) is pending.
A diversified pipeline with uncertain conversion timing. Named counterparties
include Meta (anchor of the 1.2 GWe Ohio campus), Equinix ($25m prepayment
tied to a 500 MW letter of intent), Switch (12 GW non-binding master power
agreement), Diamondback Energy (50 MW letter of intent in the Permian Basin),
and the Defense Logistics Agency (Notice of Intent to Award for Eielson Air Force
Base, OKLO's first validated military offtake). Only Aurora-INL has a Company-
disclosed commercial operation date; we model every other project on our own
timing assumptions.
Catalysts and valuation. Our base case assumes ~7.9 GW of operating capacity by
2040, but our target price depends on OKLO hitting the milestones that de-risk
that build-out. Near-term catalysts include Aurora-INL's final DSA, readiness review,
and startup authorization; first isotope revenue from the Idaho Radiochemistry
Laboratory in early 2027; and conversion of Switch, Equinix, or Diamondback into
site-specific binding agreements. OKLO shares, along with the broader advanced
nuclear group, trade as if few or no reactors get built. Each milestone narrows that
gap, and we see upside beyond our target if OKLO converts its pipeline into GW-
scale capacity.

Company Overview 
Jacob DeWitte and Caroline DeWitte (formerly Cochran), both Massachusetts Institute of 
Technology (MIT)-trained nuclear engineers, founded OKLO in 2013 and continue to serve as Chief 
Executive Officer (CEO) and Chief Operating Officer (COO), respectively. The Company's core 
 
www.wedbush.com
Page 3

 
 
technology, the Aurora powerhouse, is a sodium-cooled fast fission reactor modeled after the 
Experimental Breeder Reactor-II (EBR-II), which operated at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) from 
1964 to 1994. Oklo went public via a special purpose acquisition company merger in May 2024. 
OKLO operates under a build-own-operate model. Unlike most advanced reactor developers that 
license their designs to utilities or site hosts, OKLO intends to own and operate its own generators 
and sell power directly to customers under long-term power purchase agreements. 
The Company has organized its business into three segments:  
Power (Aurora powerhouse deployment and operation) 
Fuel (recycling of used nuclear fuel and fresh fuel fabrication) 
Isotopes (production of radioisotopes for medical, industrial, and defense applications) 
Rather than operating as independent businesses, OKLO designed these three segments to 
reinforce one another by sharing capabilities across licensing, fuel, siting, procurement, 
construction, and operations. 
Since early 2025, OKLO has acquired three businesses: 
• 
ARMEC (closed June 4, 2026, for $20.5m), an Oak Ridge, Tennessee-based precision 
manufacturing and fabrication company with prior customers such as the U.S. ITER fusion 
project; 
• 
Creative Engineers, Inc. (closed June 15, 2026, for $12.9m), a sodium systems engineering 
firm; and 
• 
Atomic Alchemy (closed February 28, 2025, for ~$25m, mostly in stock), a radioisotope 
producer now operating as Oklo Isotopes, which includes the Idaho Radiochemistry 
Laboratory. OKLO later built the Groves isotope test reactor in Lockhart, Texas, on a 
greenfield site under the Oklo Isotopes business. 
 
ARMEC and Creative Engineers generate all of OKLO’s revenue to date; Power and Isotopes 
remain pre-revenue.  
Technology Overview 
Aurora Powerhouse Design 
The Aurora powerhouse is a liquid-metal (sodium) cooled fast reactor, with individual units ranging 
from ~15 MWe up to 75 MWe depending on configuration and site. Oklo's design philosophy 
emphasizes modularity and a 20-year, single-core-load fuel cycle: because fast reactors do not 
require periodic refueling outages in the way conventional light-water reactors do, Oklo has stated 
that an Aurora unit can operate for up to two decades on its initial fuel load. 
The design can accept fresh HALEU, recycled fuel derived from used nuclear fuel, or down-blended 
material, giving Oklo multiple potential fuel pathways rather than dependence on a single supply 
chain. 
Fuel Strategy: Multiple Pathways, None Yet at Scale 
Oklo's fuel strategy explicitly avoids reliance on a single pathway and spans three potential fuel 
sources: 
 
www.wedbush.com
Page 4

 
 
The Company has a letter of intent with Centrus Energy for HALEU supply from Centrus's American 
Centrifuge Plant in Piketon, Ohio, which the Company states would support up to five Aurora 
powerhouses for multiple years, with potential deliveries beginning in 2029. Aurora-INL's initial 
core uses feedstock from recycled EBR-II used fuel, which INL itself processes under the DOE's 
Advanced Nuclear Fuel Line Pilot Program. Oklo is also in advanced negotiations with the DOE for 
access to surplus defense plutonium as a potential bridge-fuel pathway, paired with a prospective 
partnership with European developer Newcleo that could bring up to $2b in project capital, subject 
to definitive agreements. None of these three pathways is yet supplying fuel at commercial scale, 
and HALEU availability broadly remains a sector-wide constraint affecting nearly every U.S. 
advanced reactor developer. 
The Advanced Fuel Center and Fuel Recycling 
Oklo intends its Advanced Fuel Center in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, planned at up to $1.68b of total 
investment, to become the first privately funded commercial-scale nuclear fuel recycling facility in 
the United States, using a pyroprocessing (electrochemical) technique the Company states can 
recover more than 90% of the remaining energy in used nuclear fuel. 
The facility's primary stated purpose is to supply fuel internally for Oklo's own Aurora fleet; in 
Oklo's own words in its S-1 filing, third-party sale of recycling services is something the Company 
"plans" to pursue as it "has the potential to further benefit" Oklo economically, language that 
frames third-party recycling revenue as a secondary opportunity rather than the facility's core 
purpose.

Regulatory Progress 
Oklo is pursuing two distinct, parallel regulatory pathways depending on the site and business line. 
Aurora-INL and Groves are advancing under the DOE's Reactor Pilot Program, a DOE authorization 
pathway, distinct from U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) licensing, intended to compress 
timelines for test and early commercial reactors sited on or using DOE-affiliated land; Aurora-INL 
has received DOE approval of its PDSA as of June 2026, with a final DSA still pending. 
Groves completed the full DOE authorization chain, including DSA approval, a completed readiness 
review, and startup authorization received July 23, 2026, ahead of its August 5, 2026, criticality. For 
 
www.wedbush.com
Page 6

 
 
Aurora-Ohio, Eielson, and future commercial-scale fleet sites, we instead expect OKLO to pursue 
NRC licensing. The NRC issued its final Part 53 rule for advanced reactor licensing in March 2026, 
targeting 6–12-month review timelines intended to apply across a fleet of similar units rather than 
requiring a full bespoke review per site. 
Outside of federal regulatory proceedings, OKLO also faces grid interconnection risks. In August 
2026, the PJM Interconnection removed a 750 MW mixed-technology project in Virginia, which 
combines nuclear, fuel cells, and natural gas, from its Cycle 01 interconnection study cycle. The 
Federal Energy Regulatory Commission rejected Oklo’s complaint seeking reinstatement in 
September 2026, confirming a potential 14- to 18-month delay. 
While this specific dispute involves an undisclosed project rather than the Ohio campus, both Ohio 
and Virginia fall within PJM’s footprint and are subject to the same interconnection queue process. 
We considered building additional conservatism into our Aurora-Ohio timeline on this basis, but 
ultimately chose not to, given the dispute involves a different, unconfirmed project rather than 
Ohio specifically. Our Aurora-Ohio timeline still reflects a separately confirmed site-
characterization delay, unrelated to the PJM matter. 
10-Point Standardized Assessment Framework 
1. Technology Readiness Level 
Oklo has moved further than any U.S. advanced reactor peer from paper design into physical, 
operating hardware. The Groves isotope test reactor achieved first criticality on August 5, 2026, 
the first DOE Reactor Pilot Program reactor to do so on private land, under 11 months from 
groundbreaking, which the Company describes as the fastest privately funded and privately sited 
nuclear reactor build in history. 
Aurora-INL, the first commercial-scale powerhouse, is under active construction with major 
equipment, including the Siemens Energy power conversion system, under vendor contract. We 
view the Groves execution record as the most important technology-readiness data point in our 
coverage, though Groves is a smaller test and isotope-production asset, so its execution record 
transfers only partly to Aurora's larger commercial-scale design. 
2. Regulatory Pathway 
Oklo's regulatory record is genuinely differentiated: it is the only developer we are aware of to have 
taken a reactor through the full DOE Reactor Pilot Program authorization chain, culminating in 
Groves' criticality, while Aurora-INL has cleared DOE approval of its PDSA. 
The Company is pursuing a dual-track strategy, using the DOE pathway for INL and Groves while 
building toward NRC licensing, aided by the NRC's new Part 53 fleet-based framework, for Aurora-
Ohio, Eielson, and future commercial sites. That said, the DOE Reactor Pilot Program pathway is 
not equivalent to a standard NRC operating license, and OKLO has not yet tested lessons learned 
at INL and Groves against the NRC's own review process. 
3. Fuel Supply Chain 
Oklo has deliberately diversified its fuel strategy across three pathways: a Centrus HALEU letter of 
intent (potential deliveries beginning 2029), recycled EBR-II fuel processed by INL for Aurora-INL's 
 
www.wedbush.com
Page 7

 
 
initial core, and advanced negotiations with the DOE for surplus defense plutonium. No pathway is 
yet supplying fuel at commercial fleet scale, and HALEU availability remains a sector-wide 
bottleneck. Oklo intends to use the Advanced Fuel Center in Oak Ridge, planned at up to $1.68b, 
to make the Company more fuel-self-sufficient over time, though its primary purpose is internal 
fuel supply rather than third-party recycling revenue. 
4. Thermal Efficiency and Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) 
Oklo's shareholder presentation targets a levelized electricity cost of $40 to $90 per MWh, which 
the Company has compared to DOE-estimated ranges of $63-$99/MWh for natural gas with carbon 
capture, $66-$109/MWh for non-Oklo advanced nuclear, and $69-$119/MWh for renewables 
paired with storage. 
This remains an unvalidated cost target: no Aurora commercial powerhouse has yet reached 
commercial operation, and a short-seller report has argued that realistic fuel and construction costs 
could require power prices meaningfully above Oklo’s own targets to generate an attractive return. 
We assume a $150/MWh power sales price for new Aurora capacity, above Oklo’s own disclosed 
cost target but consistent with the upper end of published cost estimates for first-of-a-kind small 
modular reactors specifically, reflecting Oklo’s premium hyperscaler counterparties. 
5. Passive Safety 
Aurora's sodium-cooled fast-reactor design operates at near-atmospheric pressure, which Oklo and 
independent researchers argue provides inherent passive-safety characteristics relative to 
pressurized light-water designs, reducing the driving force behind any potential primary-loop 
breach. The design traces its lineage to EBR-II, which operated at INL for three decades and was 
the subject of passive-safety demonstration tests in the late 1980s. As with any design without 
multi-decade commercial operating history at the Aurora scale specifically, real-world validation of 
these characteristics at full fleet scale remains ahead. 
6. Constructability 
Groves is the clearest evidence of Oklo's constructability: the Company took a greenfield site to an 
operational, critical nuclear facility in under 11 months. The Company has explicitly framed Groves 
as having created a repeatable deployment blueprint, citing capabilities in operating organization, 
supplier network development, DOE readiness processes, construction execution, and project 
controls that the Company intends to carry forward into Isotopes, Power, and Fuel deployments 
alike. 
Aurora-INL's construction is ongoing, and we view its pace relative to Groves as the next meaningful 
test of whether this execution capability scales to larger, commercial powerhouse projects. 
7. Offtake and Counterparties 
Oklo's named commercial relationships span a broader set of end markets than most peers:

8. Management and Pedigree 
Co-founders Jacob DeWitte (CEO) and Caroline DeWitte (COO) have led Oklo since its 2013 
founding, and both hold graduate nuclear engineering credentials from MIT. President Trump 
appointed Jacob DeWitte to the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology in 2026, 
underscoring the Company's visibility in federal nuclear policy circles. Chief Financial Officer (CFO) 
R. Craig Bealmear, in the role since August 2023, brings over 30 years of finance and strategy 
experience, including as CFO of Renewable Energy Group (now part of Chevron) and three decades 
at BP plc.  
The Company's board expanded to eleven directors in April 2026, and Chief Technology Officer 
(CTO) Pat Schweiger transitioned to a senior technical advisor role the same month, leaving no 
separately designated CTO as the Company builds out dedicated business-unit leadership instead. 
9. Cash Runway and Dilution 
Oklo exited 2Q26 with ~$3.0b of combined cash and investments and no meaningful debt, 
following a $1.9b at-the-market (ATM) equity raise in 1H26. We estimate this liquidity is insufficient 
to fund the Company's full, multi-site capital program: we forecast cumulative capital expenditure 
of well over $10b through 2036, driven primarily by new Aurora construction, the Advanced Fuel 
Center, and continued Aurora-INL completion costs. 
We forecast Oklo's diluted share count growing from ~186m currently to ~511m by 2036 to fund 
this build-out, a substantial dilution path that we view as the central financial risk to our thesis, 
independent of operational execution. We model this capital need to be funded almost entirely 
through future equity issuances, which is consistent with the Company’s own stated intent. It is not 
clear whether consensus estimates assume a different financing mix, such as debt or partners at 
the project level, as these arrangements have not been disclosed.  
10. Policy and Subsidy Sensitivity 
Oklo is a direct beneficiary of a broad, bipartisan federal push to accelerate advanced nuclear 
deployment. The DOE's Genesis Mission, a national AI-enabled science initiative connecting 17 
national laboratories with industry, selected three Oklo-supported projects among its first round 
of funding, and the DOE has signed memoranda of understanding (MOUs) with five states, 
including Utah, Tennessee, Oklahoma, Louisiana, and Idaho, to explore Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation

support across multiple agencies, a shift in administration priorities or appropriations could 
meaningfully affect the pace of Oklo's regulatory and financing tailwinds.

Business Model and Commercialization Pathway 
OKLO's build-own-operate model means the Company, not a utility or site host, owns and operates 
each Aurora powerhouse and sells power directly to customers under long-term power purchase 
agreements, a structure that contrasts with most advanced reactor peers, which license designs 
and leave construction and ownership to project developers. We forecast Power segment revenue 
using a $150/MWh first-year power price, escalating 2.5% annually (matching our Fuel segment's 
price-escalation assumption for internal consistency), against a 92% assumed capacity factor, 
implying ~$1.21m of annual revenue per operating MW. We model the fleet growing from one 
 
www.wedbush.com
Page 10

 
 
operating unit (75 MWe) at FY28E to 13 units (0.9 GWe) by FY32E, 47 units (3.5 GWe) by FY36E, 
and 106 units (7.9 GWe) by FY40E.  
For the Isotopes segment, we model the Idaho Radiochemistry Laboratory and Groves separately: 
the Idaho Lab's growth rate converges toward independently corroborated actinium-225 and 
targeted-alpha-therapy market research (14.3% to 28.5% compound annual growth rate across five 
market-research firms), given the facility's NRC license ties its initial activity to recovering disused 
radium sources as feedstock for that specific market. We view that range as supported by the 
supply-demand gap our Biotechnology team highlights in its 2026 Radiopharmaceutical Therapy 
Review and Outlook (8/19/26): legacy actinium-225 supply of ~63 GBq annually has historically 
been enough to treat fewer than 1,000 patients worldwide, while a single widely adopted actinium-
225 therapy is expected to require 200–400 GBq annually. That said, no targeted actinium-225 
therapy is yet approved (pivotal readouts for RYZ101 and 225Ac-PSMA-617 are pending), and 
announced capacity additions, including TerraPower Isotopes' ~20x expansion beginning in 2029 
and NorthStar's commercial radium-226-based production, could pressure pricing. Notably, our 
Biotechnology team's Aktis Oncology (AKTS) initiation, a clinical-stage developer of actinium-225 
therapies, cites supply agreements with NorthStar, TerraPower Isotopes and Niowave, 
underscoring that drug developers are already diversifying actinium-225 sourcing.  
We loosely anchor Groves' growth rate to lutetium-177 demand, where consensus estimates cited 
in our Biotechnology team's Aktis Oncology (AKTS) initiation (Evaluate Pharma) imply ~16% 
compound annual growth in combined Pluvicto and Lutathera sales from 2025 to 2030 ($2.8b to 
$5.8b), moderating to ~12.5% through 2032, with total marketed radiopharmaceutical therapy 
sales growing ~12% annually over the same 2025–2032 period. Pluvicto's July 2026 mHSPC 
approval, which roughly doubles its eligible population, supports that trajectory. We hold lower 
confidence in this anchor, however, given that global lutetium-177 production capacity already 
comfortably exceeds commercial demand and that Groves is a low-power test reactor rather than 
a commercial production reactor; consistent with our Biotechnology team's view, we see 
meaningful isotope supply from OKLO as longer-dated and contingent on VIPR licensing, 
construction and scale-up. 
For Fuel, we forecast only Advanced Fuel Center third-party recycled-material sales, starting FY30E, 
since OKLO consumes Aurora Fuel Fabrication Facility output internally and we do not treat it as 
revenue. 
The great majority of OKLO's long-term forecast capacity, ~84% of total fleet MW by FY40E, sits in 
five "Pipeline" tranches representing undisclosed future fleet scale-up beyond the four named, 
disclosed sites (Aurora-INL, Aurora-Ohio, Eielson, and the two initial pipeline placeholders).

Financial Overview 
OKLO remains pre-revenue on any significant commercial basis today. The Company’s entire 2Q26 
revenue of $1.21m came from ARMEC and Creative Engineers, which were acquisitions OKLO made 
for engineering and fabrication capabilities that support, but sit outside, the core power and 
isotopes businesses. Unlike more mature peers, OKLO has no prior-year revenue base for these 
two businesses to compare against on a year-over-year basis as OKLO acquired both in mid-2026. 
This existing manufacturing base drives nearly all near-term revenue growth through 4Q26, and 
we forecast quarterly revenue of only $1.31m and $1.41m in 3Q26E and 4Q26E, respectively.  
We build our base-case forecast around specific, named milestones. We forecast Isotopes revenue 
beginning in 1Q27E with the Idaho Radiochemistry Laboratory ($1.9m), followed by Groves’ 
commercial production ramp beginning in FY28E. Power segment revenue inflects materially in 
FY28E as Aurora-INL achieves commercial operation, and scales further as Aurora-Ohio and Eielson 
phases reach COD between FY28E and FY31E. 
Fuel segment revenue does not begin until FY30E, reflecting our assumption that the Advanced 
Fuel Center’s third-party recycled-material revenue remains pre-commercial until then. Beyond 
these disclosed projects, our model includes an undisclosed pipeline assumption that brings total 
operating capacity to ~7.9 GW by FY40E. 
On this basis, we forecast total revenue of $3.9m in FY26E, scaling to $16.9m in FY27E as isotopes 
revenue begins, then inflecting to $69.4m in FY28E as Aurora-INL and Groves both reach 
commercial contribution. Revenue continues to scale through the back half of the decade, reaching 
$348.6m by FY31E and $919.5m by FY32E as the broader fleet, including Aurora-Ohio’s later phases 
and the undisclosed pipeline tranches, ramps. 
R&D and general and administrative (G&A) expense, which we model as a function of active 
construction activity rather than extrapolated historical growth, remains flat at ~$40m per quarter 
each through FY27E before gradually moderating as a percentage of revenue once the fleet scales.

We believe our margin assumption is more defensible than consensus’s implied figures. As opposed 
to assuming a single blended margin, we build each segment’s long-run margin ceiling from the 
real, reported margin of the closest publicly traded comparable in that specific end market. For 
Power, we anchor to Constellation Energy’s actual reported gross margin of ~44% as it is the most 
relevant large-scale nuclear generation comparable, as opposed to OKLO’s own, but not yet 
validated, $40-$90/MWh cost target. For Fuel, we anchor to Centrus Energy’s real, sustained gross 
margin of ~26%, the most direct comparable for nuclear fuel enrichment and cycle services. For 
Isotopes, we anchor to Lantheus Holdings’ real gross margin of ~61%, a branded 
radiopharmaceutical producer, though we discount our own assumption modestly below that 
figure given OKLO’s business more closely resembles a raw-material supplier than a fully integrated 
branded drug company. Blended across the three segments at FY35E revenue mix, this produces 
our 34.7% figure. We are not aware of any comparable company, in any of these three end markets, 
that supports a blended margin above the low-to-mid 60s even at full commercial scale, which is 
the margin level consensus’s own FY35E EBITDA estimate implicitly requires. Below the gross-profit 
line, our R&D and G&A assumptions run modestly higher than consensus in dollar terms, though 
as a share of revenue they converge toward the low-to-mid teens by FY35E as the fleet scales. 
Capital Requirements Going Forward 
OKLO reported $3.0b of combined cash and investments as of June 30, 2026, following $1.9b of 
ATM equity issuance in 1H26. We view this balance as sufficient to fund OKLO’s operations through 
the end of 2027 without any additional capital raise, reflecting the Company’s still-modest near-
term operating burn relative to its current cash position. We note that the company has a $1b ATM 
program authorized and we expect only opportunistic use of it; at current stock prices we do not 
expect use of it.  
We forecast OKLO needing to raise capital in FY28E, when we estimate a ~$1.65b equity raise 
becomes necessary. Three capital-intensive projects converging in the same year drive this need: 
1. The final completion costs of Aurora-INL; 
2. The construction ramp of the Advanced Fuel Center; and 
3. The start of new power-plant construction at Aurora-Ohio as well as other sites that are 
part of the broader fleet buildout. 
This is the point at which OKLO’s capital needs shift from a single, well-defined project (Aurora-INL) 
to multiple simultaneous, large-scale construction programs, which we view as the real inflection 
point for the Company’s financing requirements going forward.

From FY28E onward, we forecast OKLO returning to the capital markets in most years through the 
remainder of our explicit forecast window, with raise sizes generally tracking that year’s capital 
expenditure intensity rather than occurring as a single, large financing event. 
We forecast OKLO funding this capital need almost entirely through future equity issuance rather 
than project debt, with a minimum cash floor of $500m triggering follow-on raises as needed, and 
raises sized to also cover the following year’s anticipated capital expenditure once OKLO enters its 
active, multi-site construction phase. 
In aggregate, we forecast cumulative capital expenditure of ~$16.9b through FY38E, and a diluted 
share count growing from ~186m shares today to ~511m shares by FY36E, a dilution path we view 
as the single largest financial risk to our thesis, independent of the Company’s operational 
execution. 
We would note three considerations that the figures above do not fully capture: 
1. The Newcleo partnership under discussion for the Surplus Plutonium Utilization Program could 
bring up to $2b of additional project capital, subject to definitive agreements, which would 
reduce or delay the dilution burden on existing shareholders if it materializes on favorable 
terms. 
2. OKLO’s Meta prepayment agreement for the Ohio campus represents a real, disclosed source 
of capital directed specifically at fuel procurement, though we do not model its cash flow 
timing as a distinct mechanism and we do not reflect it in the raise timeline above. 
3. Our $16.9b capital estimate assumes ~7.9 GWe of total fleet capacity by FY40E. We would flag 
this as a source of upside risk to our capital dilution estimates in the other direction. To the 
extent that consensus assumptions prove too optimistic about pipeline conversion speed or 
scale, our current capital and dilution figures could prove themselves too aggressive, rather 
than too conservative.

Key Risks to Our Price Target 
No binding commercial agreements: every disclosed relationship beyond Aurora-INL itself, 
including Meta, Switch, Equinix, Diamondback, and the Eielson DLA relationship, remains a non-
binding letter of intent, memorandum of understanding, or Notice of Intent to Award. Our revenue 
build for the large majority of modeled fleet capacity depends on conversion to binding, site-
specific agreements that have not yet occurred. 
Undisclosed pipeline concentration risk: ~84% of our modeled FY40E fleet capacity sits in five 
Pipeline tranches with no disclosed site, customer, or unit count. This is the single largest swing 
factor in our model; a smaller actual build-out, slower conversion, or failure of these relationships 
to materialize would be the most consequential negative revision to our forecasts. 
Capital intensity and dilution risk: we forecast cumulative capital expenditure of ~$16.9b through 
FY38E, which we expect OKLO to fund almost entirely through equity issuance, implying a diluted 
 
www.wedbush.com
Page 18

 
 
share count more than doubling from current levels. A higher per-MW capital cost than we model, 
consistent with some bearish third-party analysis, or a larger fleet build-out than our base case, 
would directly increase this dilution burden. 
First-of-a-kind execution risk beyond Groves: Groves' 229-day construction and criticality is a 
genuine proof point, but it is a smaller test and isotope facility; Aurora-INL and the broader Aurora 
commercial fleet have not yet demonstrated the same execution speed at full commercial scale. 
Fuel-recycling economics risk: our Fuel segment revenue estimate reflects Oklo's own stated intent 
to sell third-party recycling services, but academic and industry experts have raised credible 
questions about whether recycled fuel is currently cost-competitive with a conventional once-
through fuel cycle at prevailing uranium prices. 
Regulatory pathway risk: Oklo's DOE Reactor Pilot Program authorization for Aurora-INL and 
Groves is a different pathway than the NRC licensing the Company will need for Aurora-Ohio, 
Eielson, and the broader commercial fleet; execution success under one pathway does not 
guarantee an equivalent pace under the other. 
Unit economics risk: our $150/MWh modeled power price sits above Oklo's own disclosed $40-
90/MWh cost target range; if Oklo's actual costs land at the high end of its own disclosed range, or 
above it as some bearish analysis suggests, realized margins could differ materially from our model. 
Policy and subsidy risk: Oklo is a direct beneficiary of current DOE and NRC prioritization of 
advanced nuclear deployment (the Genesis Mission, the Reactor Pilot Program, and the Part 53 
rulemaking); a shift in federal priorities across future administrations is a risk beyond Oklo's control.

Risks to the Attainment of Our Price Target and Rating:
No binding commercial agreements: every disclosed relationship beyond Aurora-INL itself, including Meta, Switch, Equinix,
Diamondback, and the Eielson DLA relationship, remains a non-binding letter of intent, memorandum of understanding, or Notice of
Intent to Award. Our revenue build for the large majority of modeled fleet capacity depends on conversion to binding, site-specific
agreements that have not yet occurred.
Undisclosed pipeline concentration risk: ~84% of our modeled FY40E fleet capacity sits in five Pipeline tranches with no disclosed site,
customer, or unit count. This is the single largest swing factor in our model; a smaller actual build-out, slower conversion, or failure of
these relationships to materialize would be the most consequential negative revision to our forecasts.
Capital intensity and dilution risk: we forecast cumulative capital expenditure of ~$16.9b through FY38E, which we expect OKLO to
fund almost entirely through equity issuance, implying a diluted share count more than doubling from current levels. A higher per-MW
capital cost than we model, consistent with some bearish third-party analysis, or a larger fleet build-out than our base case, would
directly increase this dilution burden.
First-of-a-kind execution risk beyond Groves: Groves' 229-day construction and criticality is a genuine proof point, but it is a smaller
test and isotope facility; Aurora-INL and the broader Aurora commercial fleet have not yet demonstrated the same execution speed
at full commercial scale.
Fuel-recycling economics risk: our Fuel segment revenue estimate reflects Oklo's own stated intent to sell third-party recycling services,
but academic and industry experts have raised credible questions about whether recycled fuel is currently cost-competitive with a
conventional once-through fuel cycle at prevailing uranium prices.
Regulatory pathway risk: Oklo's DOE Reactor Pilot Program authorization for Aurora-INL and Groves is a different pathway than the
NRC licensing the Company will need for Aurora-Ohio, Eielson, and the broader commercial fleet; execution success under one pathway
does not guarantee an equivalent pace under the other.
Unit economics risk: our $150/MWh modeled power price sits above Oklo's own disclosed $40-90/MWh cost target range; if Oklo's
actual costs land at the high end of its own disclosed range, or above it as some bearish analysis suggests, realized margins could differ
materially from our model.
Policy and subsidy risk: Oklo is a direct beneficiary of current DOE and NRC prioritization of advanced nuclear deployment (the Genesis
Mission, the Reactor Pilot Program, and the Part 53 rulemaking); a shift in federal priorities across future administrations is a risk beyond
Oklo's control.
