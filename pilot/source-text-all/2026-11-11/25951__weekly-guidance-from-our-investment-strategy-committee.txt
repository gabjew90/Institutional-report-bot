Higher bond yields point to the potential for longer-term returns 
If interest rates could still rise, why own bonds now? Although further interest rate increases could pressure bond 
prices in the near term, we believe today’s higher yields can offer more income and a potentially stronger starting 
point for longer-term returns. Historically, the yield available on a bond or bond portfolio at the beginning of an 
investment period has been closely related to subsequent longer-term returns. The yield is observed first; the 
return unfolds over the following years. 
Higher starting yields generally give income a larger role in total return, which combines income and price changes. 
When market rates rise, additional income may help offset bond-price declines over time; lower starting yields 
provide less cushion. However, the holding period matters, as current higher starting yields may not prevent 
disappointing returns in the following year, particularly if rates rise further. Our historical analysis examines the 
relationship over longer holding periods. 
Using data from the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index data since its 1976 inception, our analysis found a 
strong historical relationship between starting yields and subsequent annualized total returns. We examined 5-, 7-, 
and 10-year forward periods. Although the three periods produced similar results, we found that the relationship 
was strongest around seven years (see Chart 1).

The historical relationship can help frame longer-term return expectations, but starting yield is only one factor that 
can influence total returns and does not guarantee future results or an exact match between yields and returns. 
Returns over shorter holding periods are generally more sensitive to changes in market rates, while longer-term 
investors have more time for income to accumulate and potentially cushion price declines. For investors focused 
on income rather than near-term trading gains, starting yields can serve as a reference point for what bonds may 
deliver over several years, not what they might return next year. 
Importantly, a longer investment horizon does not automatically mean that extending portfolio duration1 is 
always prudent. The historical relationship between starting yields and subsequent returns does not establish that 
longer-maturity bonds will deliver better returns than shorter-maturity bonds. Extending duration increases 
exposure to price declines if rates rise further, which remains our interest rate forecast through year-end 2027. In 
our view, starting yields alone are therefore not a sufficient reason to extend maturities. We believe current yields 
in short- and intermediate-maturity bonds offer more attractive income opportunities without taking on the 
greater interest-rate sensitivity of longer-maturity bonds.  
Beyond income, high-quality bonds may offer other benefits for long-term investors, including diversification, 
reduced portfolio volatility, and support for cash-flow planning.  
• 
Diversification. High-quality bonds may help diversify equity exposure when slowing growth or market 
uncertainty increases demand for defensive assets. Allocations across asset classes can reduce reliance on 
a single market outcome, although diversification does not mean bonds will offset equity losses in every 
environment. 
• 
Reduced volatility. High-quality fixed-income investments have typically been less volatile than equities 
and may help moderate overall portfolio fluctuations. They remain exposed to price declines, however, so 
this potential benefit should not be confused with eliminating risks from rising interest rates. 
• 
Cash-flow planning. Individual high-quality bonds can have maturities aligned with anticipated spending 
needs. Most bonds repay principal at maturity unless the issuer defaults. Knowing when principal is 
expected can help investors stay invested and avoid unnecessary sales.  
Our view is that investors do not need to wait for the peak in interest rates to find opportunities in fixed income. 
Today’s higher yields can provide attractive income and a stronger starting point for longer-term returns, even if 
additional rate increases create near-term volatility. In our view, this supports maintaining bond exposure while 
remaining selective about interest-rate risk.

October’s rotation created opportunities 
October was a volatile month for equities, particularly for AI-related stocks. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.3%, 
compared with a 0.9% decline for the S&P 500 Index. We view the pullback as a reassessment of AI expectations 
rather than weakening fundamentals. S&P 500 Index earnings estimates continue to rise, with Information 
Technology among the leaders. 
Several factors contributed to the technology sell-off. After we upgraded Information Technology to favorable in 
April, the sector rallied through September and delivered more than twice the S&P 500 Index’s return. We 
downgraded Information Technology to neutral on September 29 as valuations became increasingly stretched. 
Concerns about the scale and pace of AI spending emerged first among neocloud companies and more recently 
spread to several Magnificent Seven companies.2 Large hyperscalers reported mixed third-quarter results and 
outlined elevated capital spending that pressured near-term free-cash-flow expectations.3 
Beneath the surface, market leadership broadened noticeably (see Table 1). The S&P 500 Equal-Weighted Index 
gained 5% in October, led by Energy and defensive sectors, several of which posted near-double-digit returns, 
compared to the 0.9% decline in the market-cap-weighted S&P 500 Index.

AI demand remains strong, but investors increasingly want evidence that spending can generate durable revenue, 
free cash flow, and attractive returns. We believe greater scrutiny reflects a focus on productive investment, not a 
weakening AI cycle. We expect economic growth and corporate earnings to remain supportive in 2027, making a 
lasting shift toward defensive leadership unlikely. Our view is that this environment does, however, reinforce the 
importance of selectivity.  
We remain neutral on Information Technology because elevated valuations and increasing debt-funded capital 
spending have created a more balanced risk-reward profile. We favor sectors we expect to benefit more broadly 
from AI investment, including Communication Services, Industrials, and Materials. We believe recent weakness in 
Communication Services has created a more attractive entry point, while Industrials and Materials may benefit 
from continued AI-related infrastructure spending. We continue to view market pullbacks as opportunities to add 
to those sectors.

Diesel remains the chokepoint 
Diesel prices rose roughly 71% from October 31, 2025, through November 2, 2026, outpacing regular gasoline’s 
roughly 37% increase and West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil’s 58% gain. Tight global diesel supplies and 
limited room to increase refinery output help explain the difference. Higher prices generally encourage more 
production, but capacity constraints can limit how quickly supply can respond. Having more crude available does 
not necessarily mean refiners can immediately deliver more diesel.  
Diesel’s importance extends beyond the pump. While gasoline primarily fuels passenger vehicles, diesel powers 
trucks, trains, and machinery that move goods, helps grow food, and build infrastructure. Higher diesel prices 
therefore can raise costs across the economy, potentially squeezing business profits or reaching consumers 
through higher prices. Businesses may initially absorb the increase, but persistent fuel costs can make that harder 
to sustain. 
Although core inflation excludes direct food and energy prices, it does not exclude fuel costs embedded in other 
purchases. Diesel can therefore contribute indirectly to core inflation as businesses pass those costs along. This 
makes diesel worth watching even for investors who rarely fill a diesel tank. 
Bottom line: Lower crude prices alone may not bring lasting relief if refining constraints keep diesel expensive. For 
investors, this reinforces our favorable view on commodities and our preference for using pullbacks to add 
exposure. Higher interest rates could temper further gains, making patience and discipline important. The goal is to 
manage inflation risk within a diversified portfolio, not to bet everything on continued fuel price increases.

Diversifying private real estate with credit 
Private real estate investors can gain exposure through both equity and credit strategies. While equity strategies 
own properties and seek returns from income growth and asset appreciation, credit strategies provide financing to 
property owners and generate returns primarily through contractual interest payments. 
Private real estate credit strategies have increasingly focused on bridge loans, which provide short-term financing 
for property acquisitions, refinancing, renovations, repositioning, or leasing newly constructed properties. These 
loans often have two- to five-year maturities and typically carry floating interest rates. Many are also non-
recourse, meaning repayment is primarily tied to the underlying property and its cash flow. 
Compared with equity, credit strategies generally emphasize recurring income and downside risk mitigation. 
Floating-rate loans can generate higher interest income when policy rates rise, although higher borrowing costs can 
also increase financial pressure on borrowers. Loans are typically secured by the property and rank ahead of equity 
in the capital structure, meaning equity investors generally absorb losses first. However, lenders can still experience 
losses if property values or cash flows decline substantially. 
We believe these characteristics contributed to the relative resilience of private real estate credit during the recent 
higher-rate environment (see Chart 3), as rising borrowing costs and declining property values challenged many 
equity strategies. 
Nevertheless, credit and equity strategies remain influenced by many of the same factors, including property 
fundamentals, valuations, and capital-market conditions, while also carrying risks such as illiquidity, complexity, 
and leverage. 
We believe private real estate credit can complement equity within a diversified allocation by providing a 
differentiated mix of income, risk, and capital preservation potential.

Risk considerations 
Forecasts and targets are based on certain assumptions and on views of market and economic conditions which are subject to change.  
Asset allocation and diversification are investment methods used to help manage risk. They do not guarantee investment returns or eliminate risk of loss including in 
a declining market. 
Each asset class has its own risk and return characteristics. The level of risk associated with a particular investment or asset class generally correlates with the level 
of return the investment or asset class might achieve. Stock markets, especially foreign markets, are volatile. Stock values may fluctuate in response to general 
economic and market conditions, the prospects of individual companies, and industry sectors. Sector investing can be more volatile than investments that are 
broadly diversified over numerous sectors of the economy and will increase a portfolio’s vulnerability to any single economic, political, or regulatory development 
affecting the sector. This can result in greater price volatility. Foreign investing has additional risks including those associated with currency fluctuation, political 
and economic instability, and different accounting standards. These risks are heightened in emerging markets. Small- and mid-cap stocks are generally more 
volatile, subject to greater risks and are less liquid than large company stocks. Bonds are subject to market, interest rate, price, credit/default, liquidity, inflation and 
other risks. Prices tend to be inversely affected by changes in interest rates. High yield (junk) bonds have lower credit ratings and are subject to greater risk of 
default and greater principal risk. Floating rate loans are generally considered to have speculative characteristics that involve default risk of principal and interest, 
collateral impairment, borrower industry concentration, and limited liquidity. The commodities markets are considered speculative, carry substantial risks, and have 
experienced periods of extreme volatility. Investing in a volatile and uncertain commodities market may cause a portfolio to rapidly increase or decrease in value 
which may result in greater share price volatility. Real estate has special risks including the possible illiquidity of underlying properties, credit risk, interest rate 
fluctuations and the impact of varied economic conditions. 
Alternative investments, such as hedge funds, private equity/private debt and private real estate funds, are speculative and involve a high degree of risk that is 
appropriate only for those investors who have the financial sophistication and expertise to evaluate the merits and risks of an investment in a fund and for which the 
fund does not represent a complete investment program. They entail significant risks that can include losses due to leveraging or other speculative investment 
practices, lack of liquidity, volatility of returns, restrictions on transferring interests in a fund, potential lack of diversification, absence and/or delay of information 
regarding valuations and pricing, complex tax structures and delays in tax reporting, less regulation and higher fees than mutual funds.  Hedge fund, private equity, 
private debt and private real estate fund investing involves other material risks including capital loss and the loss of the entire amount invested. A fund's offering 
documents should be carefully reviewed prior to investing. 
Hedge fund strategies, such as Equity Hedge, Event Driven, Macro and Relative Value, may expose investors to the risks associated with the use of short selling, 
leverage, derivatives and arbitrage methodologies. Short sales involve leverage and theoretically unlimited loss potential since the market price of securities sold 
short may continuously increase. The use of leverage in a portfolio varies by strategy. Leverage can significantly increase return potential but create greater risk of 
loss.  Derivatives generally have implied leverage which can magnify volatility and may entail other risks such as market, interest rate, credit, counterparty and 
management risks.  Arbitrage strategies expose a fund to the risk that the anticipated arbitrage opportunities will not develop as anticipated, resulting in potentially 
reduced returns or losses to the fund. 
Definitions 
Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index is a broad-based measure of the investment grade, US dollar-denominated, fixed-rate taxable bond market.  
NASDAQ Composite Index measures the market value of all domestic and foreign common stocks, representing a wide array of more than 5,000 companies, listed on 
the NASDAQ Stock Market. 
Preqin Global Real Estate Equity Closed-End Fund Index measures the performance of global closed-end, non-listed private capital funds that Preqin classified as 
real estate core, core plus, value added, and opportunistic. The Index is designed to measure private capital performance on a net basis from the perspective of 
limited partners. Time-weighted return is derived by geometrically linking quarterly returns calculated using the Modified Dietz method. The Index is an investment-
weighted pooled index. Under the frozen methodology, previously published quarters do not change after publication. The index included 863 funds with total 
capitalization of $1.3 trillion as of March 31, 2026. 
Preqin Global Real Estate Debt Closed-End Fund Index measures the performance of global closed-end, non-listed private capital funds that Preqin classified as real 
estate debt. The Index is designed to measure private capital performance on a net basis from the perspective of limited partners. Time-weighted return is derived 
by geometrically linking quarterly returns calculated using the Modified Dietz method. The Index is an investment-weighted pooled index. Under the frozen 
methodology, previously published quarters do not change after publication. The index included 137 funds with total capitalization of $0.2 trillion as of March 31, 
2026.
