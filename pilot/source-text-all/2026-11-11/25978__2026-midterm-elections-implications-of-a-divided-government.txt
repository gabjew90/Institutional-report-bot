Headlines 
 
 
Democrats have won majorities in both the House and Senate following the 
November 3rd midterm election – while not all races have been formally 
called yet, Democratic control of both chambers is now effectively 
assured. Democrats also flipped several Governorships, most notably in 
Texas.  
 
 
The result cements a gridlocked government for the next two years. 
President Trump and the Republicans remain in control of the executive 
branch. While Democrats have majorities in the House and Senate, they 
are not projected to hold enough Senate seats to override presidential 
vetoes. 
 
 
One of the most likely consequences of the gridlocked government is an 
extended debt ceiling standoff that goes down to the deadline, similar to 
the 2023 episode. The stakes, however, are higher this time, given the 
recent rise in bond yields and term premia. Growing U.S fiscal concerns 
could be exacerbated by another extended debt ceiling standoff. 
 
 
While Democrats are likely to use their congressional majorities to 
advocate for healthcare affordability measures and protections for the 
social safety net, gridlocked government and the presidential veto mean 
that major legislative changes to taxes and spending remain unlikely. 
 
 
The rapid increase in data center construction nationwide was widely 
cited as a top topic for voters. State-level election wins could now allow 
Democrats to enact limits and oversight on new data center construction, 
potentially slowing the pace of AI-driven capex that has been a strong 
driver of recent economic growth.

A gridlocked government 
For the last two years, Republicans have enjoyed 
majorities in both chambers of the legislature 
alongside control of the executive branch, easing 
the pathway to enact legislation.  
 
Now, Democrats swept majorities in both the House 
and the Senate in the midterm elections. This ends 
the single-party control of the legislative and 
executive branches. While Democrats will be able to 
unilaterally 
advance 
legislation 
through 
the 
legislative branch, they do not have the votes to 
overcome the presidential veto power (which 
requires a two-third’s vote in both the House and the 
Senate – Democrats will fall well short of the 
required seats in both chambers). All legislation will 
thus require some amount of bipartisan negotiation 
between the Democrat-controlled legislature and 
the Republican presidency. 
 
Democrats also fell a few seats short of a filibuster-
proof majority in the Senate. While Democrats could 
use reconciliation to mandatory spending and debt 
ceiling bills while bypassing the filibuster, they would 
still end up contending with the presidential veto. 
Bipartisan negotiation in the Senate is likely to be 
required in addition to negotiation with the 
President. 
 
Despite these constraints, Democrats are likely to 
use their congressional majorities to advocate for 
policies 
focused 
on 
healthcare 
affordability, 
strengthening the social safety net, and reducing 
the impact of spending cuts enacted in recent fiscal 
legislation. Priorities are likely to include reversing 
some reductions to Medicaid and other healthcare-
related subsidies, extending support for lower-
income households, and preserving elements of the 
broader social safety net. While Democrats may 
also seek to reverse selected provisions of the One 
Big Beautiful Bill Act, significant tax changes appear 
unlikely 
given 
divided 
government 
and 
the 
presidential veto. 
 
There are a few very likely and relevant economic 
consequences of such gridlock and the broader 
outcomes of the midterm elections. Debt ceiling 
negotiations, future budget bills, the usage of 
executive order and tariff powers by the President, 
and the regulatory environment for AI and data 
center construction all could be impacted. 
 
Divided government may also have implications for 
future Federal Reserve appointments. Democratic 
control of the Senate preserves a meaningful role in 
confirming future nominees to the Board of 
Governors and other senior Federal Reserve 
positions. This could limit the scope for rapid 
changes in the central bank's leadership while 
having little direct effect on near-term monetary 
policy. 
Negotiating the debt ceiling 
The debt ceiling is likely to become binding 
sometime in the first half of 2027, and a divided 
government would likely make negotiations more 
contentious, increasing the risk of prolonged 
brinkmanship. Once the limit is reached, the 
Treasury would rely on its cash balance and 
extraordinary measures—temporary accounting 
steps that create additional borrowing capacity—
to continue meeting federal obligations while 
negotiations to raise the debt ceiling proceed. 
Once lawmakers reach an agreement, the Treasury 
would need to issue a large volume of securities to 
replenish the Treasury General Account (the federal 
government’s checking account at the Federal 
Reserve). This wave of debt issuance could 
temporarily pull liquidity from financial markets, 
place upward pressure on short-term funding rates, 
and increase Treasury market volatility.

The X-Date Effect 
The 2023 debt ceiling episode demonstrated that 
investors actively repositioned away from Treasury 
bills maturing near the projected X-date, the date at 
which the Treasury will no longer be able to employ 
extraordinary measures to fund the government. 
During the 2023 debt ceiling episode, the X-date 
was projected at the start of June 2023. Investors 
rotated away from Treasury Bills expiring around 
that date, driving yields on the affected bills sharply 
higher than those of bills expiring later that month. 
 
Figure 4: Front-end treasury bills during the 2023 
crisis 
 
 
This time could be different for 
longer-dated treasury yields 
Historically, down-to-the-wire debt ceiling episodes 
have pushed longer-dated U.S. Treasury yields lower 
as investors seek safe assets. This time, however, the 
response could be different. Treasury yields have 
risen sharply over the past year, in part due to an 
increase in term premia. Against this backdrop, 
another prolonged debt ceiling standoff could 
intensify concerns about the U.S. fiscal outlook and 
place upward pressure on longer-dated yields.

Could the debt ceiling be lifted 
early? 
The new legislature does not get seated until after 
New Year’s Day. In the lame duck period between 
now and then, President Trump could ask 
Republicans to raise the debt ceiling early while 
they still have majorities in the legislature.  
Reconciliation can be used to pass debt ceiling bills 
while avoiding the Senate filibuster, and thus is an 
option open to the lame duck Republican-held 
Senate. As Democrats are likely to use debt ceiling 
negotiations in 2027 as a cudgel to extract 
concessions from President Trump, there’s a risk 
that Republicans instead resolve the debt ceiling 
early. This would be very atypical for Congress but 
is certainly possible. There are no economic 
downsides in our view to raising the debt ceiling

Passing a new budget 
The divided government may make it difficult for 
Congress to pass all 12 appropriations bills needed 
to fund the government for the next few fiscal years. 
Without a discretionary budget in place for each 
fiscal year, the federal government experiences a 
shutdown. The deadline to avoid a government 
shutdown for FY 2027 is December 11th, 2026 (this is 
before the new Congress gets seated), while 
another bill will be required come September 30th, 
2027 for FY 2028. Political gridlock brought about by 
a divided legislature and executive branch could 
result in increased usage of continuing resolutions to 
avoid government shutdowns. 
Continuing 
resolutions 
typically 
maintain 
discretionary funding at existing nominal levels, 
ignoring the effects of inflation. If maintained for an 
extended period, this could constrain inflation-
adjusted federal spending and modestly reduce its 
contribution to economic growth. 
With Democrats in control of the legislative agenda, 
they may seek concessions on spending priorities, 
tax policy, defense funding, foreign aid, and other 
issues during negotiations over future budget bills 
(to avoid a shutdown) and the debt ceiling. 
Any funding measure would require President 
Trump’s signature, giving both parties negotiating 
leverage into deadline dates. A more prolonged and 
contentious process could increase the possibility of 
temporary government shutdowns near budget bill 
deadlines. 
Congress and the White House may ultimately 
reach 
an 
agreement 
before 
any 
prolonged 
disruption occurs, although negotiations could still 
produce periods of elevated policy uncertainty 
Such uncertainty can weigh on business and 
household 
confidence, 
potentially 
delaying 
investment 
and 
major 
purchases. 
Short-term 
Treasury markets could also experience greater 
volatility if investors become concerned about 
policymakers’ ability to address fiscal deadlines in a 
timely manner.

Republicans may attempt to pass a debt ceiling 
raise or a full FY 2027 budget bill during the lame 
duck session of Congress to avoid having to 
negotiate with Democrats to enact such legislation 
in 2027. Republicans cannot use reconciliation on 
the discretionary budget bill, so such an effort would 
still require Democrat votes. Assuming they get such 
votes, it likely would delay some of the impact of 
split legislative and executive bodies of government 
– if Republicans make this effort, we’d expect to see 
headlines on it in the aftermath of the midterms.

legislative agenda, increasing the likelihood that 
existing executive authorities—including tariffs—
are set to play a larger role in advancing fiscal policy 
objectives.

Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta in 2025 of nearly

following six months because of policy uncertainty, 
much of which reflected trade-policy concerns. 
 
The data center debate 
The rapid buildout of AI-related data center 
infrastructure has become an increasingly visible 
political issue and appears to have been a 
meaningful campaign issue in a number of Federal 
and State level elections across the country.  
 
Sweeping federal regulation of artificial intelligence 
appears unlikely under a divided government, 
particularly 
given 
the 
Trump 
administration’s 
emphasis on accelerating AI innovation and 
infrastructure development. However, Democratic 
wins in several state-level elections could increase 
regulatory scrutiny of data center development in 
some jurisdictions.  
 
Data centers were widely cited issues in the Texas, 
Kansas, Ohio, Georgia, Maine, Iowa and Wisconsin 
governor races. While Congress’ ability to materially 
slow the broader AI infrastructure buildout is limited,
