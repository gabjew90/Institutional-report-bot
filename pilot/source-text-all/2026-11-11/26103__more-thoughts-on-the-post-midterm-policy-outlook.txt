More thoughts on the post-midterm policy outlook 
◼ 
Despite the comprehensive victory by Democrats in the 2026 midterm 
elections, which resulted in larger-than-anticipated majorities in both 
chambers of Congress, we continue to expect a base case that largely 
involves legislative gridlock in Washington. This means no scope for 
large-scale progressive actions and limited room for any major fiscal 
packages, for example. 
◼ 
While Democrats may still try to force votes on some proposals that 
have little chance of passing, we think they will realistically push for 
more targeted proposals where they see some room for compromise or 
believe a deal is possible. We offer some further details below but think 
that priorities could include trying to roll back a subset of President 
Donald Trump’s tariffs and trying to reverse some spending cuts, 
mainly focused on the cuts to Medicaid in the OBBBA and the 
enhanced ACA tax credits that were previously allowed to expire. 
◼ 
For Democrats, the most prominent point of leverage they could exploit 
to try and push these priorities through would be the looming debt 
ceiling showdown, where Democrats could hold out for some 
concessions before agreeing to raise the ceiling. A government 
shutdown fight could also act as leverage, albeit to a lesser degree.  
◼ 
That said, Republicans could theoretically both extend government 
funding and raise the debt ceiling in the remaining time before the new 
Congress takes office on 3 January, which would limit the Democrats’ 
leverage, at least in the near term. It remains to be seen if this is 
possible, however, given a seemingly fractured caucus after the 
midterms. 
◼ 
Democrats are also expected to try to end the war in Iran, though this is 
not necessarily straightforward to accomplish, in our view, given 
President Trump’s veto power and his ability to execute many aspects 
of foreign policy without Congress. This does not mean that Trump 
himself cannot choose to pull back, but it may still be more down to 
Trump than Congress. 
◼ 
Regarding AI, we remain of the view outlined in our initial reactions. We 
are still sceptical that any major restrictions will emerge from 
Washington given that Trump remains staunchly pro-AI and can 
choose to veto any aggressive regulations. However, state and local 
governments are likely to throw up some additional roadblocks that 
may slow the buildout, though not stop it entirely. 
What concessions will Democrats try to extract? 
While some in the Democratic party would prefer sweeping reforms in the 
vein of ‘Medicare for all’ type proposals, the majority are well aware that the 
filibuster and presidential veto mean these are still dead in the water even 
after the comprehensive victory in last week’s elections. This will not 
necessarily prevent Democrats from forcing a vote on some proposals they know 
have little chance of passing, but realistically they will likely focus on more 
targeted proposals where they see some room for compromise or believe a 
deal is possible.

⚫ 
Rolling back a subset of Trump’s tariffs: we do not think that Democrats 
will attempt to overturn the entirety of new tariffs levied during Trump 2.0, 
and Trump would almost certainly fight back aggressively if they did, as he 
views tariffs as a core portion of his economic strategy. However, we do think 
that they would look to roll back a subset of tariffs, for example possibly 
focusing on tariffs on Canada, or even on broader North American tariffs as 
USMCA re-negotiation moves forward. 
We would also be interested in seeing if any Republicans start to defect from 
Trump more when it comes to tariffs following the heavy defeat in the 
midterms, as many Republicans have traditionally not been a fan of such 
trade restrictions. While not our base case, if enough Republicans decided 
they had had enough of tariffs, a bipartisan group in Congress could 
theoretically move to retake the tariff powers previously delegated to the 
executive, though gaining two-thirds majorities in both chambers of Congress 
to override a potential veto would be a high bar. 
⚫ 
Reversing cuts to Medicaid in the OBBBA: Trump’s Big, Beautiful Bill 
contained provisions that would cut spending on Medicaid by an estimated 
roughly USD1trn over the next 10 years (Medicaid spending is still projected 
to grow over this time, just at a slower rate). One key provision was the 
addition of work requirements that would mandate non-disabled adults to 
document at least 80 hours per month of work, education or community 
service to be eligible for coverage. Democrats were strongly opposed to this 
but could not do anything at the time as the bill was passed by a Republican 
Congress using the reconciliation process and will likely attempt to reverse 
these cuts. 
⚫ 
Restoring enhanced ACA tax credits: the federal government provided 
enhanced subsidies for people buying health insurance coverage through 
Affordable Care Act exchanges as part of the emergency response to Covid 
in 2021, and these were extended through 2025 as part of former President 
Joe Biden’s Inflation Reduction Act. The Trump administration allowed them 
to expire, however, refusing to include an extension in the OBBBA or any 
other legislation, resulting in spiking insurance costs for many. Democrats 
were again vehemently opposed, and restoring the subsidies will likely be 
among their main priorities once the new Congress takes office. 
What leverage will Democrats have? 
Though Democrats do have some clear priorities, it remains to be seen how 
they will go about accomplishing these given the constraints highlighted 
earlier in the forms of the filibuster and presidential veto. To overcome 
these, Democrats will likely need to find some form of leverage that allows them 
to negotiate a deal and extract concessions. 
Here, we see two main points of leverage, the first likely providing stronger 
leverage than the second: 
⚫ 
The debt ceiling: the US last raised the debt ceiling in 2025, when Congress 
set a new limit of USD41.1trn. However, federal debt has continued to grow 
since then and is now projected to hit the new limit between the late winter 
and mid-summer of 2027, according to an analysis from the Bipartisan Policy 
Centre this summer. While extraordinary limits would extend the arrival of the 
x-date for a bit longer, for another six to nine months according to the same 
analysis, the debt ceiling will eventually need to be raised once again. 
With Democrats in charge of Congress starting 3 January, their support will 
be necessary for any 2027 legislation raising the debt ceiling. As it is a 100% 
necessity that Congress raises the debt ceiling, given that failure to do so 
would result in default, Democrats could hold out on agreeing to any debt 
ceiling legislation that did not include some of their key priorities, providing a 
key point of leverage. 
⚫ 
Government shutdown: the periodic fights over government funding, which 
occasionally result in shutdowns, would also provide a point of leverage that 
Democrats could try to exploit, tying some of their key priorities to a 
government funding bill. We think this would provide a smaller degree of 
leverage than the debt ceiling, as a government shutdown is less

consequential than a possible default, but could nonetheless provide another 
avenue that the Democrats could try to exploit. 
That said, Republicans could theoretically both extend government funding 
and raise the debt ceiling in the remaining time before the new Congress 
takes office. Government funding is currently set to expire on 11 December, so 
this deadline will still have to be addressed by the current Congress controlled by 
Republicans. It remains to be seen whether anything other than a short-term 
continuing resolution to temporarily fund the government (without addressing the 
debt ceiling) is possible given a seemingly fractured caucus after midterms, but if 
Republicans were to somehow agree on a deal involving longer-term funding and 
an increase to the debt ceiling, then it would delay Democrats’ main source of 
leverage until farther down the line. 
How could the midterms impact the Iran war? 
Current Senate minority leader (and likely majority leader when the new 
Congress takes office) Chuck Schumer has pledged that Democrats will 
“end the war” as soon as possible in the wake of the midterm victory, 
though we remain somewhat sceptical about how easy this would be for 
Democrats to accomplish. We see two main avenues they could pursue, which 
we touch on below, including the limitations of each approach. 
First, Congress could pass a War Powers Resolution that would limit 
Trump’s ability to initiate or escalate military action without the consent of 
Congress. The issue here is that President Trump can veto a War Powers 
Resolution, just like any other legislation, so if Democrats wanted this to be 
binding, they would need to win the support of enough Republicans to gain the 
two-thirds majorities in both chambers of Congress needed to override a veto. 
While this cannot necessarily be entirely ruled out given the unpopularity of the 
war and the key role it had in driving the heavy defeat Republicans suffered in the 
midterms, it would not be straightforward to wrangle such supermajorities in both 
chambers. 
Second, Democrats could rely on the “power of the purse”, as Schumer 
pledged to do, essentially blocking funding for the Pentagon that would be 
needed to continue the war. Here, it would be easier to prevent any new 
funding from being devoted to defence, as this would require Congressional 
approval. 
However, Congress would not have as much ability to block funding that 
has already been appropriated, which in some cases can be repurposed 
within the department of defence, allowing President Trump to find funds to 
execute the war. As noted above, if Republicans are able to pass a government 
funding bill ahead of the 11 December deadline when current funding is set to 
expire, then it would delay leverage that Democrats have. 
That said, leaving aside what Congressional Democrats might be able to 
do, it is unclear which way Trump might prefer to go. There is a possibility 
that the shellacking Republicans took in the midterms finally made clear to Trump 
the unpopularity of the war, leading him to decide to pull back even without being 
forced by Congress, and his recent rhetoric has admittedly sounded a bit more 
conciliatory. However, we still see a behavioural risk that Trump could 
eventually lash out after the heavy midterm defeat, trying to be more 
assertive in areas where he has more leeway to act without Congress, 
which would include foreign policy and the possibility of re-escalation in 
Iran. 
What about the AI buildout? 
We will not add too much to our initial reactions, as we remain of the view 
that the AI buildout could be slowed a bit, but is unlikely to be entirely 
derailed. We continue to be sceptical of major AI regulations coming out of 
Washington as the veto from the very pro-AI President Trump still looms large 
despite the clear victories by Democrats in Congress. However, more AI-hostile 
Democratic governors who won several key states last week could easily throw 
up some roadblocks, for example to data centre construction. We do not think 
this will derail the AI buildout but could contribute to some delays or decisions to 
relocate certain projects to other states.
