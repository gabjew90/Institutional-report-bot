2026 HAS EXPOSED NEW RISKS FOR THE EUROPEAN GAS MARKET

Europe’s vulnerability to the gas market has changed in recent years. Whilst its reliance on imports has remained stable, 
its gas supply has been more vulnerable to global market conditions since 2022, due to a shift in the composition of its 
imports. Furthermore, in 2026, the combination of high geopolitical risk in one of the main production regions and the 
pressure of climate change on European consumers has created an unprecedented situation, leading to price pressures 
and significant uncertainty regarding the short-term outlook. Even if they ease, tensions in the European gas market could 
persist beyond the winter, largely due to the need to refill inventories.
Whilst Europe is not heavily reliant on gas imports from the Middle 
East, it has had to contend with a fresh surge in prices since the out-
break of war in Iran last February, a rise that has, in part, been passed 
on to electricity prices this summer. Furthermore, new sources of 
vulnerability, particularly those linked to climate change, are at play. 
These call for caution regarding the short-term outlook for the Euro-
pean gas market.
EUROPE’S GROWING EXPOSURE TO MARKET VOLATILITY
Unlike the 2022 energy crisis, which hit Europe directly, the war in 
Iran is indirectly affecting the European gas market. 80% of gas exports 
from the Middle East are destined for Asia. However, the growing inter-
connection between the two main gas-importing regions — Asia and 
Europe — has resulted in the crisis spreading to Europe. Since 2022, 
the transmission of shocks to liquefied natural gas (LNG) benchmark 
prices on the European and Asian markets (TTF and JKM) has increased 
significantly, in both directions1.
Despite a fall of around 20% in European gas consumption since 2022, 
the replacement of around 60% of Russian pipeline imports with pur-
chases of liquefied natural gas (see Chart 1) has kept Europe’s vulne-
rability at a high level. Given developments in the LNG market — 
growing interconnection between European and Asian markets and 
the importance of spot contracts (around one-third of European supply 
contracts2) — Europe finds itself, through no fault of its own, increa-
singly exposed to gas price volatility.
Furthermore, the increasing autonomy and flexibility of the global 
LNG market may exacerbate this volatility. The higher proportion of 
LNG sales contracts with flexible destinations3 (rising from 29% to 51% 
of the total between 2016 and 2025), linked to the massive influx of 
US LNG onto the market, together with the reduction in the share of 
contracts indexed to oil prices (from 70% to 55% over the same period), 
have made price formation on the LNG market more autonomous, the-
reby increasing the opportunities for arbitrage and the flexibility of this 
market.
MAJOR DISRUPTIONS IN THE LNG MARKET HAVE CONSTRAINED THE REFIL-
LING OF EUROPEAN INVENTORIES
The 85% reduction in LNG exports from Qatar and the UAE in Q2 and Q3 
2026 was offset by two-thirds through, on the one hand, an increase 
in exports from other producing countries (primarily the United States) 
and, on the other hand, a fall in demand in Asia and Europe (which 
is forecast to reach, respectively, 1.5% and 3% year-on-year in 2026, 
according to IEA forecasts4). Owing to the damage caused by the war to 
infrastructure (17% of Qatar’s production capacity is out of service) and 
logistical constraints specific to the LNG market (unavailability of LNG 
carriers), the LNG market has benefited only marginally from the re-
sumption of flows through the Strait of Hormuz since July. Against this 
backdrop of tensions in the gas market, high prices in Europe (up by 
around 174% since the outbreak of the conflict) have significantly 
slowed the refilling of European stocks. They stood at 75% of full capa-
city at the end of October, whilst the official target is 80% (see Chart 2).
OVER THE WINTER, DIVERGENT FACTORS COULD STABILISE PRICES AT A 
HIGH LEVEL
Gas stock levels that are below the target set by the European Commis-
sion do not increase the risk of a shortage on the European continent. 
Nevertheless, Europe’s vulnerability towards the end of winter, in the 
event of a cold snap, would force it to buy on the market at high prices.

Furthermore, from a technical perspective, exposure to the conse-
quences of a cold snap at the end of winter is all the more pronounced 
as the rate at which gas can be withdrawn from storage (the ease with 
which a country can access its supplies) decreases (due to lower stock 
levels in storage facilities).
Apart from stock levels at the start of winter, three factors will deter-
mine the situation at the end of the season, a key period of vulnera-
bility:
1/ the resumption of exports from the Gulf, a region that is essential for 
restoring market balance in the short term;
2/ weather conditions during the winter;
3/ renewable energy production.
According to the IEA, in the worst-case scenario (with exports from the 
Middle East only resuming from the end of the first quarter of 2027, a 
cold winter and low renewable energy production), stocks would stand 
at 7% of total capacity by the end of winter, compared with 33% in 
the best-case scenario. Such levels remain significantly below the ave-
rage for the last five years at this time of year: 80% in the worst-case 
scenario and 18% in the best-case scenario. This would underpin res-
tocking demand over the next two quarters, and therefore keep prices 
at a fairly high level. For the time being, a significant recovery in LNG 
exports from the Gulf remains highly uncertain, even though indirect 
talks between the warring parties have resumed. On a more positive 
note, the current development of a very powerful El Niño event should, 
in principle, be associated with relatively mild temperatures during the 
winter in western Europe. According to the futures markets, European 
gas prices are not expected to return to their pre-crisis levels until 
2028 (see Chart 3).
ARE WE HEADING FOR A SUMMER PEAK IN GAS DEMAND?
In addition to these factors, which are likely to prolong market tensions 
during the first half of 2027, the summer period could also be cha-
racterised by increased demand for gas. During the summer of 2026,

weather conditions hampered the generation of certain forms of re-
newable energy (heatwaves and droughts restricting electricity gene-
ration from hydroelectric5 and wind power sources). They also disrup-

fell by around 10% year-on-year between August and October6). Along-

are driving up demand for electricity, particularly for air conditioning. 
Against this backdrop, as a source of flexibility within the electricity 
mix, gas remains a key determinant of electricity prices in Europe7. 
In Q3 2026, whilst the decline in European gas consumption mainly 
affected industry, the residential sector and the commercial sector8, 
consumption by gas-fired power stations was 9% higher than in 2025.
STRENGTHENING EUROPEAN PROGRAMMES
In this context, and despite the progress made in decarbonising the 
European electricity mix, high gas prices have led to a rise in whole-
sale electricity prices in most European countries. Between July and 
September 2026, these prices were on average 54% higher than in the 
same period a year earlier.
Against this backdrop of widespread rises in energy prices, European go-
vernments have primarily introduced measures to support households’ 
purchasing power when buying fuel. Measures relating to gas supply 
remain fairly limited. The European Commission has extended the tar-
get for refilling European stocks to 80% (instead of 90%) for 2026. It has 
also urged Member States to extend the stock-refill season in order 
to smooth out purchase prices. Furthermore, the measures taken in 
2022 under the RePower EU programme have been maintained: redu-
cing dependence on Russian gas with the aim of a complete phase-out 
(pipeline and LNG) by the end of 2027; and pooling gas purchases at 
European level. The ‘decarbonisation and electrification’ component of 
this programme was strengthened in April 2026 by the Accelerate EU 
programme, notably through measures to accelerate the electrification 
of demand and the development of electricity grid infrastructure at 
European level.

At national level, it is worth highlighting the price cap mechanism for 
gas purchased by power stations, which the Italian government is set 
to introduce. In 2025, gas set the price of electricity in Italy in 73% of 
cases (compared with an average of 40% across Europe). This price cap 
mechanism, similar to that introduced in Spain in 2022, is, in principle, 
only sustainable in the short term. Indeed, its cost is likely to be signi-
ficant given the Italian electricity mix’s high dependence on gas.

risk for European electricity generation are altering the vulnerabilities 
associated with the continent’s gas market. The sources of risk are 
increasing in number and becoming harder to manage. This calls for 
the continuation and strengthening of decarbonisation efforts in the 
European policies launched in 2022.

ADVANCED ECONOMIES
In brief. The week was dominated by large market swings triggered by 
the US midterms results. In the end, energy prices end the week much 
lower, bond yields somewhat lower and stock prices down in tech-heavy 
markets and slightly up elsewhere, notably in Europe. Incoming data 
confirmed a picture of resilient growth overall. The Bank of England 
hiked its policy rate by 25 bps and signaled it could do more. Market 
pressure on France eased somewhat.
EUROZONE
Energy pushes inflation up, but underlying pressures remain rela-
tively contained. Headline inflation rose to 4.0% y/y in October, its 
highest in three years, strengthening the case for another ECB rate 
increase in December. Energy remained the main driver, while core in-
flation rose by 2.6%. The ECB wage tracker points to negotiated wage 
growth slowing to 2.3% by September 2027. Meanwhile, retail sales fell 
0.7% m/m in September and the average interest rate on new business 
loans increased 13bp to 3.90%.
To watch next week: September trade balance, Q3 GDP second esti-
mate and employment (Friday).
France
EVs boost activity, market pressure eases somewhat. After already 
bolstering household consumption in Q3, new car registrations rose 
7% y/y in October (+4% y/y YTD), driven by another surge in electric 
car sales (+81% y/y in October; +78% y/y YTD). In parallel, manufac-
turing output rebounded by 0.5% m/m in September, supported by 
the automotive sector (+7.5% m/m but -5.5% y/y) and capital goods 
(+2.6%  m/m and +4.8% y/y). However, industrial production fell by 
0.5% m/m (and 1.3% y/y), as a result of a decline in electricity produc-
tion (-4.2% m/m). The composite October PMI was revised down by 
1.8 points from the flash estimate to 46.6 (-4.5 m/m), dragged down by 
services. This sharp downgrade likely reflects the hit to sentiment from 
the October 17 riots; however, precedents suggest activity itself should 
not be visibly impacted. Appropriately indifferent to the meandering 
journey of the draft budget through Parliament, the OAT-bund spread 
narrowed to 155 bps (-10 bps in one week) amid a broad-based decline 
in government bond yields across advanced economies; Thursday’s 
long-term debt auction, the last of the year, was well-subscribed. Stri-
ving to meet its deficit target of 5.4% of GDP in 2026, the government 
announced EUR3.8bn of spending cuts to offset EUR3.7bn in additional 
expenditure. The High Council for Public Finance nevertheless sees the 
deficit reaching 5.5%. The 2027 budget will start being discussed by 
the Senate from next week, in its original version, after the national 
Assembly unanimously rejected its amended version that would have 
led to a 5.9% deficit.
To watch next week: Q3 unemployment rate and the Banque de France 
monthly business survey (Tuesday).
Germany
Industrial activity picks up. New orders rebounded 4.4% m/m in Sep-
tember and industrial production increased 0.7%, with the auto sec-
tor delivering particularly striking gains of 10.5% in orders and 17.1% 
in production. Mechanical-engineering orders also recovered (+8.3%), 
while pharmaceutical production increased by 8.0%. By contrast, large 
orders in aerospace, shipbuilding, rail and defence fell by 10.4%, while 
machinery and equipment production declined by 6.5%. October's com-
posite PMI was revised up to 53.4, signaling continued strong expan-
sion.
Italy
Services keep the economy above water as manufacturing slips back. 
The composite PMI eased to 50.8 in October, its lowest since May. 
Manufacturing returned to contraction at 49.4, its weakest reading 
since January, while services strengthened to 52.0 and recorded a fifth 
consecutive month of growth. Both sectors reported increasing input-
cost and output-price inflation.
UNITED KINGDOM
The BoE hikes and leaves the door open to more. The Bank of England 
raised Bank Rate by 25bp to 4%, its first increase since August 2023, in a 
more hawkish than expected 8-1 vote, with Catherine Mann favouring 
a 50bp hike. The November Monetary Policy Report sees CPI inflation 
peaking around 5% y/y by mid-2027 and upgraded Q4 2026 growth to 
2.2%, while cutting the 2027 and 2028 growth projections. The com-
posite PMI meanwhile improved to 52.9, its highest since February, 
with new business growing at its strongest pace in nine months and 
private-sector employment increasing for the first time in 25 months.
To watch next week: Q3 GDP, business investment, industrial produc-
tion (Thursday).
UNITED STATES
Post election market jitters and a hot jobs report. The Democrats’ 
unexpectedly sweeping victory in the midterms (the largest wave 
since 1974) initially led markets to rally on (i) relief that the President 
recognised the results, and (ii) hopes of a swift end to the Iran war 
(oil prices and bond yields down, stock prices up). But this was soon 
followed by a sharp equity sell-off on 5 November, with the S&P 500 
down 4.4% and the Nasdaq down 5.0%, erasing USD2.7 Trillion of mar-
ket capitalization in one day. Why? Because of the realization that vo-
ters strongly supported AI-restrictive measures on the ballot (46 out 
of 47) and candidates — for Congress and Governor seats — in favour 
of curtailing data centers buildout. By the week-end, however, senti-
ment had turned around again, helped in part by a much stronger than 
expected jobs report: payrolls increased by 165,000 in October against 
a 70,000 consensus, while the previous two months were revised hi-
gher and unemployment edged down to 4.1%. Average hourly earnings 
rose 0.4% m/m, their strongest monthly pace since November 2025. 
Manufacturing activity also strengthened, with the ISM rising to 55.5, 
and the non-manufacturing index easing to a still buoyant 54.5.
To watch next week: October CPI (Tuesday); October PPI (Friday).
JAPAN
Wage growth accelerates. Scheduled contractual earnings increased 
3.7% y/y in September and total wage growth accelerated to 4.6%, 
helped by strong bonus payments. Real wage growth remained posi-
tive at 2.2% y/y.
EMERGING ECONOMIES
Portfolio outflows. According to IIF data, non-resident portfolio invest-
ment flows recorded USD33.8bn of outflows in October, the largest 
monthly outflow since March and up from USD19.3bn in September. 
Equities accounted for USD31.7bn, led by USD28.3bn from emerging 
Asia, as doubts over AI-investment returns and profit-taking hit North 
Asian markets particularly hard. Taiwan and South Korea suffered a 
further sharp market correction in the first week of November.

ASIA
PMI: expanding except for the Philippines. The ASEAN PMI rose to 53.1 
in October from 52.1, pointing to faster expansion. India remained ro-
bust despite a slight decline (54.7). In Taiwan and South Korea, the 
manufacturing PMIs declined but remained expansionary, at 54.9 and 
51.3, respectively. Indonesia’s real GDP growth accelerated to 5.5% y/y 
in Q3 2026 from 5.3% in Q2 marking its fastest pace since 2013.
China
The PBOC steps in as equity correction deepens. The central bank in-
creased its use of liquidity tools designed to support equity purchases, 
including the special relending facility for share buybacks and stake in-
creases and a third round of the Securities, Funds and Insurance Com-
panies Swap Facility, for which 31 institutions applied for more than 
RMB100bn. The measures follow heavy stock-market losses between 
June and October. The PBOC also raised the reserve-requirement ratio 
on foreign-currency deposits to 6% from 4%, effective 15 November, in 
an effort to slow the renminbi's appreciation.
Weak domestic inflation pressure: In October, CPI inflation slowed to 
0.7% y/y from 0.9% in September. Fuel prices increased by 20.2% y/y, 
food prices fell -2.9% and core inflation slowed to 0.8% y/y from 1.0%. 
Producer price inflation continued to rise (reaching 5.7% vs. 5.0% in 
September), principally driven by higher energy and electronics prices.
To watch next week: External-trade data (Tuesday).
EMEA
Central Europe
PMIs expansionary except in Romania. Poland’s PMI hit the 50-mark 
for the first time since April 2025 thanks to export orders. PMI was 
also higher in Czech Rep. (55), supported by new orders, output and 
employment. Hungary’s PMI fell marginally to 53.1.
Poland and Czech Republic central banks hold and hike respectively, 
as expected.
Gulf
PMIs resilient except Qatar (deteriorating further to 46.3).
Saudi Arabia: Massive budget deficit in sight. Saudi Arabia recorded a 
budget deficit of USD22.5bn in Q3, narrowing by 5% y/y. But over the 
first nine months of 2026, budget deficit reached USD65.1bn, up from 
USD48bn in 2025, despite a rather good revenue performance (+2.8% 
total; +7.8% for non-oil revenues; -1.3% for oil revenues). The 8% rise in 
spending in Q1-Q3 partly due to the war will make it difficult to meet 
the 2026 revised target (deficit of 4.9% of GDP).
LATIN AMERICA
PMIs remain weak across the region, with only Colombia in expansion.
Brazil’s central bank pauses rate cuts, whereas Mexico’s central bank 
cuts rates whilst signalling an upcoming pause. On 4 November, Bra-
zil’s central bank held its Selic rate at 13.75%, interrupting a series of 
five consecutive cuts, amid rising inflation (4.6% y/y in September, up 
from 4.2%), a sharp post-election real depreciation on fiscal concerns, 
and higher energy prices. The central bank also revised up its 2027 
inflation forecast to 4.7% (from 3.9%) and removed forward guidance 
hinting at further cuts. Mexico’s central bank unexpectedly cut its po-
licy rate by 25bp to 6.25% on 5 November. This decision came despite 
above-target inflation (3.5% against a 3% target), a peso depreciation 
of more than 9% against USD since September, and relatively strong Q3 
GDP growth (3.2% y/y). The central bank signaled that rates would now 
remain on hold for an extended period.
COMMODITIES
Geopolitical relief delivers a dramatic energy-price reversal. Mar-
kets have priced in the reduced risk of short-term conflict escalation 
through a sharp decline in prices. Beyond this, however, they lack di-
rection, wavering between positive signals (the resumption of indirect 
negotiations between the belligerents) and less favorable ones (in-
creased vessel damage in the strait). Brent dropped 7.6% over the week 
to USD96.8/bbl as markets priced a reduced risk of near-term conflict 
escalation. European gas prices fell almost in lockstep, declining 7.7% 
to EUR87.6/MWh. US commercial crude inventories continued to de-
cline, although more modestly than over the preceding four weeks. 
Diesel crack spread’s reaction to US elections has been much more 
limited than for crude. EU gas storage ended the refilling season at 75% 
of capacity (vs. 80% goal).
