Digesting the blue wave as inflation data looms 
Week ahead 
◼ 
We will have an inflation focused week coming up with October CPI, and 
to a lesser extent PPI, in the spotlight. Also on deck are existing home 
sales, jobless claims and several Fed speakers. 
◼ 
The September CPI report was not a great one for the Fed, though we 
look for some softening in October, the data will still likely be a bit firmer 
than would be ideal. Specifically, we look for headline CPI to rise 0.3% 
MoM, taking a clear step down from 0.6% MoM in September, with core 
CPI up around 0.26% MoM. For core, this would still be a bit firmer than 
the Fed would like to see, though would at least be down from 0.33% 
MoM last month, which we had seen as partially inflated by noise. YoY 
prints will not be available given the lack of October 2025 data due to last 
year’s government shutdown. 
◼ 
From the Fed’s perspective, we think a report in line with expectations 
would support our view of additional gradual tightening, including 
another 25bp hike in December, though PPI will also play a role due to 
its inputs for the Fed’s preferred PCE metric. We will watch these 
components closely, as the clear step down expected in the MoM prints 
for the main PPI aggregates is not guaranteed to translate into an 
equivalent move for the PCE relevant components. 
Week in review 
◼ 
Data was mostly solid ahead of the jobs report, though with little to 
change the big picture outlook or the Fed’s prospective rate path, 
midterm election results drew the most focus as markets digested the 
Democrats’ dominant night. While we and the consensus had expected 
a Democratic sweep, the margins of victory were larger than just about 
anyone had anticipated in a clear rebuke to President Donald Trump. 
◼ 
Despite the sweeping victory, however, we are not convinced it really 
changes much about the base case outlook for policy gridlock. Even if 
Democrats win the two remaining Senate seats to reach a net gain of 
nine, they would still be short of a filibuster-proof majority and would 
have to contend with the possibility of a Trump veto anyway. Thus, while 
there may be some room for compromise on certain smaller issues, 
which we will dig into in future articles, any major fiscal package, for 
example, may be a step too far. 
◼ 
There are also questions about the outlook for the Iran war. Democrats 
will certainly attempt to put an end to the conflict, but it is not clear they 
will be able to do so on their own given the President’s greater ability to 
conduct foreign policy without Congress. Trump’s initial rhetoric has 
sounded more conciliatory, but there is always a risk he could respond 
to the defeat by increasing his assertiveness with re-escalation still a 
possibility. We will explore this topic further in future articles as well. 
◼ 
On the AI front, we still do not think that many new regulations will 
emerge from Washington given the spectre of Trump’s veto, though we 
would note that the blue wave carried on down ballot with a number of 
key governorships changing hands in states like Texas and Ohio. With 
the new Democratic governors more hostile to data centre construction, 
local politics may offer some additional roadblocks that could delay, 
though we do not expect it to entirely derail, the buildout.

US Economic Outlook 
◼ 
Despite large amounts of noise created by the somewhat chaotic 
approach to policy shifts emanating from Washington, the US economy 
generally held up in 2025, even if the pace of growth slowed from 2024. 
Going forward, we think the policy backdrop may turn more constructive 
in 2026, helping to drive a similar pace of growth this year despite 
headwinds from the Iran war. 
Note: Any changes in the view from last week in italics 
Annual average growth came in at 2.3% in 2025, modestly above our estimate 
heading into the year. Moving forward, we look for a similar pace of 2.3% in 
2026 before a small step down to 2.1% in 2027. Still, we have taken 2026 growth 
down from 2.5% due to the Iran war, which should provide some drag, even if the 
US is less vulnerable than many others. 
Outside of the war, policy changes continue to play a key role in our outlook, 
as we think fiscal support from the Big, Beautiful Bill and a diminishing drag 
from trade policy & related uncertainty create a more constructive backdrop 
in 2026. Continued strength in AI-related investment and a potential boost to 
productivity could also provide some support, though we acknowledge there are 
risks from the more concentrated nature of recent growth, referred to as the 
K-shaped economy. 
With growth slowing but still solidly positive and net immigration dropping 
sharply, the labour market has cooled but not fallen off a cliff. We continue to 
see the unemployment rate’s 4.5% print last November as representing a peak and 
look for stabilisation in the low-4% range to persist through yearend and into 2027. 
However, the possibility of AI beginning to replace certain job functions could act 
as a headwind and contributes to only a slow grind lower following the peak. 
We had already expected inflation to struggle to return to 2% over our 
forecast horizon, and the spike in energy due to the Iran war adds further 
upside risks to forecasts that we have already revised higher. For now, we 
have headline CPI peaking at 4.2% YoY in May and remaining above 3.0% into 
early 2027, before dipping back to the low 2.0% YoY range starting in Q227. We 
see less of an impact on core but still have core hovering between about 2.6-2.8% 
YoY through early 2027 and only slowing to around 2.4% by end-2027. 
Given the resilience of the economy and stickiness of inflation, which has 
been more acute with regards to PCE inflation, the Fed delivered a hike in 
September, and we expect more to come. We see two additional 25bp hikes in 
December and March to take the upper bound to back 4.50%, reversing the late 
2025 insurance cuts. 
That said, the improvement in our inflation forecast that materialises in H227 
could limit the amount of total tightening delivered while also opening up 
some room for an eventual return to cuts, though we see only modest easing 
given that we have inflation stabilising slightly above 2% and believe the 
neutral rate has moved higher as well. Specifically, we project two 25bp cuts in 
Q427 to take the upper bound to 4.00%, before the Fed then pauses once again.

Week ahead 
◼ 
We will have an inflation focused week coming up with October CPI, and 
to a lesser extent PPI, in the spotlight. Also on deck are existing home 
sales, jobless claims and several Fed speakers in a holiday-shortened 
week. 
The September CPI report was not a great one for the Fed, though we look 
for some softening in October, the data will still likely be a bit firmer than 
would be ideal. Specifically, we look for headline CPI to rise 0.3% MoM, taking a 
clear step down from 0.6% MoM in September as energy prices should not surge 
to the same degree, with core CPI up around 0.26% MoM. For core, this would still 
be a bit firmer than the Fed would like to see, though would at least be down from 
0.33% MoM last month, which we had seen as partially inflated by noise. YoY prints 
will not be available given the lack of October 2025 data due to last year’s 
government shutdown. From the Fed’s perspective, we think a report in line with 
expectations would support our view of additional gradual tightening, including 
another 25bp hike in December. 
PPI was very strong last month, and we expect this could also soften in 
October, at least on a MoM basis. In this case, we have headline PPI up 0.3% 
MoM and core 0.2% MoM, each taking a clear step down from 1.3% MoM and 
0.7% MoM in September, respectively. On a YoY basis, the result would be a 
mixed bag with headline firming to 6.5% from 6.3% on unfavourable base effects 
but core edging down to 4.9% from 5.0%. As usual, we will be watching the PCE 
relevant components just as closely as the main aggregates. 
The housing sector continues to struggle despite the resilience of the overall 
economy, with existing home sales the weakest corner of the market due to 
the lock-in effect. This pattern is unlikely to change in October, as we expect a 
further decline of -4.0% MoM to take the unit-rate to 3.83m given fairly consistent 
declines in pending home sales in recent months as mortgage rates have risen 
further.

Week in review 
Data Review 
Ahead of the more important jobs report, which we will cover in a separate 
article, data was generally solid on the week. We see nothing that would stop 
the Fed from hiking a bit further at this point, though we continue to believe that 
inflation data will play a more crucial role than anything on this week’s calendar. 
On the labour market side of things, the September JOLTS report was a 
strong one, with job openings surprising well to the upside in jumping to 
7,523k from an upwardly revised 7,190k in August. This resulted in the 
vacancy-to-unemployment ratio edging up to 1.06x from 1.02x. The vacancy to 
unemployment ratio has now held above 1x for a sixth straight month, continuing 
to suggest a labour market that is close to balance (see Figure 2), and helping to 
leave the Fed’s focus squarely on the inflation side of the dual mandate. 
Outside of the headline job openings number, the quits rate bumped up to 2.1% 
from 1.9%, improving somewhat but remaining a bit below pre-Covid levels to 
suggest that people are still not all that confident they will be able to find a better 
job if they quit their current one. At the same time, the layoffs rate ticked down to 
1.0% from 1.1%, below pre-Covid levels as layoffs in the broader economy remain 
low. On balance, the data remains consistent with a low-hire, low-fire 
environment, albeit one in which hiring has stabilised since last autumn.

Other secondary jobs data remains consistent with this view as well, including 
initial jobless claims hovering at historically low levels, even declining further to 
189k this week from 195k prior. Continuing claims ticked up to 1,718k from 1,695k, 
though remain solidly down on a YoY basis. As well, Challenger job cuts came in 
at 44k in October, a relatively low level and down more than 70% YoY, adding to 
the evidence that layoffs in the broader economy have been limited. 
In terms of activity, the ISMs moved in opposite directions on the month but 
continue to point to solid activity overall. Specifically, ISM manufacturing rose 
further to 55.5 from 54.5 while ISM services edged modestly down to 54.5 from 
54.9. Still, both remain solidly in expansion (see Figure 3) as the economy has 
continued to grow despite the ongoing war in Iran. 
Lastly, nonfarm productivity surged 3.6% in Q326 helping to keep unit labour 
costs subdued at -0.7% on the quarter. These can admittedly be volatile and the 
YoY picture is not quite as bright with productivity at 2.1% and unit labour costs at 
2.4%, though the overall trend, especially in the most recent quarter, would 
support the Fed’s view that the labour market is not a major source of 
inflationary pressure at the moment.

Fed 
Fed speakers largely sound comfortable with a gradual approach to 
additional hikes, consistent with our current base case. This is not a universal 
view as October dissenter and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack reiterated 
that she prefers more frequent adjustment. However, Hammack does seem to be 
in a minority for now, with Richmond Fed President Thomas Barkin saying that a 
gradual pace “make sense” to him and even Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan, 
who we had thought might dissent in October, last Friday afternoon mentioning 
support for two more hikes but sounding comfortable with a more gradual approach 
that involves skipping meetings. 
Washington DC 
The Democrats delivered a dominant performance in the 2026 midterm 
elections, easily flipping control of both chambers of Congress. While a 
Democratic sweep was both our and the consensus base case, the margins of 
victory were larger than just about anyone had anticipated in a clear rebuke to 
President Donald Trump. 
Despite the sweeping victory, however, we are not convinced it really 
changes much about the base case outlook for policy gridlock. Even if 
Democrats win the two remaining Senate seats to reach a net gain of nine, they 
would still be short of a filibuster-proof majority and would have to contend with the 
possibility of a Trump veto anyway. Thus, while there may be some room for 
compromise on certain smaller issues, which we will dig into in future articles, any 
major fiscal package, for example, may be a step too far. 
That said, we are interested to see the reactions of Trump and other 
Republicans. For Trump, his initial remarks sounded fairly conciliatory, though 
there is always a chance that he could try to re-affirm his assertiveness in areas 
where he is able to act without Congress. For other Republicans, it will be 
interesting to see if some become more willing to cross Trump on certain issues, 
as the President’s major policy choices likely contributed to the comprehensive 
nature of the defeat. 
On the AI front, we still do not think that many new regulations will emerge 
from Washington given the spectre of Trump’s veto, though we would note that 
the blue wave carried on down ballot with a number of key governorships changing 
hands in states like Texas and Ohio. With the new Democratic governors more 
hostile to data centre construction, local politics may offer some additional 
roadblocks that could delay, though we do not expect it to entirely derail, the 
buildout. 
Looking ahead to 2028, we would highlight that two years is a long time, 
especially in politics, so we do not think that momentum will automatically 
carry over to tell us anything about the likely outcome in 2028. We only have 
to look to this year for an example, with the midterms offering a major shift 
compared to November 2024. For more details, please see our Economics Focus 
from earlier this week, covering our initial thoughts. 
Markets 
Fed pricing was not significantly changed on the week as of Thursday 
morning, after elections but ahead of the October jobs data. For December, 
the market still leans strongly towards a 25bp hike, pricing around 80% odds of 
such a move (see Figure 4), while farther down the line the market prices a total of 
about 60bp of additional tightening by next September, close to what was priced 
at the end of last week (see Figure 5). 
Other markets saw some larger moves post elections, though not all were 
sustained. Treasury yields moved lower, with the 2Y yield down about 3bp to 
4.56% from 4.59% and the 10Y yield down just over 6bp to 4.87% from 4.93% 
between last Friday’s close and late Thursday morning, though the decline from 
Tuesday’s close was a bit larger after a spike earlier in the week. The S&P 500 
was roughly flat from last Friday’s close through Thursday morning, though this 
masked a post-election jump on Wednesday that was substantially reversed in the 
earlier portion of Thursday.
