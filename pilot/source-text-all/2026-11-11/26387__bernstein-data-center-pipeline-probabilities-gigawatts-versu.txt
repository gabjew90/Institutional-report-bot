We published our first version of this analysis on June data, attempting to pull apart the
~600GW of U.S. data center pipeline and estimate what was likely to be built. We updated
the analysis for August, and are now refreshing for October. Excel version here.
In some ways, the data center landscape is evolving rapidly - we’ve had neocloud credit
shocks, election surprises, various lab commitments, and plenty of individual provider
moves. But in other ways, the landscape can’t move that fast. It takes 2-3 years to build a
data center - more in extremely tight markets - and plans aren’t changed on a whim. While
our absolute number of credible GWs continues to tick up with a growing project
pipeline, our completion rate continues to hover around 33-35%.
Our normal, full pipeline dataset casts a deliberately wide net across the North American
market: operating facilities, canceled projects, land banks, announcements, and projects
already under construction. But for this credibility analysis, we probability-weight the
remaining projects - we’re not giving full credit for press releases and PowerPoints. Our
project probability score reflects four things for each build: stage of development, tenant
quality, developer credibility, and state-level regulatory risk. Construction progress
carries the most weight—a facility with concrete poured is simply more credible than an
announced megabuild—while pre-leasing to an IG hyperscaler, a demonstrated ability to
secure power and capital, and a track record of actually delivering capacity all improve the
odds of completion and up our weighting.
Of the 586 facility GW of capacity currently in our October data, we anticipate that 34% of
the builds are credible (199GW). This is in line with our 33% June number and our 35% for
August. At an individual level, projects are (slowly) marching through the pipeline, raising
their credibility, while the top of the funnel (often at lower credibility) is also continuing to fill.
Major scoring changes to our last update include: upgrades to Crusoe’s capital score (4→5)
and TeraWulf’s power score (3→4), alongside downgrades to Nscale’s offtake score (5→4)
and Fermi America’s (3→2). On tenants, we reduced Oracle’s multiplier from 0.80 to 0.75
and the Oracle/OpenAI combination from 0.85 to 0.80, while raising another large buyer
from 0.60 to 0.65.
Notably, we haven’t made massive changes to our state-level scoring. More on that in our
election note. We were already discounting likelihoods in high-risk states (including TX)
and view most of the credible data center capacity as very likely to be built. While the cost
of an individual project may increase as additional grid upgrades or community concessions
are required, we do not believe construction will be canceled.
Our top stock picks in this space continue to include the most credible developers around:
EQIX ($1,270) and DLR (OP, $226), both of whom have strong footprints in major metros
and robust, diversified pipelines. In US Machinery & Electricals, our top picks are PWR (OP,
$804), ETN (OP, $534), HUBB (OP, $560), FPS (OP, $48), LGN (OP, $103), all of whom
we believe are well positioned to benefit from the short and long-term tailwinds.

DETAILS
In June, we published a detailed analysis trying to separate "credible developers from dudes with powerpoints" that implied
~1/3 of the pipeline capacity (from announced to construction) would actually be built. Our normal, full pipeline dataset
casts a deliberately wide net across the North American market: operating facilities, canceled projects, land banks,
announcements, and projects already under construction, capturing project-level details including provider, location,
construction status, estimated power, disclosed tenant, and other available development factors, with status validated monthly
through satellite imagery. But for the credibility analysis, we strip out what is already active and what is knowingly
dead, then probability-weight the remaining projects rather than treating every press release as a future build. The
probability score is project-specific and reflects four things: stage of development, tenant quality, developer credibility, and
state-level regulatory risk. Construction progress carries the most weight—a facility with concrete poured is simply more
credible than an announced megabuild—while pre-leasing to an IG hyperscaler, a demonstrated ability to secure power and
capital, and a track record of actually delivering capacity all improve the odds. For the full methodology, please see the bottom
of the note (Exhibit 32-Exhibit 36).
Of the 586GW of capacity currently in our October dataset, we anticipate that 34% of the builds are credible based on today's
data. Project Stage remains the most meaningful haircut—we consider physical construction progress to be a major indicator of
credibility. The tenant haircut is the next most pronounced: for a high-quality, IG tenant, certainty goes up; whereas for some of
the miners or longtail neoclouds, credibility is lower. The sponsor haircut is also meaningful, though less so than the prior two—
this is a fast-moving space, and we do not want to overpenalize new entrants who are showing clear signs of development and
tenant signing. Finally, we layer in a haircut for the location of the build. This was a relatively small cut in June; it is no longer. With
the November 3 elections, three of the largest data center states (TX, GA, OH) elected governors who campaigned on moratoria,
and we now haircut announced and land-banked capacity in eight states versus six in July. Our view is still not that most of those
projects will be canceled—they are more likely to be delayed.
Since August, our data center pipeline capacity estimates have been updated to reflect changes in tenant credit quality,
developer and project execution profiles, and state legislation.
On the tenant side, credit is bifurcating. Well funded sovereign and enterprise payers moved higher, including HUMAIN
following Aramco’s closing of its 49% stake, Lambda on a $1B IG rated term loan, and Cohere on a Series E with Canadian
government participation. Conversely, OpenAI was trimmed after cutting its funding round target to $20B from roughly $30B,
Oracle was reduced again following the Project Jupiter force majeure notice and Moody’s maintained negative outlook, and
TensorWave was cut after its Fermi lease missed closing. We also added ten tenants newly appearing in the project data, notably
Jane Street and Bloomberg as terminal enterprise payers.
On the company side, capital is getting easier, while offtake is getting harder. Crusoe, Sabey, and Serverfarm were
upgraded on new financing, TeraWulf and xAI on power milestones, and Alpha Compute on binding compute contracts. Nscale
was downgraded after Microsoft walked from its Loughton site and its Norway flagship slipped, Fermi America after the
TensorWave lease failed to close, and PNK Group after Union County, PA halted its substation build.
The hyperscalers remain at the top of the scale despite Amazon’s withdrawal from a 427 acre Ashburn project, S&P’s
negative outlook on Meta, and Microsoft’s Loughton exit. These single site decisions do not alter hyperscaler credit.
On the regulatory side, we had already incorporated governor actions in TX, PA, and NY, while this update reflects the
election. TX and PA move to our 0.70 floor, reflecting Abbott’s ERCOT and TCEQ pauses and Hinojosa’s pledged statewide
moratorium in TX, and Shapiro’s EO 2026 05 making grid commitments binding in PA. GA sees the largest single move,
driven by Keisha Lance Bottoms’ moratorium platform and Democratic control of the PSC, followed by OH on municipal bans
passing in 14 of 18 localities and Amy Acton’s conditional moratorium pledge, OR on Governor Kotek’s DEQ permit pause
through mid 2027, and IN as local restrictions now cover more than half of counties. MD and LA enter the haircut table for the
first time.
The key open question coming out of the update is whether Texas, already at our 0.70 floor, now warrants a tier below it.

development, all under construction, plus ~0.9GW live at Crusoe's Abilene campus).

PROJECTS BY STATE
~68% of forward credible pipeline capacity sits in the 13 states we've flagged as risky (~63% in the original ten). Texas is now
the single most penalized state, a ~12GW impact: it is the largest state in the pipeline by a wide margin (150GW database,
43GW credible, 22% of the credible total), and we cut the announced/land-bank multiplier to 0.70 after Abbott's grid-
interconnection and permit pauses and Governor-elect Hinojosa's pledge of a statewide moratorium until the 2027 session.
We would stress this is delay risk more than cancellation risk - the tax incentives are still on the books, 21GW is already under
construction and grandfathered, and the incoming administration has said it is not against data centers as a rule - but the "most
data center-friendly state" framing no longer holds. Virginia is second, a ~6GW impact. New York carries the same multiplier (a
30% haircut just for zip code), but has much less active development to be impacted (~1GW credible). Pennsylvania (~3GW) is
third, and Georgia and Ohio each took a ~2GW hit after their new governors also campaigned on moratoria.

TRACKING THE RISE IN BEHIND-THE-METER (BTM) POWER
Behind-the-meter growth continues. BTM projects added 22.5 GW to the pipeline in October (+11% M/M), taking the BTM
pipeline to 231 GW, up from 185 GW in August. A 9.6 GW nuclear-powered announcement in Utah (Valar Atomics' Project
Beehive) was the most notable contributor, followed by a 2.9 GW campus in Cameron Parish, Louisiana that we attribute to
xAI (permits were filed by Venture Global; the developer is unconfirmed). BTM projects accounted for 48% of the additions to
October's pipeline, above the 44% YTD average. Credible capacity has not kept pace, though: credible BTM rose only ~5 GW
to 47 GW since August, so the credible share of the BTM pipeline slipped from 23% to about a fifth (Exhibit 20). The 15.1 GW
of BTM capacity under construction remained essentially flat M/M (only ~0.2 GW broke ground in October). BTM projects also
remain an elevated proportion of stranded capacity (28% of cancelled, withdrawn and delayed capacity, up from 16% in May).
October's largest cancellation, the 4.0 GW DCIP De Soto campus in Florida, was a BTM project.

A DEEP DIVE INTO THE TOP U.S. MARKETS
The three market archetypes are essentially based on how developers and customers trade off latency vs. power availability
vs. cost—with urban markets optimizing for proximity, rural markets optimizing for scale and power, and secondary markets
sitting in between as an equilibrium zone. We see a clear gradient across data center market tiers: Major Metros are optimized
for latency-sensitive inference and enterprise colocation, with the highest pricing ($120–150/kW hyperscale, $160–230
colo), highest build costs ($12–15B per GW), tightest cap rates (≈4.5–6.5%), and the longest power delays (4–7 years), while
Minor Metros sit in the middle with slightly lower pricing, costs, and delays but higher yields; in contrast, Rural markets are
primarily focused on large-scale AI training, offering the lowest costs ($7–9B per GW), fastest power availability (1–3 years),
and cheapest hyperscale pricing ($70–120/kW), but also the highest cap rates (≈6.5–9%+) and minimal colocation presence—
leading to a broader industry shift where growth and new capacity increasingly migrate from constrained urban hubs to lower-
cost, power-rich rural regions.

STATE-LEVEL DETAIL
TEXAS
Texas changed governors but not much else, at least on data centers. Abbott already had us in a pause. His August audit and the
TCEQ permit hold were in place well before the vote. Permits kept trickling out until TCEQ shut down its automatic filing system,
which is partly why plenty of investors figured a re-elected Abbott would quietly ease off. Nobody expects that from Hinojosa.
She has promised a formal moratorium on approvals until the Legislature passes new protections. With a Democratic House
and a Republican Senate, "until the Legislature acts" could mean a while. Both candidates also wanted the sales-tax exemption
gone, so a repeal has supporters on both sides of the aisle. The honest read is that the pause just got longer and maybe deeper.

OHIO
Ohio's governor race was a non-event for data centers, since both candidates were anti. AEP Ohio's large-load tariff had already
cleared out the speculative queue last year. The action was local. Bans passed in at least ten towns, and on a quick read it looks
like ~3 GW of credible capacity got zoned out. Look closer at city limits and parcels, though, and the bans bind on a lot less:
• Piqua's charter amendment hits Meta's 1.1 GW campus, but Meta already has agreements in hand, so a legal fight is likely
• In Trenton, Prologis is caught. AWS's 1.5 GW campus sits outside city limits and hinges on a November 10 township hearing.
• Wilmington's vote hit other parcels, and EdgeConneX in Ashville is already zoned and building
All in, bans actually bind on ~1.1 GW of Ohio credible capacity.

They also leak, because the projects tend to move to the next town rather than leave the state. AWS has ~2.8 GW of nameplate
elsewhere in Ohio (Licking, Fayette and Union counties), roughly ten times what it lost in Sunbury. Meta has ~0.5 GW in Licking
and Wood, about half of Piqua. Prologis has nowhere obvious to go, which makes Trenton the closest thing to a true loss in the
state. The thing to watch is November 10. If St. Clair Township adopts its ban, AWS Trenton (~1 GW credible) moves into the
binding column and Ohio becomes a much more interesting story.

OUR METHODOLOGY
We start with our Data Center Pipeline Tracker, underpinned by data from Aterio. This includes 8,858 data center projects
across North America, each with a provider, location, status (validated monthly via satellite imagery), power estimates, tenant if
disclosed, and a host of other project factors (imperfect).
We first remove any of the operational facilities - those are functional today and not relevant for a pipeline analysis. We also
remove anything knowingly canceled or withdrawn, assuming no zombie projects. We're then left with builds somewhere
between land banked, announced, and under construction (we treat "delayed" as "announced"). This is the group we probability-
weight - a total of 5,697 projects.
We begin with a base probability for the construction stage. "Under construction" projects are much higher probability than
"land-banked" projects, where a developer buys land semi-speculatively. This is split by builder - for hyperscalers self-building
and for established retail or wholesale colocation providers, their probabilities are much higher. We assume they are more
practiced and more conservative about announcements and updates - they have more to lose by being wrong and less to gain
by grabbing headlines.

to a 4.9 credibility score. STACK is the case study in institutional backing translating to model trust — even its paper pipeline
gets the benefit of the doubt because it's built a reputation for delivering hyperscale-grade capacity on schedule. The flip
side is geography: Virginia is its center of gravity (4.5 GW credible), so it absorbs more of the state haircut than its peers.
• Vantage Data Centers — 7.2 GW credible (the execution story). Vantage rounds out the top three at 7.2 GW credible on
9.0 GW data set (80% survival), and it's the most execution-forward of the group: 3.6 GW is under construction — ~40% of
its book, the highest share among the large private developers, versus ~30% at QTS and ~20% at STACK — with Wisconsin
(the Port Washington campus) and Texas as its centers of gravity. It scores a near-perfect 4.9 on credibility and is the only
top-3 name with a marquee signed anchor outside the hyperscalers, the Oracle + OpenAI (Stargate) tenancy, which alone
accounts for 1.9 GW of credible capacity. Vantage is the clearest embodiment of the "dirt-in-motion beats paper" thesis: less
operating base than its peers, but the largest share of its pipeline genuinely under construction among the top-three private
developers.
There is a longtail of smaller providers who are keen to make big announcements, but three we'd consider particularly high-
risk:
• O'Leary Ventures — 15 GW announced, 3% survives. No developer better captures the announcement-fiction gap than
Kevin O'Leary's venture: 15.0 GW of data set— larger than QTS's entire book — of which the model credits just 489 MW, a
3.3% survival rate and the lowest credibility score in the entire database at 0.35 out of 5. Every single one of its 110 projects
is "announced only," nothing is under construction, and the portfolio is split evenly between Wonder Valley in Alberta (7.5 GW,
outside the core US demand corridors) and a second 7.5 GW "Stratos" concept in Box Elder County, Utah unveiled in April.
This is the model working exactly as designed: celebrity capital and a press-release pipeline get filtered down to a rounding
error, and O'Leary Ventures is the single largest source of "vaporware gigawatts" in the dataset.
• Joule Capital Partners — 11.7 GW announced, 9% survives. After O'Leary, Joule carries the largest low-credibility book
in the entire database: 11.7 GW of data set across 50 projects at a single Utah campus, all of it "announced" or "delayed"
— and the model credits just 1.1 GW, a 9% survival rate against a 2.05 credibility score. It's the archetypal capital-partner-
branded pipeline: a headline gigawatt figure impressive enough to anchor a fundraising deck, backed by a $60M Series
B, no visible construction progress in satellite imagery, no signed anchor tenants, and a credibility profile that discounts
roughly nine of every ten announced megawatts. Where the top three earn the model's trust through steel in the ground and
hyperscale leases, Joule earns its discount the same way most of the pipeline does — by being an announcement in search of
a project.
• Vermaland — 2.2 GW announced, 0% survives (withdrawn). Vermaland is the case study in why the model discounts
a press release so heavily - and the one we can now mark to market. It isn't actually a data-center developer, it's one of
Arizona's largest land bankers — 25,000+ acres accumulated since the mid-2000s and monetized by selling or leasing dirt
to others, the same playbook it ran with 16 solar sites where it never built a panel. Its entire 2.2 GW "pipeline" was a single
speculative Arizona concept — the $33B La Osa megasite near Eloy — where the plan was to sell or lease parcels and let
buyers build. With no construction authorization and every public comment opposed, Vermaland first cut the project 80%
- from 59 buildings and 3GW down to 11 buildings and 1GW - and on August 26 the Pinal County Board of Supervisors
rejected the rezoning outright, 4-1. All 11 projects now sit in our cancelled/withdrawn bucket, the credibility score is 0.40,
and the capacity drops out of the pipeline entirely - roughly what our prior 4% survival rate implied.
