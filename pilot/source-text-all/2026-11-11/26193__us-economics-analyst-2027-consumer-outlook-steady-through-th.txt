2027 Consumer Outlook: Steady Through the Shocks 
 
 
The US consumer has proven more resilient than looked likely when the Iran war began 
and energy prices spiked. We estimate that consumer spending will rise 2.5% in 2026 on 
a Q4/Q4 basis, reflecting continued solid services demand, a rebound in durables 
spending after tariff-driven price increases cooled demand in late 2025, and a 
temporary boost from election-related spending by nonprofits ahead of the midterms 
(Exhibit 1). 
 
As Exhibit 2 shows, this year’s resilience largely reflects strong consumption growth in 
Q2 and Q3, when boosts from larger-than-usual tax refunds introduced by the One Big 
Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) and positive wealth effects from equity market gains more 
than offset the drag from higher energy prices. Households also drew down savings to 
smooth through the energy shock, with the saving rate falling about 1.5pp since the start 
of the year. While consumption growth is likely to soften sequentially in 2026Q4 as the 
cashflow boost from tax refunds fades and the drag from high energy prices persists, we 
expect spending to remain solid in 2027, growing at a near-potential 2.2% pace on a 
Q4/Q4 basis.

Stronger Income Growth and Healthy Balance Sheets Will Support 
Spending in 2027 
The two key drivers of spending growth—real income growth and household wealth—
should both be supportive next year. 
On the income side, higher inflation not matched by an offsetting pickup in wage growth 
has made 2026 a challenging year, and we now estimate that real disposable income will 
rise just 1.4% this year on a Q4/Q4 basis. But we expect real income growth to pick up to 
2.3% in 2027. As Exhibit 3 shows, the acceleration mostly reflects a fading drag from 
inflation as the impulses from tariffs, energy prices, and AI-related prices gradually 
abate in our baseline. We also expect boosts from higher net interest income—since 
households are net lenders in aggregate—and from sizable 3.6% cost-of-living 
adjustments (COLAs) to Social Security and other transfer programs. We expect labor 
compensation to contribute about as much as it did this year, consistent with our view 
that the labor market will remain roughly in balance through 2027.

income as those in the top quintile. Third, nominal wage growth has slowed more sharply 
for lower-income households over the last two years, reflecting normalization after 
several years of outperformance driven by a historically tight labor market (Exhibit 4, 
right panel).

The Spending Outlook 
We conclude by summarizing our outlook for consumer spending. Combining our income 
and balance sheet forecasts, we expect solid real income gains to deliver a 2.2% 
increase in consumer spending in 2027 on a Q4/Q4 basis, a somewhat slower pace than 
the 2.5% we estimate for 2026 but still close to our estimate of potential. Our forecast is 
modestly above consensus expectations (Exhibit 10). 
We look for a softer start to the year. The drag on durable goods demand from higher 
interest rates should peak in Q2, and energy prices should remain elevated through Q1 in 
line with our commodity strategists’ forecasts. We also expect payback from the 
elevated election-related spending by nonprofits that regularly accompanies the 
electoral cycle: assuming this normalization plays out by Q1, we estimate a roughly 
0.2pp drag on annualized consumption growth at the start of the year. Spending growth 
should then pick up as lower inflation lifts real disposable income and as tax refunds and 
a likely partial reversal of last year’s benefit cuts turn the fiscal impulse modestly 
positive.
