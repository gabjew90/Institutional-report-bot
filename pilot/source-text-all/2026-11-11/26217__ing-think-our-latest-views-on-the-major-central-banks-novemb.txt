Our latest views on the major central
banks
Here’s our take on what could be next for the Federal Reserve, the
European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan and the
People's Bank of China over the coming months
Federal Reserve
Strong growth, low unemployment and above-target inflation prompted the Fed to hike rates
in September and signal that at least one additional rate increase is likely.
This was all the ammunition markets needed to price in at least three additional 25bp moves.
However, caution is creeping back into market pricing with officials skipping the October
Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting despite higher-than-expected inflation prints.
Instead, the near-uniform message is that the Fed is under no immediate pressure to hike
amid growing evidence that interest-rate-sensitive parts of the economy are cooling while
wage pressures look increasingly benign.
Additional Fed rate hikes will do nothing to ease tensions in the Middle East and likely do little
to cool the strong pipeline of tech investment. We think the Fed will hike once more, at the
December FOMC meeting, after which we expect a prolonged pause well into 2027.

European Central Bank
Another rate hike in December looks like a done deal, but looking beyond this year, the outlook
for the ECB is clearly less certain. In fact, two main factors will be crucial for the ECB in 2027.
One, the intensity and duration of the war in the Middle East and, with it, the trajectory of
energy prices. Two, the degree to which higher energy prices translate into broader
inflationary pressures. While the ECB expects these knock-on effects to materialise in 2027, so
far, actual underlying inflation has remained well-behaved.
In our base case scenario, we expect energy prices to fall in 2027, while weak final demand
limits companies' pricing power. This will keep the current inflation wave mainly an energy
price shock. Against this background, the ECB can keep its powder dry in 2027 and might even
consider a rate cut towards the end of the year.
Bank of England
We expect one more rate hike in February, after the BoE hiked rates for the first time since
2023 in early November. Yet we don't rule out a hike sooner in December, if the energy crisis
worsens. Inflation is headed above 4% and the Bank is worried that the crisis has now reached
sufficient depth and longevity to justify further action.
Still, there's little evidence so far of second-round effects. Food inflation is remarkably benign.
Our gauge of "energy intensive" inflation is actually down this year. And the jobs market is
sufficiently cool, meaning wage pressures are unlikely to build materially. For now, it's too
early for the Bank to let its guard down.
The data through winter and early spring will be crucial, particularly the April CPI report
released in May, which is heavily affected by annual price resets. If by then there are still
minimal signs of second-round effects, we think the Bank will feel comfortable turning more
dovish by the summer. Ultimately, this could enable the BoE to cut rates before the end of
2027.
Bank of Japan
BoJ policy normalisation will continue. In October, the BoJ reached a milestone: finally hitting
its 2% inflation target. That target had been set in 2013. The BoJ openly admits that real
interest rates are negative. The debate naturally moves on to how quickly the 1.25% policy
rate will be taken to neutral – commonly seen in the 1.75-2.00% area. We now look for 25bp
hikes at the December and March meetings as the quarterly tightening cycle continues.
Market expectations are a little more aggressive than our view, looking for a 2.00% policy rate
next summer. Our take is that 1.75% may be sufficient before government support measures
weigh on headline inflation from next April. At the same time, government pressure to avoid

People's Bank of China
The PBoC will remain in easing mode despite global tightening pressures. China’s economic
backdrop remains conducive to further policy easing. First, inflation remains very mild, likely to
end the year under 1%, well below the 2% target set at the Two Sessions. Second, growth has
moderated and become more unbalanced, raising risks for the economy. Domestic activity
data weakened to the worst levels since the pandemic, and could clearly benefit from further
policy support. Third, the labour market looks soft, with lower wage growth and high youth
unemployment.
The PBoC has preferred targeted easing measures rather than policy rate cuts. Bank net
interest margins hit record lows and global central banks shifted into rate hike mode in 2026.
We still see scope for both in 2027, especially if external demand starts to show signs of
weakening. We currently look for a 10bp rate cut in mid-2027.
