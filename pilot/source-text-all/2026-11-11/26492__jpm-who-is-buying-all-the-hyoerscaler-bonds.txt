With $232bn of hyperscaler issuance and $42bn of datacenter issuance in USD HG 
YTD, an increase of 160% y/y, investors are concerned over the prospects for demand 
for these bonds going forward given the expectation that issuance will not slow next 
year. One of the areas of particular focus has been insurance demand. We laid out our 
thesis, which boils down to ‘price (e.g. wider spreads) creates additional capacity’ in 
our Growing Pains report last summer. Recent insurance earnings reports give us 
some new clarity on this topic as many insurers have begun reporting their AI 
exposures on a standalone basis for the first time. In this report, we compile these new 
statistics as well as perform a separate ownership analysis which points to the notion 
that indeed insurers have remained somewhat underweight this segment YTD relative 
to asset managers. This is an important consideration in light of the pending surge in 
GPU financings, and we discuss relative value considerations for these as well.
•
For the 8 insurers equating to $1.8tr of General Account assets that provided 
new disclosures this quarter, we estimate AI-related assets (hyperscalers + 
data-centers for most, though a few included semis too) make up about 2.27% 
of their asset base. This is well below the 6.87% AI issuers represent in our 
JULI HG index. 
•
For the 6 hyperscalers, we additionally calculated bottoms-up ownership 
estimates using publicly disclosed holdings data: For the roughly 45% of par 
where we have holder data, we estimate that insurance owns 25% of this sample 
of hyperscaler bonds versus about 45% for the remainder of the HG market 
where we have equal visibility. For data-centers, insurance ownership is even 
lower and we estimate it to be 16%. In fact, insurance ownership of 
hyperscalers appears lower than any other HG sector currently.
•
Asset managers have done most of the AI bond buying so far and make up 53% 
of hyperscaler ownership and 79% of data-center ownership in the bond 
samples where we have visibility on ownership. ETFs own significantly more 
hyperscaler bonds than data-center bonds, likely a reflection of the latter’s 
144A for Life status.

The insurance community does not appear 
to be leaning into AI risk so far
Over the past few weeks, 25+ publicly traded insurers have reported earnings. New this 
quarter for 8 insurers equating to $1.8tr of General Account assets was additional 
disclosure in earnings presentations regarding their AI exposures. Athene also published 
an AI-Related Infrastructure presentation in November, while Global Atlantic published 
a Credit Investor presentation in September. We presume this is in response to the 
market fears in recent months that insurers are already ‘full’ on AI risk and, given the 
volatility of these assets are in turn running higher risk profiles than in the past. This 
isn’t the first time insurers have provided similar transparency, with recent episodes 
being Private Credit in 2025-2026, Commercial Real Estate circa 2022, and Energy 
circa 2015 being a few examples that come to mind. Below, we have compiled a 
snapshot of these disclosures. A few important caveats are in order: Only a handful of 
insurers have started providing this type of disclosure so far, though we feel this is a 
representative sample of the broader industry and includes both high beta and low beta 
insurers from a trading perspective. Second, the definition of AI/hyperscaler/datacenter 
risk is not perfectly uniform across these 8 insurers (for example, some include SPCX as 
a ‘hyperscaler’ and some don’t) and some expand their definition to Semiconductors too 
and some don’t. 
Overall, disclosed exposures appear manageable relative to insurers' broader investment 
portfolios, generally representing ~1%-3% of invested assets, with Corebridge, Athene, 
and Global Atlantic toward the higher end of the group. More importantly, management 
teams consistently emphasized the underlying credit characteristics rather than simply 
the size of the exposure. The data center/hyperscaler exposure is predominantly 
investment grade where ratings were disclosed, generally backed by large, highly rated 
counterparties or diversified tenant pools, and often benefit from structural protections 
including secured claims, conservative LTVs, lease-backed cash flows and financing 
maturities that precede or fully amortize within the underlying lease term. Management 
teams also emphasized limited speculative construction and neocloud exposure, 
suggesting the portfolios are better characterized as contractual infrastructure credit than 
a direct bet on continued AI spending. Life insurers continue to see this as a growth area 
for the firm, allowing for good risk adjusted returns with structural protections.

What’s next ? A relative value framework for GPU financings
In terms of the next stage of AI capex financing, we are likely to transition to a heavier 
mix of GPU financings in the coming months. The financings that have launched in 
recent weeks have weighed on spreads for the broader hyperscaler complex. Part of 
why we believe these financings are having an outsized impact on spreads is not 
only their size (a reported $103bn in total for the two currently in motion) but the 
private to public nature of these transactions which means the broader market is 
fearful of the relative value implications these may have for traditional senior 
unsecured hyperscaler risk once pricing details emerge to the rest of the market 
post-facto. In some ways, this dynamic is very similar to the interplay between data-
center bonds and hyperscaler bonds earlier this year. But there is one critical 
difference, in our view, which is the shorter tenor of the GPU financings (thus far, 
all appear to be shorter than 10 years). Inside 10 years, spread buyers can rotate across a 
broad range of spread products including ABS, CMBS, CLOs, agency/MBS, HY and 
Munis. This is a double-edged sword, in our view. On the one hand, every existing bond 
has an owner so clearly the addressable pool of ‘demand’ is significantly larger in 
the front-end than the long-end. Below, we show the amount of UST + USD Spread 
products at each maturity point from 1 year to 30 years and the amount of spread 
product between years 1 and 10 is $21.1tr vs just $4.5tr beyond 10 years, e.g. the 
front-end is nearly 5x larger than the long-end. However, the flipside of this 
dynamic is that the diversity of product at the front-end should entail a wider 
relative value hurdle for these GPU financings to the extent demand is fungible 
between FI products. Below, we show a snapshot of all relatively liquid products at 
each point along the curve, extracted from our Fixed Income Cross Product Relative 
Value Monitor: IG corporate spreads are currently 71bp in the front end, 89bp at 5yr and 
106bp at 10yr, versus ranges of roughly 33-190bp, 28-174bp and 39-542bp, 
respectively, across the other spread products shown in Figure 13The cros-product RV dynamic is radicaly diferent at front-end vs. long-end . But these alternatives 
largely disappear further out the curve. At 30yr, munis are essentially the only 
competing spread product at 90bp currently versus 121bp for IG corporates. In other 
words, at the 2yr point in Figure 14,Fixed-income cros product relative valuthere are 15x different products that GPU financings 
need to compete against so the odds of a relatively wider pricing outcome are arguably 
higher vs. at the 30yr point hyperscaler unsecured issuance is for all intents and 
purposes just competing against other IG corporates.
