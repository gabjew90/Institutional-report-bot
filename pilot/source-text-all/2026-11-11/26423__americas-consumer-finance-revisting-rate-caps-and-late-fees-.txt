Americas Consumer Finance: Revisting rate caps and late fees post 
midterms 
10 November 2026 | 6:20PM EST
Following the midterms, we’ve fielded a number of questions from investors 
over the potential risk to the card issuers from either interest rate caps or late 
fees given investors views that a Democrat-led congress could revisit prior late 
fee litigation or begin to explore new attempts to cap credit card interest rates. 
With card issuer loan yields at 27% on a core basis (adjusted for 0% balances), 
an interest cap would result in meaningful pressure on profitability at the 
issuers and could have major knock-on effects, including significantly restricting 
credit to the real economy. While the headwinds on a gross basis would be 
meaningful to the issuers (as disclosed below), there could be a handful of 
offsets including shrinking loan portfolios and accelerating capital return but we 
think the industry would likely push back meaningfully on a change of this 
magnitude (similar to late fees).  
While there is uncertainty regarding what level legislators may pursue and we 
take no view on the likelihood of any changes, we look at it under 3 scenarios 1) a 
10% cap (similar to presidential tweet in January), 2) an 18% cap which is the 
cap imposed on certain credit unions and 3) 36% (usury cap for service 
members), with the former two resulting in significant impacts to earnings, and 
likely large restrictions on credit supply.  While there is limited information on 
what a 36% cap would mean (given lack of disclosure), we estimate a more 
modest 2-3% impact to EPS, with BFH and SYF seeing the largest impact and a 
smaller impact at COF (and nil at AXP).   
For late fees, we note that any new rulemaking would likely face significant 
pushback from the industry (in-line with the prior litigation) and could look very 
different from the original proposal, which included an $8 cap on late fees. While 
there is the potential for a wide range of outcomes, for hypothetical purposes, if 
we assume late fees are reduced 20/35/50%, assuming minimal amounts of 
mitigants, it would result in an average EPS impact (on 2027E) to the 4 main card 
issuers of ~9%, with BFH (~22%) and SYF (~9%) seeing the largest impact while 
COF (~4%) would be lesser and AXP (~2%) would see a minimal impact. 
Potential interest rate caps would likely result in a contraction in credit: Credit 
card interest rates average over 17% across the 7 largest issuers (ranging from 
low-teens to mid-twenties) but include promo balances and transactors, and 
adjusting for transactor balances are closer to low twenties to low thirties. Therefore, 
any cap would have a meaningful impact on credit card profitability and it would 
restrict the amount of credit available given the hit to profitability. Just looking at

Within the potential migitants, we think the issuers could look to apply account fees,

shrink losses and with a smaller balance sheet would be able to return more capital.   
There have been unsuccessful attempts to cap interest rates in the past: We note 
that Sens. Sanders (I-VT) and Hawley (R-MO) had introduced legislation in 2025 to cap 
interest rates at 10% but it was not passed primarily due to concerns that it would result 
in a large contraction in the availability of credit. Additionally, it was reported that 
capping interest rates was looked at as part of the CARD ACT of 2009 but ultimately it 
didn’t happen given concerns over the impact on the availability of credit. 
A revisiting of late fee litigation would likely not come with the same level of 
reduction as the prior rulemaking: Along with interest rate caps, we’ve fielded 
questions from investors on the sensitivity to earnings from late fee caps. Prior late fee 
litigation had been centered around the CFPB finalizing a rule to reduce late fees 
charged by issues (with more than 1mn open accounts) from $41 to $8 which was 
without mitigants and represented a meaningful headwind to card issuers EPS (ex-AXP). 
The final rule was ultimately vacated by the U.S. District Court for the Northern District 
of Texas due to failure to allow card issuers to charge penalty fees reasonable and 
proportional to violations which it noted violated the CARD Act. Given this, any new 
litigation could require a narrower scope and result in a smaller impact to card issuer 
EPS.  
Hypothetical sensitivity points to ~5% - ~13% earnings downside to issuers: To 
hypothetically assess the potential EPS headwind to the card issuers from a reduction in 
late fees, we first assume 1) units of late fees assessed by taking late fee $s divided by a 
$38 avg fee (in-line with prior company commentary), 2) BFH / SYF have ~50% / ~75% 
of late fee revenue included in the RSA’s, 3) of that ~50 / ~75% we assume ~25% / ~50% 
is shared with partners (SYF has a higher mix given different RSA structure), 4) assume 
BFH / SYF are able to offset 20% of the impact with COF offsetting ~30% which could 
come in the form of further APR optimization or credit tightening. Next, we lay out 3 
scenarios where late fees are reduced by ~20% / ~35% / ~50% given the vacating of the 
prior rulemaking was due to the court’s view that the ~80% proposed reduction did not 
allow card issuers to charge penalty fees reasonable and proportional to violations. On 
average, in the 20% reduction scenario, we estimate a ~5% EPS headwind for the group, 
while in the ~35% reduction scenario we see a ~9% headwind for the group, and in the 
~50% reduction we estimate a ~13% headwind.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.
