The great reset 
◼ 
Global rates strategy: Iran peace hopes triggered oil/gas futures 
collapse, rippling through global front ends. Still, nine months of 
elevated energy prices keep hawkish bias intact for front-end meetings 
and limit the extent of a front-end rallies. 
◼ 
Durable Iran resolution would increase policy divergence across 
jurisdictions. Energy importers focus on second-round effects and 
activity data while key question for the US will be whether expansionary 
cycle continues despite lower fiscal impulse and AI capex slowdown. 
◼ 
Long-term real yields in G4 have room to rally further. 
◼ 
EUR rates strategy: the growth narrative continues to dominate near-
term direction and we turn slightly long duration, especially in the belly 
of the curve. We see value in 5-30Y steepeners. Key risk area is re-
escalation of the war in Iran. 
◼ 
We see room for normalisation in EUR rates volatility, supportive of 
front-end credit, Schatz invoice tighteners. Still, budget season in 
France and Belgium will keep a constant noise and the front-end rally 
potential remains limited. We continue to favour OAT/BTP credit curve 
steepeners vs Bunds.  
◼ 
France and Belgium face the greatest budget adoption uncertainties 
among EGB issuers. We include a summary of the current status of 2027 
budget adoption across all countries and the main risks each faces. 
◼ 
Eurozone inflation analysis: earlier this week, headline inflation 
surprised a bit to the upside at 4.0% YoY, namely from gas and 
electricity. Core reached 2.54% – consensus 2.5% – and should 
accelerate further, around 2.9-3.0% next year. 
◼ 
Eurozone inflation strategy: unsurprisingly, inflation swaps and 
inflation forwards declined following the dive in energy prices this week. 
The magnitude of the move looks very consistent. French inflation 
forwards in contrast increased, but this is all about the perspective of a 
higher Livret A ceiling in our view. 
◼ 
US inflation analysis: it now looks likely that US headline CPI will not 
exceed 3.7% YoY in the coming months, while we were expecting a peak 
around 3.9% in November just a few days ago. We will publish an 
updated US CPI path in the October CPI preview tomorrow.

Global rates strategy: with that out of the way… 
Hopes that the results of the US midterm elections will bring the war in Iran to an 
end triggered a sharp decline in oil and gas futures reverberating into global front-
end pricing. The relative magnitude of the move across jurisdictions does not 
particularly reflect the energy external balance (Figures 1 and 2). Jurisdictions 
whose central banks had a delayed start to monetary tightening, like the UK and 
Canada, outperformed as STIR markets removed premia that the local central 
banks would have had to play catch up. 
Fig 1. G6 3M STIR Futures (%) 
Fig 2. 1-week change of G6 STIR Futures (bp) 
 
 
Source: Crédit Agricole CIB 
Source: Crédit Agricole CIB 
Beyond the initial relief rally and the obvious risks on whether a true resolution of 
the conflict can be reached, the compounding effect of nine months of energy 
prices should leave the hawkish bias intact at least for the front meetings, and one 
hike over the upcoming quarter remains the base case for G4 jurisdictions. The 
STIR structure dynamics should further concentrate the correlation to energy 
prices to the very front end (with implied hikes past the next live meeting more as 
inertia), with the medium-term inflation / GDP tightrope taking over after a couple 
of quarters. 
If diplomatic progress indeed materialises, a much lower floor for energy prices 
amid tighter financial conditions should lead to more policy divergence. The focus 
of the energy importers would remain solely on second round effects and indirectly 
on activity gauges, with current EZ GDP growth and core inflation expectations 
not helping to build a case to embed cuts in the medium term. On the other hand, 
the main theme in the US should ultimately boil down to whether the current 
expansionary cycle can be sustained with a lower fiscal impulse and if other 
sectors can pick up in case of an AI capex slowdown.

With the sustainability of a higher policy rate now the central variable, we expect 
the risky asset / bond correlation to decrease as inflation or monetary policy 
shocks subside and monetary policy becomes more readable and less 
constrained. This supports further rally in long-term real yields in G4 rates, with 
two key caveats in USD rates: (1) the debt ceiling and the budget; and (2) the 
effect of Kevin Warsh’s taskforces on Federal Reserve policy going forward. 
As for longer parts of the curves, this remains supportive of steeper 5-30Y and 
10-30Y IRS curves (from both the lower terminal rates and the subsiding volatility). 
With regards to swap spreads, the picture is mixed. JGB and Bund swap spreads 
should tighten as the collateral excess theme combines with lower volatility and 
safe haven demand. High debt/GDP issuers should benefit from the lower volatility 
and lower pressure on the government to counteract the rise in energy prices. 
For the UST curve specifically, a divided government makes it harder to pass large 
fiscal packages like tax relief or targeted corporate support that would be funded 
via new debt issuance. On the other hand, the chances for a frugal budget are 
virtually zero, which means UST swap spreads should be driven by rates 
directionality, banks’ balance sheet space and the Treasury’s debt management 
programme rather than the credit aspect. This should prove a tailwind for UST 
swap spreads widener.

EUR rates strategy: some support 
The big picture 
We lean cautiously long EUR duration on the back of the energy price rally. 
We continue to think that the core inflation trajectory next year will prevent the 
ECB from helping prop up slightly below-trend growth, making it difficult to embed 
some easing, especially as current growth remains resilient. On the other hand 
there is room to reprice the front end slightly lower as energy prices collapse, while 
rising debt servicing costs negate part of the fiscal impulse and prevent a 
meaningful repricing higher for the 2Y1Y to 4Y1Y segment. All in all, the front end 
appears more or less anchored around current levels, with wages data one of the 
potential catalyst. This supports a lower rates volatility leading to duration 
extension.  
We see room for the belly to outperform in this environment, alongside a 
steepening of the 5-30Y IRS curve and a lower belly in the 2-5-10Y fly. There is 
some room for OATs to tighten further if hopes of a resolution of the war in Iran 
materialise, but the ongoing budget process and limited downside in the front end 
prevent too strong an outperformance. 
We see EGB spreads tightening slightly under the supportive environment. 
A material rally in short-end rates does not seem possible under current 
circumstances, and budget warfare in Belgium & France on top of Spanish 
elections prevents EGB spreads to fully benefit from a lower implied volatility. This 
is not the case for front end spreads, which are bound to benefit from Schatz ASW 
normalisation and a better-behaved front end.

2027 budget adoption: current status and remaining challenges 
Across EGB issuers, France and Belgium present the biggest obstacles to 2027 
budget adoption. In France, persistent social unrest and calls for public aid are 
complicating budget negotiations as the country heads toward next year's 
presidential election. Belgium’s political instability is delaying the implementation 
of fiscal stabilisation measures. By contrast, the outlook for other issuers appears 
more favourable; while some budgets’ final adoption remains pending, their fiscal 
trajectories show fewer complications. 
France: the question is how. This week, the National Assembly rejected the 
revenue part of the finance law. Because of this, the expenditure part was never 
debated in Parliament, and the government’s original text goes to the Senate. 
After the deadlines for the Senate vote, the government can use Article 49.3 
(allowing the government to force passage of a law without a vote) which risks a 
no-confidence motion, or wait for the 70-day deadline on 17 December and adopt 
the budget “by ordinance” (bypassing parliamentary procedure entirely), which 
has never been done under the Fifth Republic.  
Belgium: the question is when. Belgium’s political crisis deepened in early 
November when Prime Minister Bart De Wever tendered his resignation following 
the failure of budget negotiations. King Philippe is currently reviewing the 
resignation while consulting with coalition party leaders, with De Wever scheduled 
to meet with the King again tomorrow. Three outcomes are possible: the King 
could reject the resignation and allow talks to resume, keep it under review or 
accept it & trigger new elections. If negotiations remain deadlocked, Belgium may 
resort to “provisional twelfths”. This is a mechanism that divides the current year’s 
budget into twelve equal monthly allocations to serve as spending caps for the 
following year. However, this delays fiscal reforms and carries a cost to public 
finances. 
Italy: on track, waiting on ECOFIN. Italy’s Council of Ministers approved the 
EUR26bn 2027 budget bill on 28 October. The EU Economic and Financial Affairs 
Council will rule on 9 November regarding Italy’s EUR29bn Escape Clause 
deviation request. Committee hearings in the Chamber of Deputies began today, 
with amendments due by 17 November. Final adoption must occur by 
31 December to avoid provisional budgeting. 
Spain: no 2027 budget possible before the election. Prime Minister Pedro 
Sánchez called a general election for 29 November after Congress struck down 
his housing decrees. The budget has been rolled over since 2023, and with the 
Parliament dissolved, no budget can be sent to Congress during this legislature.  
Germany: final vote later this month. The core budget pencils EUR555bn total 
spending, about EUR31bn more than 2026. The budget committee’s final session 
(Bereinigungssitzung) is on 12 November, and changes to individual chapters are 
still possible there. The plenary debates the budget from 24 to 27 November. 
Netherlands: minority cabinet looking for support. It is still unsure whether the 
minority Jetten cabinet has enough votes for the proposed 2027 tax plan. The 
government has revised the initial plan by scrapping EUR1.5bn of tax increases 
hoping that this would help pass the bill. The House of Representatives votes on 
12 November, then the Senate.  
Portugal: first reading passed, 2027 budget with a surplus. Portugal’s budget 
cleared its first reading in late October, passing despite opposition from the far-
right Chega party, after the Socialist opposition abstained from voting. The budget 
projects a surplus of 0.1% of GDP for next year, with public debt forecast to decline 
to 84.5% of GDP in 2027 from 87.5% in the current year. 
Finland: budget to be officially adopted in December. The 2027 budget draft 
has been submitted to Parliament with a deficit of EUR12.3bn, so EUR1bn lower 
than what was budgeted for 2026. Parliament is expected to adopt the budget at 
a December plenary session. 
Austria: done. The Nationalrat adopted the 2027/28 double budget this summer. 
The 2027 headline deficit is expected to be at 3.5% of GDP vs 4.2% in 2026.

Inflation: ramifications of the energy price dive 
Eurozone inflation analysis  
A last look at October numbers 
Back to the preliminary inflation numbers for October, we highlight: 
⚫ 
The upside surprise in headline inflation at 4.0% YoY, mostly related to 
natural gas and electricity. The upside surprise vs consensus was +100bp in 
Italy, +20bp in France but -30bp in Germany. 
⚫ 
A modest upside surprise in core inflation as well, with core at 2.54% YoY, vs 
consensus at 2.5% 
Midterms results and Eurozone inflation 
With the rising hopes of de-escalation – rather than escalation – in the US-Iran 
conflict before and after the US midterm election, energy prices sharply corrected 
this week, although they remain at very elevated levels, see Figure 7. 
The decline since 30 September was: 
⚫ 
USD20bl for the first Brent future, of which USD5/bl from the monthly roll, so 
let’s retain USD15/bl 
⚫ 
EUR15/MWh for European natural gas 
⚫ 
EUR27/MWh for the German power contract for Q127 
These are material moves of any short-term inflation path. Of course, what matters 
is not the first future contract but rather the whole futures curve of these different 
energy products. For example, the May 27 Brent future contract, which roughly 
drives fuel inflation at the end-Q1 horizon, declined by USD12/bl and the 
corresponding natural gas contract lost around EUR10/MWh.  
Considering the sensitivities we use, these moves are consistent with headline 
inflation at mid-2027 horizon losing roughly 60bp: -30bp from direct effects from 
oil, -20bp from direct effects from gas (and power) and say -10 bp from undirect 
effects (core/food) which always take more time to materialise. So this would 
be -60bp overall.

From this perspective, the 50bp decline in the Eurozone inflation swap with mid-
2027 maturity – EUSWIF6 code in Bloomberg – since 30 October does not look 
excessive.

The 50bp decline may even look a bit shy vs our 60bp sum-up estimate, but we 
would also highlight that the result of the midterms was also taken positively by 
the market, and better risk sentiment is generally positively correlated with higher 
inflation expectations. 
Conclusion: very logical market reaction at the very front end of the inflation 
curve. 
We will publish our updated Eurozone inflation forecasts next Tuesday in the 
Inflation-Linked Weekly, once the results of the midterm election are fully digested 
by the market. For now, we still expect core around 2.9-3.0% on average next 
year, vs 2.6% in the ECB’s September forecasts, with probably some modest 
downside risk to our number considering last week’s decline in energy prices. 
Eurozone inflation strategy  
Further on the Eurozone inflation curve (Figure 8), the 1YF1Y – ie, YoY inflation 
priced in by the market at the end-Q328 horizon – has declined by 10bp in a week, 
to 2.1%. This is very good news for the ECB as the case for dis-anchoring 
inflation expectations has further weakened.  
The 5YF5Y inflation forward barely moved – 3bp week-on-week – to 2.1%. So 
there too, hard to say that market-based inflation expectations are dis-anchoring.

In contrast, the French inflation forwards (Figure 9) widened, especially the 5YF5Y 
and 10YF10Y. This may sound counterintuitive considering the energy move, but 
is probably all about a domestic-related story.  
Indeed, French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu communicated on an increase 
in the ceiling of the French Livret A early next year, which will likely imply 
significant inflows – from households – in the Livret A, and in turn probably more 
demand for French inflation hedging from French banks (as the Livret A is partly 
linked to French inflation).  
In turn, we closed our sell 3YF2Y FRCPIx (French inflation) vs HICPx (Eurozone 
inflation) on Tuesday. 
US inflation analysis 
With the lower crude oil prices, and using – as usual – the gasoline futures curve, 
it now looks likely that US headline CPI will not exceed 3.7% YoY in the coming 
months, while we were expecting a peak around 3.9% in November just a few 
days ago. 
We will publish an updated US CPI path in the October CPI preview tomorrow.

Appendix 1: Positioning & momentum 
Here we highlight a few of our positioning and momentum indices. You can find 
them, updated daily, in this dashboard at Red Mount Analytics. 
Our open-interest positioning indicator focuses on the relationship between 
daily changes in price and open interest in order to determine the gauge in bullish 
and bearish exposure over a given period of time. 
Within this bullish/bearish framework, there are four possible positioning 
indications: 
⚫ 
Outright longs: price and OI increases (bullish exposure) 
⚫ 
Outright shorts: price decreases, OI increases (bearish exposure) 
⚫ 
Reducing longs: price and OI decreases (bearish exposure) 
⚫ 
Reducing shorts: price increases, OI decreases (bullish exposure) 
To get the magnitude of the positioning indications, we use the correlation of the 
daily changes of open interest and futures prices. More precisely, we use the 
weighted average price/open interest of the two future contracts being traded to 
limit the impact from futures contracts rolling over. The correlation has the 
advantage of keeping the index scaled between -1 and +1.
