Trade survives, but the battle for
control intensifies: 3 calls for global
trade
Global trade will remain resilient in 2027, but the focus will shift from
tariffs to ownership, technology and investment. While not our base
case, an escalation in an EU-China trade war would rank among the
most significant risks to global trade
Europe is undoubtedly facing a second 'China shock' – only this time around, it's largely negative
How to read our three calls for 2027
Source: ING
ING's base call: Trade survives politics
Global goods trade continues to weather geopolitical shocks. Over the past few years,
trade has proved far more resilient than global headlines suggest. We expect that
resilience to continue into 2027. Disruptions in global shipping, shifting tariff regimes
and recurring geopolitical tensions have repeatedly altered trade routes without

derailing trade itself.
Strong demand for AI-related investment and infrastructure should continue to
support trade flows, particularly across Asia, while selective US-China decoupling is
likely to benefit third countries that attract production and investment. For 2027, we
expect global goods trade to remain positive, expanding about 3% year-on-year. In
short, trade survives politics in 2027 because companies can still adapt faster than
governments can restrict them.
Our risky call: Ownership matters more than trade
Tariffs were only the first step. By 2027, the real battleground shifts to capital, technology and
ownership. As companies invest behind tariff walls, governments stop merely screening new
investment and begin requiring companies to restructure, separate sensitive operations or
divest existing strategic assets.
A foreign-owned battery plant, semiconductor facility, or data centre once supported for
bringing local investment and jobs is now treated as a national-security risk. Companies that
once navigated geopolitical tensions through diversification find themselves forced to choose
sides. The question is no longer who exports the most, but who controls the productive
capacity on which economies depend. Trade would continue, but the corporate structures that
have kept it moving would start to fracture as governments alter trade flows. This could
potentially cause global goods trade growth to stall or even contract in 2027.
Our bold call: EU-China trade war
The EU and China enter an outright trade conflict. Europe abandons its largely open-market
approach and increasingly applies reciprocity principles to market access and investment.
Chinese firms investing in strategic sectors face mandatory 50:50 joint ventures, local
ownership requirements and growing restrictions on acquisitions.
At the same time, the EU imposes broad tariffs on Chinese imports, with rates reaching 50% in
politically sensitive sectors such as vehicles, chemicals, machinery and electronics. China
retaliates with measures of similar scale.
The consequences are significant. China accounts for roughly 15% of global goods trade, while
the EU accounts for around 13.5%. Together they represent almost 30% of world goods trade.
With the EU being China's largest trading partner in goods and China being the EU's second-
largest trading partner in goods, a full-scale trade conflict would rank among the most
consequential economic conflicts of the modern era.
