The Wedbush View
We are initiating coverage of X-Energy (XE) at OUTPERFORM with a $17 price target.
XE holds pole position among advanced high-temperature gas reactor developers,
with the Xe-100 nearing an NRC construction permit at Dow's Seadrift site and
the first new NRC-licensed commercial fuel fabrication facility in over 50 years.
Investors remain deeply skeptical of first-of-a-kind (FOAK) nuclear, and we believe
that skepticism leaves XE's 11.5 GW pipeline sharply discounted. As XE clears each
licensing, fuel and offtake milestone, we expect that discount to narrow.
A vertically integrated business model. XE's vertical integration across reactor
licensing and TRISO-X fuel fabrication is a structural advantage versus single-
product competitors. The February 2026 NRC Category II fuel fabrication license
and the August 2026 definitive HALEU supply agreements with Centrus Energy and
General Atomics meaningfully de-risk the fuel side of the equation, which in turn
reduces FOAK execution risk on the reactor side. Owning the fuel step also positions
XE to earn recurring fuel revenue over each plant's operating life.
Strong potential project pipeline. XE's U.S. pipeline of ~5.5 GW equals ~3% of
the ~200 GW of new U.S. nuclear capacity that DOE expects by 2050, and ~9% of
the 58+ GWe of advanced reactor capacity Wood Mackenzie projects by 2040. At
the midpoint of XE's per-plant economics (~$3.6b), the pipeline represents roughly
$129b of lifetime revenue. Amazon is both an equity investor and a counterparty,
with an option on more than 5 GW, an Energy Northwest project in Washington
structured to upsize to 960 MWe, and a 960 MW, 12-unit Indiana project with AEP
announced in October 2026.
A funded path through FOAK. Nuclear construction has a poor record on cost and
schedule, but XE has reduced its risk. Dow is a well-capitalized, highly motivated
first customer that needs to replace a fossil fuel energy source at its Seadrift
ethylene plant, and the DOE's ARDP cost share is set to rise to $2.115b on a 50/50
basis. With $1.9b of cash and investments and no debt at 2Q26, XE can fund its
remaining share of Xe-100 design and TX-1, though we expect external financing
for TX-2.
Catalysts and valuation. Our $17 PT is anchored on the 11.5 GW pipeline but
given heavy investor skepticism, we believe the key to the stock is XE advancing
past milestones: the Seadrift Final Safety Evaluation Report (by November 2026),
the Seadrift construction permit (guided to early 2027), Energy Northwest's
construction permit application (1H27), DOE obligation of the additional $1.0b of
ARDP funding (4Q26 to 1H27), TX-1 progress toward 1H28 operations, and follow-
on AEP/Amazon agreements in Indiana.

Company Overview 
XE is an energy technology company that licenses reactor technology and provides engineering services across a 
project's lifecycle while separately manufacturing and processing nuclear fuel through its TRISO-X, LLC subsidiary. 
The Company's core product is the Xe-100, a Generation IV high-temperature gas-cooled small modular reactor 
(SMR), sold and licensed alongside TRISO-X fuel. Each Xe-100 plant comprises four 80 MWe reactor units feeding a 
common steam system for a combined 320 MWe of output, with the shared steam header allowing the operator to 
take one reactor offline for maintenance or refueling while the other three continue generating, supporting a 
targeted plant availability of 95% or higher. 
XE does not intend to provide engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) services, and it does not own or 
operate projects once built. The Company also carries no direct commodity exposure to HALEU or natural uranium; 
customers and their EPC partners bear that price and supply risk directly, though the terms on which XE contracts 
fuel supply on their behalf are important consideration for any customer evaluating an Xe-100 deployment. Under 
this asset-light model, XE collects service fees, technology fees, and fuel-processing fees rather than construction 
margin, with revenue beginning ahead of a project's commercial operation date (COD) and continuing over the 
reactor's expected 60-year operating life. 
Founded in 2009 by entrepreneur Kam Ghaffarian, XE built its founding thesis on decades of pebble-bed reactor 
research originating in Germany during the 1960s and 1970s, later advanced through tri-structural isotropic (TRISO) 
fuel development at U.S. national laboratories. The Company licenses this technology into the Xe-100 design. Early 
funding came from private investors and the DOE; in 2016, the DOE selected XE for its Gateway for Accelerated 
Innovation in Nuclear (GAIN) program. 
In October 2020, the DOE selected XE alongside TerraPower for the ARDP, establishing a cost-sharing agreement that 
the Company disclosed on the 2Q26 call had grown to a $2.115b total DOE commitment after an August 2026 
increase. Through 2021 and 2022, XE completed a preliminary Xe-100 design, began NRC pre-application review, and 
began planning the TRISO-X fuel-processing facility in Oak Ridge, Tennessee. In 2022, XE signed its first demonstrated 
commercial agreement, a deployment of four Xe-100 reactors at Dow's Seadrift facility in Texas. 
XE priced its upsized initial public offering on April 23, 2026, at $23.00 per share (44.25m Class A shares, ~$1.02b 
gross proceeds, above a marketed range of $16.00-$19.00), and began trading on Nasdaq under “XE” the following 
day. The Company operates through an Up-C structure: X-Energy, Inc. holds an economic interest in X-Energy Reactor 
Company, LLC (XERC), with pre-IPO owners retaining LLC units exchangeable into Class A shares. As with peer SPAC 
nuclear issuers, we evaluate share count and dilution on a fully exchanged basis rather than off the public float alone. 
Technology Overview 
Xe-100 Reactor Design 
The Xe-100 is a helium-cooled, graphite-moderated high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) that delivers steam 
at 565°C, enabling both electricity generation and industrial process heat supply from the same plant rather than a 
single end use. Helium is chemically inert and does not become radioactive in the way that water coolant can, which 
simplifies maintenance access and reduces corrosion-related failure modes relative to light-water designs. Graphite 
moderation slows neutrons to sustain the fission chain reaction while also acting as a large thermal mass that resists 
rapid temperature swings during upset conditions, a passive safety characteristic shared with other HTGR designs, 
including the pebble-bed reactors from which the Xe-100 design lineage descends. 
Fuel takes the form of TRISO particles embedded in graphite pebbles, with ~220,000 pebbles loaded per reactor core. 
Each TRISO particle encases a uranium kernel in four layers of carbon and silicon carbide coatings that independently 
contain fission products at the particle level; a 2019 Department of Energy-sponsored study at Idaho National 
Laboratory (INL) found the coated particles withstand temperatures up to 3,000°F without releasing radioactive

material, well above any credible accident temperature for the Xe-100 design. Because containment happens at the 
level of each individual pebble rather than relying solely on a reactor-scale pressure vessel, the design requires less 
concrete and steel used for containment in conventional light-water reactors, which the Company points to as a 
driver of lower construction cost and shorter build schedules. 
Each of the four 80 MWe reactor units in a standard Xe-100 plant feeds into a shared steam system, which is the 
structural source of the Company's targeted 95%-plus availability, which sits a few points above the 90%-plus capacity 
factors the U.S. fleet has posted every year since 2012. An operator can take one reactor offline for refueling or 
maintenance without shutting down the other three. The Company has disclosed limited detail on the specific supply 
chains and lead times behind  “off-the-shelf” claims, and long lead times on specialized long-lead components are a 
more useful place to underwrite schedule risk than a blanket assumption that commercial availability equals fast 
delivery. 
TRISO-X Fuel Fabrication 
TRISO-X, LLC, XE's wholly owned fuel-fabrication subsidiary, has produced TRISO fuel in kg batch quantities at a pilot 
facility co-located with Oak Ridge National Laboratory since 2016. In August 2026, the Company disclosed that the 
Oak Ridge National Laboratory research partnership would extend for an additional thirty months and that the pilot 
facility would remain on site, rather than converting it entirely to a research-only laboratory as originally planned. 
The first commercial facility, TX-1, began construction in September 2025 and was ~50% complete as of the May 2026 
investor update; interior buildout began in August, with completion targeted for 2027 and operations beginning in 
1H28. At full capacity, TX-1 is designed to support eleven Xe-100 reactors at a nameplate capacity of 6.25 metric tons 
(MTU) of pebbles per year with a target output of 5.0 MTU (700,000 pebbles) per year. A planned second facility, TX-
2, would roughly quadruple that capacity to an estimated 25 metric tons of fuel per year, sufficient to support up to 
44 reactors.

Regulatory Progress and the Seadrift (Project Long Mott) Timeline 
XE has engaged the NRC through pre-application review for eight years. In February 2026, the NRC granted TRISO-X 
the first-ever Category II special nuclear material license for commercial fuel fabrication, a meaningful and 
differentiated regulatory milestone. 
At Dow's Seadrift site in Texas, XE has a first-of-a-kind deployment of the Xe-100 comprising four 80 MWe reactor 
units (320 MWe total). Long Mott Energy, a subsidiary of Dow Inc., owns the plant; XE licenses the technology and 
supplies fuel and services but does not own or operate the asset. 
Project Long Mott submitted its construction permit application in March 2025, and the NRC docketed it in May 2025. 
Environmental reviews are typically one of the longer processes in large energy-infrastructure permitting given its 
broader scope than pure nuclear-safety review and its formal public-comment periods. The NRC's May 2026 
Environmental Assessment finding of no significant impact, delivered roughly a year after docketing, represents a 
notably fast turnaround relative to typical multi-year environmental reviews for comparable energy infrastructure. 
The Draft Safety Evaluation Report followed on schedule. The NRC completed the Advanced Safety Evaluation Report 
in August 2026 as planned, and the NRC issued the Final Safety Evaluation Report on October 30, 2026, ahead of its 
November 2026 target. The report documents the NRC staff's conclusion that the proposed plant and site provide 
the safety basis to begin construction under 10 CFR Part 50, and the Advisory Committee on Reactor Safeguards 
separately supports issuance of the construction permit. 
With both the environmental and safety reviews complete, the project moves to the final stages of the construction 
permit review, which the Company guides to early 2027. A construction permit authorizes XE and Dow to begin 
building; it is a distinct, and generally faster, milestone than the operating license the plant will separately need 
before it can produce power. There is no current precedent on timeline between construction permits and the 
operating license but the NRC targets 16 to 25 months for a Part 50 license review.

10-Point Standardized Assessment Framework 
1. Technology Readiness Level 
The Xe-100 design is complete and XE has engaged the NRC for eight years, and TRISO-X has produced pilot-scale 
fuel since 2016, but no Xe-100 reactor and no TRISO-X commercial-scale fuel line has yet operated. Continued 
progress at TX-1 and at Seadrift would meaningfully raise our confidence here; a construction delay at either site 
would be the more useful signal to watch than the design maturity itself, which is credible. 
2. Regulatory Pathway 
XE's regulatory progress is a substantial differentiator: the first-ever Category II fuel fabrication license (February 
2026) and an environmental review that closed in roughly one year with a finding of no significant impact are both 
faster than the multi-year timelines common to comparable energy-infrastructure projects. The Company expects 
the Seadrift project to be issued a final construction permit in early 2027.  
While unlikely, we see one issue that could delay the operating permit application stage: graphite dust produced via 
mechanical wear from the pebbles that move with the helium gas and deposits on various surfaces and in flow dead 
zones in the primary loop. Graphite dust is quite porous, so some radioactive fission products will adhere to the dust, 
which leads to radioactive fission products being distributed on the surfaces of the primary loop, and this dust can 
also leak into the environment during break accidents. 
XE has a defined methodology and NRC engagement history (XE first submitted a dedicated graphite topical report 
in 2022, withdrew it in January 2023 on NRC feedback to expand scope, then resubmitted with expanded detail) but 
the issue is not yet closed out. While there may be significant documentation on dust generation, the dust production 
validation (Phase 3 of XSTERM V&V) is calibration-dependent on very limited operating pebble-bed data, increasing 
NRC scrutiny and risk. 
The Company indicates that dust is an issue primarily during maintenance, but its helium circulators operate in low 
temperatures (i.e. 250°C) and it doesn’t foresee any maintenance on the circulators during the lifetime of the 
circulator. XE is also performing wear testing. It has several ways to blow down its systems and it is designing them 
to reduce dust accumulation locations. It also has a dust filter as part of its Helium Services System to remove dust 
from the system. While dust remains a consideration, the Company indicates that its assessment found dust is not a 
risk. Nonetheless, XSTERM V&V Phase 3 continues, and XE will address it in an update to the topical report or 
disposition it in the operating license application.  
3. Fuel Supply Chain 
XE's vertically integrated model, pairing reactor licensing with in-house TRISO-X processing, gives customers a single 
counterparty for both the reactor and its fuel, a structural advantage most peers cannot offer. The Company carries

no direct commodity exposure to HALEU or uranium, though the price and availability terms XE negotiates on its 
customers' behalf remain an important underwriting question for any counterparty evaluating an Xe-100 project. 
The August 6, 2026, definitive HALEU/low-enriched uranium (LEU) supply agreement with Centrus Energy (deliveries 
beginning 2030 from Centrus's American Centrifuge Plant in Pike County, Ohio, funded partly through XE 
prepayments) and a parallel agreement with General Atomics reduce the domestic HALEU shortage. Combined with 
a 7.6 MTU allocation from the DOE, XE should have enough HALEU to complete and maintain the Seadrift and Energy 
Northwest projects if these agreements are enacted. TX-1 and TX-2 construction risk and HALEU supply risk remain 
linked as a delay at either the fuel-fabrication facilities or at Centrus's enrichment build-out would compress the 
schedule buffer for the other, since the Xe-100 has no qualified alternative fuel source today. 
4. Thermal Efficiency and Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) 
The Xe-100's dual-use design (process steam and power generation from the same plant) and its ability to ramp 
output from 100% to 40% within minutes, combined with a 95%-plus availability target, are credible advantages of 
the HTGR design. XE has not disclosed a standalone LCOE figure, unlike some peers, which limits direct cost 
comparison; project-level LCOE will also vary by site given differing capacity factors, transmission upgrade costs, and 
local labor markets. The thermal/output claims are directionally sound based on established HTGR physics, but note 
that verification at commercial scale, rather than a third-party technical advisor, is key. 
5. Passive Safety 
The self-contained TRISO particle design is a credible, independently verified safety feature: DOE/INL testing confirms 
particle integrity to 3,000°F, well above any Xe-100 accident temperature, and the graphite moderator's large thermal 
mass provides an inherently slow, forgiving response to transients. This is a strength of the design, not merely a 
marketing claim, and we do not discount it for the absence of commercial operating history. The underlying particle 
and materials testing is independently sourced and well documented. The Company says the design's safety profile 
allows applicants to pursue a 400-meter emergency planning zone, versus 10 miles for conventional light-water 
reactors, enabling siting inside the perimeter of a large industrial facility or closer to populated areas. 
6. Constructability 
TX-1 has made tangible, verifiable progress with ~50% complete as of May 2026, and vertical construction completed 
at the end of September 2026, but XE has not yet executed a nuclear construction project or a fuel-fabrication line 
at commercial scale. The Company's claims around off-the-shelf and factory-built components remain largely 
unverified at the project level; long lead times on specialized long-lead components are the more specific risk to 
underwrite here, rather than a generic comparison to unrelated large-scale nuclear cost overruns, since Seadrift's 
smaller modular scope and existing DOE-funded supply chain differ materially from a conventional gigawatt-scale 
light-water build. 
7. Offtake and Counterparties 
Dow’s Seadrift project remains the Company’s only binding, contracted deployment and is the only project actively 
in the NRC’s construction-permitting process. The Amazon relationship is a funding and development commitment, 
not yet a binding power purchase agreement (PPA), and it now covers two projects. The first is the Energy Northwest 
Cascade project, with an initial 320 MWe and a stated ambition of scaling to 960 MWe. The second, announced 
October 19, 2026, is a proposed 960 MW, 12-unit project in Sullivan County, Indiana, under a joint development 
agreement with AEP, in which Amazon provides milestone-based development funding and would buy the power 
under a long-term contract if the project is built. 
Both sit within Amazon’s broader commitment to more than 5 GW of new nuclear by 2039. The Company has not 
disclosed timing or a binding build commitment for the Indiana project, and it notes that the feasibility work may not

support moving forward. Amazon’s status as both a strategic customer and an investor increases the likelihood that 
PPAs follow once Seadrift and TX-1 de-risk the technology. 
Centrica (targeting ~6 GW in the U.K., including an initial 320 MWe Xe-100 plant at Hartlepool), Talen Energy (TLN-
NC), and Louisville Gas and Electric (LG&E)/Kentucky Utilities (KU) remain in earlier-stage letters of intent or 
collaboration agreements without disclosed binding commitments. In the U.K., U.K. regulators recently accepted the 
Xe-100 into Generic Design Assessment, an initial licensing step for a Centrica deployment. The Company’s disclosed 
11.5 GW / 144-reactor pipeline, which includes the Indiana project, assumes its customers ultimately exercise their 
rights in full.  
AEP, a regulated utility, is XE’s first utility partner, and it differs from XE’s existing customer base of industrials, 
hyperscalers, and a U.K.-based energy company. In the proposed Indiana project, the Company says the project 
combines AEP’s power generation and nuclear operating expertise, Amazon’s milestone-based development capital, 
and XE’s technology. Amazon would buy the power under a long-term contract that covers the costs of the facility 
and its operations, so recovery of project costs from AEP’s ratepayers is not the model here, and AEP says its 
agreements provide all necessary capital protections and financial safeguards. 
Utilities in general bring several advantages. First, they typically have strong balance sheets and, where it applies, 
can recover project costs from ratepayers through rate cases, which lowers financing risk. Second, many U.S. utilities 
and generators already operate nuclear fleets (e.g., Constellation Energy (CEG-NC) and Southern Company (SO-NC)), 
so they understand NRC licensing, siting, and operations. Third, utilities plan 5 to 20 years ahead through integrated 
resource plans (IRPs), so they can order multiple units at once. 
The AEP joint development agreement is the closest public example of XE working with a utility. The April 2026 
collaboration with LG&E and KU, both regulated subsidiaries of PPL, is a second, earlier-stage example. It covers 
feasibility work rather than a construction commitment. Together they show XE moving toward regulated utilities 
that serve large-load customers. The Indiana project sits within the Company’s disclosed 11.5 GW pipeline, and we 
have not seen the LG&E and KU collaboration counted in it.  
The main drawback of working with utilities is speed. The Indiana project illustrates the point as it begins as a joint 
development agreement for feasibility work, and the Company has not disclosed timing or a binding build 
commitment. Utilities need approval from their state public utility commission both to build a project and to recover 
its costs, so they move more slowly than behind-the-meter hyperscaler or industrial customers. That same regulatory 
structure also makes utilities attractive partners. In exchange for an exclusive service territory, a utility has a legal 
obligation to serve its customers and must add generation as load grows. This obligation to serve makes utilities a 
durable long-term customer base. 
 We also note that Amazon is entitled to a future payback tied to cost reductions as XE scales and builds more units. 
A portion of the learning-curve benefits will flow back to Amazon rather than XE. Our model accounts for this 
obligation as a series of payments that begin five years after the first Amazon-sponsored project reaches commercial 
operation, which we assume is in FY33, meaning the first payment is in FY38. The obligation covers the initial project 
or every unit placed through Amazon’s allocation rights. For modeling purposes, we assumed a 10-year payment 
term and full pass-through of the efficiency gain. We estimate $322m PV of payments, impacting our equity value 
per share by $0.78.  We note that the payout is capped at an undisclosed portion of annual free cash flow (FCF).

constructability. Continued on-schedule progress at Seadrift and TX-1 would be the clearest evidence of the 
Company's execution credibility. 
9. Cash Runway and Dilution 
XE is well capitalized in the near-term. The April 2026 IPO priced at $23.00 per share and generated ~$1.0-1.1b in 
proceeds, and the Company reported $1.9b of cash and short/long-term investments as of 2Q26 with no interest-
bearing debt. DOE cost-sharing further reduces near-term capital intensity: the ARDP agreement increased in August 
2026 to a total $2.115b DOE commitment, funds ~50% of the design, licensing, and TX-1 construction costs, meaning 
the Company is not solely reliant on its own balance sheet to fund those specific programs. 
XE has not yet disclosed TX-2 capital requirements; applying a standard capacity-scaling cost rule (cost scales with 
capacity raised to ~0.6 power, since most large-facility costs such as site work, buildings, and utilities do not scale 
linearly with a four-fold increase in throughput) to TX-1's disclosed ~$300m cost structure ($148.5m from the 48C 
tax credit, $48.2m from a vertical-consumption award, and $40m of long-lead procurement authorization) implies a 
TX-2 cost in the range of $700-800m for a four-fold capacity increase, with potential for a lower figure given co-
location synergies with TX-1 and pre-existing Category II licensing. 
Investors should evaluate Class A and Class B shares together, since Class B LLC units convert 1:1 into Class A shares 
under the Company's Up-C structure. Combined with ~15m stock options, restricted stock awards (RSAs) and 
restricted stock units (RSUs), the Company had 414.2m non-GAAP fully diluted shares as of 2Q26. XE also has 14.1m 
warrants outstanding exercisable contingent upon future events or performance conditions that are not disclosed 
but we assume are far out of the money and do not include these in our diluted share count. 
10. Policy and Subsidy Sensitivity 
XE is less directly exposed to the Inflation Reduction Act's production and investment tax credits than utility and 
independent power producer peers, but the Section 48C credit tied to TX-1's construction cost and the DOE cost-
sharing agreement both carry policy risk. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed in July 2025, largely 
preserved nuclear-specific credits relative to other clean-energy provisions but tightened the in-service deadline for 
certain credit eligibility; a schedule slip at TX-1 beyond 1H28 could jeopardize eligibility for the associated tax credit. 
Delays interacting with the 2026 midterm and 2028 presidential election cycles add an additional layer of policy-
timing risk given the multi-year, multi-administration construction and licensing timelines involved.

Business Model and Commercialization Pathway 
XE’s disclosed unit economics describe three revenue categories across a project’s lifecycle. These figures reflect nth-

towards TX-1’s 11th reactor and beyond. On a per-MW basis XE carries stronger unit economics than Terrestrial 
Energy (IMSR) and NANO Nuclear Energy (NNE), driven by a long project life, high fuel revenues and high margins. 
Like XE, NNE also has a high proportion of lifetime revenues from high-margin TRISO fuel—a “razor / razor blade” 
model.

Capital Requirements Going Forward 
XE’s April 2026 IPO generated net proceeds of ~$1.1b, and the Company held $1.9b of cash and investments with no 
debt as of 2Q26. In August 2026, the DOE notified XE that the ARDP cooperative agreement will receive up to an 
additional $1.0b, which would raise the DOE’s total cost-share contribution to $2.115b on the same 50/50 basis as 
the first $1.1b that the DOE has already allocated; the Company expects the DOE to obligate the additional amount 
through the normal contractual process. The DOE had reimbursed $547m as of June 30, 2026, up from ~$438m at 
the end of 2025, which leaves ~$1.57b of the $2.115b to be recognized. We expect most of that to arrive as services 
revenue and grant income, with the remainder as reimbursed capital expenditures, and our forecast recognizes it 
through 2031.  
The Company’s most recent public estimate of total ARDP program cost, from June 2023, was $4.75b to $5.75b, and 
the Company’s IPO prospectus notes that costs have risen since 2020 without giving a current figure. DOE cost sharing 
covers only the scope currently disclosed: design, licensing, fuel fabrication facility construction, and demonstrator 
reactor construction.  It does not extend to TX-2, the TXL laboratory or subsequent fuel facilities. If we assume the 
cost is ultimately $6-$7b, including ~$4.5b for the plant, $500m for TX-1 (see below) and $1b for the design at the 
midpoint, XE would be responsible for ~$750m (50% of $1.5b for TX-1 and design not covered by grants). With 
~$530m already spent, that leaves only ~$220m of costs left for XE. At the same time, we look favorably on the 
financing prospects for the Dow Seadrift project, given the DOE support, Dow’s strong balance sheet and need to 
replace the fossil fuel energy source at its ethylene plant. 
In April 2022, XE estimated the cost of TX-1 at ~$300m, for a facility with a capacity of 8 MTU per year and a planned 
start in 2025. Since then, capacity has fallen to 5 MTU per year and the planned startup has moved to 1H28. The 
Company has not published an updated cost figure. We anchor on the April 2024 48C allocation of $148.5m, which 
at a 30% credit rate implies ~$495m of qualified investment and gives a floor for the cost of TX-1. We estimate that 
TX-2 will cost ~$1.2b, for four times the capacity of TX-1. We model $1.2b spread over 2029 to 2031. The ARDP cost 
share covers TX-1 but not TX-2, so we model TX-2 at full cost. While XE’s $1.9b cash and investments at 2Q26 would 
cover this and ~$220m remaining costs for Xe-100 design and TX-1, the cushion would be thin. We expect the 
Company to raise capital to fund TX-2.  
Lastly, we note that beginning on the fifth anniversary of the first Amazon-sponsored project’s COD, XE must refund 
Amazon the cost efficiencies achieved relative to the PPA prices underpinning the initial projects, a FOAK-to-NOAK 
lookback that grows as XE drives costs down. We model a 2033 COD, first payment in 2038, a ten-year term, and a 
10% learning rate per doubling of the reactor base applied across all Amazon-sponsored capacity added from the 
first COD through the following five years ( 2.9GWe). That yields a ~$322m present value at our 17.3% weighted 
average cost of capital (WACC), or $0.78 per share, reducing our discounted cash flow (DCF)-implied value from 
$18.05 to $17.27. XE has disclosed neither the pass-through rate nor the FCF deferral threshold associated with this 
agreement, but we believe the costs will not be overbearing. 
Valuation and Price Target 
We value XE using a discounted cash flow framework built on our own project-by-project capacity and revenue build, 
which reaches the full 11.5 GW disclosed pipeline in operation by 2040. We apply values within XE’s own disclosed 
per-plant unit economics range where available (~$2.4b to ~$4.7b of lifetime revenue per four-reactor, 320 MWe 
plant, or ~$7.5m to ~$14.8m per MW, with a ~$3.6b midpoint) and our own assumptions elsewhere, using a 17.3% 
WACC and a 6.0x enterprise value (EV)-to-revenue exit multiple on FY40E revenue, on a fully diluted share count that 
combines Class A shares outstanding with exchangeable Class B/LLC units. Our DCF yields $18 per share before the 
Amazon cost-efficiency lookback and $17 after it.  
Our $17 price target implies ~56% upside from the current price. We flag that this valuation is unusually sensitive not 
only to the pace at which the 11.5 GW pipeline converts to binding, but also the WACC and EV/Revenue exit multiple.

• 
Graphite dust-driven delays: Pebble circulation in the Xe-100 wears graphite into micron-size dust that travels 
with the helium coolant, deposits in the primary loop, and can carry adsorbed fission products, complicating 
maintenance and adding a radiation source term to accident analysis. Both German pebble-bed reactors 
experienced this. XE has engaged the NRC on the issue, including a resubmitted graphite topical report, and

Valuation
Our $17 PT is based on a DCF of our project build to 11.5 GW by 2040, using a 17.3% WACC and a 6.0x EV/revenue exit multiple on
FY40E revenue, net of the Amazon lookback; ~47% upside.
Company Description
X-Energy develops the Xe-100, an 80 MWe helium-cooled HTGR deployed in four-unit, 320 MWe plants for power and industrial heat,
and makes TRISO fuel through TRISO-X. It licenses its technology and does not own or operate plants.
Risks to the Attainment of Our Price Target and Rating:
Graphite dust-driven delays: Pebble circulation in the Xe-100 wears graphite into micron-size dust that travels with the helium coolant,
deposits in the primary loop, and can carry adsorbed fission products, complicating maintenance and adding a radiation source term
to accident analysis. Both German pebble-bed reactors experienced this. XE has engaged the NRC on the issue, including a resubmitted
graphite topical report, and its design uses low-temperature circulators, blowdown capability, reduced dust-accumulation zones and a
helium dust filter. However, dust-production validation (XSTERM V&V Phase 3) relies on limited operating pebble-bed data and remains
open, which could slow the operating license review.
Quality control risk to manufacturing TRISO-X fuel at scale: Each TRISO-X fuel pebble contains ~19,000 individual TRISO particles that
must each be coated correctly. Defective pebbles could cause changes in the neutron flux in a reactor due to increased or decreased
moderating effects. This means that fabrication defect rates matter for both safety margin and predictable reactor performance, and
this is part of why TRISO-X's TX-1 facility (a first-of-its-kind commercial HALEU/TRISO plant) carries FOAK manufacturing-scale-up risk
on top of the usual construction risk for any new fuel facility, both of which could materially impact the plant’s economics.
First-of-a-kind execution risk at Dow Seadrift and TX-1: nuclear construction has a well-documented history of cost overruns and
schedule slippage, and while Seadrift's smaller modular scope and existing DOE-funded supply chain differ from large gigawatt-scale
precedents, XE has not yet executed a project of this kind at commercial scale.
Offtake conversion risk: the 11.5 GW disclosed pipeline depends on development agreements, letters of intent and MOUs converting
to binding, priced agreements. The Indiana project with AEP and Amazon is at the joint development stage, and the Cascade project is a
funding and development commitment. Slower-than-expected conversion, or conversion at economics below the Company’s disclosed
unit revenue, would directly compress our base case.
Fuel-supply and construction interdependency: a delay at TX-1/TX-2 or at Centrus's and General Matter uranium enrichment build-
out would compress the schedule buffer for the other, given limited alternative fuel sources.
Regulatory risk beyond the construction permit: the pace at which the NRC moves from a Seadrift construction permit to an operating
license is largely without precedent for this design.
Policy and subsidy risk: OBBBA's tightened in-service deadlines for certain credit eligibility create a real cost consequence if TX-1 slips
beyond 1H28, and multi-year construction timelines span multiple election cycles.
Capital-structure risk: TX-2 and reactor projects beyond Seadrift will each require dedicated financing beyond the current DOE cost-
share scope, and further capital raises could dilute existing shareholders depending on financing structure and timing.
