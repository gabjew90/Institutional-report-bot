Europe is selling more into the US AI
build-out, but losing market share
European exports of equipment supporting the US AI build-out have risen
strongly since 2022, with power and cooling suppliers gaining both sales
and market share. But Europe’s share of US imports of AI-related
equipment is falling as computing takes a larger slice of the market,
where European suppliers have a smaller and declining share
Europe is supplying more of the infrastructure behind the US AI boom
In our recent article on AI and the global economy, we highlighted Europe’s role as a supplier
of some of the equipment behind the AI build-out. Europe is therefore not simply watching the
US artificial intelligence boom from the sidelines. But how much is it actually benefiting from
the surge in US infrastructure investment?
To track Europe’s role in the US AI build-out, we use a selected basket of products relevant to AI
infrastructure, based on Waugh (2026), including servers, transformers and cooling equipment.
These products are not exclusive to AI infrastructure, so the figures do not identify exports
specifically destined for AI projects. Comparing 2022 with the 12 months ending July 2026,
annual EU exports of the selected products to the US increased by approximately €10bn, or
55%, in electrical power equipment; €8bn, or 77%, in compute hardware; and €3bn, or 30%, in
cooling systems. European suppliers also gained shares of US imports within the selected
power and cooling categories.

suppliers are gaining ground in some expanding markets, but those markets represent a

A falling aggregate share does not mean Europe’s established industrial strengths are failing. It
does show that success in those industries is not enough to keep pace with the changing

Power and cooling suppliers are gaining both exports and
market share
Electrical power equipment accounts for the largest increase in European exports to the US
since 2022. The category includes transformers, switchgear, static converters and generator
sets. Cooling and HVAC equipment has also recorded substantial export growth.
In both categories, Europe has increased its share of US imports. The EU’s electrical-power
share has risen by approximately 1.6 percentage points, while its cooling share has increased
by around 2 percentage points. European suppliers have therefore expanded faster than the
corresponding US import markets.
Compute hardware presents a different picture. European exports have increased
substantially, making computing the second-largest contributor to the growth in EU exports to
the US within the infrastructure basket. However, the EU’s share of US compute imports has
fallen from roughly 4.5% to below 3%.
Networking exports have also increased, although by much less, while Europe’s share of the
corresponding US import market has declined by approximately 4 percentage points.
Speciality materials show a particularly large fall in European market share, but their small
weight in the basket means they contribute little to the overall decline.

Computing is taking a much larger slice of the market
Europe’s share of US imports in the selected infrastructure basket initially rose to nearly 10% in
early 2024, then fell to about 5.7% by July 2026. The reversal began in 2024, before the broad
US tariff increases on EU imports in 2025.
Instead, the turning point coincided with a sharp change in the composition of US imports.
Compute and networking increased from roughly 54% of the basket in early 2024 to
approximately 77% by the latest observation. Because Europe has a much smaller position in
these categories than in power and cooling equipment, their growing weight has pulled down
its aggregate market share.
This does not mean that US demand for supporting infrastructure is falling. Power and cooling
markets continue to expand, creating substantial opportunities for European suppliers. Still,
they are growing more slowly than computing and therefore account for a declining share of
the total. Europe’s share of compute and networking imports has also fallen, reinforcing the
effect of the changing product mix.

China’s decline in US AI-related imports has not produced an
aggregate European gain
China’s share of US imports in the selected basket has fallen from around 20% in 2020 to
below 5% at the latest observation. Taiwan and Southeast Asia have gained strongly,
particularly in the later period. Vietnam and Thailand account for more than 90% of Southeast
Asia’s increase between the 2022 average and the 12 months ending July 2026. Mexico retains
a large but comparatively stable position, while Europe’s share rose until 2024 before
reversing.
These movements should not be interpreted as direct transfers of orders from China. Some of
the expansion reflects new demand for different products, while customs data identify the
country of origin but not the ownership of manufacturers or the origin of upstream
components.
China’s declining direct share has therefore coincided with a rapidly expanding US market, but
not with a stronger aggregate European position. Europe’s gains remain concentrated in
particular product categories.

The trade figures show where US demand is being met, but they do not indicate how far the
resulting industrial benefits are spreading across Europe. Production data provides a tentative
indication, although the industries concerned are considerably broader than the AI-related
product basket used in the trade analysis.
Production gains remain uneven across Europe
Despite the export gains, output in the broader industries supplying this equipment remains
below its early-2022 level across the EU as a whole. Compared with the first half of 2022,
production in computer and electronic products, electrical equipment and machinery has risen
strongly in the Netherlands, Sweden and Hungary. In the Netherlands, this increase is likely to
be influenced by ASML and the wider semiconductor-equipment cluster. The corresponding
EU27 composite, however, remains around 1.5% lower, with Germany recording a decline of
approximately 9%.
These industries have substantial uses beyond AI and data centres, so the figures cannot
isolate the effect of the US investment boom. Nevertheless, the divergence suggests that any
production benefits are concentrated in particular industrial locations rather than visible across
the EU as a whole.

Europe benefits, but its strengths lie outside computing
Europe is benefiting from the US AI infrastructure boom, particularly in power and cooling
equipment. Yet its share of the selected US import basket has fallen as demand shifts towards
computing, and European shares within compute and networking decline. Despite higher
exports, production in the broader industries supplying this equipment remains below its
early-2022 level across the EU as a whole.
