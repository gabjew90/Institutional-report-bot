Beyond the ballot box: 
positioning for what’s next
Bottom line up top
Midterm elections delivered a decisive outcome. Democrats captured 
control of both chambers of Congress, setting the stage for a divided government 
and potential gridlock on Capitol Hill during President Trump’s remaining two years 
in office. Historically, equity markets have trended positive over the 12 months 
following an election, regardless of which party prevails in either the legislative or 
executive branches. 
That said, the midterm results were likely one impetus for last Thursday’s sharp 
market selloff, which included a -5.5% plunge in the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite. 
That steep drop underscored concerns that the power shift in Washington could lead 
to unfavorable policies around AI regulation, data center development and future 
infrastructure investment. We think Thursday’s market action was likely more a 
case of investors repricing the pace and scope of future data center buildouts rather 
than an existential wave of worry that AI may no longer represent a major secular 
investment cycle. A modest rebound on Friday suggested markets were already 
beginning to reassess the magnitude of the selloff.
In our view, nothing that happened last week materially changes the long-term AI 
investment thesis. The structural drivers remain intact, with the world’s largest 
technology companies continuing to communicate enormous levels of planned 
investment in AI infrastructure, and the demand signals we see remain robust. 
This week’s volatility serves as a reminder that shifting policy expectations and 
crowded positioning can drive abrupt market moves, reinforcing the importance 
of separating short-term noise from fundamental developments and identifying 
opportunities that market dislocations may create.
Economic outcomes will also inform market behavior. Last week’s 
data gave investors an opportunity to weigh in on the state of the economy, with 
some indicators earning a thumbs up and others a potential no-confidence vote. 
The ISM Services Purchasing Managers Index (PMI) came in at 54.5, signaling 
continued expansion (>50). However, the PMI’s prices-paid component reached

Leading up to and immediately after the election, both oil prices and interest rates 
began retreating (Figure 1). Specifically, Brent crude dropped to $95 per barrel for 
the first time since early September, while the 10-year Treasury yield fell below 
4.9% as markets considered the implications of a split government and a possible 
de-escalation of the Iran conflict. Lower energy prices could help ease inflation and 
support household purchasing power, while lower Treasury yields reduce borrowing 
costs and provide a tailwind for equity valuations, particularly in rate-sensitive 
sectors. But the sustainability of such relief will depend on further calming of 
geopolitical tensions and continued moderating inflation. 
This week brings more inflation data, with October’s Consumer Price Index (CPI) 
arriving Tuesday and the Producer Price Index (PPI) on Friday. We’ll be watching 
underlying services inflation, energy-related price pressures and the extent to 
which producer costs are passed through to consumers. A combination of resilient 
economic activity and stubborn inflation could extend the higher-for-longer 
interest-rate environment. That calls for an emphasis on earnings, balance-sheet 
strength and valuation discipline across capital markets.

Portfolio considerations
Democrats will control both chambers, but legislative math may 
not change much. Without veto-proof majorities, enacting any substantive 
policy will require bipartisan agreement or executive action. How might the 
political calculus play out in the markets? There are no guarantees, but history 
shows the S&P 500 Index has posted positive average annual returns under 
every combination of party control of Congress and the White House since 
1933 (Figure 2). 
Of these combinations, a Democratic Congress paired with a Republican 
president has produced the smallest gains at +4.9%, versus +13.3% under unified 
Republican control and +9.0% under unified Democratic control. However, 
with so few historical examples of midterms comparable to this year’s (either in 
magnitude or result), we would not over-interpret the chart. Ultimately, policy 
mix, not party label, has driven sector outcomes.
Data centers top the agenda. AI infrastructure rules are being set as much by 
governors and state legislatures as by Congress. In Virginia, higher energy taxes 
preceded data center project cancellations. In Washington, the battle over 100% 
expensing of capital expenditures — a key support for AI capex spending — may 
depend on 2027 debt ceiling negotiations. Moratoriums on site development will 
likely prove temporary, and we see limited supply inputs (e.g., power equipment, 
grid, cooling and semiconductors) as being more resilient than developers.
Affordability is a common thread. Electricity bills, credit card rates, 
interchange fees and health insurance premiums are under scrutiny by both 
political parties. This calls for examining the investment thesis of utilities, card 
issuers, payment networks and managed care names.
Other debates vary by sector or topic:
Health care: Any compromise deal to restore Medicaid and Affordable Care Act 
(ACA) subsidies would have implications for hospital and insurer stocks, while 
NIH funding decisions would affect the life sciences tools sector. A legislative 
agreement on these matters would likely come later rather than sooner, and may 
be smaller in scope than hoped.
Defense: Spending on defense will probably increase, but the mix among well-
established contractors and newer technology firms is less certain.
Trade: Tariff rollbacks would reduce tax revenue, adding to worries about the 
federal deficit — a key issue for bond markets.
Energy and infrastructure: Permitting reform and highway reauthorization 
could potentially benefit industries such as liquified natural gas (LNG) production 
and distribution, as well as materials companies and power grid builders. 
Renewable tax energy credits are likely to remain a contested issue.
The takeaway for investors in this shifting political and macro landscape is 
that a sound strategy stays diversified, avoids binary, either/or policy bets and 
emphasizes earnings quality as market leadership broadens. When policy-
driven market dislocations occur, we think they are most useful as meaningful 
opportunities to rebalance portfolios rather than chase short-term gains.
