Treasury yields moved sharply higher across the curve during the third quarter as the notion of higher-for-longer rates appeared 
to embed in the market’s psyche. The two-year Treasury traded up about 74 basis points to finish the period at 4.9%, while the 
10-year advanced 85 basis points to 5.3%. The bulk of these moves occurred in September, coincident with higher levels of interest 
rate volatility.1
We attribute the shift in the rate markets to a number of factors. Persistently above-target inflation in the US has pressured the short 
end of the curve, while concerns over the massive and growing sovereign debt burden were reflected in higher longer rates. Interest 
rates are now expected to be not only higher for longer but higher than previously anticipated. 
Even though the Federal Open Market Committee voted unanimously to raise the federal funds target rate by 25 basis points in 
September, there are concerns that the central bank may be behind the curve in its battle against inflation and will need to act more 
aggressively than previously anticipated. Futures markets entered 2026 expecting a year-end policy rate around 3.1% but at the end of 
September were pricing in a rate of 4.2%.2 While end-2026 levels implied by the market are consistent with the Fed’s latest dot plot of 
policymaker forecasts, futures traders are biased toward three additional hikes in 2027 compared to zero in the dot plot.3
The trajectory of inflation in 2027 may depend on the status of the conflicts in the Middle East. The resurgence in the Iran war later 
in the third quarter pushed Brent crude oil back above $100 per barrel and intensified the inflation pressures facing policymakers 
worldwide.4 The latest US consumer price index release, for September, showed core inflation of 3.7% driven by a 20.4% increase in the 
energy component.5

The fundamental backdrop for credit investing remains constructive even as rates have moved to multidecade 
highs. After limited growth in fourth quarter 2025, real gross domestic product has gained momentum in 2026; 
after expanding at a rate of 2.5% and 2.2% in the first and second quarters, respectively, the economy acceler­
ated to 3.4% in the third, driven by business investment and consumer spending.6 The resilient economy has been 
a tailwind for corporate earnings; the S&P 500 Index is expected to deliver earnings growth of 36% and 15% in 
2026 and 2027, respectively.7
Structured Credit: Higher All-In Yields Continue to Attract Flows
Generally, the high yields available in structured credit have attracted investors even as the prospect of mean­
ingful spread tightening seems remote.  
Asset-backed security (ABS) issuance remains on track for a new annual high-water mark in 2026.8 Consumers 
have been resilient in the face of ongoing economic uncertainty and spiking energy prices, but the K-shaped 
economy suggests caution may be warranted in 
lower-rated cohorts; the uncertain path of conflict 
in the Middle East and its impact on inflation remain 
wildcards for consumers. Issuance of nonagency 
residential mortgage-backed securities (RMBS) is also 
trending toward a new record, though it has slowed 
somewhat of late in the face of higher mortgage rates.9 
New issuance of collateralized loan obligations (CLO)—
the primary driver of demand for broadly syndicated 
loans (BSL)—rebounded in the third quarter, though 
the market continues to run about 17% behind last year’s pace. Around $46 billion of CLOs were priced over the 
last three months, a 37% improvement over the very slow second quarter. Retail demand for loans has continued 
to improve, posting inflows in five of the past six months after a very poor first quarter.10
Middle Market Direct Lending: Weak Dealmaking Activity Dented Volumes
Direct lending deal volume slowed to a three-year low during the quarter as leveraged buyout and add-on 
merger and acquisition (M&A) activity were both weak; at $152 billion year-to-date, volume is running 31% short 
of 2025’s pace. Spreads in the lower middle market widened by 11 basis points in the quarter to 534 basis points. 
Spread per turn of leverage, in contrast, narrowed to its lowest level since August 2025.11
Covenant-lite structures accounted for 17% of direct lending deals through third quarter 2026—up from 14% in 
2025—concentrated in the upper middle and large cap markets; cov-light structures represented less than 3% 
of deal flow in core and lower middle market loans.  Lower middle market default rates over the past 12 months, 
at 1.8% by dollar amount and 1.5% by deal count, declined since last quarter and remain below 2025 levels. 
Software continued to see the most default activity.12
Asset-Based Lending: Attractive Yields from Asset-Rich Borrowers
For many borrowers looking to optimize their capital structures, asset-based lending (ABL) can represent an 
attractive financing alternative to syndicated or direct loans. With higher rates and still-tight spreads relative to 
post-global financial crisis norms, large money-center banks remain selective about the companies and indus­
tries they will lend to. At the same time, some middle market companies may face numerus challenges to their 
margins, including supply-chain disruptions and soaring prices for input materials, wages and transport.

Conditions like these historically have created opportunities for nonbank lenders to work with borrowers and 
their sponsors to provide flexible access to capital, including asset-based loans. ABL facilities are governed by 
the value of specific assets of the borrower—such as inventory, accounts receivable, real estate, machinery and 
equipment, and intellectual property—and feature structural provisions and covenants designed to preserve the 
value of those collateral assets.
US Residential Real Estate Credit: Persistent Shortages Create Opportunity for Lenders
Higher mortgage rates—7.28% at the end of the quarter versus 6.48% at the start13—continue to challenge 
housing markets. Though higher mortgage rates can weigh on housing demand, historical experience suggests 
they do not necessarily trigger widespread home-price declines when accompanied by continued economic 
growth and low unemployment, as we are seeing today.14  
At the same time, strong demand triggered by persistent housing shortages and an aging housing stock 
contribute to a supportive environment for nonbank providers of capital to the real estate industry. This includes 
capital to finance residential transitional loans (RTLs)—short-duration, value-add renovation loans—and builder 
finance transactions— which provide homebuilders with capital to acquire and develop entitled and permitted 
land, including finished lots ready for construction. The short durations and fast payoffs associated with RTLs 
may generate higher cash flows, potentially providing additional optionality to redeploy capital as investment 
opportunities shift.
Differing characteristics of individual opportunities 
underpin the importance of underwriting the specifics 
of each potential investment. Rather than a single 
“housing market”, the opportunity set is perhaps better 
characterized as a “market of homes”. Focus on experi­
enced developers with deep knowledge of local markets 
characterized by favorable household income trends, 
population growth, limited housing supply and relatively 
few days on market may be warranted.
Broadly Syndicated Loans: Healthy M&A Demand Supported Steady New Issuance
A decline in repricings and extensions weighed on headline issuance of broadly syndicated loans (BSL) during 
the quarter, but healthy merger and acquisition activity supported a steady volume of new paper. While total 
issuance of $195 billion represented the slowest quarter of the year, the $113 billion raised ex-amendment 
activity was higher than the first and second quarters.15 Generally speaking, new issuance has been biased 
toward higher-rated credits, and a meaningful widening in the spread between the weakest and strongest 
borrowers suggests lenders are seeking greater compensation from the former. In the secondary market, the 
weighted average bid of leveraged loans continued to rebound from March lows as software credits slowly 
recaptured losses from earlier in the year.16

Frictions in the AI Narrative May Trigger Some Asset Repricing 
The artificial intelligence (AI) capital expenditure cycle has been a driving force behind equity returns, index-level 
earnings expectations and private investment in the economy, but we’ve begun to see frictions emerge in the 
data-center narrative. While demand for such facilities remains very strong, the marginal economics of supply 
have come under pressure; higher financing costs and rising input prices are pushing prices higher while 
political reticence and concerns over potential power shortages have slowed permitting. Acknowledgement that 
the emergence of such a transformative technology is unlikely to follow a straight line may result in some general 
repricing of assets.

Asset-backed securities (ABS) are debt securities whose payments of principal and interest are backed by the cash flow generated by pools of income-producing credit assets.
Asset-based lending (ABL) is corporate borrowing supported by specific assets of the borrower rather than its cash flows. 
Broadly-syndicated loans (BSL) typically refer to floating-rate commercial loans provided by a group of lenders—the syndicate—to a noninvestment grade borrower. 
Collateralized loan obligations (CLO) are financial instruments collateralized by a pool of corporate loans. 
Direct lending refers to a loan agreement negotiated between a borrower and single or small group of nonbank lenders. Direct lending can also be referred to as “private credit” or 
“private lending.” 
The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) is the committee of the Federal Reserve System that sets US monetary policy to promote maximum employment and price stability. 
Residential transitional loans (RTL) are short-term loans to real estate developers for the purpose of renovating a residential property. The loans are secured by the property 
being renovated. 
Structured credit is a financial instrument that pools together groups of similar, income-generating assets.
10-year Treasury yield is the interest rate paid on a US Treasury bond with a maturity of 10 years upon issuance. 
Two-year Treasury yield is the interest rate paid on a US Treasury note with a maturity of two years upon issuance. 
Consumer price index (CPI) (Price) measures inflation as experienced by consumers in their day-to-day living expenses by capturing the average change over time in the prices 
paid for a representative basket of consumer goods and services. A price-return index only measures price changes. 
S&P 500 Index (Gross/Total) measures the performance of 500 of the top companies in the leading industries of the US economy and is widely recognized as a proxy for the US 
market as a whole. A total-return index tracks price changes and reinvestment of distribution income.
