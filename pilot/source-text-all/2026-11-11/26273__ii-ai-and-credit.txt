AI and credit:  
Looking beyond the technology race 
 
Since the middle of 2025, we have seen a surge in debt issuance across credit 
markets to finance the artificial intelligence (AI) build-out.  
In this paper, we set out a framework for how we are thinking about the implications 
of that issuance. Our central argument is that, for a credit investor, the question of 
who wins the AI race matters far less than who wins the financing arms race, and that 
much of the debt being raised today is better understood through an infrastructure 
lens than as a binary decision on the technology itself. 
 
Key takeaways 
• Unprecedented issuance. Credit markets are absorbing this for now. As expected, 
large issuers have seen spreads widen, but away from AI and adjacent sectors, 
credit markets are holding up well. This is a key risk to monitor. 
• Credit analysis of AI requires a very different lens to equity. Cash flow visibility 
varies across the AI ecosystem, from the physical infrastructure supporting AI 
through to the models and applications built on it. Greater predictability is typically 
found towards the infrastructure end.  
• Bull vs. bear. The credit implications of AI could run counter to equity expectations: 
For the highest-quality credits, spreads may tighten if demand disappoints, while 
strong balance sheets could support credit even if AI investment accelerates and 
heavier issuance creates technical pressure.  
• Investment implications. Issuer selection matters more than the theme. Selective, 
defensive and patiently scaling in.

Unprecedented issuance volumes hitting credit markets 
Like many transformative technologies, the evolution of artificial intelligence is driving 
an investment wave of historic proportions. According to figures from the Bank for 
International Settlements, the level of AI investment over the past three years has 
risen at a faster pace than several major historical investment booms, including the 
dotcom buildout, the Roaring 20s and 19th-century railway mania1.   
Financing the AI build-out has led to a dramatic increase in debt issuance in public 
and private markets. In the investment grade (IG) market, for example, the five major 
hyperscalers (Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle) have issued $240.7 
billion in debt year-to-date as of 31 August, with $66 billion issued in non-dollar 
currencies. Including US dollar issuance from SpaceX and Nvidia takes combined 
issuance by these companies to $290.7 billion across currencies2. The figures 
illustrate the scale of supply that credit markets are being asked to absorb. 
While most IG issuance has consisted of senior unsecured debt, the market has also 
produced bespoke, structured finance issues that companies have engineered to 
support the construction of large data centres. For example, the largest single-
tranche bond issue in terms of dollar value in the US IG market belongs to Beignet 
Investor LLC, a holding company created by Blue Owl Capital to fund construction of 
a 2.1-gigawatt Meta data centre campus in Louisiana.  
Due to the public-private nature of the offering, these bonds are not index-eligible, 
yet they are very liquid securities, reflecting the broad acceptance structured finance 
issues have gained in the IG market.

This does not mean hyperscaler spreads will immediately tighten. Spreads have 
widened since large-scale issuance began, and technical pressures may continue 
until supply stabilises. The key question is whether that pressure diffuses to the rest of 
the credit market. So far, it has not. 
 
Will this dynamic continue? 
The central question is whether the market can keep absorbing this scale of supply, 
or whether at some point, the widening bleeds through into broader credit. Our 
working view is that so long as economies remain reasonably healthy, corporate 
fundamentals stay strong, and strong demand for yield continues, the market can 
continue to digest the supply, albeit at the cost of some ongoing technical pressure 
and periodic indigestion. 
Nevertheless, the volume of AI-related issuance is a key risk to monitor for broader 
credit markets. Here, there are a few things we are watching closely: 
• Early signs of AI issuance fatigue: Thus far, demand for AI-related financing has 
been exceptionally strong, with the hyperscalers' jumbo deals in H1 particularly 
well received. However, enthusiasm for more recent issuance has become less 
uniform as supply has increased. While too early to determine whether this 
represents a shift in investor appetite or simply a one-off event, there have also 
been isolated signs of strain in smaller markets. For example, a high yield data 
centre financing earlier this year was postponed and subsequently restructured 
following weak demand. Taken individually, these examples may not be significant, 
but they are worth monitoring as potential early indicators of the market's capacity 
to absorb continued AI-related issuance. 
• Demand for yield: Near decade highs for yields in credit has brought a range of 
new buyers who were largely absent from dollar credit during low-rate period of 
2010s. This includes annuity providers and non-US private investors, two segments 
with a strong income focus. If yields fall below a certain threshold, demand for 
credit could fall away, removing an important technical support. 
• Balance sheet discipline: So far, the largest AI issuers continue to benefit from 
strong operating performance, robust balance sheets, low leverage and substantial 
operating cash flow. However, sharply higher capital expenditure has reduced free 
cash flow and, in some cases, pushed it temporarily negative. Investors generally 
remain willing to absorb large issuance volumes when leverage remains 
manageable and the investment is supported by resilient operating cash flows. A

Applying a credit lens to AI: infrastructure not a technology call 
Credit investors often approach AI investment from a different perspective to equity 
investors. The latter are principally concerned with the scale of the potential earnings 
upside, whereas credit investors also need to assess whether an issuer can fund the 
investment and continue meeting its obligations across a range of outcomes. This 
places greater emphasis on the resilience of operating cash flows, starting leverage, 
balance-sheet flexibility and, in structured financings, the strength and enforceability 
of contracted revenues. 
For credit investors, identifying the eventual winners of the AI race remains relevant. 
But it is equally important to determine which companies can sustain the financing 
required to compete, and which individual structures provide sufficient protection if 
outcomes fall short of expectations 
It is helpful to think of the AI ecosystem as a spectrum of cash flow certainty. The 
closer a business sits to scarce, physical inputs such as compute, power and 
infrastructure, the more confidence we can have in its economics, and the more the 
profile resembles an infrastructure investment: high capex up front, steady 
contracted revenue over the long term, and limited upside from the AI decision itself. 
The closer a business sits to the application layer, the less certain the economics 
become, the more the investment resembles a call on the success of AI and on being 
a winner within it, and the more binary the outcome.

The difference matters a great deal due to the asymmetric nature of credit investing, 
with upside ultimately capped at yield plus par value. In that framing, the 
infrastructure layer – with hard asset backing and contracted cashflow – is the more 
attractive layer of the AI ecosystem. The model layer and application layer face much 
less certainty on end outcomes; terminal value risk and hence potential defaults need 
to be considered carefully. 
 
Assessing credit risk in AI-related bonds  
Against that backdrop, there are several characteristics that can potentially “de-risk” 
some AI-related bonds: 
• Strong existing operating cash flow and a nearer term path to monetisation, for 
example with data centre sites secured and power locked in. 
• A diversified client base, ideally with high-quality tenants/compute offtake; 
contractual terms are critical to ensure these partners are contractually “on the 
hook” when issuance is through a special purpose vehicle. 
• Balance sheet flexibility, meaning the ability to raise capital through multiple 
channels including equity, corporate bonds, structured finance and private markets. 
• A clear signal on how the issuer prioritises the maintenance of its credit rating in 
making decision around capital structure optimisation and future investment plans.  
This last point is critical as large scale issuance increases leverage and potentially 
raises the risk of credit rating downgrades. Many companies, particularly in the IG 
universe, have levers that they can use to manage the trajectory of their credit profile. 
This could be various forms of scaling back capex plans or shifting the debt-equity 
financing mix. Such decisions expose the tension between pursuing earnings growth 
and protecting leverage, funding costs and credit ratings.  
 
Unpacking the AI infrastructure layer and understanding the true risk bondholders 
are underwriting  
The bulk of the current wave AI-related bond issuance is financing the build-out of 
data centres by a small group of AI infrastructure companies (the hyperscalers). Some 
of this has been in the form of vanilla corporate bonds, but increasingly, we are 
seeing more novel structures to finance specific projects.  
The latter is worth exploring in detail as the structuring – typically through special 
purpose vehicles (SPVs), with a hyperscaler providing some level of backstop – 
introduces a different set of risks (and rewards, as the deals typically trade with a yield 
pick-up to the hyperscaler parent).  
Below we set out a framework that may be useful in assessing these project finance 
deals.  
We begin by understanding these deals as more of an infrastructure project rather 
than a binary technology decision. The projects involve heavy up-front capex, with 
long-term contracted cashflows once the data centres come online along with some 
level of credit backstop from a high-quality parent. The principal risks to cashflows 
are around delivery and durability: delays in bringing data centres online, whether 
through site selection, construction or power; and the durability of the ongoing cash 
flows, which depends on whether the site is backstopped by a high-quality tenant 
and whether structural protections, such as residual value guarantees, genuinely 
place the tenant on the hook.

AI bulls versus bears: Could bonds diversify AI equity risk? 
The implications of AI for credit are more nuanced than a simple bull-or-bear 
outcome. The economic returns generated by AI and the ability to expand capacity 
could both materially influence outcomes for bondholders. The four scenarios below 
illustrate how these factors could interact:

AI scenarios: different paths, different implications for credit.  
The outlook for AI-related credit depends on two key uncertainties: the economic 
value ultimately generated by AI and the ease with which additional capacity can be 
financed and built. The four scenarios below look through to 2033, but the path 
matters as much as the end state. Over shorter horizons, issuance and financing 
conditions may dominate spread performance; over time, realised returns, cash-flow 
generation and balance-sheet adjustment are likely to become more important. 
Capacity glut 
Capacity remains relatively easy to build, but returns on AI investment disappoint. 
Most capacity is used, although pricing may be insufficient to justify the scale of 
investment. 

8 
 
capitalgroup.com 
Credit outcomes are likely to diverge. Lower investment could reduce hyperscaler 
financing needs and support free cash flow, while weaker borrowers and financings 
dependent on high utilisation or continued access to capital would be more exposed. 
 
Build and they come 
AI investment generates sufficient returns, while additional capacity remains relatively 
easy to build. Utilisation remains healthy and financing expands alongside cash flow. 
This would generally be supportive for credit, particularly for issuers able to fund a 
significant share of investment from operating cash flow. However, the pace of 
expansion matters. A more aggressive acceleration in AI investment could drive a 
much larger increase in capex and debt issuance, creating greater technical and 
balance-sheet pressure. Companies entering the cycle with diversified operating 
cash flows, low leverage and strong balance sheets should be better placed to 
absorb this than those already operating with more stretched credit metrics. 
Seller’s market 
AI generates attractive economic returns, but constraints on power, sites, chips or 
permitting limit additional capacity. Existing assets benefit from scarcity and stronger 
pricing, while new entrants face higher costs. 
Credit outcomes would vary according to issuers’ starting positions. Companies with 
strong balance sheets, diverse cash flows and access to scarce inputs should be 
better placed to manage continued investment than more leveraged borrowers. 
AI hangover 
AI remains useful, but economic returns disappoint and spending settles at a lower 
level. Utilisation takes longer to normalise, and the investment cycle extends rather 
than ending abruptly. 
Lower capital expenditure could support hyperscaler free cash flow and balance 
sheets. Refinancing risk would be greater for borrowers that depend on continued AI-
related growth, narrow revenue streams or ongoing access to external financing. 
The same AI outcome may have different implications for equity and credit investors. 
Strong demand can support revenues but also sustain capital expenditure and 
borrowing. Weaker demand may weigh on earnings while prompting stronger 
issuers to reduce investment and financing needs. Credit outcomes will therefore 
depend on realised AI economics, the pace of capacity growth, financing conditions 
and each issuer’s financial starting point 
These outcomes may also evolve over different horizons. An acceleration in AI 
investment could initially weigh on spreads through heavier issuance, even where the 
longer-term effect is supportive because the investment generates stronger revenues 
and cash flows. Conversely, a slowdown in investment could initially support free cash 
flow and reduce supply, while creating longer-term pressure for issuers and 
structures dependent on continued AI-related growth. For long-term investors, it is 
therefore important to distinguish temporary technical pressure from a lasting 
deterioration in credit fundamentals. 
 
AI disruption: opportunities and risks beyond the direct stack 
The credit implications of AI extend beyond the companies directly financing the 
infrastructure buildout. Demand for power, cooling, networking and electrical 
equipment may support selected utilities and industrial issuers. Elsewhere, AI can

have very different effects even within the same sector, benefitting companies able to 
use it to improve productivity or strengthen margins while disrupting businesses 
whose competitive advantages can be replicated or automated. The effects of AI will 
therefore be uneven, including within individual sectors. 
Spotlight on software and high yield 
The software sector has been in the spotlight this year and serves as somewhat of a 
cautionary tale for disruption by AI. This has negatively impacted credit markets as 
investors re-price “terminal value” expectations for companies within this sector, 
particularly at the lower quality end of the spectrum. 
The pressure has not fallen evenly. Businesses with contracted revenues, 
embedded enterprise relationships and genuine pricing power may be better 
placed to withstand disruption. By contrast, companies whose value depends 
on continued high growth, easy refinancing or a technological moat that AI 
can erode face a more difficult refinancing equation.  
To date, this has primarily impacted the Leveraged Loans and Direct Lending 
markets where much of the lending to the software sector has taken place. 
Noting the challenges of limited transparency in these markets, we estimate 
that 15-25%4 of these markets is loans to software companies. By contrast, in 
public high yield and investment grade markets Software accounts for 2-3% 
of the index.  
Many of the companies within this sector borrowed heavily from private credit and 
loans market, during the ultra-low rate period immediately after Covid. As the 2020/1 
vintage of loans approaches maturity, those companies face significantly higher 
financing costs, potentially leading to write downs from private credit lenders (and 
related private equity sponsors).

Putting these ideas into action in portfolios 
1. Defensive tilt with a focus on cashflow certainty  
For credit investors, the framework we have outlined argues for a defensive bias 
toward cash flow certainty. Higher-quality infrastructure financings can combine 
established operating cash flows, strong balance sheets and cash flow predictability 
from capacity leased to high-quality hyperscalers. Moving further towards the 
application layer of the AI stack introduces greater terminal value risk without the 
uncapped upside available to equity investors. Such markets therefore require a 
more cautious and selective approach for credit investors.  
 
2. Selectivity at the issuer level matters more than thematic exposure 
The expansion of AI-related debt has widened the opportunity set, but thematic 
exposure alone is not sufficient. We favour liquid securities issued by companies with 
diversified business models and infrastructure that can be deployed across several 
customers or use cases. 
In terms of our broad portfolio positioning, we maintain a modest, selective 
overweight to hyperscalers and investment-grade data centres. This positioning 
reflects our confidence that these companies can generate meaningful AI-related 
revenue while balancing the risk of continued heavy debt issuance. Importantly, even 
within the hyperscaler we are differentiating amongst issuers and, even more 
crucially, amongst the myriad of SPV structures hyperscalers are using to issue bonds.  
We also favour select industrial suppliers, including companies that provide power 
generation and infrastructure equipment. In our view, these companies benefit from 
AI-related infrastructure demand without necessarily carrying the same financing and 
contingent-liability risk as hyperscalers.  
On the other hand, we have less exposure to semiconductor-related issuers where 
companies are taking on additional balance sheet risks to facilitate customers chip 
purchases. 
 
3. Patience and agility: gradually scaling into positions 
The volume of AI-related issuance is unprecedented and, given the scale of financing 
required, is likely to remain elevated. This creates an unusual market environment in 
which spread volatility may be driven as much by financing needs and supply 
dynamics as by underlying credit fundamentals.  
For active investors, this distinction matters. The persistence of elevated levels of 
issuance is likely to create periods of market indigestion and greater valuation 
dispersion, resulting in more attractive entry points even when the underlying credit 
thesis remains intact. Rather than seeking to identify the precise peak in issuance or 
the widest point in spreads, we believe it can be more effective to build positions 
gradually, the flexibility to add exposure as valuations evolve. 
Higher-quality AI-related bonds can play different roles across portfolios. Some 
investors view them primarily as a carry opportunity, offering attractive income from 
fundamentally strong credits. Others are more cautious in the near term and see the 
opportunity as one of medium- to long-term spread compression once supply 
pressures begin to normalise. They may therefore prefer to scale into positions 
patiently as issuance creates more attractive entry points.

Diversification beyond the AI ecosystem also remains important, both to preserve 
portfolio resilience and to avoid an excessive concentration of correlated technology 
and financing risk.  
Ultimately, the AI build-out reinforces an enduring principle of credit investing: 
durable cash flows matter more than compelling narratives. The scale of the capital 
cycle may be unprecedented, but the questions facing credit investors remain 
familiar. Which issuers can sustain elevated investment, maintain resilient balance 
sheets and continue generating the cash flows needed to meet their obligations? The 
disciplines required to answer those questions remain unchanged.
