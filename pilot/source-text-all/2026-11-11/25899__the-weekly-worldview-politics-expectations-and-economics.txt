The near-term economic impact of the US midterms is narrow. 
Tariffs, immigration, and deregulation remain under executive 
control, while the fiscal impulse is small.  Imports offset a large 
part of AI capex, and shifts in AI policy would have a limited 
effect on growth.  
M  
Elections have consequences, but the economic implications are not always 
immediate or broad – or so we wrote in the Sunday Start last week. There is no 
denying that last week's US midterms elections brought sweeping change to 
Congress and a number of states. And post hoc ergo propter hoc fallacies aside, the 
market has moved a lot in the wake of the results. But we still think that the near-
term macro impulse is narrower. While Democrats swept the legislative branch at 
the federal level, the White House is, of course, unchanged. Because the President 
has authority over tariffs, immigration, and much of the regulatory agenda, 
legislative change, if it comes, might be a marginal adjustment to some domestic 
spending but not the key policy themes for macro. 
For AI, and particularly in Texas and Ohio, where the executive branch turned over 
to Democrats, but the legislatures did not, state and local governments matter. 
Whereas the election was necessarily partisan, some Republicans ran on anti-data 
center platforms – AI policy in the United States is still evolving. There is a risk that 
some projects could slip from 2028 to 2029, but we remain constructive on the 
overall buildout. In our view, newly elected Governors are unlikely to disrupt 
projects already under construction, so we leave 2027 projections unchanged. It is 
possible that some 2028 projects in Texas and Ohio could get pushed to 2029 as 
governors put new data center legislation in place. 
Those types of adjustments would not be substantive for overall US GDP growth. 
Estimates out to 2028 and 2029 are inherently murky anyway, and chip financing 
looks relatively insulated, so our ~$1.6trn hyperscaler capex base case for 2027 is 
intact. Given that much of the AI equipment is imported, a downside to headline 
capex would have a smaller impact on domestic GDP.

The trade read-through is similar. US AI imports have supported export growth 
across Asia, which makes Asian trade data an important lead indicator for the 
investment cycle. The election may slow US capacity additions in some places, but it 
does not point to a broad reversal of the buildout. The more likely outcome is a 
more conditional cycle: projects proceed where power, permitting, and local politics 
are supportive, and capacity migrates away from places where approval gets harder. 
That changes the geography of investment more than the direction of demand. 
Indeed, the shift from microchips to broader Korean exports is likely an early signal 
that trade flows next year become less concentrated and broader based. Taiwan and 
Korea remain central to chips and memory, but Mexico is important for server 
assembly, Southeast Asia is gaining exposure in networking and optical equipment, 
and Japan and Europe are increasingly involved in semiconductor equipment, 
turbines, transformers, and other power infrastructure. At the same time, a larger 
power shortfall in the US could allow datacenter demand to spill over to Asia and 
EMEA, accelerating a dynamic our equity analysts have already been highlighting.

Fiscal policy is the more direct federal channel, but this is not primarily a deficit 
story. A Democratic majority could delay or soften the scheduled SNAP and 
Medicaid cuts, but the growth effect would be small: even a full rollback of the 
measures due to take effect in January 2027 would add less than ½ pct pt to real 
spending growth. Nor do we expect a material change in the deficit. Even if these 
SNAP and Medicaid cuts were fully reversed, we think deficits would stay around

The bottom line is one of scope. The political change may well be meaningful and 
have implications for AI permitting, fiscal negotiations, and therefore differentiated 
implications across asset classes. But the near-term aggregate economic channels 
are narrower than the political headline. Tariffs, immigration, and much of the 
deregulatory agenda remain largely under executive authority, so the election 
mainly shifts the probability distribution around a limited set of legislative and local-
policy outcomes.
