Europe in the AI universe 
 
 
Where does Europe stand on AI ? 
Europe is not at the centre of the AI economy. The US has captured the first stage of 
the cycle because it is home to the leading innovators, the largest spenders through the 
Hyperscalers and several of the most direct beneficiaries. As markets increasingly 
question the returns on this investment, we argue that Europe can benefit by supplying 
the ‘picks & shovels’ of the AI build-out and, ultimately, capturing productivity gains. 
Europe should therefore be viewed as a second-order AI beneficiary, not as a failed 
version of the US. 
AI adoption is already broad on both sides of the Atlantic. Around 70% of S&P 500 
companies and 60% of STOXX 600 companies mentioned AI during their latest earnings 
calls. We do not view this as a significant gap and, at the current pace, it could close 
within a year (Exhibit 1). The comparison is also influenced by sector composition. As 
expected, AI features more prominently in services and labour-intensive businesses: 
Technology, Communication Services and Financials are among the sectors that mention 
it most frequently, while Materials and Utilities rank towards the lower end (Exhibit 2). 
 
Europe in the AI universe 
Europe captures less of the immediate AI earnings upside, but its earnings profile is 
also less dependent on the AI cycle. AI Infrastructure companies (Phase 2) represent 
c.15% of STOXX 600 market capitalisation and consensus expect them to contribute 
2pp of the 9% increase in European earnings forecast for 2027 (Exhibit 3). By contrast, 
NVIDIA, the Hyperscalers and other US AI Infrastructure companies represent c.40% of 
the S&P 500 and consensus expect them to account for 9pp of its 13% earnings growth. 
Europe’s lower AI concentration therefore comes with a trade-off: less participation in 
the upside, but broader and more diversified earnings growth. If the AI earnings boom 
were to pause, underlying earnings growth would be stronger in Europe than in the 
US.

Visible earnings have beaten distant optionality. In the US market, investors have 
rewarded Phases 1 and 2 because AI capital expenditure has translated rapidly into 
orders, revenues and EPS upgrades (Exhibit 6). NVIDIA is up around 11-fold since the

Europe AI Phase 3: AI-enabled revenues  
Phase 3 now demands delivery rather than promises. In the early days of AI, Software 
and IT Services were seen as potential major beneficiaries. Investors expected Software 
providers to embed AI rapidly into existing products, while IT Services companies would 
lower their own costs and help traditional enterprises deploy new tools. That investment 
case has come under scrutiny as AI lowers barriers to entry and raises questions about 
incumbent economics. In Europe, Software and IT Services have de-rated from nearly 
25x P/E in early 2025 to 17.5x today (Exhibit 16). Importantly, this multiple compression 
has not been driven by weaker earnings or a broad deterioration in fundamentals: 
margins and ROE remain high and broadly stable (Exhibit 18). Instead, investors are 
discounting the risk that cheaper, AI-native entrants erode competitive moats, displace 
existing offerings and weaken terminal growth and margins. The rotation towards Heavy 
Assets, Low Obsolescence (HALO) businesses reinforced the pressure, leaving many 
capital-light companies trading at materially lower valuations (Exhibit 17). 
 
Disruption fears may have gone too far. GS Hedge Fund positioning data suggest that 
the AI winners-versus-losers trade became increasingly simplified as long Hardware 
versus short Software, with positioning reaching extremes on both sides over the 
summer. More recently, this has started to reverse, alongside a rebound in Software. 
Management commentary during the latest earnings season also suggests that 
enterprise customers continue to purchase premium AI functionality from established 
providers rather than broadly abandoning trusted vendors to build solutions internally. 
The feared “SaaSpocalypse” is therefore yet to materialise. Software and IT Services 
companies are also responding with greater cost discipline and cautious hiring, 
consistent with the weak employment component of the PMI despite resilient headline 
activity (Exhibit 19).

Europe AI Phase 4: AI productivity opportunity 
Productivity is the least proven but potentially largest opportunity. Phase 4 has not 
yet worked as an equity strategy, but we think it remains the most important long-term 
part of the AI story. If AI is the revolution implied by current investment, its benefits 
cannot remain confined to Technology. Our economists’ base case is that AI ultimately 
pays off through higher productivity and economy-wide profits. 
Europe’s structural constraints could become an AI advantage. Labour costs 
represent a larger share of sales in Europe than in the US, increasing the potential 
benefit from automation. An ageing workforce, persistent labour shortages, rigid labour 
markets and demanding regulation have also weighed on European productivity. AI 
could ease these constraints by automating routine tasks, simplifying processes and 
increasing output from existing labour resources. However, employment protection, 
collective bargaining and regulation may slow the conversion of productivity gains into 
margins. 
The opportunity should be assessed through both revenue and margins. We 
estimate the potential earnings boost from AI adoption for each STOXX 600 company by 
combining (1) labour costs as a share of revenue with (2) the share of each company’s 
wage bill exposed to AI automation. We then estimate two potential benefits:

We introduce a screen of Long-term AI Beneficiaries. We screen STOXX 600 
companies under two AI productivity scenarios: higher revenue at stable labour costs, 
and stable revenue at lower labour costs. First, companies must rank in the top half of 
their sector for potential earnings uplift under both scenarios. Second, we select those 
with the largest potential margin uplift. Finally, we combine exposure with evidence, 
requiring management commentary to identify AI as a driver of productivity or 
efficiency. The resulting screen contains 50 STOXX 600 stocks, is equal-weighted and 
sector-neutral (Exhibit 32).  
The screen provides strong exposure to the potential productivity gains from AI. It is 
by construction labour-intensive, with a bias towards services and capital-light 
businesses despite being sector-neutral. The median company has labour costs 
equivalent to 26% of sales, more than 1.5x the median STOXX 600 company. At the 
same time, more than 35% of the median constituent’s wage bill is exposed to AI 
automation, placing it around the 70th percentile in the STOXX 600. Combined, these 
characteristics translate into potential EPS gains of c.45%, roughly twice the market 
median, and around 4.5pp of potential margin uplift, both ranking around the top 
quartile of the market. 
 
The productivity opportunity comes with some risks: 
 1. Theoretical exposure may not translate into realised EPS. Companies must 
successfully implement AI, absorb the associated costs and retain enough of the 
resulting productivity gains for them to translate into higher profits. Some benefits may 
accrue to customers, employees or suppliers rather than shareholders. The gap between 
discussion and delivery remains wide: in 2026, 57% of STOXX 600 companies mentioned 
AI and 41% discussed productivity or efficiency, but only 10% quantified the impact on 
a specific application and just 2% quantified the impact on earnings (Exhibit 27). The 
burden of proof must therefore move from identifying use cases to demonstrating 
operational savings, margin expansion or EPS growth.

 2. The largest productivity beneficiaries may also face the greatest disruption risk. 
Business Services, Telecommunications and Insurance have large labour and 
administrative cost bases, creating substantial scope for AI-driven efficiencies. Yet many 
of the same activities have low barriers to entry and are vulnerable to automation and 
AI-native competitors. Cost savings may support near-term margins without protecting 
the long-term value of the underlying business model.  
 3. AI agents could weaken pricing power and customer retention. By automating 
product comparisons, contract negotiations and switching, agents could reduce 
customer inertia in Insurance, Telecommunications and Business Services. Companies 
with the greatest scope to lower costs may therefore also face the greatest pressure on 
revenues and margins. Phase 4 exposure should therefore be combined with a 
disruption test: high theoretical productivity potential is attractive only where AI 
reinforces, rather than undermines, the company’s competitive position.  
 4. Labour displacement could amplify the economic cycle. Across US 
non-manufacturing industries, higher AI adoption is already associated with weaker 
employment growth and higher unemployment (Exhibit 28 and Exhibit 29). Historically, 
a 1% technology-driven productivity gain has temporarily raised unemployment by 
0.3-0.4pp, mainly through higher job losses. Under our economists’ assumption of a 15% 
gross productivity boost from AI, up to 9% of workers could be displaced from their 
current roles. The effect has typically been larger when unemployment is already rising, 
suggesting that AI-driven labour substitution could amplify the next downturn.
