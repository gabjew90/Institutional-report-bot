Saudi Arabia cuts December Asia oil prices to fresh 6-year low 
Saudi Aramco cut its December price for its Arab Light grade for Asian customers by $2 a barrel versus November on 
Friday, to a discount of $6.50 a barrel to the Oman/Dubai average, the lowest since May 2020 during the pandemic. The 
producer also cut December prices across all grades for Northwest Europe and the Mediterranean by $1 a barrel while 
keeping prices unchanged in the US, a pricing document showed. 
Market participants were expecting Aramco to keep prices for Asia unchanged or cut by up to $2 a barrel to entice buyers 
due to ample Middle East supplies and weaker spot market premiums, according to a Reuters survey last week. 
Spot premiums for Middle Eastern crude benchmarks returned to levels seen before the US-Israeli war on Iran this week 
as producers ramped up exports despite rising attacks on tankers crossing the Strait of Hormuz, while cooling refining 
margins in Asia slowed buying interest. 
HSBC raised its fourth-quarter crude production forecast for Saudi Arabia to 8.3 million bpd from 6.3 million bpd, citing a 
faster-than-expected export recovery. The bank raised its 2027 production forecast to 9.8 million bpd from 8.8 million bpd 
and said it expects output to return to about 10 million bpd by mid-2027. 
 
Soaring freight rates give US refiners edge as crude discounts widen 
The Iran conflict has sent tanker rates soaring, forcing sellers to deepen discounts on heavy crude grades and giving US 
Gulf Coast refiners a cost advantage over Asian rivals, executives said after reporting bumper third-quarter profits. Security 
threats around the Strait of Hormuz, a key maritime chokepoint through which about one-fifth of global oil and liquefied 
natural gas supplies passed before the war started, have disrupted tanker traffic, while higher war-risk insurance premiums 
and longer voyages have driven up shipping costs.  
The disruption particularly benefited US Gulf Coast refiners, which could buy discounted heavy crude produced closer to 
home, including from Canada and Venezuela, while Asian rivals faced sharply higher freight and insurance costs to import 
similar grades through longer and riskier shipping routes. US refining margins more than doubled in the third quarter. 
Rising freight costs have widened discounts on Canadian heavy crude, including Western Canadian Select (WCS), which 
typically trades below US benchmark West Texas Intermediate (WTI), creating a windfall for Gulf Coast refiners, as more 
than 70% of their facilities are configured to process heavier crude. 
"The main story right now in the market is freight rates," Randy Hawkins, vice president of Crude And Feedstocks Supply 
And Trading at Valero Energy, said on a post-earnings conference call. 
Freight costs for shipping crude from the US Gulf Coast to Asia have jumped to $30-$35 a barrel from about $5 a year 
earlier, Hawkins said. "Higher freight rates actually give us an advantage into the typical export markets into Latin America," 
said Gary Simmons, chief operating officer of Valero, adding that it mainly competes with European barrels in that market. 
Ample heavy crude supplies from Canada and Venezuela, combined with elevated freight rates, have widened the WTI-
WCS differential, lowering feedstock costs, US refining executives said.

Canadian heavy crude prices have lagged the US benchmark for most of the year after the US-Israeli war on Iran began. 
For Phillips 66 every $1 widening in the WCS-WTI differential is worth about $140 million in gains, Brian Mandell, executive 
vice president of marketing and commercial, said.  
Higher shipping costs have also been more than offset by stronger fuel margins, said Delia He, an oil and tanker market 
analyst at Arrow Shipping. 
"For every extra dollar of freight, the diesel margin has grown by at least eight," He said. 
POSITIONED FOR A STRONG 2027 
US refiners have also been shielded from the impacts of higher spot tanker rates following the war due to their advance 
shipping arrangements. Phillips 66 said it had secured more than 40 time-charter vessels before the Iran conflict, covering 
about 40% of its asset-based shipping needs. 
Valero, PBF, Marathon and Canada's Suncor also secured chartered fleets before the war began, insulating them from the 
surge in prices. 
Wider differentials are expected to continue to lend strength next year as well, some industry executives said.  
Top US refiner Marathon Petroleum expects record Canadian heavy crude processing in the fourth quarter, while CEO 
Maryann Mannen has said that margins are expected to remain elevated through at least mid-2027.  
"In the near term we will take advantage of it," HF Sinclair said of the wider discounts, adding, "we do see the differentials 
stay wider than they have throughout the near term, at least through 2027." 
 
 
FOCUS- Farmers fight to adapt as droughts loom in COP31 host Turkey 
Farmer Mehmet Kabak has grown cotton on the same land in drought-prone western Turkey for decades. This year he is 
planting wheat and sunflower instead, in part because they require less water, making them cheaper to grow. Kabak is one 
of many farmers worldwide forced to find their own way to cope with a changing climate rather than wait for countries to 
settle long-standing arguments at annual climate talks over who should finance adaptation. 
Agriculture accounts for around 5% of Turkey's economic output. It also consumes 79% of the country's water, almost half 
of which is lost because of inefficient infrastructure and irrigation, the government has said. As the next round of climate 
talks begins on Monday at COP31 in Antalya on the Mediterranean coast, 59-year-old Kabak, who farms 900 decares 
(about 220 acres) in Soke, hundreds of kilometres to the west, cannot afford to keep making losses.  
Government support payments have not kept pace with rising fuel and fertiliser costs linked to irrigation and the US-Israeli 
war with Iran. "We had increases in irrigation costs even though our harvest is damaged," Kabak said. "Our input costs 
increased by 40%, 50%, 60% continuously. Fuel prices did not stop."  
SHRINKING COTTON ACREAGE 
Turkey was the world's eighth-largest cotton producer, according to 2023 data from the UN's Food and Agriculture 
Organization, but its overall seed cotton output is expected to fall 38% year-on-year to 1.2 million metric tons in 2026, 
according to the Turkish statistics institute.

Drought and increased costs have led farmers in the Soke region to shrink cotton acreage by around 60% to around 120,000 
decares in 2026, a local farming group said. More than 30 farmers, lenders, companies and non-profit organisations told 
Reuters in interviews the sector needed more technical support and help gaining access to finance. "Adaptation is no longer 
optional for farmers, but it is expensive, and today they largely pay for it themselves," said Aysegul Selisik, Turkey 
representative of the FAO. 
TEMPORARY REPRIEVE FROM WATER SHORTAGE 
For agriculture, water is the most pressing adaptation issue. Last year, Turkey suffered the worst drought in over 50 years. 
Rainfall was 27% below the long-term average. Dam levels have recovered to an extent, government data showed, but 
analysts say the reprieve is likely to be temporary. 
Reuters analysis of data from the WWF and Lincoln Institute of Land Policy showed water use in Turkey's cotton-producing 
basins needs to fall 25% to 60% to sustain healthy ecosystems. Existing infrastructure is inefficient. Water drawn from dams 
and rivers often arrives unfiltered, clogging drip hoses before infrastructure to clean it is built. But farmers are reluctant to 
invest for a variety of reasons. In some cases, switching to newer systems can lead to an initial drop in yield that farmers 
may struggle to absorb. In addition, many work on rented land, meaning they are reluctant to invest, industry representatives 
said. 
STATE AND PRIVATE SECTOR OFFER SOME SUPPORT  
Turkey said in October, when it laid out next year's budget, the amount set aside for land and water resources management 
would rise 40.5%, with 174 billion lira ($3.5 billion) earmarked for agricultural irrigation investments. The Agriculture Ministry 
told Reuters further support would be needed to supplement that, including from the private sector. 
Turkey's largest private lender İşbank has opened 56 specialist agricultural branches and offers financing for drip and 
sprinkler irrigation with repayments tied to harvest cycles. Şekerbank, which has a history of supporting farmers, says 
around a quarter of loans made under its sustainable-finance framework in the first nine months of 2026 went to agricultural 
water management and climate adaptation. 
A lack of equity or collateral and the inability of some farmers to repay the loans, however, have limited the amount of help 
the private sector can deliver. Many farmers also have to pay upfront and get reimbursed later, if conditions are met. "That 
makes it very difficult for them," said Liam Chicca, country director for Turkey at the International Fund for Agricultural 
Development. 
The fund, which has invested $850 million in Turkey since 1982, estimates between 5% and 10% of Turkish farmers have 
virtually no capacity to finance major investments themselves. For Turkey — and other governments — failure to adapt 
could cost more than acting now. 
World Bank analysis in 2022 estimated a 10% reduction in water supply could cost Turkey 6% of GDP, or about $50 billion 
because of the impact on agriculture. Apart from the planned budget increase, Turkey's government has said private 
companies could help more by adopting water-efficiency certification and using green-finance instruments to fund 
adaptation, an approach it hopes to advance at COP31. 
Those monitoring progress are keen that whatever is proposed amounts to more than the kind of non-binding pledges that 
governments have made at previous UN conferences without delivering concrete change. "Success in Antalya should be 
measured not by pledges, but by how much finance reaches the farm and the watershed," said FAO's Selisik.

Citi says it remains bearish on coffee, sees arabica falling to $2.25/lb 
The commodities research arm of Citi said on Friday it remained bearish on coffee prices, forecasting arabica coffee futures 
on the ICE exchange to fall to $2.50 per lb in three months and $2.25 per lb in 12 months. The bank said the market is 
nearing an inflection point, with potential for a downward correction before year-end. 
Brazilian exports have been strong, with October shipments seen topping 5 million bags, while Vietnam's robusta supply 
continues to surprise to the upside, with output estimates raised by 2 million bags. 
Citi expects inventories to build meaningfully into year-end as demand weakens. Roasters are using more robusta, 
optimizing blends and cutting inventory coverage. 
Some institutional clients see arabica falling below $2 per lb. Money managers remain net long, though positioning has 
eased and gross shorts are rising. 
A strengthening El Niño, likely to persist through January-March 2027, is the main upside risk, Citi said, with exposure 
concentrated in Central America, notably Honduras, and Colombia. 
Low ICE-certified stocks pose a risk to market bears, while a milder-than-feared EU deforestation rule could add downside 
pressure. 
 
 
REUTERS OPEN INTEREST-Gold's short-term volatility contrasts with long-term bullishness: Russell 
Gold appears to be finding a floor around $4,000 an ounce as it battles competing short-term influences and a shift in how 
it is traded. The spot price of the precious metal ended at $4,065.63 an ounce on November 6, having recovered from its 
low so far this year of $3,869.85 on October 30. 
Gold's recovery from its most recent drop below $4,000 an ounce came after it performed a similar trick in July, when it 
dipped on several occasions below the key psychological level, only to recover. The yellow metal also came close to 
dropping below $4,000 an ounce in March, when it slipped to $4,097.99 on March 23, as it corrected amid profit-taking after 
reaching a record high of $5,594.82 on January 29. 
It has been a fairly wild ride for gold in 2026 and much of the commentary has focused on how it appears to have largely 
lost its traditional role as a hedge against inflation and geopolitical turmoil. Rather it seems that gold has witnessed more 
speculative trading, although much of the froth and hot money has been washed out in recent months as it flirted with a 
break below $4,000 an ounce. 
However, gold remains in short-term limbo, caught between competing influences and vulnerable to swings in either 
direction. The main risk is weakness in equity markets as investors question the AI investment boom, especially in the wake 
of the overwhelming victory in the US midterm elections by Democrats, many of whom questioned the need to build more 
energy-hungry data centres. 
Declining equities tend to spark a need for investors to liquidate other assets, and selling gold fits a historical pattern when 
stock markets correct. But short-term equity weakness may translate into a medium-term positive for gold, especially if 
weakness extends into the real economy. 
Part of the reason gold has struggled since its record high in January was the shift in monetary policy expectations in the 
United States to likely higher interest rates. But the emerging equity weakness and the recent drop in crude oil prices amid 
renewed hopes for an end to the Iran conflict are likely to once again shift the monetary policy dial back to at least neutral, 
and possibly even to rate cuts next year.

LONG-TERM BULLS 
If the short-term outlook for gold is cloudy and subject to developments in equities and Iran, the longer-term picture is 
somewhat clearer. Gold remains an attractive alternative for the world's central banks, especially if they are concerned 
about the long-term fiscal outlook in the United States, given what many investors believe to be an unsustainable fiscal 
deficit and a debt mountain that is eating up too big a share of government revenue to service. 
Central banks bought 321.2 metric tons of gold in the third quarter, up from 296.3 the previous quarter and the most since 
the last three months of 2024, according to World Gold Council figures. It is also worth noting that central bank demand is 
price-sensitive, with the weakest quarter this year being the first, when demand was just 57.7 tons as the gold price hit its 
record high. The recent drop to below $4,000 an ounce is likely to encourage central bank buying in the current quarter. 
Investors are also showing more interest in gold, with council data showing inflows of 19 tons in October into exchange-
traded funds, adding to the 211.2 tons seen in the third quarter, which reversed a decline of 47.3 tons in the second. The 
third leg of gold demand is jewellery, which is led by China and India, which together account for almost two-thirds of global 
demand. 
Lower prices in recent months are likely to boost jewellery demand, with traders in India reporting increased interest as key 
festivals approach. India's jewellery demand was 99.9 tons in the third quarter, according to the World Gold Council, the 
most since the fourth quarter of last year. China's jewellery demand rose to 71.9 tons in the third quarter, up from 55.7 tons 
in the second. 
Overall, the short-term drivers of gold have shifted to be more reliant on speculative trading driven by moves in equities and 
crude oil, especially as they relate to changing monetary policy expectations. But the longer-term bull case of asset 
diversification amid global fiscal issues and rising jewellery demand in India and China remains in place.

REUTERS OPEN INTEREST-Tin hit by AI stock rout despite physical market tightness: Andy Home  
The AI stocks meltdown is spilling over into the metals markets as investors rapidly reassess the prospects for data center 
demand. Tin, which had been riding the AI wave on the back of its use as an electronic solder, has taken a particularly hard 
knock after Wall Street lost $3.2 trillion in a single session on Thursday as investors feared Democrats could slow the 
buildout of AI data centers. 
London Metal Exchange (LME) three-month tin CMSN3 has slumped from over $50,000 per metric ton to an eight-month 
low of $45,360 in the space of a week. The bursting of tin's AI-investment bubble is ironically ill-timed since the market is 
the tightest it's been in many months. 
FALLING INVENTORY 
Even as the outright tin price tumbles, LME time spreads are flaring into wide backwardation, with metal for immediate 
delivery trading at a premium to later-dated contracts. The benchmark cash-to-three-months spread CMSN0-3 was in a 
deep contango, with prompt tin at a discount of more than $350 per ton to three-month delivery in August. Now, however, 
cash tin is commanding a $125-per-ton premium over three-month delivery, its highest premium since December.  
The tightening of the forward curve in London is mirrored by the Shanghai Futures Exchange (ShFE) contract, which has 
also moved into steep backwardation.  In both cases, the shift in curve structure is down to low exchange inventory.  
LME registered tin stocks have trended steadily lower since June and now total just 3,885 tons, their lowest level since 
December last year. Off-warrant inventory has also fallen to 1,089 tons, down 1,535 tons since the start of the year. ShFE 
inventory has collapsed even more dramatically, from 12,358 tons in June to 4,004 tons. Total exchange inventory, at just 
under 9,000 tons, stands at a four-year low.

SUPPLY STORY 
Low exchange stocks are largely a supply story. Indonesia, the world's largest exporter of refined tin, has seen an intensified 
government crackdown on illegal mining operations this year. That has fed through to lower exports, which fell by 14% 
year-on-year to 32,800 tons in the first nine months of the year.  
The Chinese market has tightened due to a 45-day maintenance shutdown at Yunnan Tin's 000960.SZ Gejiu smelter. The 
work began at the end of September, according to the International Tin Association (ITA).  
The company is the world's largest tin producer, and Gejiu produces 5,000-6,000 tons of refined metal each month. Its 
maintenance schedule can therefore significantly affect short-term dynamics in the Chinese market.  
Chinese demand, meanwhile, has moderated this year due to the slowdown in solar installations after a period of red-hot 
growth. The solar sector accounts for around 10% of global tin usage, in the form of tin-coated ribbons connecting individual 
photovoltaic cells.  
As for AI data center demand, it remains a powerful long-term driver but has yet to translate into a step-change in 
consumption, according to Tom Langston, senior market analyst at the ITA, speaking at last month's LME Seminar.  
The biggest impact of AI this year "has been on investor sentiment," Langston added.  As the tin market is currently finding 
out.

Iraq's Khor Mor gas field suspends output after drone attack, operator says 
Production was halted on Sunday at Iraq's Khor Mor gas field, one of the largest in the Kurdistan region, following a 
"suspected" drone attack, joint field operator Dana Gas said in a statement. 
The company based in the United Arab Emirates said in Monday's statement it was assessing the damage after dousing a 
fire sparked by the attack, with no injuries to staff. About 2.8 GW of power generation in the Kurdistan region was taken out 
by the attack, the region's electricity ministry said in a statement. 
Production at Khor Mor, which supplies regional power generation, has repeatedly been disrupted after attacks that Kurdish 
officials have blamed on Iranian-backed militias. In July, Dana Gas shut down the field's main production facilities, citing 
credible security threats and regional tension before restarting production later that month. 
There was no claim of responsibility for Sunday's attack. Two sources in the region's electricity ministry said a single drone 
strike hit the LPG storage tank in the field, causing the fire. Smoke was seen rising from the field, two security sources 
added. Employees at the field were evacuated to shelters in the facility after the attack, the ministry sources added. All the 
sources spoke on condition of anonymity. 
In a joint statement, the region's natural resources and electricity ministries described the incident as a "terrorist attack" and 
said they were working to minimise its impact. The Kurdistan Regional Government exercises autonomy in northern Iraq, 
where it maintains close ties with Washington, underpinned by significant US investments in the region's energy sector. 
The federal government in Baghdad, has sought to balance ties with both Iran and the US, amid their military escalation. 
That balancing act has often been complicated by the Iran-backed militants still holding power in Iraq, who targeted attacks 
on Gulf neighbours at the beginning of the conflict. 
Iran, in turn, has launched attacks against Iranian Kurdish opposition groups based in northern Iraq.

EU grid investment to keep rising toward 2030, ACER says  
The European Union Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER) said electricity grid investment is expected 
to keep rising towards 2030, while delays and congestion could push up costs for consumers. Of the 18 countries that gave 
estimates for the report, 15 expect electricity grid investment in 2030 to exceed 2025 levels. 
The Netherlands expects investment to reach almost five times its 2025 level, while Estonia, Latvia and Cyprus foreceast 
declines. Grid costs for consumers could rise 50% to 100% by 2050 compared with current levels, according to sector 
estimates. 
A need for higher grid investment, which should push up network tariffs, and rising manufacturing prices are expected to 
be among the main drivers of the cost increase, ACER said. EU gas demand is expected to fall about 18% from 2025 levels 
by 2030 to meet climate targets, leaving fewer customers to share fixed network costs and putting upward pressure on 
tariffs. 
 
 
 
 
Brazil coffee exports hit record in October, government data 
Coffee exports from top producer Brazil reached a record of around 287,300 metric tons in October, the equivalent of 4.79 
million 60-kg bags, government data showed on Friday. The volume of Brazilian coffee exports rose 23.3% in October from 
a year earlier, the data showed. 
Export revenue stood at $1.6 billion in the month, rising 5.2%, though prices declined nearly 15%. The previous monthly 
record for Brazilian coffee exports by volume was set in November 2024. 
A record month had been expected by the market, as traders mentioned pressure on futures contracts from rising shipments 
out of Brazil. Brazil's coffee exports have gathered pace after harvest delays due to rainfall. 
Spain to hold back 12% of olive oil production to stabilise prices after bumper harvest 
Spain on Friday ordered olive oil producers to hold back 12% of their production for the 2026/27 season to control prices 
following estimates for a bumper harvest, the Agriculture Ministry said in a statement. Olive oil producers were ordered to 
store 12% of production until September 30, 2027 starting on Friday. The order implies removal of 188,000 tonnes from the 
market.  
"The aim is to regulate supply in order to improve and stabilise the functioning of the market and the sector," the Agriculture 
Ministry said in a statement. The Agriculture Ministry estimates production for the 2026/27 season of 1.6 million tonnes, 
23% bigger than last season. 
Mild temperatures and abundant rain during spring created conditions for what Spain expects to be its fourth best harvest 
on record. The olive oil market has recovered from a period of low production caused by drought and heatwaves in the 
2022/23 and 2023/24 seasons when prices hit record levels, leading households to buy more sunflower oil instead.
