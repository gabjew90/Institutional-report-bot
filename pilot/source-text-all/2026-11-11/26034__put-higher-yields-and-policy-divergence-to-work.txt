Thought of the day
Major central banks have continued to respond to elevated inflation 
pressures, with the Bank of England (BoE) the latest to tighten policy. The BoE 
raised its bank rate by 25 basis points to 4% on Thursday, its first increase 
in more than three years. G10 central banks have now delivered 350bps of 
increases across 14 hikes this year, including 150bps from six central banks in 
September alone, the largest monthly tightening push since June 2023. The 
pace slowed in October, as the Federal Reserve held rates at 3.75-4%, the 
European Central Bank (ECB) kept its deposit rate at 2.5%, and the Bank of 
Japan (BoJ) maintained its policy rate at 1.25%. With inflation still elevated 
and energy prices risks lingering, policymakers have kept the door open to 
further increases.
But we think investors should be careful about extrapolating this tightening 
much further. Our base case remains for a relatively shallow hiking cycle, with 
the scale and timing of additional moves likely to differ across economies. 
We expect 25bps in Fed hikes in December and March 2027, while the BoJ 
is expected to hike in December and March before pausing at 1.75%. We 
expect another 25bps ECB hike in December and a further 25bps BoE increase 
in February. We also expect the Swiss National Bank to raise rates twice in 
the first half of 2027, taking its policy rate to 0.5%. Beyond this, we believe 
the case for a prolonged tightening cycle is less assured.
The cumulative impact of higher rates and bond market pressures 
should limit further rate rises. Long-term government bond yields touched 
multi-decade highs in several major markets, adding to the tightening already 
delivered by central banks. In the US, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said financial and 
credit conditions have tightened for some businesses and households since 
September. And in the Eurozone, the ECB has acknowledged that tighter 
financing conditions are likely to weigh on growth and inflation, while its 
latest lending survey showed tighter corporate credit standards and weaker

Inflation dynamics are likely to limit the need for a prolonged hiking 
cycle. US inflation remains above target, with September core PCE at 
3.1% year over year, yet annualized core PCE slowed to 2.5% in the third 
quarter from 3.3% in the second. While higher inflation in the Eurozone has 
been driven significantly by energy, underlying inflation has remained more 
stable and wage pressures relatively contained. The data warrant continued 
vigilance, but do not yet point to broad second-round inflation pressures.
Lower energy prices could reinforce moderation in inflation pressures.
Brent crude prices are down around 15% this past week, while European 
natural gas prices dropped close to 11% as expectations that US-Iran 
negotiations could offer a path to deescalation have improved. While 
significant differences remain and risks around the Strait of Hormuz have 
not disappeared, we see scope for a gradual path to lower energy prices 
that should reduce headline inflation and limit the risk of higher energy costs 
feeding into wages and other prices.
So, we think the combination of a likely shallow hiking cycle and today’s 
elevated yields create an attractive entry point for fixed income. We 
favor quality short- and medium-maturity bonds, while remaining selective 
across duration and credit. Diverging monetary policy paths should also 
create opportunities in currencies, where we favor the British pound and 
Norwegian krone alongside selected emerging market carry opportunities, 
while remaining Neutral on the US dollar.
Caught our attention
Stronger US jobs data support gradual rate hikes. US nonfarm payrolls 
rose by 165,000 in October, well above consensus expectations for a 70,000 
increase. August and September payrolls were revised higher by a combined 
84,000, lifting average monthly job growth over the past three months 
to around 137,000. The unemployment rate edged down 3 basis points 
to 4.14%. The strength in employment was broad but not universal, with 
pockets of weakness in leisure and hospitality, as well as housing-related 
sectors. Average hourly earnings rose by a stronger-than-expected 0.4% over 
the month and 3.1% from a year earlier, although annual wage growth 
remained on a softening trend. Friday’s labor report came at the end of a 
volatile week for equity markets. The S&P 500 rose 0.3% on Friday but was 
down around 3% for the week, amid sharp swings in technology stocks 
as investors reassessed the outlook for AI infrastructure and data centers 
following Democratic gains in the midterm elections.
Our view: The October labor report supports continued gradual Fed 
tightening. Stronger employment momentum helps ease concerns that 
additional rate hikes could cause a sharp slowdown in the economy. 
We expect 25-basis-point increases in December and March, but stable 
unemployment and moderating annual wage growth limit the case for a 
more aggressive hiking cycle. With economic activity and corporate earnings 
growth solid, we expect further gains for broad equity indices over the next 
six to 12 months, despite the prospect of moderately higher policy rates. 
We also view the recent sell-off in tech stocks as a positioning reset rather 
than indicating a lasting downturn in the AI investment cycle. At the same 
time, with further volatility possible, investors should focus on managing 
concentration risks, rebalancing excessive technology exposure, and building 
a strong multi-asset core portfolio. Elevated yields offer an opportunity to add 
selectively to quality bonds.

Taiwan, Japan lead Asia technology rebound. Taiwanese stocks led a 
rebound across Asian technology markets on Monday, with the Taiex surging 
nearly 6% higher and ending a seven-session decline. The rally followed 
newly tightened short-selling restrictions and the authorities’ decision to 
activate the TWD 500 billion (USD 15bn) stabilization fund. Japan’s Nikkei 
225 also rebounded by more than 3%, while South Korea’s memory-heavy 
Kospi posted a more modest 0.7% rise. The regional recovery followed 
sharp AI-centered selling late last week, and has so far overshadowed a 
separate governance-related development involving the head of a leading US 
chipmaker.
Our view: While the dust is still settling after the global technology selloff, 
consensus earnings estimates for Taiwan for this year through 2028 continue 
to be revised higher as hyperscaler capital spending rises. We maintain our 
Attractive view on Taiwan, which remains a key beneficiary of sustained AI 
infrastructure investment and, in our view, offers the highest visibility on 
AI supply-chain earnings into next year. We also maintain our Attractive 
view on Japan, where current earnings, guidance, and orders remain solid, 
although we acknowledge visibility on AI capital expenditure for this market 
has weakened somewhat heading into 2028. Within Japan, we continue to 
like banks, supported by anticipated Bank of Japan tightening, loan growth, 
and consolidation among regional lenders. Across both markets, we favor 
quality companies with strong balance sheets.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
In line: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
