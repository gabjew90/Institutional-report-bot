The woke-broke redemption tour
Victoria’s Secret shares are getting smoked after earnings, down double digits as a strong quarter
collided with even stronger expectations.
Which seems like the perfect excuse to revisit one of corporate America’s great cautionary — and
redemption — tales. None of what follows is particularly new. But few companies have produced a
cleaner get woke → go broke → bring sexy back arc than Victoria’s Secret.
And with the stock having rallied more than 300% under its new CEO before today's reality check, the
story is simply too good not to tell again. And worth learning from.
The great unsexying
At its peak, Victoria's Secret was one of the most recognizable brands on the planet: Angels,
supermodels, lingerie and an annual fashion show watched by millions.
Then came the cultural reckoning. The fashion show disappeared in 2019. The Angels were retired.
And in 2021 Victoria's Secret unveiled the VS Collective, replacing them with ambassadors including
soccer star and activist Megan Rapinoe, actress Priyanka Chopra and plus-size model Paloma Elsesser.
Rapinoe called the old Victoria's Secret "patriarchal" and "sexist."
Apparently selling sexy underwear by being sexy was the problem.
Go Woke...
The reinvention didn't fix the business.
Victoria's Secret went from roughly $8.1 billion of sales and nearly one-third of the U.S. bra market in
2019 to around $6.2 billion and an 18% share by 2024.
To be fair, wokeness didn't cause all of that. The company was already struggling with competition,
stale merchandising, discounting and a brand that had lost relevance.
But its response to an identity crisis was remarkable:
It abandoned the identity that made it famous in the first place.

Bringing sexy back
Enter Hillary Super.


[Page 2]
Super took over as CEO in late 2024 and began doing something revolutionary:
Selling Victoria's Secret like Victoria's Secret again.
The fashion show returned. Glamour returned. Sexy returned. Promotions came down. Product
improved.
And in perhaps the greatest corporate ticker change of all time, Victoria's Secret changed its NYSE
symbol:
VSCO → VSXY
"Sexy has always been part of our DNA," the company said.
Turns out customers remembered.
The pictures are of Victoria's Secret's turnaround team: one runs the P&L, the others run the runway.
Talking stock
The chart tells the story better than we can.
Victoria's Secret was spun out of L Brands in August 2021. Over the next three years, the shares went
from post-spin highs to roughly $14 — an 80%+ drawdown.
Then look at the inflection. Hillary Super's appointment was announced in August 2024. The stock
jumped immediately, and what followed was one of retail's more spectacular reratings: from the 2024
lows to the recent peak above $100, VS rallied more than 600%. From around Super's arrival, the gain
was 300%+.
In fact, Victoria's Secret itself recently calculated total shareholder return since the announcement of
Super's appointment at 314%, versus roughly 30% for the S&P 600.
A little too sexy rally
There was just one problem with Victoria's Secret's turnaround:
Everyone knew. Everyone noticed. That was the backdrop into yesterday's earnings.
Q2 was objectively strong: sales +10.4%, comps +9%, EPS beat by 23% and EBIT margins crushed
expectations. Full-year guidance was raised. But after that rally, strong wasn't strong enough. Q3 EPS
guidance came in around breakeven versus $0.06 expected, while EBIT guidance missed consensus.
There's a better lesson here: turnaround stocks eventually become momentum stocks, and
momentum stocks stop trading on whether results are good and start trading on whether they're better
than increasingly heroic expectations.
Or put differently:
Victoria's Secret rediscovered sexy. Wall Street may simply have gotten a little too excited about it.
