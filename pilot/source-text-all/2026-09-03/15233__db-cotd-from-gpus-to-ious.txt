DB CoTD: From GPUs to IOUs  
 
One well-cited reason Treasury yields have risen in recent months has been the surge in non-government 
bond issuance, with the biggest surprise coming from the technology sector. As our US credit strategist 
Karthik Nagalingam highlighted in a note here yesterday, the upside surprise in issuance has been driven by 
three main factors. 
First, the hyperscalers have been raising capex guidance aggressively, with spending plans revised higher 
almost every quarter since last November. As a result, several companies have shifted from being free-cash-
flow positive to negative, increasing their reliance on debt financing. 
Second, SpaceX's bond issuance was largely unexpected, particularly given its sizeable recent equity raise. 
Third, semiconductor companies have brought large deals to market despite appearing to have relatively 
modest funding needs compared with expected operating income. 
US investment-grade corporate spreads are now around 8bp wider than the near-record tights reached at the 
end of May. Some of this supply has therefore created modest market indigestion. Even so, at roughly 79bp 
over Treasuries, the index remains relatively close to the tightest levels on record. 
As the first chart shows, our forecasts suggest that net US IG supply will reach a new all-time high this year, 
narrowly surpassing the refinancing surge seen during the pandemic in 2020. For now, however, strong 
inflows continue to absorb the increase in issuance, as highlighted in the second chart. Supply cannot be 
viewed in isolation from demand, and so far investor appetite has remained more than sufficient to absorb 
the increase in issuance. 
Moreover, the broader credit backdrop remains supportive. As Steve Caprio, Head of Credit Strategy, 
highlighted recently here, non-hyperscaler US corporate issuance has actually been fairly subdued this 
year, with little sign of a broader releveraging cycle emerging. Steve also notes the continued scarcity of long-

duration corporate supply. This stands in sharp contrast to 2015, when IG spreads were buckling under the 
weight of heavy net issuance driven by M&A activity and share buybacks, particularly at the long end given the 
exceptionally low-yield environment at the time. 
So, while US tech issuance may be creating some localised indigestion across US fixed-income markets, and 
any further increases in hyperscaler capex are certainly worth monitoring, it is important to view this in the 
context of both demand and the leverage cycle outside the technology sector. The broader supply-demand 
backdrop, together with the absence of a wider releveraging cycle, suggests that comparisons with previous 
supply-driven spread-widening episodes remain premature.
