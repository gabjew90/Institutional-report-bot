PM Summary 
 
 
The typical electric bill consists of three primary components: supply charges, 
delivery charges and other customer charges. In short, this covers the cost to generate 
power, deliver it to the end customer, maintain the grid, and (in some cases) implement 
state policy goals. Supply charges are primarily driven by natural gas prices, wholesale 
power costs and capacity markets; delivery charges reflect transmission and distribution 
investments; while other charges include taxes, public benefit programs, low-income 
assistance, clean energy mandates and wildfire-related costs. As a result, customer bill 
increases can stem from commodity costs, infrastructure investment, public policy 
programs, or a combination of all three. 
 
The drivers of affordability vary by market structure. In regulated markets, regulators 
set the price charged to customers, so bill growth is more closely linked to approved 
investments in generation, transmission and distribution infrastructure to maintain 
reliability, replace aging assets and support growing electricity demand. In deregulated 
markets, affordability pressures are often driven by wholesale electricity prices and 
capacity costs, which are determined in competitive markets and passed through to 
customers. As a result, identical bill increases can have very different underlying causes 
and implications depending on the regulatory framework. Consistent with this 
distinction, many of the states that have experienced the largest recent bill increases — 
including DC, New Jersey, Maryland and New York — are located in competitive power 
markets such as PJM, where tighter supply-demand balances and higher capacity prices 
have contributed to bill inflation.

n

Fixed customer charge 
What it is: A flat monthly fee (e.g., $10 to $20 per month) charged to every 
n

How customer bills differ across regulated and deregulated markets 
There are differences between customer bills in states that are deregulated versus 
regulated. For deregulated markets (as shown in Exhibit 8), supply and delivery charges 
are shown separately, allowing customers to see which portion of the bill is driven by 
wholesale power markets versus utility-owned infrastructure. In the interactive ConEd 
sample bill example shown above, the customer pays roughly $31 of supply charges and 
$72 of delivery charges. Supply cost is ultimately the revenue for the power generator, 
which in a deregulated state is not the regulated utility. The delivery charges reflect the 
regulated utility’s charge for recovering transmission and distribution costs to bring the 
power to the customer. Bills for regulated markets could be shown in a more consolidated 
way, due to the fact that the regulated utility provides the generation, transmission, and 
distribution services, and charges a regulator approved rate, alongside any approved 
riders/surcharges. Consequently, long-term bill growth tends to be more closely linked 
to utility investment in generation, transmission, and distribution infrastructure, as well 
as regulatory decisions regarding cost recovery, rather than short-term fluctuations in 
wholesale power markets, which are often deferred and recovered over longer time 
periods.

Snapshot on Utility Bill Trends 
 
 
The average monthly US residential electric utility bill is $171 as of June 2026 on a 
TTM basis, versus $165 as of June 2025. The range of electric utility bills for residential 
customers is wide, with the lowest monthly bill today in New Mexico at $76, and the 
highest monthly bill in Hawaii at $255. Utility bill inflation lagged CPI from 2008, but 
starting from 2021, it has outpaced CPI by 7pp. This accelerated bill inflation, coupled 
with the heavy election cycle this year, has driven utility bills into the national spotlight. 
Looking forward, we expect bill inflation across the US to average a 3.7% CAGR through 
2029, with bill growth moderating from 5.9% in 2026 to 2.1% by 2029.

De-Regulated Markets 
In deregulated states (such as the Mid-Atlantic, Northeast, and parts of California), the 
utility business is unbundled. The utility is legally prohibited from owning generation and 
acts solely as a delivery company. Consequently, the revenue requirement is split across 
different regulatory bodies and different bills: 
Distribution revenue requirement (State Level): The state PUC determines the 
n
revenue requirement solely for the local distribution system (the local poles, wires, 
and customer service). This is what dictates the distribution charge on a bill. 
Transmission revenue requirement (Federal Level): This is the revenue an electric 
n
utility is authorized to collect from customers to cover the costs of transmitted 
electricity over high-voltage power lines. These are regulated by the Federal Energy 
Regulatory Commission (FERC), while the state may set the retail rate. The utility files 
a separate transmission revenue requirement with FERC, with the exception of 
ERCOT, which is electrically isolated from other grids and regulated instead by the 
Public Utility Commission of Texas (PUCT).  
Generation / Supply charges (market-based): In deregulated states, there is no 
n
regulated revenue requirement for generation. Instead, the supply charge is 
determined by competitive wholesale energy and capacity markets (such as PJM or 
ISO-NE). The price is set by market supply and demand, which is then passed directly 
through to the customer’s Supply Charge.

investment, the capital structure that the utility is allowed to have and the revenue 
requirement (or revenue increase) a utility company is allowed to make after it has 
deployed capital investments to improve, expand or maintain its assets. That allowed

Stock Implications 
 
 
An implication for utilities of affordability concerns around rising electricity bills is that 
regulators may be less willing to approve rate increases, returns, or incremental capital 
investment programs. While we continue to view the regulated utility sector as being in a 
multi-year investment upcycle, the sustainability of that opportunity will depend in part 
on customer willingness and ability to absorb higher bills. 
Many of the jurisdictions that have experienced the largest bill increases since 2023 are 
located within competitive power markets, where higher capacity prices, tighter 
supply-demand balances, and rising wholesale power costs have contributed to 
affordability pressures. States such as DC (+55.8%), New Jersey (+43.4%), Maryland 
(+40.0%), New York (+29.1%) and Ohio (+27.4%) have experienced some of the largest 
customer bill increases nationally. As a result, Sell-rated EXC, Sell-rated ED and 
Neutral-rated PEG screen among the more challenged names in our coverage given their 
exposure to these jurisdictions and higher bills at the individual utilities in certain cases.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.
