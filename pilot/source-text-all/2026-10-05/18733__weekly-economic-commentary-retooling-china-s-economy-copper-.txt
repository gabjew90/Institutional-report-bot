Observers of construction projects, from roadbuilding to home renovations, find it difficult 
to gauge progress. Weeks may go by with ample activity, but it feels like nothing changes; 
then, in a day, the site will look like everything is new. 
China’s economy is in its own moment of rebuilding. The nation has enjoyed a long 
interval of economic growth and rising global influence.  But it faces a series of 
headwinds, some of which were front and center during the summit between Presidents Xi 
and Trump in Washington last week.  If China hopes to sustain success, it must engineer 
a successful reconstruction. 
China’s ascent has few parallels. After an insular interval, China reopened its relations 
with the world in the late 1970s. The industrial capacity it developed during the Great Leap 
Forward left it in a strong position to compete globally. The nation’s gross domestic 
product grew an average of 10% each year for three decades, starting in 1978.   
The economic turnaround was fueled by a great deal of debt, surpassing total 
indebtedness seen in many advanced economies. The nation’s strong culture of saving 
meant that profits and income gains were reinvested in bonds to support continued 
growth. That cycle could not carry on forever. Not long ago, markets were shaken by news 
of property developers facing default. Construction of new buildings has slowed, with 
some new residential developments halted midstream. Local government bodies are 
focused on debt management, limiting new infrastructure projects and further impairing 
construction employment.

China’s growth push was directed by a strong central government that was aware of the risk of 
falling into a middle-income trap: An emerging market enjoys a burst of growth from producing 
lower-value goods, but then stagnates as it fails to move higher up the value chain.  Since 1953, 
the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) has set a Five-Year Plan (FYP), outlining development and 
economic ambitions for the nation. The 15th FYP was set in March 2026, making a break from the 
COVID-era plan it replaced. The new FYP contains a clearer focus on protecting supply chains and 
supporting businesses, with less emphasis on wealth equality.  
CCP communications employ memorable sets of growth themes. In recent years, the theme was 
“new three productive forces” to meet the world’s demands, namely electric vehicles, batteries and 
solar power. With China’s leadership secured in those sectors, the priority has more recently turned 
to the “new new three” family of AI, robotics and pharmaceuticals. These themes reflect an overall 
push to continue growing by meeting the world’s export needs in higher-value sectors.  
AI has become a two-party race between the U.S. and China, who outpace the world in investment 
and production of new models. Chinese developers are hobbled by a lack of access to cutting-edge 
chips, which are subject to U.S. export controls. Their models have rarely advanced the frontier of 
AI capabilities. However, China’s engineers have proven adept as fast followers, catching up to the 
most advanced models within months, using computing resources more efficiently. Combined with 
the nation’s ample power generation, their AI prowess could become formidable. 
China’s technology firms may struggle to commercialize their models. Many western nations fear 
espionage and further lock-in to Chinese technology. And domestically, Chinese leaders may face 
similar concerns to those seen in the U.S., like worries of job displacement and new systemic risks 
from uncontrolled usage.  
Central planning has worked well for China, but the economy will not adapt overnight. China’s 
output depends on exports of both low- and high-tech goods. While the U.S. is the only nation to 
strike against China’s trade practices, most of the world has grown dependent on China’s exports—

China’s shadow looms over other issues in international relations. While not engaged in conflict, 
China is allied with Russia and Iran, especially as the largest buyer of oil from those nations. China 
cooperates with North Korea while asserting control over Taiwan, a continual source of uncertainty. 
This accumulation of challenges could have made for a conflagration when Presidents Trump and 
Xi met in Washington last week. But the summit was large in ceremony and light on substance. The 
two sides agreed to a two-month extension of the trade truce to January 2027, and a reciprocal 
reduction in tariffs for $30 billion of goods. China has no interest in voluntarily slowing its AI 
development, but the nations agreed to keep a “hotline” open to stay apprised of AI risks. The two 
sides agreed to future meetings, where more difficult geopolitical issues may be unavoidable. 
Looking ahead, a prolonged stalemate seems the most likely outcome. The U.S. depends on China 
for so many goods and crucial rare earth minerals; it may take a firm stance indirectly, like raising 
rule of origin requirements in its trade agreements with other nations. China will stay in the good 
graces of its trading partner. Escalations like cutting off rare earths or taking military action against 
Taiwan would do damage while the nation works through its own internal challenges. 
China has demonstrated its ability to execute large construction projects. If all goes to plan, 
renovations of its economy will ensure that it is built to last.  
Dr. Copper 
Long before modern scanners and monitors became common, doctors often checked a patient's 
health with something as simple as a finger on the wrist.  A pulse could reveal a surprising amount 
about a person's condition.  Economists have their own version of this practice.  Rather than relying 
solely on complex statistical models, they often look for signals that offer a quick health check.   
For decades, copper has been one of those barometers.  Traders even dubbed it "Dr. Copper" for 
its perceived ability to diagnose the state of the world economy.  Today, however, the red metal is 
signaling more than the economy's pulse.  Prices have climbed more than 40% over the past year 
to reach record highs, reflecting a growing recognition that demand is changing in ways that may 
prove more durable, while supply is becoming harder to expand.       
Historically, copper demand has largely tracked construction and industrial activity.  Those drivers 
remain important, but the need is evolving.  Once considered a cyclical commodity, copper is 
increasingly taking on strategic value.  Every major trend shaping the modern economy requires 
more of it.  The rapid buildout of data centers to support artificial intelligence is creating a 
substantial source of demand that barely existed a decade ago.  Electric vehicles use significantly 
more copper than conventional automobiles.  Renewable energy systems and expanding power 
grids require vast quantities of the metal.  As a result, copper usage may be losing its link to the 
traditional business cycle.   
China remains the world's largest copper user, accounting for more than half of global copper 
consumption.  But the next phase of demand growth appears increasingly global.  Massive 
investments in data centers and power infrastructure in countries like the United States, the energy 
transition in Europe, and India's push to modernize its electricity network and manufacturing base 
are all expected to compete for the world’s supply.

Large copper deposits remain to be developed, particularly in Latin America and Africa.  Bringing 
additional supply to market, however, is slow, costly and exposed to a multitude of risks.  Copper 
projects are among the most expensive and time-consuming in the mining industry.  Environmental 
approvals can take years, with local opposition becoming more common.   
Trade policy has added another wrinkle.  Expectations that the U.S. could impose tariffs on refined 
copper prompted a rush of shipments into the United States earlier this year.  This drew inventories 
away from other markets.   
These pressures are increasingly reflected in long-term forecasts.  According to BloombergNEF, 
the global copper market is expected to move into a supply deficit beginning in 2026 and remain 
undersupplied throughout the next decade.  Yet the nature of the challenge differs across 
countries.  For the United States, the bottleneck lies less in securing raw copper than in refining 
and processing it.  China faces the opposite problem.  Despite its dominant position across many 
critical mineral supply chains, it remains heavily dependent on imported unrefined copper, creating 
a rare strategic vulnerability in an area where it otherwise enjoys considerable leverage.   
Copper sits quietly at the center of many of the world's economic ambitions.  The challenge is that 
technological progress can move quickly, while expanding resource supply rarely does.  Just as a 
physician once learned a great deal from a patient's pulse, copper's recent strength offers a simple 
but revealing signal.   
Bond Markets, Beware 
Vigilante movies have always been popular with audiences.  From Clint Eastwood (Dirty Harry) to 
Halle Berry (Catwoman) to Denzel Washington (The Equalizer), characters that take justice into 
their own hands when systems fail are seen as heroes. 
With global interest rates rising rapidly, some are wondering whether investors are meting out fiscal 
justice where policy makers have failed to provide it.  The current day is reminiscent of forty years 
ago, when bond vigilantes arrived on the scene.

inflation had fallen to 2.5%; pressure was mounting on the Federal Reserve to reduce overnight 
interest rates, which still stood near 10%. 
Paul Volcker was the Fed chair at the time.  He was concerned that the government’s trend toward 
running big budget deficits could cause the economy to overheat.  The annual deficit was close to 
5% of gross domestic product (GDP), just below today’s levels.  High interest rates, he argued, 
were an important counterbalance. 
This did not sit well with the Reagan Administration, which favored lower borrowing costs.  A series 
of new appointments to the Federal Reserve Board began turning the tide; rates were gradually cut 
to 6%, and Volcker eventually chose to resign rather than be out-voted. 
Concerned by a perceived lack of policy discipline, investors bid interest rates up by more than 200 
basis points in seven months in 1987.  The economist Ed Yardeni attributed the retreat to “bond 
vigilantes.” Ever since, the threat of vigilantism has loomed over government bond markets; recent 
expressions have been seen in the United Kingdom and France. 
As of this writing, the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield has risen 120 basis points since February.  War-
related inflation is certainly a factor, but the trend may also reflect investor discontent with monetary 
and fiscal policy that is seen as too loose.  A commitment to better discipline on both fronts may be 
needed to keep the vigilantes at bay.
