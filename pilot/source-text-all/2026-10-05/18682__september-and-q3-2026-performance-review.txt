September and Q3 2026 Performance 
Review
Q3 was a difficult one for markets, as the global economy grappled with a fresh 
inflationary impulse and the resumption of a globally synchronised rate hiking 
cycle. This was primarily driven by the US-Iran conflict, with Brent crude oil up 
+42.0% over Q3 as both sides resumed strikes. But the pressures went beyond just 
energy, with Bloomberg’s agriculture spot index posting its biggest quarterly jump 
since 2022 as well. That contributed to a major global bond selloff, with US 10yr 
Treasury yields up to their highest level since 2007. But it wasn’t all bad news, and 
with global growth resilient for the most part, alongside robust earnings, the S&P 
500 still posted a +2.3% quarterly gain in total return terms.
Quarter in Review - The high-level macro overview
For markets, one of the biggest stories throughout Q3 was the US-Iran conflict, and 
what that meant for oil prices and inflation. Initially, the quarter got off to a rocky 
start, with oil prices surging back as Trump declared the US-Iran MoU was “over”. 
Meanwhile, as both sides resumed strikes, Brent crude rapidly moved up from 
around $73/bbl going into the quarter, to close above $100/bbl again on July 23. And 
even with the pullback at the end of July, Brent was still up +23.6% on the month.
In August, oil prices initially saw a sharp decline after President Trump posted that 
that he’d agreed to cancel an attack on Iran “subject to being able to rapidly make 
a DEAL.” So that helped Brent crude to close beneath $80/bbl on August 4. 
However, as no deal materialised, the US and Iran exchanged strikes again towards 
the end of the month, with Brent crude back above $90/bbl by end-August.
Then in September, there were wider fears around energy supplies. For instance, 
Saudi Arabia halted operations at multiple energy sites after they were attacked, 
with the Houthis claiming responsibility shortly after. Then in mid-September, the 
Saudi East-West pipeline was closed, although it has since begun to restart. 
Moreover, despite hopes that some kind of deal could happen around the UN 
General Assembly, those hopes were dashed by month-end, with Trump saying on 
September 26 that “They made a proposal but I rejected it”. And Iran’s foreign 
minister Araghchi said on September 27 that “we have no reason to come back to 
diplomacy and engage with this administration once again”. As a result, Brent 
crude ended Q3 up +42.0% at $103.53/bbl. And it was also clear that investors were 
pricing in a more protracted period of high oil prices, as Brent futures for December 
2027 were up +15.9% in Q3 to $81.25/bbl, their highest to date.

This backdrop meant central banks shifted in a hawkish direction over Q3, with the 
Fed, the ECB and the Bank of Japan all delivering a rate hike. At the Fed, September 
marked their first rate hike since 2023, and their dot plot signalled another hike by 
year-end. Then at the ECB, they also hiked in September, and the decision had 
several hawkish elements, as their statement said that “inflation is set to remain 
well above target for an extended period”. Then in Japan, they raised the policy rate 
to 1.25%, its highest since 1995.
One factor giving central banks the space to hike rates was how resilient growth 
remained throughout Q3. Indeed, the flash PMIs for September showed that if 
anything, growth was accelerating across many of the world’s biggest economies. 
For instance, the flash composite PMI for the US hit a 5-year high, whilst the 
Eurozone PMI hit a 3-year high. So despite the inflationary pressures building, there 
was still plenty of optimism on the growth front.
However, that backdrop of building inflation and strong growth proved very 
negative for sovereign bonds, which lost ground around the world. For instance, the 
10yr Treasury yield moved up +82bps in Q3, closing at a post-2007 high of 5.28%, 
whilst the 10yr bund yield was up +73bps to 3.58%. On a monthly basis as well, the 
10yr Treasury yield posted a 7th consecutive monthly increase in September, which 
is the first time that’s happened since 2011. Separately, there were growing signs 
that fiscal concerns were a factor, and the Franco-German 10yr spread widened to 
127bps by the end of the quarter, its highest since 2012.
Finally, global equities generally held up in Q3, supported by resilient growth and 
robust earnings. That helped the S&P 500 to reach a new record in August, with the 
index up +2.3% in total return terms over Q3 as a whole. That was supported by a 
particular boost from the Magnificent 7, which was up +11.1% in total return terms. 
Nevertheless, there were weaker performances elsewhere. For instance, Europe’s 
STOXX 600 also hit a new record in August, but it then slid into quarter-end, falling 
by -0.6% in total return terms. Meanwhile, South Korea’s KOSPI fell back 
significantly after an incredibly outperformance in Q2 when it rose +67.9% in total 
return terms, with Q3 then seeing a -19.2% decline.
Which assets saw the biggest gains in Q3?
Commodities: Q3 was a strong one for commodities across the board. Brent crude 
oil was up +42.0% amidst the re-escalation of the US-Iran conflict. In Europe, 
natural gas futures were up +66.6%. Metals saw gains, including gold (+3.7%), 
silver (+3.1%) and copper (+6.9%). And there were huge jumps for some 
agricultural commodities, including wheat (+16.3%), corn (+21.3%) and sugar 
(+22.9%).
Japanese Yen: At the start of August, the US and Japan intervened to support the 
Japanese yen, whilst the Bank of Japan also delivered a 25bp rate hike in 
September. That boosted the yen, which was the top-performing G10 currency in 
Q3, up +3.3% against the US dollar.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay 
fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases 
in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the 
maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in 
inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to 
receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets 
holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency 
conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are 
also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by 
indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in 
emerging markets.  The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables 
they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating 
coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. 
Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk. Options on swaps 
(swaptions) the risks typical to options in addition to the risks related to rates movements.
