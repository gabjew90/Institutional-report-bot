Recovery is gradually building across the gold market, after a highly volatile 1H26. Gold has 
recently rebounded towards $4,500/oz following sharp corrections triggered by the US-Israel-Iran 
conflict and rising Fed rate hike expectations. At the same time, volatility has normalised, speculative 
positioning has recovered above its two-year average, and the GLD put/call ratio has fallen to a six-
month low, signalling a renewal in bullish sentiment. 
Interest rates remain one of the most important variables for gold. The traditional inverse 
relationship between gold and US real yields has re-emerged at several points YTD, particularly as 
markets shifted from pricing three Fed rate cuts to expectations of two or more rate hikes, contributing 
to a sharp correction in gold prices. However, unlike previous cycles, elevated real yields have not 
been sufficient to derail the longer-term bull market, suggesting that additional structural forces are 
now supporting gold. 
The US dollar has become a key driver of gold price performance in recent months. Dollar 
weakness boosts non-US investors’ purchasing power and supports gold demand, contributing to a 
price rebound. More broadly, the gradual decline in the dollar’s share of global reserves and growing 
expectations for continued reserve diversification structurally reinforce the de-dollarisation narrative, 
providing a favourable backdrop for gold as an alternative reserve asset. 
Central bank purchases continue to provide a strong structural foundation for gold. Buying 
remains concentrated but robust, led by Poland and China, with additional new demand emerging 
from countries such as the Czech Republic. Reflecting this trend, 84% of reserve managers expect gold 
to represent a larger share of reserve portfolios over the next five years.   
ETF flows suggest that financial investor demand is gradually recovering. After sustained 
outflows between March and June, holdings have rebounded over the summer, indicating that 
financial demand is beginning to realign with the constructive long-term outlook for gold. 
We had decreased our gold allocation based on much higher volatility earlier in the year. Volatility has now 
pulled back significantly, and central banks (China, among others) are buying the dips while continuing to 
reduce US Treasury holdings at a steady pace, as the de-dollarisation theme continues unabated.

Gold market update: recovery building across indicators 
After gold’s exceptional 64% rally in 2025, prices have largely consolidated in 2026 ytd, with the 
metal up only 3% despite significant volatility. Gold surged to a record high above $5,400/oz in 
January, supported by strong central bank demand and geopolitical uncertainty, before correcting 
sharply as crowded positions unwound. A second major sell-off followed the outbreak of the US-
Israel-Iran conflict and rising Fed rate hike expectations, briefly pushing prices below $4,000/oz in 
July. Since then, China’s central bank dip-buying and the US Treasury’s surprise decision in 
August to double long-end bond buybacks have helped gold recover towards $4,500/oz.  
Volatility (GVZ) spiked above 45 during both the January correction and the Iran-related turmoil 
before gradually normalising, suggesting that recent gains have been absorbed in a still 
constructive long-term moderately bullish environment.

Positioning has recovered strongly from the May 2026 trough of 154,260 contracts to 243,334 – 
above the two-year average of ~221,000, but well below the September 2024 peak of 315,390. 
Longs are rebuilding, not crowded. The ratio has fallen steadily from ~0.60 in late April to a 
six-month low of 0.41 on 25 August, closing at 0.45 on 28 August. A declining ratio means open 
interest is increasingly skewed toward calls – a bullish signal that has built over the summer.

Key drivers of gold: inflation real rates and de-dollarisation 
Gold has historically served as an effective hedge against inflation, as reflected in the long-term 
relationship shown in the first chart. However, the 2026 zoom highlights that this link is not 
always straightforward: while elevated inflation supports demand for gold as a store of value, it 
can also trigger tighter monetary policy and higher interest rates, which weigh on prices. This 
dynamic was visible in 2026, when gold retreated from above $4,700/oz after stronger-than-
expected US inflation data reinforced expectations for a prolonged “higher-for-longer” Fed 
stance, despite inflation remaining above target. 
Gold has long proved to be a good protection against inflation  
2026: US 10y inflation rate vs gold price ($) 
 
 
 
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/Global Asset Allocation/Bloomberg 
Gold has traditionally exhibited a strong inverse relationship with US real yields: lower real rates 
reduce the opportunity cost of holding non-yielding gold and typically support prices. This 
relationship has re-emerged at various points in 2026, particularly as markets shifted from 
pricing three Fed rate cuts to expectations of two or more rate hikes, contributing to gold’s 
correction. 
However, a clear post-2022 regime shift has emerged. Despite persistently positive real yields, 
gold has continued to trade near record highs, breaking away from historical models that would 
imply significantly lower prices. Structural factors such as sustained central bank purchases, de-
dollarisation trends, geopolitical uncertainty and sovereign debt concerns appear to be 
providing a higher floor for gold prices, limiting the downside impact of elevated real rates.

Real rates have driven gold prices since early 2026

The inverse relationship between gold and the US dollar has reasserted itself in 2026, with dollar 
weakness emerging as one of the main drivers of the recent rally in gold prices. A weaker dollar 
increases the purchasing power of non-US investors, allowing them to buy more gold in local 
currency terms and thereby supporting global demand. This dynamic became particularly 
evident in August, when the dollar index fell to a three-month low and gold surged nearly 14%, 
putting the metal on track for its strongest monthly performance since 1999.  
The move was further amplified by the US Treasury’s surprise decision to double long-end bond 
buybacks, which weighed on the dollar and revived concerns over future dollar purchasing 
power. Notably, gold has recently outperformed what its historical relationship with the dollar 
would suggest, highlighting growing investor demand for hard assets as hedges against currency 
debasement and fiscal concerns.

Gold demand  
Global gold demand appears to be softening in 2026, with both Q1 and Q2 falling below their 
respective 10-year historical averages: demand reached 1,009 tonnes in Q126 (vs a 10y average 
of 1,129 tonnes) and 942 tonnes in Q2 (10y average: 1,028 tonnes). 
As a result, total demand for the first half of 2026 stood at 1,951 tonnes. To reach the average 
annual demand observed over the past decade, an additional 2,470 tonnes would be 
required during the second half of the year. Matching the stronger 2025 annual demand level 
would require approximately 3,076 tonnes over the remainder of 2026. 
Sector-wise, the slowdown appears to be broad-based. In Q126, central bank demand dropped 
to its lowest level since Q322 before recovering in Q226, although overall demand remains below 
historical norms.

Turkey: a major gold buyer but a market swing factor 
Turkey: central bank gold holdings (tonnes) vs gold spot ($)  
Source: SG Cross Asset Research/Global Asset Allocation/IMF, Bloomberg 
Turkey has been far more active in its gold reserve management than most central banks, making 
it both a major buyer and a significant source of market supply. After repatriating most of its gold 
reserves and securing the right to purchase all domestically mined gold, the CBRT embarked on 
an unprecedented accumulation phase that culminated in record reserve levels in 2022.  
However, unlike other central banks, Turkey has repeatedly used its gold holdings as a policy 
tool, selling large quantities to meet domestic demand, support the lira, and ease liquidity 
pressures. This was evident in 2023, when more than 130 tonnes were released into the local 
market following import restrictions, and again in 2026, when around 80 tonnes were sold in the 
first half of the year as the Iran war triggered renewed pressure on the Turkish currency. The 
simultaneous decline of the lira, rising import costs, and periodic gold sales highlight how 
Turkey’s reserve strategy is closely linked to domestic macroeconomic stability, making the CBRT 
an important but sometimes unpredictable participant in the global gold market.

Gold ETF holdings experienced significant swings throughout 2026. Strong inflows in January 
pushed holdings to a multi-year high in late February, before the outbreak of the Iran war 
triggered a prolonged period of outflows between March and June amid risk-off liquidations and 
rising rate expectations. Investor sentiment improved over the summer, however, with renewed 
dollar weakness and the Treasury buyback announcement supporting a recovery in ETF demand. 
Institutional demand for gold continues to broaden as pension funds, family offices and 
sovereign wealth funds increasingly view gold as a strategic portfolio allocation rather than a 
tactical trade. This trend is particularly visible in India, where regulatory changes have expanded 
access to precious metals across a wider range of investment vehicles. Following the decision to 
allow National Pension System (NPS) funds to invest in gold and silver ETFs in late 2025, India’s 
capital markets regulator relaxed the diversification rules further in March 2026, allowing equity 
funds to allocate up to 35% of their assets to gold and silver. Asset managers have already begun 
to take advantage of this flexibility, with firms such as JioBlackRock Mutual Fund incorporating 
gold and silver ETFs into multi-asset and flexible allocation strategies. Together, these 
developments point to a growing institutionalisation of gold demand across Asia, providing a 
durable source of support for the market.
