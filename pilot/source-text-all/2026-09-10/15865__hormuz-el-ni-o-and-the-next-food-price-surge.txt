The agriculture tail
Crop inventories remain comfortable, but GS GIR’s Lina Thomas and Daan Struyven see the upside
risks to agriculture prices building.
Hormuz disruptions, renewed Black Sea tensions and a potentially historic Super El Niño are arriving at
the same time, while increasingly protectionist trade policies could amplify any supply shock.

Fifty million gallons
A traditional Panama Canal transit uses roughly 50 million gallons of fresh water.
A powerful Super El Niño could put further pressure on the reservoirs supporting the canal, while also
threatening key sugar-producing regions across Brazil, India and Thailand. This would hit both
agricultural supply and transit capacity at a time when other global trade routes are already facing
disruption.
GS
Hormuz reaches agriculture
Middle East disruptions are threatening important nitrogen and phosphate fertilizer flows just as the Q3
planting procurement window opens.
At the same time, energy-security concerns are encouraging major exporters such as Brazil and India
to divert more crops into domestic biofuels, reducing the amount available to global markets.

The next corn catalyst
Renewed Black Sea tensions threaten a corridor responsible for roughly 15–20% of global grain trade.
Wheat prices have already risen 41% year-on-year. GS sees corn as the next potential catalyst as its
export season gets underway in October.

The protectionism multiplier
The bigger issue is how markets respond to disruption. A relatively small local shock can trigger export
restrictions and precautionary stockpiling, removing substantially more supply from global markets
than the original event.
GS estimates that if regional fragmentation halves the effective size of the market, the same supply
shock can have twice the impact on prices.
With Hormuz, the Black Sea and El Niño risks converging, agriculture is carrying considerably more
upside risk than current inventories suggest.
Rates are paying attention
The BCOM Agriculture Index and long-end yields have been rising together.
There is no simple one-for-one relationship, but another leg higher in food prices would add to
headline inflation pressure and make it harder for central banks to turn dovish. The longer-term chart
shows that major agriculture and rates moves have often coincided.
The agriculture tail could quickly become a rates story.
