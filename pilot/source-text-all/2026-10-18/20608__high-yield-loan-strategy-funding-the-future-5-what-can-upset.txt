Funding the Future #5: What can upset the 
Hyperscaler apple cart? 
     
   
 
 
AI’s growing sphere of influence 
AI’s rapidly growing sphere of influence is now touching the whole economy. BofA 
economists estimate that datacenters added ~70bps to US growth in 1H’26 and could 
contribute 30-50bp in ’27. Today, amid mounting concerns around the ability of the 
hyperscaler complex to maintain margins, we ask what if the forward-looking ROIC is 
too rosy?  When should creditors start to worry, and what should they look out for? 
Falling ROIC not an issue, but watch the CoD gap 
Hyperscaler ROIC has declined 3.3% YTD, while the cost of debt (CoD) has marginally 
increased due to higher clearing yields and funding spillage into costlier private credit, 
pushing hyperscalers’ CoD by ~40bp YTD. However, declining ROIC is not necessarily a 
catalyst for spread widening. Credit risk increases if its cushion to CoD narrows: we’ve 
seen evidence of this across issuers and time periods. Credit investors should focus on 
ROIC alongside changes in funding costs rather than business profitability in isolation. 
Leverage is the first sign of trouble in paradise 
Increasing leverage could be the first sign that the hyperscaler capex trade is faltering. 
There is adequate historical precedent to suggest issuers may increase leverage in the 
face of declining business profitability. Our two decades of data suggests that leverage 
is a more dominant driver of spreads than the ROI- CoD gap. If hyperscalers follow the 
same playbook, rising leverage could pressure ratings and spreads. And in a high-rate 
backdrop, stakes are higher as CoD jumps could prove to be punitive. 
Mitigating factors 
That said, leverage has a stronger relationship to ratings than spreads, implying rating 
agencies typically respond more concretely than the market, as investors tend to also 
take into account other factors at play. The credit outlook therefore should remain 
supportive even in a declining ROIC environment provided leverage remains in check and 
the ROIC – CoD gap doesn’t converge materially from current levels. This gap was the 
canary in the coalmine during the dotcom bust, and is what we are watching today.

 What can upset the Hyperscaler apple 
cart? 
AI-adjacent issuers have been driving fundamentals, primary issuance, investor returns, 
and more recently the entire economy. We are already well aware of the wedge AI 
impact has driven across credit fundamentals (see report: Q2 earnings: AI Winners 
Extend Their Lead), and we also know that over 25% of new money issuance YTD is now 
AI-led. AI’s rapidly growing sphere of influence is now touching the whole economy. 
Estimates suggest that 1.35% of the 2.2% in Q2 GDP was due to fixed investment, a 
jump of 0.3ppt vs Q1. BofA Economists estimate that datacenters added nearly 70bp to 
US growth in all of 1H 2026 and are projected to contribute 30-50bp in 2027 (see 
report: Washington Matters: Getting local: an FAQ on data center deployment). This 
naturally begs the question- where could all this go wrong? 
We have maintained that the lynchpin of the AI capex cycle is monetization- which is the 
primary factor setting this cycle apart from the Telco/Dotcom bubble of the late 1990s. 
We have also introduced tools to scrutinize monetization- namely relationship of ROIC, 
WACC and cost of debt/equity, introduced in our prior FTFs. Today, amongst growing 
concerns around the ability of hyperscalers/labs/chipmakers to maintain margins, we 
assess the forward-looking trajectory of ROIC, and ask what if it falls? When should 
creditors start to worry, and what should they look out for to assess if this cycle is 
devolving dotcom style? 
While we cannot credibly predict the long-term profitability of compute providers, what 
we can do is determine the key observable metrics that can serve as the canary in the 
coalmine, based on historical issuer behavior. 
ROIC – Cost of debt gap is narrower today 
After peaking last year, Hyperscaler ROIC has been gradually decreasing YTD. Extending 
beyond the top 5, ROIC registers a decline of -3.3% YTD. Some of this is optical as the 
asset base has inflated on account of unrealized investment gains, but stripping that out 
also shows a sequential decline, leaving investors concerned if this trajectory is likely to 
continue. At the same time cost of debt has marginally increased as a function of higher 
yields in the public bond markets, and capex funding increasingly spilling over into 
private credit at a higher cost. Given these changes to debt funding mix, our Cost of 
Debt (CoD) calculation now also factors in cost of financing in private markets.

Low ROIC not a problem; but watch cost of debt 
To determine what matters to credit investors, we test the constituents of S&P 500 
(and the broader R3K) with bonds outstanding over the last 20 years. We extract bilateral 
relationships across a variety of pertinent factors – ROIC, capital structure leverage, 
ratings, and spreads, after standardizing for sector spreads and where possible interest 
rates.  
ROIC largely influences ratings 
Across all metrics, the only dimension that ROIC reasonably impacts is Issuer Rating that 
too on a short run. Average ratings have a 31% -41% Rsq with trailing 1yr average ROIC 
(Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6). Extending the analysis back to 2000 using current S&P 500 
constituents, the relationship disappears (Exhibit 7) due to changing rates backdrop. 
Rating correlation increases marginally when measuring against ROIC-Cost of debt (a 
measure of prevailing interest rates), rather than just ROIC alone (Exhibit 8). Similarly, 
spread too doesn’t display a coherent relationship to ROIC stand-alone, which we 
discussed later.  
When looking at the broader 3K index, ROIC also has a reasonable (inverse) correlation to 
Leverage, defined as total debt / (total debt + book equity) – meaning that issuers with 
lower ROIC tend to run higher leverage more systematically (Exhibit 17, Exhibit 18). All 
relationships generally become stronger if looked at vs ROIC less CoD.

Even so, leverage appears to matter more for ratings than for spreads, suggesting rating 
agencies may respond more directly to balance-sheet deterioration than investors. 
Similarly, trailing profitability measures alone do not drive spreads, as credit investors 
also price the forward outlook and broader credit fundamentals. Neither metric, 
therefore, provides a standalone signal for spread performance. This is the reason our 
CSI system exists and is more reflective of issuer credit risk than ratings and leverage 
alone. 
Leverage is the first sign of trouble in paradise 
If Hyperscaler ROIC continues to decline, investors should look out for leverage. Past 
precedent suggests issuers could lever up balance sheets to boost equity returns in the 
face of declining business profitability. We can see some evidence of this in sector-level 
aggregates across both large caps and the broader market, where lower-ROIC sectors 
tend to carry higher leverage (Exhibit 17 and Exhibit 18).  
Should this start happening with hyperscalers, it could serve as the first warning sign for 
spreads, possibly even culminating in downgrades. Adding leverage is likely more 
punitive today given that a higher rates backdrop will increase cost of debt, and 
ultimately impact spreads. Our fundamental team estimates $214bn in current debt 
capacity across the Big 4 hyperscalers before exceeding ratings agencies’ leverage 
thresholds (see report: High Grade Technology: DebtMaxxing to continue but valuations 
attractive).

Even then, the case for spread underperformance is less convincing as there are other 
factors at play such as- why the issuer is adding debt, or why they are buying back 
equity, or if there is a case for long-term business profitability. Sometimes the 
underlying reason is more compelling than the sheer outcome of increasing leverage (or 
deteriorating ratings) itself, thereby keeping spreads in check. AT&T provides a good 
example for this: leverage (debt/IC) increased from 37% in early 2011 to 60% at the end 
of 2022 against a backdrop of declining ROIC, yet bond spreads remained largely 
ranged-bound at around 150bps for much of the period. This was likely because of a 
couple of large acquisitions that drove leverage higher, while the market focused on the 
longer-term deleveraging narrative.  
If today’s hyperscalers are sacrificing current ROIC to bump up future business 
profitability, then investors might be more amenable to overlooking interim margins 
even if rating agencies don’t.

Long-Term ROIC matters 
Markets are inherently focused on forward-looking outlook whereas our analysis thus far 
has largely been based on trailing metrics. We therefore also discuss here forward ROIC 
based on Bloomberg consensus estimates for earnings, holding invested capital at 
current levels to reflect the lag between capex deployment and earnings generation.  
On this basis, ROIC for the hyperscaler complex including fringe issuers is estimated to 
settle above 20% by end-2028 (Exhibit 21). Our conclusion: even if these estimates 
prove too optimistic and ROIC settles at a lower level, conditions should still remain 
supportive for credit, provided issuers do not increase leverage to bolster ROE, and gap 
between ROIC and CoD doesn’t converge to concerning levels.
