Who will capture the AI windfall? 
AI adoption appears to have boosted UK and US GDP

• 
From demand boost to supply transformation? Investment in the infrastructure necessary to run artificial 
intelligence (AI) is boosting aggregate demand. This temporary counter-cyclical impulse helps to explain why global 
growth has not slowed this year despite the headwinds from rises in energy prices and interest rates. However, the 
bigger question remains whether AI will prove to be a transformative “general purpose technology” that raises long-
run trend growth. Recent surges in “information and communication” and “professional, scientific and technical 
services” output in some economies raises the likelihood that the answer will be a qualified “yes”. 
• 
Initial demand boost: To date, investment in the hardware needed to run AI has predominantly boosted Asian exports. 
In September, exports from China were up 36% yoy, from Taiwan 61% yoy, and from South Korea 84% yoy. The surge 
in share prices of major software and hardware companies in the industry has also created a big wealth effect in the 
US, where households hold more of their wealth in equities than real estate. As US business investment outside 
software and tech hardware is stagnant, and much of US hardware spending leaks into imports, consumption remains 
the key driver of US growth (see US: AI investment boom cannot sustain rapid growth, 10 June 2026). This makes US 
growth vulnerable to an equity market correction, which could hit consumer sentiment and the spending that 
currently depends on the wealth effect. 
• 
Investors do society a favour: The voracious appetite to finance and undertake AI investment is driven by the belief 
that the fixed assets, software and intellectual property built will deliver major payoffs. This may or may not be true. 
Strong competition between the designers of large language models (LLMs), the homogeneity of data centres, and 
rapid depreciation in chip values both due to their longevity (chips last only two to six years) and rapid improvement 
in chip design suggests to us that some investors will be disappointed. But even if that proves to be the case, the 
investment boom will leave behind infrastructure that society will be able to use. Both Britain’s oft-cited 1840s railway 
mania and the late 1990s telecoms bubble left investors with losses – but also gifted society with infrastructure that 
underpinned major gains in total factor productivity, economic output, and living standards. 
• 
Signs of progress: In many economies, growth has picked up in sectors most likely to make early use of AI. This 
suggests that it can provide a lasting boost to supply. In Q2, the “information and communication” and “professional, 
scientific and technical activities” sectors contributed more to growth in most major economies than their 13-21% 
shares of total economic output – see Chart. For example, the detailed output breakdown of UK monthly GDP for 
August showed an 11% yoy gain in “computer programming” and a 20% yoy jump in “scientific research and 
development” (R&D). The boom in IT and R&D could herald broader gains in productivity. Of the eight economies 
included in out chart, the US, UK, Spain and France appear to be making best use of AI so far. Whether consumers or 
corporates ultimately capture the gains from AI will depend on the competitive structure of the market in each product. 
• 
Employment risk? The evidence of the AI effect on employment remains mixed. In the UK, employment in both of the 
two main AI-adopting sectors has declined. But we should be wary of reading too much into this given weak hiring 
demand across the board following policy-driven increases in the costs of employment there. Sector employment has 
held broadly steady in the US and France and increased in Spain. Predictions of imminent mass unemployment look 
far-fetched. Like previous tech revolutions, AI will almost certainly create new jobs as well.
