The September US inflation data has kept the focus on rising prices aggravated by war-related supply-side 
pressures. Obviously, this is not good news for the 47th President. Nor is the latest data on inflation 
expectations from the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index published last Friday. US headline 
CPI rose by 0.6% MoM and 3.7% YoY in September, up from 3.4% YoY in August and above consensus 
estimates of 3.6% YoY. While core CPI rose by 2.6% YoY in September, up from 2.4% YoY in August and above 
consensus estimates of 2.5% YoY (see Exhibit 1). As for energy-related cost pressures, energy CPI surged by 
20.4% YoY in September, up from 16.3% YoY in August. Meanwhile, year-ahead inflation expectations from 
the preliminary October Michigan survey rose from 4.6% in September to 4.7% in October, well above the 
recent low of 3.4% in February prior to the onset of the Iran war. The five-year inflation expectations also rose 
from 3.4% to 3.5% (see Exhibit 2).

Meanwhile, there is new evidence of the extreme nature of America’s K-shaped economy. GREED & fear refers 
to the Federal Reserve’s triennial Survey of Consumer Finances released last Friday. It shows that real median 
family net worth increased by only 2% from 2022 to 2025 to US$216,000. That compares with 37% growth in 
family wealth in the previous period from 2019 to 2022 boosted by the decision to pay people money to do 
nothing during the pandemic (see Exhibit 17).  
Still breaking the data down real median wealth for families in the top 10% income percentile rose by 31% 
between 2022 and 2025, while wealth for the bottom 20% of earners declined by 6% (see Exhibit 18). As for 
the elderly, wealth increased by 37% in real terms over the past three years for Americans 75 and older. As a 
result, they became the richest age group with a median net worth of US$505,000, while wealth for those aged 
under 35 declined by 23% over the same period to US$33,000 (see Exhibit 9). The Fed defines family wealth 
or net worth as the difference between families’ total assets and liabilities.

The survey also found that the share of US families reporting being behind on loan payments surged from 
12% in 2022 to 20% in 2025 (see Exhibit 10). But the median and mean values of a family’s debt remained 
broadly stable over the same period at US$88,100 and US$179,100 respectively. This compliments the pattern 
of rising credit card delinquency. The share of credit card loans in serious delinquency (90+ days delinquent)

years to US$36,800 while it increased for all other racial and ethnic groups. Median wealth also plunged by 
55% over the same period to US$18,600 for people with no high-school diploma, the biggest decline for the 
group since the triennial survey began in 1989.

data discussed above and in terms of Trump’s approval ratings on the economy. The latest Fox News poll 
conducted on 9-12 October shows that 70% of Americans disapprove of Trump’s handling of the economy 
with only a 29% approval rating.

As regards the AI thematic, one of the messages of the new Asia Maxima is that investors need to keep laser 
focused on credit spreads for any evidence that the market is no longer willing to fund the ongoing AI capex 
boom, or to put it more crudely, that the credit card will be taken away (see Asia Maxima 4Q26 – Reverberating 
tremors, 9 October 2026). Spreads have now started to move up again. The Oracle and CoreWeave 5-year CDS 
spreads have surged from 186bp and 749bp respectively on 11 September to 288bp and 1,118bp (see Exhibit 
12). These are the highest levels seen so far since the AI capex cycle got underway in early 2023. The yield of 
the CoreWeave bond maturing 2032 is now 14.8%.  
On a related subject, the point made by Jefferies’ Global Head of Quantitative Strategy Desh Peramunetilleke 
in a report published this week is that it has become time to diversify beyond AI (see Jefferies research 
Diversifying Beyond AI, 13 October 2026). The report notes that global AI stocks’ consensus earnings are 
expected to slow materially from about 100% in 2026 to 30% in 2027 and 22% in 2028. This is based on the 
AI-related stocks in the MSCI AC World Index. By contrast, outside of AI, the global ex-AI basket’s earnings 
growth is expected to slow only from 16% this year to 9% for both 2027 and 2028 (see Exhibit 13). From a 
valuation perspective it is no surprise to hear that the ex-AI basket is much cheaper. The report notes that 
global AI stocks trade at 23x forward PE on an equal-weighted basis, compared with 13x for the ex-AI basket 
(see Exhibit 14).

There continues to be a lot of focus on whether full-scale hostilities will recommence in the Iran conflict either 
prior to or after the Mid-term election now less than three weeks away. But investors are in danger of forgetting 
that there is now a full-scale war going on between Yemen’s so-called Houthis, more properly known as Ansar 
Allah, and Saudi Arabia.  
Despite the advent of AI and the supposedly ultra efficient information age, there continues to be remarkable 
ignorance about what is going on in this conflict, partly because of a news blackout and partly because of 
general ignorance with the “Houthis” long portrayed in the Western media as flip-flop wearing bandits hanging 
out in caves.   
GREED & fear has done a little checking and the facts do not appear to fit the perception. The Houthis govern 
an area in Yemen where about 70% of the country’s population of 43m live. Moreover, just as Persia is an 
ancient civilization in the case of Iran, so is Yemen dating back 7,000 years GREED & fear reads. Still there is 
a more relevant historical point dating back to the last century. That is Yemen’s claim to the three disputed 
provinces of Asir, Najran and Jizan which Saudi Arabia took control of in 1934 with help from the British. 
The current war overlaps with these so-called “lost provinces” with the Houthis having their origins in Zaydi 
Shia Islam which dates back to the late 9th century. Moreover, there are cross border tribal links with these 
provinces.

The conclusion from the above is much more complicated than the Houthis simply acting as some kind of 
Iran proxy and also poses a much greater potential threat to Saudi stability than many suppose. This is why 
the Saudi decision to attack the airport of Sanaa in July looks an ever greater miscalculation as previously 
discussed here (see GREED & fear - World War III and capex aversion, 30 July 2026). For now the Houthis appear 
to have control of the Bab al-Mandab Strait, the Red Sea chokepoint. They have also not confined their attacks 
to Saudi’s East-West pipeline, which used to process 7m barrels of oil a day, but have also attacked oil 
refineries in the West of Saudi as well as the Riyadh airport on several occasions over the past week as has 
been widely reported. These are historic developments with potentially serious implications.  
GREED & fear has been in the UK for the past several days and has been reminded once again of some of the 
more grotesque features of Britain’s welfare state. That was the topic of a recommended Wall Street Journal 
article (“The Welfare State Is Crushing Britain”, 11 October 2026). GREED & fear will mention only a few of the 
details. Some 7m people, or more than 10% of the population, now claim one or more sickness or disability 
benefits with the number rising fastest among working-age people, up more than 60% in the past six years 
from 2.9m in 2019 to 4.7m in 2025. The UK is due to spend more than £100bn on such benefits this year (see 
Exhibit 17). A minimum-wage worker who works 40 hours per week will receive take home pay of around 
£1,880 a month after tax. However, if a person is classified as too sick to work and claim so-called Personal 
Independence Payments (PIPs), that person can earn £2,000 tax free per month. PIP is a tax-free welfare 
benefit in the UK intended to help working-age people with the extra costs of living caused by a disability or 
long-term physical or mental health condition.

As discussed here previously (see GREED & fear - Down Under shokku, 2 July 2026), the budget announced in 
May by the Labor government has increased the risk of a housing downturn with two dramatic changes. First, 
from July 2027 capital gains at the marginal tax rate will be levied on capital gains on all assets, not just 
property. This is 47% for a higher income earner. Uptil now the capital gains tax was only levied at half the 
marginal rate. Second, the longstanding tax deduction on negative gearing will be exempted on all new 
property purchases save for a newly completed property. The latter exemption is designed to encourage 
construction given the shortage of housing. 
Still the reality is that the changes, which passed parliament in late June as expected, will likely lead to a 
significant decline in property transactions. First, homeowners will not want to crystalise the capital gains tax. 
Second, the negative gearing deduction is maintained, importantly, for existing properties. National home 
sales declined by 20.9% YoY in September and were down 17.4% YoY in the three months to September, 
according to Cotality estimates (see Exhibit 24).

Meanwhile housing affordability was already an issue prior to this year’s rate hikes. The median dwelling value 
of A$937,722 has risen to 8.7x annual medium pre-tax household incomes at the end of June with the 
percentage of income to service a mortgage rising to a record 51.1%, according to Cotality. It is also the case 
that about 70% of mortgage debt remains linked to variable rates. The variable-rate mortgage rate for owner 
occupiers is currently 6.24%. 
Australia’s core macro issue remains the lack of productivity growth in an economy characterized by heavy 
regulation and structurally high wage growth where the public sector is the main driver of employment. Real 
GDP per hour worked declined by 0.2% YoY in 2Q26 and is down 5.0% since peaking in 1Q22 (see Exhibit 25).
