Dominus providebit?
Weekly viewpoints
Burkhard Varnholt, Senior Financial Market Adviser, UBS Switzerland AG
Are markets too complacent? With interest rates, government debt, and 
deficits rising, should investors reduce their market exposure—or rather 
add to it? Is stoicism in investment policy a virtue, or is it reckless? We 
explore these questions, examine the claims underpinning currencies in 
Switzerland and the US, and consider the momentum of a global economy 
driven by investment and productivity. We find both reasons for optimism 
and caution that, from different perspectives, lead to the same conclusion: 
“Stay invested at all times.” If that all sounds like a bit much, we can at 
least recommend a dream cycling route for mild-fall outings. Che bello!
 
1. Inflation, government debt, and 
concerned central bankers
Money. Few things symbolize human foresight quite as clearly. An intriguing 
inscription runs around the narrow edge of the Swiss five-franc coin: 
“DOMINUS PROVIDEBIT” or “The Lord will provide,” engraved alongside 13 
stars and taken from the Old Testament (Genesis 22:8). How did this biblical 
phrase find its way onto our money? More importantly, can it still tell us 
something today? Should we stop worrying about money and simply leave 
everything to God? We know, however, that complacency is no guide for life. 
With its Swiss cross and the bust of an Alpine shepherd created by artist Paul 
Burkhard (1888–1964), the five-franc coin is more likely telling us that money 
and trust are not opposites: Neither works without the other.
The human tendency to worry about the future may be a driver of business, 
development, and innovation, but it is not enough on its own to ensure 
success. This—long with a more practical reason—is why the inscription first 
appeared on Bernese coins in 1716 and on Swiss federal coins in 1888. At 
the time, most coins were made of silver (“liber” derives from the French 
“livre,” the pound as a measure of silver), with the aim to prevent anyone 
from shaving metal from the coin for personal gain. Clearly legible lettering 
served as proof that the coin was genuine and intact.
Three thoughts come to mind:
First, inscriptions of this kind were popular in the 18th and 19th centuries. The 
US dollar has carried the words “In God we trust” since 1864, when they first 
appeared on the two-cent coin. Such inscriptions are a reminder and a form 
of self-commitment. In the modern era, a motto or biblical quotation such as 
“DOMINUS PROVIDEBIT” emphasized the social responsibility of the nobility 
or the civic elite. It was hardly intended to invoke the New Testament parable 
of the carefree birds of the air and lilies of the field, which are provided for

by the heavenly Father.
Second, we know that a shrug and the words “The Lord will take care 
of it” can be a lazy excuse for avoiding responsibility. Markets are often 
uncompromising judges that quickly expose empty claims. Rising bond yields 
are currently challenging many fiscal policy reassurances, while rising equity 
markets confirm the strength of the private sector.
Third, in business, investing, and everyday life, trust goes hand in hand with 
practical concern for the future. Money and trust are two sides of the same 
coin—that is the message of the five-franc piece. Sustainable development 
can succeed only when economic concern for tomorrow rests on deep 
confidence in the social order. Investors with a long time horizon can then 
invest with a measure of equanimity.
Why are we writing about this now?
For three reasons. First, the question of supposedly “complacent markets” 
is the one investors ask me about most often these days. Second, rising 
inflation, government debt, and interest rates around the world fall within the 
domain of fiscal and monetary policy. Third, the corresponding investment 
policy response falls within the domain of this letter.
2. In praise of corporate profits
One hallmark of every bull market is that its strongest drivers eventually come 
under general suspicion. What lifts prices is suddenly seen as a threat; what 
creates growth becomes a harbinger of decline. The recent rise in bond yields
—evidence of a booming US and global economy with a growing appetite 
for capital—has likewise unsettled many investors. But a closer look shows 
that corporate profit growth remains remarkably robust. At the same time, 
US private sector debt has fallen from around 97% to 67% of US GDP since 
the global financial crisis. This not only confirms the private sector’s resilience, 
but once again supports the “Roaring Twenties” scenario that we have seen 
unfolding in the economy and markets for years.
Profits drive markets, not hope
The euphoria of the dotcom bubble was fueled by unrealistic hope and the 
“fear of missing out” (FOMO). By contrast, the current bull market is not 
fueled by FOMO, but by FEMO—“fabulous earnings momentum.”
Since the S&P 500 began its advance from around 3,500 points in October 
2022, the earnings power of US companies has improved across the board. 
Analysts’ average expected earnings per share (EPS) for the S&P 500 has more 
than doubled over the past four years, from USD 150 to about USD 420 for 
the 2027 financial year. For 2029, analysts expect average EPS of USD 500. 
If this scenario materializes, applying a price-to-earnings ratio of 20 implies 
the potential for the index to reach 10,000 points.
The fact that revenue, profits, and profit margins rose for all 11 of the S&P 
500’s sectors last quarter contradicts the view that a handful of technology 
giants are artificially lifting the market. In fact, profit growth this broad 
has occurred only once in the past 25 years: when COVID-19 lockdowns 
ended. Current profit growth, by contrast, reflects a flourishing private sector, 
as the latest IMF World Economic Outlook also suggests. But it warns of 
vulnerabilities from geopolitical shocks that could destabilize inflation, global 
supply chains, and public finances. On balance, however, the IMF expects 
global economic growth to accelerate next year, to 3.4% from 3.1%, and

That said, markets are not calculators, and the rise in government debt 
worldwide is an unmistakable burden on future generations. From an 
investment-policy perspective, however, diversified equity portfolios offer 
more effective protection against government debt burdens, financial 
repression, and inflation than cash.
Markets are therefore sending an important signal of their own: Strong and 
broad-based profit growth—in developed and emerging markets alike—is 
acting like a powerful tide that could lift many boats. The bull market now 
rests on a broader foundation than it did at the outset.
Why profits matter more to society than their reputation suggests
Profits are far more than a reward for successful entrepreneurs. They 
provide the financial foundation for investment, innovation, lending, and 
employment. They are the resource from which companies pay taxes 
and finance new factories, software platforms, data centers, research 
laboratories, and business models. When profits rise, an economy also gains 
room to act.
This is precisely where one of the defining developments of our time can 
be seen. US companies are currently generating record pre- and post-tax 
profits of around USD 4.7 trillion and just under USD 3.9 trillion, respectively. 
Remarkably, about USD 1.2 trillion is earned outside the US. The global 
economy is proving far more resilient than the pervasive crisis narrative would 
suggest.
At the same time, profit margins are at record highs. Adjusted for one-off 
revaluation gains at individual technology companies, expected net margins 
in the S&P 500 are around 12%.
And because dividends are growing more slowly than profits, companies are 
retaining around USD 1.7 trillion in earnings—a substantial sum for future 
investment.
The productivity revolution is becoming visible
Artificial intelligence, data centers, and capital investment are booming, as 
are services such as tourism, finance, logistics, health care, and real estate. 
This shows how economic and market momentum is spreading throughout 
the economy. The deflationary impact of the artificial intelligence revolution 
has yet to be proven, but the increase in productivity is becoming increasingly 
apparent. Another clue is that employment growth in developed economies 
is lagging well behind profit growth. Given the shrinking size of younger 
generations, this is a macroeconomic blessing and supports our long-held 
view that a broad productivity surge is under way.
Why yields are rising
Does this mean investors can afford to become complacent? Not at all.
The rise in long-term bond yields raises valid questions. Four explanations 
currently dominate the debate: the return of inflation, the unwinding of the 
yen carry trade, fears of a debt crisis, and competing demand for capital from 
indebted governments and an exceptionally strong global economy.
We favor the final interpretation, without underestimating the risks.

Nominal US economic growth of around 6% remains above long-term 
bond yields. Companies are investing more than they have in years, while 
governments are financing enormous deficits in capital markets. When 
demand for capital is competing, it is hardly surprising that its price also rises.
Structural factors also play a role. Aging societies save less, while demand for 
capital to finance reindustrialization, digitalization, energy infrastructure, and 
artificial intelligence is rising sharply. This does not move policy rates, but it is 
likely to push up the so-called “neutral rate,” as Federal Reserve Chair Kevin 
Warsh recently noted on CNBC.
We also cannot ignore that some inflationary forces remain persistent. 
Tensions in the Middle East are reflected in higher gasoline and diesel prices. 
A global shortage of petrochemical products is adding to price pressures. And 
the worldwide boom in data center construction is further increasing demand 
for energy, raw materials, and industrial equipment. All this points to a world 
in which inflation and interest rates may remain higher than during the zero-
rate decade that followed the 2009 global financial crisis.
“Stay invested at all times”
None of this necessarily means the bull market is ending.
Unrestrained fiscal deficits make a stronger case for equities than for cash 
or bonds. And as long as nominal economic growth exceeds bond yields, 
systemic sustainability remains intact. More importantly, when profits grow 
faster than employment, inflationary wage-price spirals are unlikely.
Ultimately, our case for staying invested is twofold.
On the positive side, companies are earning more, investing more capital, 
and increasing productivity. This is the foundation of our “Roaring Twenties” 
scenario.
The less pleasant reason is fiscal. In a world where governments increasingly 
depend on the cooperation of their creditors, productive capital remains one 
of the most effective safeguards against the gradual erosion of wealth.
Anyone who views current corporate profits merely as a by-product of a good 
year for markets is missing their true significance. They are fueling a historic 
investment wave. They should be used to finance technological leaps, raise 
productivity, and strengthen the resilience of the global economy.
Rising yields deserve attention. Profits deserve respect. And as long as the 
latter grow faster than concerns about the former, confidence remains the 
more rational investment philosophy. The real story of this decade is not 
government debt. It is the growing productivity and earnings power of 
companies. And that is the true sound of the new “Roaring Twenties.”
3. Che bello! Cycling to Venice
Some cycling routes are worth doing again and again. The route from the 
Ofen Pass through South Tyrol to Venice, which my wife and I enjoyed last 
week, is one of them. It offers variety and magnificent views, and is suitable 
for athletes and families with children alike.
The route begins with an exhilarating descent from the Ofen Pass into 
Europe’s largest and highest continuous apple-growing region: the sun-

drenched Vinschgau and Adige valleys. Visitors find a mild, fertile climate, 
picturesque historic towns such as Laatsch and Glurns with welcoming 
restaurants, and superbly developed cycle paths.
Merano, the old spa town with its storied promenade, provides the 
first highlight. Its elegant setting forms an impressive stage for striking 
architecture against a majestic mountain backdrop. The route continues to 
Trento, where the magnificent cathedral and Renaissance palace recall the 
region’s former prosperity. The next day, Bassano del Grappa—nomen est 
omen—looks like a picture-postcard promise: The beautiful wooden bridge, 
the winding old town, the castle, and that cheerful Italian ease that makes 
every stop last a little longer.
Next up, the celebrated Venetian villas and gardens of the 16th, 17th, and 
18th centuries along the Riviera del Brenta, on the way to Padua’s spacious 
squares and palaces. The old cathedral city, with its attractive canals, is 
enlivened by its venerable university and the abundant market on Piazza delle 
Erbe.
And then Venice: Approaching and entering along the Lido is uplifting. Those 
who stop in Chioggia along the way are rewarded with charming osterias 
and one last taste of lagoon life. A cycling trip filled with scenery, culture, 
and dolce vita.
Che bello!
I wish you a wonderful weekend.

judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid

Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.

Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives

Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.

Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
