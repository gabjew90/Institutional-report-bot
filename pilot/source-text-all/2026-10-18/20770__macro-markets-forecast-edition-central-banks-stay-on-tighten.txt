Higher yields, unchanged Fed view
We keep our Fed forecast unchanged. We still expect one more hike this year and another next year,
with the risks tilted to the upside. Long-term yields have risen sharply, but the move mainly reflects a strong
economy and heavy demand for capital.
The case for more hikes still holds. The US economy remains strong, the labor market is close to full
employment and inflation is above the Fed’s 2% target. This week’s data back that up. Consumer prices
rose 0.6% in September, and annual CPI inflation rose to 3.7% from 3.4%. Core CPI, which excludes food
and energy, rose 0.3% for the second month in a row, and its annual rate edged up to 2.6% from 2.4%.
September’s PPI also points to further price pressures in the pipeline, with input prices rising sharply.
Monetary policy still does not look restrictive enough for an economy in this shape.
The annual revision of the national accounts has however lowered PCE inflation, the Fed's preferred measure.
Core PCE inflation now stands at 3.0%, against 3.3% before the revision. Part of the revision reflects new
methods for measuring a few service prices, such as portfolio management fees, legal services and software.
That changes the measured level of inflation, not the strength of demand. Inflation is still clearly
above target, and the September CPI shows it moving up, not down.
Long-term yields rose sharply in September, to their highest levels in more than two decades. The 10-year
Treasury yield rose by around 50 basis points over the month, and both the 10-year and the 30-year yields

reached their highest levels since 2002. Yields have come down somewhat in October, but they do remain
high.
This ﬁts an economy with strong growth and high demand for capital. AI investment, public spending
and defense all need ﬁnancing. When demand for capital rises, so does its price. Over time, the 10-year yield
has broadly followed nominal growth, the sum of inflation and real growth. Nominal GDP grew at an annual
rate of about 7% in the ﬁrst half of this year. Against that backdrop, long-term yields above 5% are not
particularly restrictive.
Higher yields are not only a US story. Government bond yields have risen across several major markets, and
public debt keeps climbing around the world. According to the IMF, global public debt rose to just under 94%
of GDP last year and is set to reach 100% by 2029. Governments face growing spending needs, not least on
defense. That means more bonds for investors to absorb. With heavy government borrowing on top of
strong private demand for capital, it is no surprise that yields are going up.
All in all, our view is unchanged. A strong economy and above-target inflation still call for tighter policy. We
expect one more hike this year and another next year. Higher long-term yields do not change that call. They
mostly reflect the same strong economy that keeps the Fed hiking.

Two more ECB hikes still our baseline
We continue to expect two further 25bp rate hikes from the ECB, one in December and another in
March 2027, taking the deposit rate to 3%. The latest inflation data and the ECB’s communication remain
consistent with further tightening, but the limited evidence of second-round eects and broadening inflation
pressures still favours a gradual, quarterly pace rather than another move already in October.
Euro-area inflation rose more than expected to 3.8% in September, while core inflation edged up to 2.5%.
The rise was still driven mainly by energy and food, and signs of broadening price pressures remain
limited for now. The ECB’s September projections already had core inflation remaining above 2% through

2028, while the economy has proved more resilient than expected. This combination should be sucient to
deliver another hike when new sta projections become available in December.
The account of the September meeting supported the case for further action. Members saw the risks
to inflation as tilted to the upside and considered the policy decision a choice between hiking immediately
and waiting for more information, rather than a debate over whether rates needed to rise at all. However,
the account contained little support for accelerating the pace of tightening, especially as clear second-round
eects have yet to emerge.
The next few months should bring more information about whether higher energy, freight and other input
costs are spilling over into food, goods and eventually wages. President Lagarde has emphasised that the
ECB cannot wait until second-round eects are fully visible, but must instead watch for early signs, including
in wage demands. This supports a pre-emptive December hike if price pressures continue to broaden,
while leaving room for the ECB to move more cautiously if core inflation and wages remain contained.
We continue to see modest upside potential for longer EUR yields in the medium term. Further ECB
hikes, elevated inflation uncertainty, large public-sector ﬁnancing needs and the continued reduction in the
Eurosystem’s bond holdings all support higher term premia. However, the sharp rise in yields has already
brought market pricing closer to, or in some areas beyond, our fundamental view. This leaves room for a
correction lower in the short term, especially if incoming inflation data show only limited broadening or
concerns about growth become more prominent.
France represents the most important downside risk to our ECB and bond yield outlook. The French-
German 10-year yield spread has climbed to levels not seen since the sovereign-debt crisis, reflecting
doubts about whether a fragmented parliament can deliver a credible ﬁscal adjustment ahead of the 2027
presidential election and, even more importantly, what happens after the election. The pressure has so far
been concentrated mainly in French bonds rather than signalling a broad euro-area funding crisis, but a
more disorderly sell-o could trigger safe-haven demand for Bunds and pull core yields considerably lower.
Lagarde has stressed that the ﬁrst solution lies with French ﬁscal policy, playing down expectations of
imminent ECB intervention.
The risks are thus unusually two-sided, with the balance of risks recently shifting from the upside
towards the downside. More visible spillovers from energy prices into core inflation, wages and inflation
expectations could lead to earlier or additional ECB hikes and lift yields further. By contrast, escalating
French ﬁscal stress, weaker growth or a faster easing of energy prices could put further ECB hikes on hold
and support a bigger move lower in German bond yields. Our baseline remains between these outcomes:
quarterly 25bp hikes in December and March, accompanied by modestly higher long yields over the medium
term but considerable volatility along the way.
