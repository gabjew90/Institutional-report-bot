Capital in Demand  
As demand for capital has increased, the cost of that capital has risen, pushing interest rates higher. While 
higher rates can reflect strong growth opportunities, they can also weigh on economic activity and equity 
valuations. How should investors think about this dynamic? 
 
 
 
Strong Negative Correlation Between the  
S&P 500 P/E and 10-Year Real Rates 
 
    
 
 
• 
Interest rates, as measured by the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield, are up over 100 basis points (bps) 
year-to-date through September. Rather than being driven by rising inflation expectations, most of that 
increase reflects higher “real,” or inflation-adjusted, interest rates, due in part to the strong demand for 
capital associated with the roughly $1 trillion of investment in artificial intelligence (AI) worldwide.  
• 
Equity investors value a company by discounting its future cash flows back to the present. Because 
those cash flows typically include inflation, investors use nominal discount rates, which combine real 
rates and expected inflation. When inflation rises, the higher discount rate is partly offset by higher cash 
flows, since companies can often raise prices. Higher real rates, however, lift discount rates without 
automatically boosting cash flows, reducing the present value of future cash flows, all else equal. This 
helps to explain why real rates tend to weigh more heavily on valuations than inflation, as reflected in 
the strong negative correlation between the S&P 500’s price-to-earnings multiple and real rates shown 
in the chart above.  
• 
With real rates at their highest level since the Global Financial Crisis, we believe there may be limited 
room for further increases, as higher borrowing costs begin to cool the more rate-sensitive parts of the 
economy. Should real rates stabilize, pressure on valuations could ease, removing a drag that has 
offset part of this year’s earnings growth and allowing more of that growth to show up in equity returns 
(see also Raising the Earnings Bar).1 In our view, companies at the center of the AI buildout may be 
well positioned for long-term growth, as their earnings are fueled by the very investment helping to push 
rates higher.
