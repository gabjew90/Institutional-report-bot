Monthly Review
September was the month the global tightening cycle 
arrived. The Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank 
and the Bank of Japan all raised rates within nine days of 
each other, and developed-market government bond yields 
sold off sharply across most major markets and maturities. 
The striking feature was where the adjustment came from: 
real yields rather than inflation expectations. Credit then 
repriced broadly in order of quality, with investment grade 
proving relatively resilient while the weakest high yield 
cohorts gapped wider. Volatility rose steeply in rates and 
only modestly in equities.
The selloff was broad and concentrated in real rates. 
The U.S. 2-year Treasury yield rose 55 basis points (bps) 
and the 10-year 53bps to 5.28%, crossing 5% for the first 
time since 2023 in the days before the Fed met, with 
the back of the curve flattening as the 5s30s spread 
narrowed 20bps. Crucially, 10-year real yields rose 49bps 
while breakevens widened only 5bps, so almost the entire 
move was a repricing of real rates rather than of inflation 
expectations, an unusual split in a month when Brent crude 
reached $106 a barrel. Gilts and Australian and Canadian 
government bonds sold off in sympathy, Italian yields rose 
46bps, and only Japan was spared, where normalization 
was already well advanced. Euro-area spreads to Germany 
widened, with France the clear outlier at 42bps wider. In 
France, longstanding fiscal concerns were compounded by 
deteriorating technicals, as domestic buyers failed to step in 
and stop-loss activity accelerated the move, contributing to 
broader deleveraging across European government bonds.

Three of the four major central banks tightened. The Fed 
raised its target range by 25bps to 3.75%-4.00% on 16 
September, its first increase since 2023, in a unanimous vote 
and with most participants projecting at least one further 
hike this year. The ECB had moved six days earlier, lifting its 
deposit facility rate to 2.50% and warning that the conflict 
in the Middle East would keep inflation above target for 
an extended period. The Bank of Japan followed on 18 
September, raising its policy rate to 1.25% by a seven-to-two 
majority, the highest level since 1995. The Bank of England 
was the exception, holding at 3.75% by six votes to three 
after UK CPI rose to 3.1% in August. U.S. CPI held at 3.4% 
year over year with core little changed. The common thread 
was energy: each statement pointed to the oil shock, and 
none to domestic demand.
The dollar reversed its summer decline, with the broad dollar 
index rising 2.0%. The move was close to universal, the 
euro falling 2.5% and sterling, the Swedish krona and most 
commodity currencies losing more ground still, while the 
Mexican peso was the weakest major at 5.9% lower. The yen 
was the notable exception, appreciating 1.5% as the Bank of 
Japan tightened, with the Korean won the only other gainer 
of note. For a month in which three central banks raised 
rates, the dollar’s strength is a reminder that relative real 
yields rather than policy direction alone drive currencies.
Investment grade (IG) credit was conspicuously resilient. 
U.S. spreads widened only 2bps to 80bps OAS, absorbing 
the move in underlying yields with little difficulty, though 
financials underperformed industrials. Euro investment 
grade fared considerably worse, widening 12bps to 91bps 
and led by financials and subordinated insurance, while 
sterling credit widened 5bps to the same level. The regional 
divergence reflected weaker European technicals rather than 
a comparable deterioration in fundamentals, with French 
sovereign volatility, higher energy prices, heavier issuance 
and fund outflows weighing on Euro IG while U.S. credit 
continued to benefit from stronger inflows and attractive 
all-in yields.

widened 50bps to 311bps, but the move was heavily skewed,

sharper move in its lowest tier, and sterling high yield was 
weaker again. This is the mirror image of August, when 
compression was concentrated in the stronger tiers and 
CCC cheapened against a rallying index. Two consecutive 
months in which the lowest-rated cohort underperformed 
in both directions is a more informative signal than either 
month taken alone. Heavy primary issuance, fund outflows 
and greater idiosyncratic volatility added to the pressure, 
reinforcing the preference for higher-quality credits as lower-
rated capital structures became increasingly sensitive to 
issuer-specific news.

DEVELOPED MARKET RATE/FOREIGN CURRENCY
(Selective long duration, neutral curve positioning, 
selective high-carry FX)
Our views remain grounded in a macro environment where 
growth has proven resilient, but the impact of increasingly 
restrictive monetary policy is likely to become more visible 
over the coming quarters. In the U.S., continued strength in 
activity and persistent inflation have supported expectations 
for additional tightening, while higher yields have already 
tightened financial conditions materially. Although growth 
may remain resilient in the near term, we expect it to 
increasingly meet rather than exceed expectations as the 
cumulative effects of higher rates work through the economy.
Against this backdrop, we retain a selective long-duration bias, 
with our strongest expression concentrated in New Zealand 
relative to U.S. rates. Rather than taking broad directional 
duration exposure, we continue to favor cross-market 
opportunities where relative growth and policy expectations 
appear mispriced. Recent volatility has prompted a reduction 
in overall duration risk, particularly in the U.S., while higher 
yields have also created more attractive entry points in 
markets where growth appears weaker relative to prevailing 
policy settings.
The recent rise in global yields has improved the prospective 
return profile for high-quality fixed income. Current yield 
levels provide a meaningful income cushion, while we believe 
the U.S. 10-year Treasury is approaching levels that should 
become increasingly restrictive for corporate, consumer, 
and housing activity. This creates a more balanced duration 
opportunity, even as near-term inflation and policy risks argue 
against taking excessive directional exposure.
Inflation remains a central macro risk, and we maintain 
exposure to U.S. 10-year breakevens. Persistent underlying 
price pressures, elevated nominal growth, and uncertainty 
around the timing and magnitude of further Federal Reserve 
tightening continue to support mitigation against the risk 
that inflation remains above target for longer than markets 
currently anticipate.
We remain broadly neutral on global curve direction while 
expressing selective relative-value views across markets. 
Current positioning favors differences in curve shape 
between the U.S. and Canada, alongside a flatter long-end 
profile in Japan, rather than a broad global steepening or 
flattening thesis. This approach reflects differing policy 
paths, fiscal dynamics, and term-premium behavior across 
developed markets.
In foreign exchange, positioning remains selective and risk-
controlled. We favor the Japanese yen relative to the U.S. 
dollar, while using options to help limit downside risk. More 
broadly, currency exposure remains modest as relative policy 
expectations, growth differentials, and volatility continue to 
shift quickly across markets.

EMERGING MARKET DEBT
(Reducing overweight)
We have started reducing our overweight on emerging 
market (EM) debt following a period of strong performance, 
tight credit valuations, and a less supportive global policy 
backdrop. Elevated real yields and improving fundamentals 
continue to create attractive opportunities in select countries, 
but broad EM credit spreads offer less compensation than 
earlier in the year and the stronger U.S. dollar has increased 
the hurdle for indiscriminate risk-taking.
Portfolio risk has consequently been reduced, with greater 
emphasis on preserving flexibility until more compelling 
opportunities emerge. EM credit spreads remained relatively 
resilient through much of September, but deteriorating 
technicals and renewed widening late in the month reinforced 
our preference for selective exposure rather than broad 
credit beta. Portfolio risk remains near multi-year lows, 
reflecting a more cautious approach while macro and political 
uncertainty remain elevated.
Within the asset class, we increasingly favor local rates 
and country-specific opportunities where monetary policy, 
political developments, and external balances provide 
identifiable catalysts. Recent developments in Brazil 
illustrate the potential for political outcomes to create 
meaningful opportunities in both rates and currencies, while 
markets such as Argentina, Romania, and Ecuador highlight 
the importance of avoiding broad regional generalizations.
Carry and income remain important sources of expected return, 
but valuations require greater selectivity. We continue to favor 
countries with credible monetary frameworks, improving 
external balances, and attractive real-yield differentials, while 
maintaining a more cautious posture toward areas where strong-
dollar pressure, political uncertainty, or tight credit spreads 
leave limited margin for error.
CORPORATE CREDIT
(Underweight IG, small overweight HY)
We remain underweight investment grade corporates as 
tight valuations continue to offer limited mitigation against 
macro and rates volatility. September’s widening improved 
valuations somewhat, but the move remained orderly and 
was driven primarily by broader market volatility rather than 
a deterioration in corporate fundamentals. Healthy balance 
sheets, resilient earnings, and attractive all-in yields continue 
to support the asset class, but carry rather than broad spread 
compression remains the primary source of expected return.
We no longer maintain a broad preference for European 
investment grade over the U.S. European credit 
underperformed materially during September as French 
sovereign volatility, higher energy prices, heavy primary 
issuance, and weaker fund flows pressured spreads. By 
contrast, U.S. IG technicals remained comparatively stronger, 
supported by continued inflows, attractive all-in yields, and 
issuance that came in below expectations. We therefore 
favor a more balanced regional approach, with security 
selection and relative value taking precedence over a broad 
geographic preference.
The recent widening has also created selective opportunities 
within areas of U.S. investment grade that previously offered 
insufficient compensation. We are increasingly willing to 
consider technology, hyperscaler, and data-center-related 
issuance where spreads have repriced materially and 
investors are being better compensated for construction, 
financing, and execution risks. The threshold for participation 
remains high, particularly given the expected growth in AI-
related issuance and the increasing weight these securities 
may ultimately represent within fixed income benchmarks.
Fundamentals remain broadly solid, though later-cycle 
pressures continue to build through elevated M&A activity, 
infrastructure investment, AI-related capital expenditure, 
and higher shareholder distributions. We continue to favor 
issuers with resilient cash flows, strong balance sheets, and 
manageable refinancing needs while remaining cautious in 
sectors where technological disruption, leverage, or complex 
financing structures could weaken credit quality.
We maintain a modest overweight to select high-yield 
issuers. Recent widening has improved the opportunity 
set, but dispersion remains pronounced and lower-quality 
credits continue to lag. We therefore favor BB and higher-
quality single-B issuers with resilient cash flows, manageable 
maturities, and strong pricing power rather than broad high-
yield beta.
With much of the fundamental backdrop still supportive 
but macro volatility elevated, we expect active security 
selection to remain critical. Higher all-in yields have improved 
prospective income, but tight spreads across many higher-
quality sectors continue to leave limited room for error.

LEVERAGED LOANS
(Neutral)
We maintain a neutral stance on leveraged loans. The asset 
class continues to benefit from strong CLO demand and 
resilient retail flows, but recent outperformance has reduced 
its relative attractiveness versus high yield and other fixed-
rate credit sectors. As a result, we are more selective about 
adding risk and increasingly focused on opportunities where 
spread compensation remains compelling.
Dispersion remains elevated across the market. Software 
and technology-linked issuers continue to face uncertainty 
around AI-related disruption, while lower-quality and more 
cyclical sectors have also experienced greater idiosyncratic 
weakness. We continue to favor higher-quality, mission-critical 
businesses with durable cash flows, strong interest coverage, 
and manageable maturity profiles.
Although policy rates remain elevated, current floating-rate 
borrowing costs have not yet translated into a material 
increase in defaults, in part because many issuers remain well 
hedged. CLO demand also continues to provide an important 
technical anchor for the market.
Overall, we continue to see selective opportunities in loans, 
but recent relative performance and tighter technicals argue 
for a more balanced stance versus high yield and other fixed-
rate alternatives.
SECURITIZED PRODUCTS 
(Overweight)
We remain overweight securitized products, although our 
view within the sector has become increasingly differentiated. 
Recent volatility has created more attractive relative-value 
opportunities across both agency and non-agency markets, 
while underlying credit fundamentals remain broadly stable.
Agency (MBS) have cheapened materially as higher Treasury 
yields, increased rates volatility, and extension risk pressured 
the sector. Exposure was reduced ahead of much of the 
widening, and the subsequent repricing has improved the 
opportunity set meaningfully. We remain constructive on 
agency MBS from a strategic perspective and are evaluating 
opportunities to rebuild exposure as valuations become 
more compelling, rather than treating the sector as an 
unconditional high-conviction overweight at current levels.
The fundamental mortgage backdrop remains supportive. 
With mortgage rates elevated, less than 1% of outstanding 
borrowers are currently in the money to refinance, materially 
reducing prepayment risk across most coupons. Recent 
extension has also improved convexity, with much of the 
mortgage stack now trading at discounts and closer to full 
extension, making the sector easier to hedge against further 
rate increases. The principal near-term question is technical 
demand, as banks, overseas investors, hedge funds, and REITs 
remain sensitive to stabilization in Treasury yields before 
increasing purchases.
Non-agency residential MBS remains one of the more 
attractive areas of structured credit. Spreads widened 
only modestly through the recent market volatility, while 
fundamentals remained broadly stable. Higher yields and 
somewhat wider spreads have improved prospective returns, 
particularly in higher-quality fixed-rate structures where 
investors can capture both Treasury yield and additional credit 
spread without assuming excessive lower-quality risk.
Commercial (MBS) remain attractive selectively, though 
refinancing risk is becoming increasingly important as higher 
rates feed through to property valuations and debt-service 
requirements. We continue to favor higher-quality properties 
with strong sponsors and visible cash-flow generation, 
where equity holders have both the ability and willingness 
to contribute capital if necessary. Lower-quality conduit 
exposure remains less attractive given the risk that refinancing 
challenges translate into more significant credit impairment.
Asset-backed securities (ABS) continue to provide attractive 
diversification and income, supported by their shorter-duration 
profile and generally resilient fundamentals. Supply remains 
elevated across ABS, RMBS, and CMBS, making technical 
conditions an increasingly important consideration heading into 
year-end. We therefore continue to prioritize structures where 
collateral quality, cash-flow visibility, and spread compensation 
justify the additional complexity.
Overall, the recent widening has improved the relative-
value opportunity across securitized markets, but we 
remain disciplined about entry levels. We favor higher-
quality structures and sectors where volatility rather than 
deteriorating credit fundamentals has driven repricing, 
preserving capacity to add risk should technical pressures 
create further opportunities.

Risk Considerations
Diversification neither assures a profit nor guarantees against loss in a declining market.
There is no assurance that a portfolio will achieve its investment objective. Portfolios are subject to market risk, which is the possibility 
that the market values of securities owned by the portfolio will decline and that the value of portfolio shares may therefore be less than 
what you paid for them. Market values can change daily due to economic and other events (e.g., natural disasters, health crises, terrorism, 
conflicts, and social unrest) that affect markets, countries, companies, or governments. It is difficult to predict the timing, duration, 
and potential adverse effects (e.g., portfolio liquidity) of events. Accordingly, you can lose money investing in a portfolio. Fixed-income 
securities are subject to the ability of an issuer to make timely principal and interest payments (credit risk), changes in interest rates 
(interest rate risk), the creditworthiness of the issuer and general market liquidity (market risk). In a rising interest-rate environment, bond 
prices may fall and may result in periods of volatility and increased portfolio redemptions. In a declining interest-rate environment, the 
portfolio may generate less income. Longer-term securities may be more sensitive to interest rate changes. Certain U.S. government 
securities purchased by the strategy, such as those issued by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, are not backed by the full faith and credit of 
the U.S. It is possible that these issuers will not have the funds to meet their payment obligations in the future. Public bank loans are 
subject to liquidity risk and the credit risks of lower-rated securities. High-yield securities (junk bonds) are lower-rated securities that 
may have a higher degree of credit and liquidity risk. Sovereign debt securities are subject to default risk. Mortgage- and asset-backed 
securities are sensitive to early prepayment risk and a higher risk of default and may be hard to value and difficult to sell (liquidity 
risk). They are also subject to credit, market, and interest rate risks. The currency market is highly volatile. Prices in these markets are 
influenced by, among other things, changing supply and demand for a particular currency; trade; fiscal, money and domestic or foreign 
exchange control programs and policies; and changes in domestic and foreign interest rates. Investments in foreign markets entail special 
risks such as currency, political, economic and market risks. The risks of investing in emerging market countries are greater than the risks 
generally associated with foreign investments. Derivative instruments may disproportionately increase losses and have a significant 
impact on performance. They also may be subject to counterparty, liquidity, valuation, and correlation and market risks. Restricted 
and illiquid securities may be more difficult to sell and value than publicly traded securities (liquidity risk). Due to the possibility that 
prepayments will alter the cash flows on collateralized mortgage obligations (CMOs), it is not possible to determine in advance their 
final maturity date or average life. In addition, if the collateral securing the CMOs or any third-party guarantees are insufficient to make 
payments, the portfolio could sustain a loss.
Investing in companies in anticipation of a catalyst event, such as AI adoption, carries the risk that such catalysts may not occur, may be 
delayed, or that the market may react differently than expected. Companies focused on AI may have limited product lines, markets or 
financial resources, and their management and performance may be particularly impacted by events that adversely affect AI adoption, such 
as rapid changes in product technology cycles, product obsolescence, government regulation, cybersecurity concerns and competition.
