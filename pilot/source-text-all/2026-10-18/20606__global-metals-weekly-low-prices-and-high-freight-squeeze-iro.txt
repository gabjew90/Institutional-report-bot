Low prices and high freight squeeze iron 
ore miners 
     
   
 
 
Iron ore prices have fallen on weak fundamentals…  
China's steel-intensive sectors remain under pressure. The prolonged downturn in the 
property market continues to weigh on construction activity, while infrastructure 
investment has also lost momentum. As a result, Chinese steel demand is still weak, with 
crude steel output down 3% YoY in Jan-Aug. At the same time, seaborne supply growth 
has remained resilient, driven by strong Australian exports and the ramp-up of 
Simandou. On the back of this, iron ore prices have fallen from above US$110/t in May 
to around US$90/t CFR China. This has prompted output cuts in China, while rising 
freight rates have also put pressure on production ex-China. We still expect a surplus in 
2027, but the supply discipline suggests that prices are closer to finding a bottom now. 
Hence, we are comfortable with our 2027 $98/t forecast. 
…and cost support is now limiting downside risk  
At current levels, iron ore prices are close to the 90th percentile of the global CFR China 
cost curve. Roughly 170Mt of annualised supply is now economically challenged. While 
not all of this may be removed from the market, the current price environment is 
increasingly testing the economics of marginal tonnes across China, West Africa, 
Australia and South America. However, if prices remain near current levels, supply 
rationalisation could accelerate and rebalance the market relatively quickly, potentially 
mirroring what happened in the met coal market this year (see report: Met coal supply 
crunch).  
Chinese domestic output is declining, supporting imports… 
China's domestic iron ore sector continues to struggle with declining ore grades, rising 
costs and tighter environmental and safety requirements. Output is down 8.8% YoY YTD 
despite ongoing policy support aimed at improving resource security. The combination 
of weaker mill profitability and falling iron ore prices has further curtailed activity among 
smaller producers. As a result, iron ore imports have continued to rise, facilitated by 
resilient Australian shipments and the ramp-up of Simandou: as domestic Chinese supply 
contracts, import dependence continues to rise, helping support seaborne demand even 
as Chinese steel production remains in structural decline.  
…while freight costs are triggering output cuts in Brazil 
The sharp increase in freight rates has compounded the impact of lower iron ore prices, 
particularly for longer-haul Brazilian producers. Brazil-China freight costs climbed above 
US$40/t in September and remain well above historical norms, pushing netbacks to an 
eight-year low and compressing margins. The deterioration in economics is now 
translating into a supply response, with high-cost exporters announcing production cuts, 
suspensions or operational slowdowns. In our view, continued weakness in Chinese steel 
demand suggests that the next leg higher in iron ore prices is likely to be supply-driven.

 Iron ore margin squeeze 
Iron ore prices have been falling since the May peak… 
Iron ore prices have been declining steadily since 2021 as market fundamentals have 
gradually softened, reflecting a combination of rising mine supply and weaker Chinese 
steel demand. This year, however, prices proved resilient through the first half of 2026, 
supported by higher freight and energy costs amid Middle East disruptions (the iron ore 
benchmark is quoted CFR China and therefore incorporates freight costs), as well as the 
CMRG1-BHP dispute, which temporarily restricted trading of certain BHP products at 
Chinese ports. Together, these factors helped keep prices above US$100/t and push 
them to around US$112/t in early May.

Structural challenges are also becoming more apparent. China's ore bodies are generally 
low grade and require extensive beneficiation, while the industry is increasingly 
transitioning from lower-cost surface mines to deeper underground operations, raising 
costs further and extending development timelines. Although investment in ferrous 
mining has increased and a pipeline of new projects is under development, regulatory 
hurdles and lengthy construction schedules mean new capacity is unlikely to offset 
current declines in the near term. As a result, China's domestic iron ore sector remains 
caught between rising costs, falling competitiveness and abundant imported supply. 
In the seaborne market, exports from Australia and Guinea have been strong… 
Turning to seaborne supply, Australian shipments have remained exceptionally strong, 
supported by a combination of new capacity ramp-ups and productivity improvements 
across the Pilbara. BHP continues to benefit from the full ramp-up of South Flank, which 
has reached its 80 Mtpa nameplate capacity and, together with Mining Area C, now 
forms the world's largest iron ore hub. Rio Tinto has offset depletion at some legacy 
operations through the ramp-up of Gudai-Darri and the start-up of Western Range. 
Outside the majors, Mineral Resources' Onslow Iron project has emerged as a 
meaningful new source of supply, having ramped up to its 35 Mtpa nameplate rate in 
2025.

Alongside strong Australian shipments, Guinea is rapidly emerging as a new source of 
seaborne supply growth. Simandou began exporting in late 2025 and is already having a 
measurable impact on trade flows, with China's imports from Guinea reaching around 8 
Mt in January-August. At the current pace, imports could exceed 15 Mt by year-end. 
While volumes remain modest relative to the established suppliers, we expect Simandou 
to ramp up steadily over the coming years, becoming the single most important growth 
project in the seaborne iron ore market.  
In its 3Q production results, Rio Tinto highlighted that “Simandou hit an annualised 
production run-rate of ~10 Mtpa (100% basis) in Q3 as the project ramps up and 
remains on track to reach a 60 Mtpa production run-rate during H2 2028”. With ore 
grades exceeding 65% Fe and strong backing from China, the project offers Chinese 
steelmakers an attractive source of high-quality feedstock while supporting Beijing's 
broader objective of diversifying iron ore supply away from traditional exporters.

…unlike Brazilian shipments, which have slowed recently 
By contrast, Brazilian export growth has lost momentum this year, with shipments down 
0.6% YoY YTD. Vale's decision to lower its 2026 production guidance to 335-345 Mt 
from 340-360 Mt last December reflects this. The revision is broadly consistent with 
management's "value over volume" strategy, which prioritises product quality, realised 
prices and margin optimisation over maximising tonnes shipped, while still targeting 
longer-term growth through Serra Sul +20Mt and the broader Carajás expansion 
programme. More recently, July exports were affected by a temporary disruption on the 
Carajás Railroad, constraining shipments from Vale's Northern System and S11D, while 
early-August volumes have faced additional pressure from compressed margins.  
The slowdown also highlights the importance of freight costs for longer-haul suppliers. 
Looking at the fourth quartile of the delivered CFR China cost curve, more than one-third 
of supply originates from Brazil and other South American producers, compared with 
only a small contribution from Australian miners. This underscores how transportation 
costs remain a key determinant of competitiveness for longer-distance exporters, 
particularly in a lower-price environment.

Iron ore freight costs to China have risen sharply… 
Indeed, Exhibit 12 highlights the sharp increase in freight costs from Brazil to China 
(Tubarão to Qingdao), which reached a record US$43/t in September and are still

the start of the year. As a result, freight now accounts for a disproportionate share of 
the delivered CFR China price for Brazilian producers, currently approaching 40% or 
double historical norms (Exhibit 13). By comparison, Australia-to-China freight (Western 
Australia to Qingdao) also reached a record high of US$19/t in September and has 
averaged around US$14/t month-to-date. While materially lower in absolute terms, 
freight costs now represent roughly 16% of the delivered China price for Australian 
producers, also around twice historical averages.

…keeping steel prices and mill margins depressed 
While Chinese steel prices have moved higher since August alongside the rally in coking 
coal prices (see report: Met coal supply crunch), mill margins remain under pressure, 
suggesting that steelmakers have been unable to fully pass through higher input costs. 
China's traditional steel demand drivers also remain challenged. The property sector is 
still in a prolonged downturn, with recently unveiled housing-sales reforms unlikely to 
provide meaningful support for construction activity (see report: China Watch: Chart 
book series #204: Housing policy reform likely to weigh on investment).  
Although the government's "Six Networks" strategy should support overall fixed-asset 
investment through spending on energy, computing, communications and other strategic 
sectors, these areas are generally less steel-intensive than the property and 
infrastructure projects that underpinned previous demand cycles. Against this backdrop, 
demand for steelmaking raw materials remains constrained by structural headwinds. As 
a result, we believe the next leg higher in iron ore prices is more likely to be driven by 
supply-side disruptions than a meaningful recovery in Chinese steel demand.
