This week was a travel week, beginning at the National Association for Business Economics (NABE) annual
conference in Cleveland, Ohio, and ending in Phoenix, Arizona, for several advisor and client
presentations. As you might expect, the NABE conference was dominated by discussions about AI and its
potential impact on long-term economic growth.
We also heard from Cleveland Federal Reserve President Beth Hammack, who entered this year as one of
the more outspokenly hawkish members of the Federal Open Market Committee. She has consistently
argued for higher interest rates to stay ahead of inflationary pressures, and her remarks at the conference
confirmed she remains firmly in that camp. While she acknowledged the challenges facing many
households and businesses within her district, she views restrictive monetary policy as necessary.
We understand the Fed's concerns. After all, high inflation disproportionately harms lower-income
households, which have limited ability to shield themselves from rising prices. However, as we argued
several weeks ago, fiscal policymakers should also bear some responsibility for moderating economic
growth. Without greater assistance on that front, the Fed may be forced to tighten policy to a degree that
unnecessarily increases recession risks.
The Fed currently finds itself in a difficult position. Lower- and middle-income households are often the
first to be priced out of credit markets when interest rates rise, creating a double burden as they
simultaneously contend with higher borrowing costs and elevated prices. The objective of higher rates is
to slow demand, but demand is already soft for much of the population. In contrast, the spending power of
higher-income households continues to be supported by the same factors that have driven roughly half of
all consumer spending in recent years.

For these households, higher interest rates can actually provide an income boost through increased returns 
on interest-bearing assets. In that sense, monetary policy may be acting less as a restriction and more as a 
source of additional income. Combined with gains from wealth accumulation, rising interest income has 
helped support spending among upper-income consumers. As we noted following the Bureau of Economic 
Analysis revisions to the national accounts, interest income receipts were revised higher, reinforcing this 
dynamic. As a result, it is difficult to argue that higher-income households will materially reduce 
consumption simply because interest rates move higher.
At the same time, consumer sentiment surveys continue to paint a troubling picture. They suggest growing 
economic anxiety among households and increasing political challenges for the incumbent party heading 
into the midterm elections. These concerns are not new, but earlier in the year consumers still benefited 
from lower taxes and larger tax refunds, which helped support spending. Those tailwinds have largely 
faded.
A recent Harris poll conducted for The Guardian found that three-quarters of Americans are concerned 
about a recession, while 61% believe the economy is already in one. Among Democrats, the share believing 
the economy is in recession rose from 67% in the May-June survey to 72% today. Among Independents, it 
increased from 54% to 61%, while among Republicans it climbed from 39% to 50%.
This week's retail and food services report for September may have provided the first meaningful indication 
that consumers are beginning to feel the effects of higher prices. Headline retail sales increased a strong 
0.8% on a month-over-month basis, but that figure was significantly boosted by higher sales at gasoline 
stations, reflecting rising gasoline prices rather than stronger underlying demand.

Excluding gasoline stations, nominal retail sales rose just 0.2%. More importantly, control group retail sales, 
which feed directly into personal consumption expenditures in the GDP accounts, increased only 0.1%. 
After adjusting for inflation, that translates into negative real growth for September.
Although consumer spending remained solid during the third quarter, much of that strength was 
concentrated in August. Both July and September showed signs of weaker demand. Monthly consumer 
spending data can be volatile, so one month does not establish a trend. Nevertheless, it is a development 
worth monitoring closely.
One area that remained resilient was spending at restaurants and bars, where sales rose 1.1% during the 
month. That result is consistent with the view that higher-income households, which have benefited the 
most from asset gains and higher interest income, continue to support overall consumer spending.
Bottom Line: The Federal Reserve faces an increasingly difficult balancing act as elevated inflation 
continues to weigh on lower- and middle-income households, while higher-income consumers remain 
relatively insulated by wealth gains and rising interest income. September’s retail sales report suggests that 
higher prices may finally be taking a toll on underlying consumer demand, even as nominal headline 
spending remains strong. With monetary policy more than likely having an uneven impact across income 
groups, further interest rate hikes risk placing additional pressure on already stretched households without 
meaningfully slowing spending among higher-income consumers.

Existing Home Sales: Existing home sales were stronger than expected in September, boosted by a strong performance 
from the South and the West. The Midwest region was flat month on month while sales were lower in the Northeast. Overall, 
existing home sales were lower in September of this year compared to last year, both at the national level as well as for all 
the regions of the country. Meanwhile the number of months of inventory for existing home sales was up compared to a 
year earlier while the median price of homes increased by 1.3% compared to September of last year. Commentaries by the 
Chief Economist of the NAR indicate that the existing home sales market is a buyers’ market even as higher mortgage rates 
are reducing the pool of potential buyers. Existing home sales were stronger than expected in September, at a seasonally 
adjusted rate of 3.99 million, up from a downwardly revised rate of 3.93 million in August of this year, according to the 
National Association of Realtors (NAR). This was a 1.5% increase compared to August of 2026, but a 2.2% decline compared 
to September of last year. The strength in existing home sales was due to a very strong month-on-month growth rate in the 
South and West regions, both recording an increase of 2.8%. On a year-over-year basis, the South region recorded a decline 
of 0.5% while the West region recorded a decline of 5.2%. In the Northeast, existing home sales declined 2.1%, month on 
month, and 4.1% compared to a year earlier while the Midwest showed a flat month-on-month print and a decline of 2.1% 
compared to September of last year. The inventory of homes at the end of September was 1.6 million, up from 1.57 million 
in August of this year and also up from 1.53 million in September of last year. This amounted to a 4.8 months’ supply of 
existing homes, according to the report, unchanged compared to August but up 6.7% compared to the 4.5 months’ supply 
recorded in September of last year.  The median price of existing homes was $417,500 in September of this year, up 1.3% 
compared to the same month last year when the median price of existing homes was $412,300. According to the Chief 
Economist of the NAR, Lawrence Yun, “Despite the small monthly gain on a seasonally adjusted basis, August and 
September marked the two slowest months for home sales this year.” He also indicated that an increase in inventory of 
homes and the reduction in potential buyers due to higher mortgage rates is giving “buyers improved negotiating power.
Consumer Price Index: This was certainly not a good inflation report for the Federal Reserve (Fed). Headline CPI inflation 
increased by 0.6%, month on month, and by 3.7% on a year-over-year basis. Although the month-on-month increase was in 
line with expectations, the year-over-year change was higher than expected. The core CPI increased by a higher than 
expected rate, up 0.3% on a month-on-month basis and by a higher than expected rate of 2.6% compared to a year earlier. 
Although slightly more than half of the increase in headline inflation was due to the increase in fuel prices, shelter cost 
prices were also strong during the month, increasing by 0.4%. The most negative part of this release was the increase of 
0.4% in services less energy services prices, which was driven by a 0.4% increase in shelter, a 0.6% increase in 
transportations services prices, and a 0.3% increase in medical care services prices, all month on month. This means that 
inflation is broadening out, something that is not a good omen for the Fed and would raise the probability for an increase in 
rates at the end of the October FOMC meeting. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) was in line with expectations in September, 
increasing by 0.6% after a 0.4% increase in August while the core CPI, which excluded energy and food prices, increased by 
0.3%, and was higher than expected, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The increase in gasoline prices accounted 
for about 50% of the increase in the CPI during the month of September. The increase in energy prices was 5.2% during the 
month of September as gasoline prices increased by 9.1%. Fuel oil prices surged by 15.3% after an increase of 10.1% during 
the month of August. Once again, shelter prices also had an outsized increase in the index, increasing by 0.4% after a 0.3% 
increase in August. Food prices were unchanged during the month of September, according to the release. The price of used 
cars and trucks was also strong during September, increasing by 0.7%, month on month. Transportation services prices also 
increased, up 0.6% after increasing by 0.5% in August. On a year-over-year basis, CPI inflation increased to 3.7% versus 
expectations for a 3.6% increase while the core CPI increased by 2.6% during the same period of time and higher than 
expectations for a 2.5% increase.  This was not a good CPI report for the Federal Reserve (Fed) and will add pressure on Fed 
members to increase interest rates during the October FOMC meeting.
Producer Price Index: The Producer Price Index (PPI) for final demand increased a much stronger than expected 1.3% 
month over month in September—the largest monthly increase since March 2022. The increase was driven by final demand 
for goods (+2.6% MoM), which rose at the fastest monthly pace since 2009 when data were first calculated. Most of the 
increase, however, can be attributed to energy prices, and when excluded, final demand for goods rose 0.3% MoM only. 
Meanwhile, final demand for services were relatively contained but still recorded a strong 0.5% month-over-month 
increase. Overall, today’s report was not particularly encouraging and was broadly in line with CPI report from yesterday. 
This report further raises the probability for an increase in rates at the next FOMC meeting at the end of October. The 
Producer Price Index (PPI) for final demand increased 1.3% MoM in September, much higher than FactSet consensus of 
0.5%. On a year-over-year basis, the PPI for final demand reached 6.3% in September compared to reading of 5.6% in 
August, according to the BLS. Final demand less foods, energy, and trade services increased 0.5% MoM.

Producer Price Index (continued): The PPI final demand goods increased by 2.6% in September, while PPI final demand 
goods less foods and energy increased by 0.3% month-over-month. Within the PPI final demand services, trade prices 
(which measures changes in margins charged by retailers and wholesalers) increased by 1.4%, while prices of 
transportation and warehousing services jumped 3.4% MoM. Roughly half of September’s increase in final demand goods 
prices was driven by a 20.3% month-over month surge in gasoline prices.
Retail Sales: Retail and food services sales were stronger than expected in September, driven by a strong increase in sales 
at gasoline stations. However, the increase in sales at gasoline stations was fundamentally driven by the increase in 
gasoline prices. Overall, the report was mixed, as control group sales were much weaker, increasing by just 0.1% compared 
to the top line number of 0.8%. Sales at department stores was the second strongest group during the month of September, 
up by 1.6% month on month, while sales at general merchandise stores declined by 0.1%. Retail and food services sales 
were stronger than expected in September, up 0.8% month on month, after an upwardly revised increase of 1.3% in August. 
Compared to September of 2025, sales were up 6.3%. Excluding sales at motor vehicle dealers, sales were up 0.9%, and if we 
exclude sales at gasoline stations, sales increased just 0.2%. If we exclude sales at vehicle dealers and at gasoline stations, 
sales increased by 0.2%. Retail trade sales, which exclude sales at food services and drinking places, increased by 0.7% 
month on month. Sales at gasoline stations surged by 6.4% during the month of September but since this report measures 
nominal sales, most of the increase in gasoline sales is due to the increase in gasoline prices. Other notable increases by 
distribution channel were: a 1.3% increase in sales at furniture stores, a 1.1% increase in sales at food services and drinking 
places establishments and a 1.6% increase in department stores sales. The weakest sectors were sales at miscellaneous 
store retailers, whose sales declined by 3.0%, sales at sporting goods stores, down 0.5%, and sales at building material 
stores, declining by 0.6%. Control group retail sales, which are used to calculate personal consumption expenditures in 
GDP, was weak, increasing by just 0.1%, month on month while being revised down to an increase of 1.2% in August. 
Although the increase in retail and food services sales was strong in September, it was driven by the increase in sales at 
gasoline stations, which is reflecting the increase in gasoline prices during the month.
Business Inventories: Business inventories were stronger than expected in August, up 0.4% compared to July of this year. 
Sales were high during the month, so the inventories to sales ratio came down to 1.33 in August of this year compared to a 
ratio of 1.40 in August of 2025. This decline in the inventories to sales ratio was due to a much lower ratio for both 
manufacturers and wholesalers while retail inventories to sales ratio came down but just slightly. This may be pointing to 
some weakness in retail that could start showing later in the year in the distribution chain. The value of distributive trade 
sales and manufacturers’ shipments were up 1.1% during the month of August compared to July of this year, to $2.123.5 
billion. At the same time, they were up by 10.2% compared to August of last year, according to the US Census Bureau. 
Manufacturers’ and trade inventories were up by 0.4% to $2.818.8 billion in August and compared to July of this year. 
Compared to a year earlier, inventories were up by 0.4% compared to the previous month and up by 4.6% compared to a 
year earlier. The inventories to sales ratio at the end of August was 1.33, down from a ratio of 1.40 in August of last year, 
according to the release. Both manufacturers’ and merchant wholesalers’ inventories to sales ratio were much lower in 
August of this year than a year earlier. Manufacturers’ inventories to sales ratio was 1.48 in August of this year compared to 
1.56 in August of last year while merchant wholesalers’ inventories to sales ratio was down to 1.18 compared to 1.28 in 
August of 2025. Retailers’ inventories to sales ratio was slightly lower in August of this year, at 1.36, compared to 1.37 a year 
earlier.
Import/Export Price Indices: Although the strong increase in import prices has been captured in both the CPI and the PPI 
released earlier, today’s report was also not good for the Federal Reserve and the prospects of monetary policy. Fuel import 
prices drove the strength in the index in September, but non-fuel import prices were also strong during the month. Higher 
prices for capital goods imports, non-fuel industrial supplies and materials, etc., contributed to the increase in import 
prices, underscoring the still-strong effects of the AI buildup.Import prices increased by a more than expected 1.1% in 
September while export prices surged by twice as much, 2.2%, compared to August, according to the Bureau of Labor 
Statistics. On a year-over-year basis, import prices increased by 8.1%, the largest year-over-year increase since July 2022. 
Fuel import prices increased 4.8%, month on month, after declining by 0.2% in August. Fuel import prices increased by 
34.5% on a year-earlier basis. All import prices excluding fuel prices increased by 0.7% during the month, according to the 
release. Nonfuel import prices increased by 6.0% on a year-earlier basis. The 2.2% increase in export prices was driven by a 
2.6% month-on-month increase in agricultural export prices and a 2.1% increase in non-agricultural export prices. On a 
year-over-year basis, export prices were up by 11.6%. This was the largest year-over-year increase in export prices since July 
of 2022.

Import/Export Price Indices (continued): Import prices from China increased by 0.7% in September, the largest monthly

Industrial Production: Industrial production increased more than expected in September while being revised upwardly in 
August. The improvement in industrial production was broad based, with expansion in every major industry group. The 
leading sector within the production of final products—the production of business equipment—remained weak for a second 
consecutive month, raising concerns that the leading sector for the recovery in manufacturing production is starting to 
falter. Still, business equipment production remained the strongest sector within the major market groups. The industrial 
production index increase by a more than expected 0.7% during the month of September, month on month, while August’s 
performance was revised higher, from a flat reading to a 0.1% increase, according to the Federal Reserve. On a year-over-
year basis, industrial production increased by 2.2%. By major industry groups, manufacturing production was up 0.3% 
during the month of September, month on month, while up 1.1% compared to a year earlier. The manufacturing production 
index was revised slightly higher in August, from a decline of 0.3% to a decline of 0.2%. Mining production increased by 0.2% 
during the month of September and was up by 2.8% compared to a year earlier. Finally, the production of utilities surged by 
3.5%, month on month, and was up 8.7% compared to a year earlier. By major market group, the production of final 
products increased by 0.5% after declining by 0.1% in August, boosted by a 0.7% increase in consumer goods production. 
Final products production was up 1.9% compared to September of last year. Within this segment, the production of 
business equipment was flat in September after declining by 0.1% in August. Still, the production of business equipment 
was up 6.7% compared to a year earlier. The production of nonindustrial supplies was up 0.9%, month on month, with 
construction increasing by 1.0% after declining by 0.7% in August. Finally, the production of materials was up 0.7%, month 
on month in September. Capacity utilization for the total industrial sector increased to 76.9% in September compared to 
76.4% in August.
