AI valuation concerns have rekindled market volatility, but it should 
be corrective and 
not mark 
an inflection point in the 
current cycle 
• 
The underlying AI investment story
 still looks durable

With classical risk off dynamics making a bit of a comeback this week, 
it is worth a dig through some of the charts that inform about these 
relationships, particularly at a point in the cycle where the market has 
refocused on the “AI bubble” theme.  We are
 hoping to avoid becoming 
a proxy for the cover of the Economist
 with that . 
We won’t look at credit, which many feel is where the canary will 
topple.  But we will muse on AI.  For the moment that investment cycle 
looks sustainable, which took me some time to get comfortable with.  
Sure, it is going to be expensive, the hyperscalers are spending one Elon 
Musk on it this year
 alone (Figure 1).  But making comparisons to 
previous bubble periods 
– US railroads seem to be the popular one 
– 
probably misunderstands the situation.  Even in simple terms that 
framing looks wrong, the 
duration is different and a railway can 
only get 
you from A -B (and in 1836 there was often a barren
 patch of sand at 
point B).  AI, in contrast, has practical uses across the economy, globally 
with demand at every point.  And the firms doing the spending are the 
highest earning businesses in history, with (currently) very little leverage 
on their balance sheets (
Figure 2).  This time is different, oh dear!

running behind demand
, despite all 
the hype about the cost.  We look to have shifted into Jeavons Paradox 
territory, where the rate of improvement in

infrastructure embeds these technologies.  It’s not going to be like 
swapping Netflix (dross) for Apple TV (currently not dross), you 
will not 
be able to cut the cord

That is an unprecedented level of leverage across the economy and 
society; it would be a miracle if they didn’t reap a proportionally large

technology itself; we’re already at a point 
where it is transformative.  
We’re not starting at dial up where the internet was just promise as

the turn of the Millennium

all singing and dancing.

that does not absolve all of the risks.  The economy 
has faced numerous 
challenges in recent years
.  Its ability to weather 
those shocks has been 
encouraging.  Central bankers

One other aspect of the AI buildout has been the change in equity flow

ve people complained that

of firms lack
ing viable

investment bets.  Equity markets have proved resilient to that

Filling the gap has been overseas buyers (Figure 8), lured by the AI story

As AI washes through
, opportunities to innovate and increase 
productivity ought to multiply beyond the hyperscaler/chip ecosystem.
  
Whether that is good for labour is another matter 
— we’re sceptical 
that human cognition is uniquely superior for many tasks (creativity is 
by definition rare
, lots of work is mundane
).  B
roader steps in 
automation/robotics are going to add further competition.  The 
optimists are left with historical precedents for ‘the new types of jobs 
that are always created’; failing that we’ll just have to be thankful the 
public sector lost interest in
 generating its own productivity a long time 
ago.  There look good odds that the owners of capital will reap the 
bulk 
of the gains. 
There is also an inflation effect, rising investment demand is running 
ahead of the capacity to deliver.  That creates a cost headache for that 
sector directly, but 
it also bleeds across the economy via the many 
shared inputs (not just iPhone memory, many other components, 
energy, construction etc).  That suggests a higher equilibrium rate
 
and /or a need for tighter financial conditions
, certainly if 2% inflation is 
going to be durably achieved
, as Chair Warsh has pledged
.  Markets 
have not worried too much about inflation thus far, despite energy 
price rises (evident in steady breakeven rates), but that looks to 
underestimate the cyclical risks present and broader pricing power that 
a strong US economy facilitates.
  It’s not a good backdrop for fixed 
income, even before considering fiscal profiles.
 
Equally, if monetary transmission is compromised, as households are 
largely fixed and corporates insensitive, delivering tighter financial 
conditions is harder.  It would require durably lower equity prices.  That 
would stomp on the 
(concentrated) 
wealth effects 
that have been 
supporting demand (Figure 11
, 12).  If the dot.com bust is anything to go 
by, that would pass most of Main Street by.  
The Fed funds rate is not 
the tool to deliver that
 though.  We remain concerned second round 
risks are underestimated, 
by th e Fed and the market.  Both seem 
more 
focused on expected favourable energy base effects.
 
This week’s equity wobble initially took a bite at the dollar, which 
probably reflected an unwind of the faster money flows into yield and 
the AI trade.  But with the lure of AI investment spending, asset price 
returns and higher 
bond yields (real and nominal)
, it’s hard to take a 
negative view at this point.  Doing so is really picking a turn in the US
