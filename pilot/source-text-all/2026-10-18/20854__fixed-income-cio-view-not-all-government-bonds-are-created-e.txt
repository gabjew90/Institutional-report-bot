Rising yields in developed market government bonds may offer 
buying opportunities, but take care with French debt. 
While it is premature to adopt an outright bullish stance, it’s clear that 
developed market government bonds are no longer as overvalued as they once 
were. But it pays to be discriminating. Had George Orwell written Bond Farm 
instead of Animal Farm, he might have said, “all OECD bonds are equal, but 
some are more equal than others”.
Treasuries are no longer an obvious underweight
Buying opportunities for US Treasuries are emerging. The reason is simple. They 
finally offer some value again. Relative to their own recent history and relative 
to equities, much of the extreme overvaluation has been worked off.
Admittedly, we are still in an inflationary boom period, which continues to be 
a headwind for government bonds. But real yields are approaching levels 
that have historically put stress on parts of the economy. The asymmetry has 
improved enough to justify a change in positioning. In addition, the potential 
for more aggressive Treasury buybacks under Secretary Scott Bessent could 
provide some technical support to the market.

That does not mean we should expect a sustained Treasury rally. Growth 
remains too strong, while supply from both the Treasury and corporate issuers 
remains too abundant. But yields are now high enough for government bonds 
to earn their place in portfolios again, offering meaningful real income with 
valuable diversification benefit.  
Put simply, holding US Treasuries, even if primarily as a hedge against a 
potential growth disruption, is considerably more attractive than it was a 
few years ago.
With the Federal Reserve's next few moves largely priced, the more important 
question is where higher real rates might begin to break things. Corporate 
earnings and defaults are key indicators. So far, aggregate corporate 
earnings remain resilient. Stress has been concentrated in CCC credit, where 
spreads have widened as default risk has risen. That is unsurprising given the 
vulnerability of these borrowers, and the segment remains too small to pose a 
broader systemic concern.
What would concern me more is weakness spreading into single-B credit, 
particularly among highly leveraged cyclical sectors such as consumer 
cyclicals, software and communications. That would be a clearer signal that 
higher rates and tighter financial conditions are not just an equity valuation 
challenge, but beginning to damage the broader economy. 
French bonds – don’t try to catch a falling knife
Numbers matter. France is running a debt-to-GDP ratio of around 120%.1 
Long-term interest rates are now more than 100 basis points above nominal 
GDP growth. Fiscal deficits remain in the mid-5% range. More than 60% of 
government debt is held by foreign investors.2 France cannot devalue its 
currency because of the euro. And a major election is approaching, making 
meaningful policy decisions extremely difficult. 
These are not comforting dynamics. 
French sovereign spreads have widened significantly, to levels last seen 
around the euro-area crisis. It is important, however, to distinguish between 
the global bond-market beta and France-specific risk. Part of the move reflects 
the broader sell-off in global government bonds; stripping out that beta, the 
idiosyncratic widening in France is less dramatic.
A full-blown sovereign crisis still feels remote. Since 2012, the euro area has 
built meaningful financial backstops that substantially reduce default risk and 
the probability that stress in one sovereign develops into a systemic crisis.
Italy in 2018 may therefore offer a more relevant comparison than the euro 
crisis. If France were to follow a similar path, spreads could widen towards 300 
basis points and remain elevated for a prolonged period.
Simply put, French 
spreads may already 
have widened 
considerably, but the 
risk-reward still looks 
asymmetric. They 
remain a falling knife I 
would be reluctant to 
catch.

At the same time, it is difficult to see the catalyst for a meaningful and 
sustained tightening in French spreads. That would likely require a material 
improvement in the country's fiscal trajectory, which I do not expect anytime 
soon.
Simply put, French spreads may already have widened considerably, but 
the risk-reward still looks asymmetric. They remain a falling knife I would be 
reluctant to catch.
