MACRO STRATEGY 
Consumers Feel One Way, but Spend Another 
Kirsten Cabacungan, Vice President and Senior Investment Strategist 
Americans have spent much of the past several years telling surveys they are cautious 
about the economy. Yet they continue to spend as though they are not. The University 
of Michigan’s Consumer Sentiment Index remains near multidecade lows, while the 
Conference Board’s Index of Consumer Confidence has also been in a yearslong downtrend. 
These surveys reflect a list of persistent concerns from elevated food and gas prices to 
geopolitical uncertainty and labor market anxiety. 
Even so, consumer spending has remained remarkably resilient. The final estimate of Q2 
real consumer spending, as measured by PCE, was revised up to an annualized rate of 
3.8%, well above the 2.3% 20-year average. More recent data suggest that momentum 
has continued. According to Bank of America Institute, the latest monthly total credit and 
debit card spending data per household rose 6.2% year-over-year (YoY) in September, and 
spending excluding gasoline was up 5.1% YoY, both the third strongest growth rates in the 
past four years.1 
The persistence of consumer spending is particularly notable given that temporary cyclical 
boosts like larger tax refunds are starting to fade. That raises an important question: What is 
actually funding strong consumption today? Recent data suggest the drivers are becoming 
increasingly more diversified, with broadening labor market trends, healthy consumer 
finances and elevated wealth effects all reinforcing one another and underpinning consumers. 
First, the labor market continues to provide an important foundation for consumer spending, 
and recent trends suggest wage growth is supporting a broader set of households than in 
recent years. Jobs growth has slowed from the exceptionally strong pace seen following the 
pandemic, but broader indicators suggest labor market conditions remain relatively stable. 
Although the September jobs report came in softer than expected, the three- and six-month 
rolling averages indicate that the trend in jobs growth likely bottomed out last year and has 
gradually improved since (Exhibit 1A). Meanwhile, the unemployment rate remains low by 
historical standards and layoffs remain contained. And while wage growth has moderated 
and recently trailed headline inflation (Exhibit 1B), cumulative income gains over the past 
several years have likely helped many households absorb higher prices better.
An important development though has been improving wage growth trends among lower-
income households, where spending tends to be most sensitive to changes in wages. Bank 
of America Institute consumer deposit account data show after-tax wage growth for lower-
income households outpaced higher-income households for a second consecutive month 
in August, rising 4.7% YoY compared with 3.5% for higher-income households. Importantly, 
this improvement appears to be supporting discretionary spending, not just spending 
on necessities. Lower-income discretionary spending rose 5.7% YoY in August, nearly 
matching the 5.9% pace for higher-income households. In other words, the narrowing gap 
in wage and spending growth across income cohorts suggests consumer resilience looks 
to be becoming less dependent on affluent households alone, creating a broader and more 
durable foundation for consumption. 
Second, household finances remain in relatively good shape, providing consumers with the 
flexibility to continue spending. The ratio of household debt to disposable personal income 
remains near its lowest level since the late 1990s, excluding the temporary distortions 
caused by pandemic-related income support in 2020 and 2021 (Exhibit 1C). Recent 
revisions to the Bureau of Economic Analysis national income accounts also suggest 
consumers have a larger financial buffer than previous data suggested. Personal saving 
estimates over the past year have been revised higher by $320 billion, or 39%, and the 
revised savings rate remains above 4% after previous estimates showed it fell to 3% during 
the summer.2 Concerns that consumers may be rapidly exhausting their savings buffers 
may therefore be overstated. Supporting this view, Bank of America Institute consumer

Third, elevated household wealth continues to provide a powerful tailwind for consumer 
spending, particularly among households that account for an outsized share of overall 
consumption. U.S. household net worth rose $12.8 trillion (T) to a record $195.9T in Q2, 
driven largely by gains in Equities, which contributed $10.7T of the increase (Exhibit 1D). 
As a result, household net worth relative to disposable income climbed to a record 8.3, 
meaning households now hold more than eight dollars of wealth for every dollar of annual 
disposable income. Households are also sitting on $35.8T in home equity, an all-time high 
level that provides a significant balance sheet cushion. Rising asset values have helped to 
support spending through what economists refer to as wealth effects. In other words, when 
households feel wealthier, they tend to be more willing to spend.
For investors, the key takeaway is that consumer spending is being supported by multiple 
engines despite the weaker sentiment data. Labor income trends are supporting wider 
participation, healthy balance sheets are helping households absorb economic pressures, 
and elevated household wealth continues to sustain higher-income spending. Together, 
these forces are supportive of economic growth and corporate profits, which is particularly 
important since the consumer accounts for roughly 70% of U.S. gross domestic product. 
That said, it does not eliminate risk. A meaningful risk to the consumer would be a scenario 
in which both the labor and wealth engines weaken simultaneously. For now, labor market 
conditions remain stable, and wealth effects remain supportive. Equity market pullbacks in 
recent years have done little to derail consumer spending, suggesting it would likely take a 
deeper and more prolonged drawdown to alter consumer behavior.

MARKET VIEW
The Bull Market Turns Four: Older, Unloved—But Still Kicking
Joseph Quinlan, Managing Director and Head of Market Strategy
Ariana Chiu, Assistant Vice President and Investment Strategist 
Four years ago this month, virtually no one was in the mood to buy U.S. Equities, and for 
good reasons.
Rewind to October 2022: Inflation the month before clocked in at an annual rate of 8.2%. 
The yield curve was inverted, a signal that has preceded most past recessions. Consumer 
sentiment was abysmal, having dropped to a record low in June 2022. Recession 
concerns were mounting, with an October Wall Street Journal poll survey putting the 
odds of a recession within 12 months at about 63%. And realizing (belatedly) that 
inflation was not “transitory,” the Fed was in the midst of administering one of the most 
aggressive monetary tightening cycles in decades, raising the fed funds rate five times 
between March and September 2022 by a stunning 300 basis points. 
Reflecting the mood at the moment, the S&P 500 closed at 3,577 on October 12, 2022, 
down 25% below its January peak, leaving investors battered and bruised but blind to the 
fact that the market had bottomed. Four years on, the bull market that emerged from 
the gloom of 2022 is still standing and kicking, with the S&P 500 up roughly 130% since 
the October 2022 lows.  
The more than doubling of the S&P over the past four years has hardly been linear—and 
has been accompanied with plenty of pain and doubt. Exhibit 2 maps the rocky path 
trodden by the bull over the past four years. Less than six months into the rally, the 
markets were rocked by the failure of Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) on March 10, 2023. The 
S&P Regional Banks Select Industry Index lost 18% that week and stoked concerns of 
massive deposit runs and credit stress across the capital markets. However, owing to 
emergency intervention by the government, lower yields and strength elsewhere in the 
markets, the crisis passed. Some banks failed that month, but the bull market didn’t.

But recessionary concerns hung like threatening clouds over the markets for most of 
2023. Aggressive Fed tightening, an inverted yield curve, and soft economic data were 
all taken as harbingers of an economic slowdown and profits recession. Yet the S&P 500 
continued to climb in 2023, up 26.3% for the year, driven in large part by the outsized 
price performance of the Magnificent Seven. 
Investors spent much of 2024 fretting about a market too dependent on technology, 
and therefore a market too narrow and too expensive. The unwinding of the Japan carry 
trade in August 2024 engendered more angst, as did uncertainties around the 2024 
U.S. presidential election. But despite a host of misgivings, the S&P 500 delivered total 
returns of 25% in 2024. The unloved bull marched on.
Total returns in 2025 were robust as well—roughly 18%—notwithstanding—yet 
again—a number of headwinds. In late January, DeepSeek, a cheaper China AI model, 
raised questions about America’s technology leadership and the need to spend enormous 
sums on chips and data centers. The Nasdaq promptly dropped by 3.1% thereafter.

January’s jolt was followed by “Liberation Day,” or the warning of punishing U.S. tariffs 
on America’s major trading partners. The trade-policy shock of April 2025 caused the 
S&P 500 to drop nearly 19% from its February high to its April 8 low on uncertainty 
that higher import costs would decrease corporate margins and consumer spending and 
upend global supply chains. Amid the trade tumult, Treasury prices fell and the U.S. dollar 
weakened on concerns that foreigners were abandoning U.S. assets. Remember all the 
angst over the dollar debasement trade? If that wasn’t enough, expensive valuations and 
enormous capital commitments raised doubts about the AI trade and triggered talk of an 
AI bubble. Yet despite this wall of worry, the S&P 500, for the third year running, posted 
double-digit gains in 2025.  
And double-digit gains could be in store this year, with the S&P 500 up 14% through 
October 9. The bull has rumbled on, taking in stride the energy shock from the war in 
the Middle East, the shift in interest rate expectations from the Fed (from rate cuts 
to rate hikes), and the rising uncertainty associated with the midterm elections. These 
headwinds have been countered by tailwinds such as better-than-expected corporate 
earnings and better-than-anticipated real growth.
Finally, as Exhibit 3 makes clear, what has given this bull so much muscle has been 
the U.S. Technology sector, specifically the Magnificent 7. Powering the bull has been 
the Magnificent 7, with a staggering 344% return since the 2022 lows, while U.S. 
technology has delivered a 277% return versus the S&P 500’s return of 130%. Note the 
outperformance of the S&P 500 relative to the main international indices. 
Looking forward: Is the bull’s greatest strength also its Achilles’ heel? Bull 
markets don’t die of natural causes. And they typically don’t expire because of one 
particular signal or indicator. Bull markets turn to bear markets when a cluster of signals/
metrics turn south. Think deteriorating and downward earnings revisions, higher interest 
rates, widening credit spreads and tighter lending standards, growth disappointments, 
stretched valuations and sentiment, deteriorating market breadth, and an external 
shock. Any one of these signals could flash red and not trouble the bull.  But when they 
converge or feed off each other, that’s trouble.
In addition, since a great deal of the earnings growth, market leadership and capital 
expenditures (capex) spending of this bull market have come from the technology sector, 
the bull’s greatest strength could become its greatest vulnerability. AI has become an 
investment ecosystem unto itself, and hence the fifth year of the bull market will pivot 
in part around AI revenue, frontier model adoption rates, infrastructure spending, free 
cash flows, depreciation costs, debt issuance, data center utilization, cloud growth, and 
competition via cheaper models and computing alternatives. To put it mildly, there is a 
great deal at stake when it comes to the bull and AI.
However, this is where we stand today: We believe the bull will continue to rumble 
ahead next year, with AI revenues and productivity gains justifying the costs of the AI 
infrastructure. Critically, AI is moving from the buildout phase, dominated by chipmakers 
and hyperscaler capex, into the adoption phase, where software firms, healthcare, 
finance, energy and a myriad of other firms start to see productivity gains and margin 
expansion from rising AI adoption rates. We believe this will give better breadth to the 
S&P 500; so too will continued profit growth across various U.S. sectors, AI investment 
that starts to pay off, and an economy capable of absorbing setbacks or periodic bouts 
of uncertainty. 
The path forward won’t be linear—and there will be fits and starts, and market 
drawdowns. That’s normal for any market. But that aside, the bull will keep rumbling, 
roaming and kicking in 2027.

What is our view of the macro and market landscape heading 
into 2027 and beyond?
Christopher Hyzy, Chief Investment Office
A supply side-led economic expansion continues, driven by capital investment in the AI 
buildout, solid consumer spending and fiscal tailwinds still blowing, albeit with less force. 
This creates a backdrop that keeps nominal growth well-above trend and, importantly, 
above the interest cost on national debt. 
The interest rate backdrop largely remains the same as of today, but, instead of the 
sharp increases that we experienced in 2026, the curve should settle down, and yields 
should remain relatively range bound. The Fed is likely to tap on the breaks a couple of 
more times before reassessing new inflation, growth and employment trends later in 
2027. 
Concerns surrounding credit risk and "circular" financing are likely to rise the further we 
move into the AI buildout, but we do not expect a default cycle to develop. Rather, we 
expect the sharp backup in yields to place pressure namely on lower-quality credits in 
areas having a difficult time building the cash flow necessary to pay the higher interest 
costs. 
We expect a broadening out within the market but technology to still maintain its 
heavyweight positioning. As we move from training and evolving the large language 
models to agentic, and physical AI new leaders are likely to develop. In addition, we 
anticipate AI adopters such as biotechnology, transport, logistics, advance manufacturing 
and financial companies to enter new growth and productivity phases. 
Lastly, overall earnings growth in the U.S. should be low to mid double-digit percent 
stages in 2027, which is about half of the growth from 2026 thus far but still around 
double from normal levels. 
Credit risks developing and higher energy prices for longer are the main risks to our bull 
case for Equities extending further into 2027 and beyond.

Index Definitions 
Securities indexes assume reinvestment of all distributions and interest payments. Indexes are unmanaged and do not take into account fees or expenses. It is not possible to 
invest directly in an index. Indexes are all based in U.S. dollars.
S&P 500 Index is a stock market index tracking the stock performance of 500 leading companies listed on stock exchanges in the United States.
University of Michigan’s Consumer Sentiment Index is a monthly survey of consumer confidence levels in the United States conducted by the University of Michigan.
Conference Board’s Index of Consumer Confidence measures what consumers are feeling about their expected financial situation, whether that's optimistic or pessimistic.
Consumer Price Index is a statistical measure that tracks the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a representative "market basket" of everyday goods and 
services.
Nasdaq Composite Index is a stock market measure that tracks the performance of more than 3,000 stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange.
MSCI Emerging Markets c is a stock market index that tracks the performance of large-cap and mid-cap companies across 24 developing and fast-growing nations.
MSCI All-Country World Index ex USA is a market-capitalization-weighted stock market index that tracks large- and mid-cap equity performance across developed and emerging markets 
worldwide, excluding the United States.
MSCI Europe, Australasia, and the Far East (EAFE) Index is a stock market index that measures the performance of large- and mid-cap companies across 21 developed.
Dow Jones Industrial Average Index is a stock market index that tracks 30 major public companies listed on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and Nasdaq.
S&P 500 Equal-Weighted Index is an alternative version of the standard S&P 500 that gives every constituent company an identical weight of 0.2% during each quarterly rebalance, rather 
than weighting companies by their market capitalization.
Russell 2000 Index is a stock market index that tracks the performance of approximately 2,000 small-cap (small-capitalization) public companies in the United States.

Investments have varying degrees of risk. Some of the risks involved with equity securities include the possibility that the value of the stocks may fluctuate in response to events specific to 
the companies or markets, as well as economic, political or social events in the U.S. or abroad. Small cap and mid cap companies pose special risks, including possible illiquidity and greater 
price volatility than funds consisting of larger, more established companies. Investing in fixed-income securities may involve certain risks, including the credit quality of individual issuers, 
possible prepayments, market or economic developments and yields and share price fluctuations due to changes in interest rates. When interest rates go up, bond prices typically drop, and 
vice versa. Investments in high-yield bonds (sometimes referred to as “junk bonds”) offer the potential for high current income and attractive total return, but involves certain risks. Changes in 
economic conditions or other circumstances may adversely affect a junk bond issuer’s ability to make principal and interest payments. Income from investing in municipal bonds is generally 
exempt from Federal and state taxes for residents of the issuing state. While the interest income is tax-exempt, any capital gains distributed are taxable to the investor. Income for some 
investors may be subject to the Federal Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT). Treasury bills are less volatile than longer-term fixed income securities and are guaranteed as to timely payment of 
principal and interest by the U.S. government. Bonds are subject to interest rate, inflation and credit risks. Investments in foreign securities (including ADRs) involve special risks, including 
foreign currency risk and the possibility of substantial volatility due to adverse political, economic or other developments. These risks are magnified for investments made in emerging 
markets. Investments in a certain industry or sector may pose additional risk due to lack of diversification and sector concentration. There are special risks associated with an investment in 
commodities, such as gold, including market price fluctuations, regulatory changes, interest rate changes, credit risk, economic changes and the impact of adverse political or financial factors.
