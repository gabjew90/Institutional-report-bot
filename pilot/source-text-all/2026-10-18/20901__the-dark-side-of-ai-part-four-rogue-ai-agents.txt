Artificial intelligence (AI) has evolved far beyond simple chatbots 
that answer questions. Today's AI agents can browse the web, 
write and execute code, manage files, send emails and complete 
complex, multi-step tasks with minimal human intervention. Their 
rapid adoption is driven by the promise of greater productivity, 
efficiency and cost savings. 
Despite their potential, AI agents present important 
limitations and risks. A study by Carnegie Mellon University 
found that AI agents can become disoriented, choose 
incorrect shortcuts or struggle to complete even simple 
tasks.1  They can cause unintended record updates, break 
workflows and push flawed logic into production systems. 
Those incidents often go unnoticed until something breaks 
or sensitive data are affected.2  According to The 
Economist, “AI agents, which are supposed to work on 
people’s behalf in “alignment” with their values, lie, cheat 
and steal if necessary. They break free from captivity and 
form harmful posses to do harm to people.”3  Taken 
together, these developments point to the emergence of a 
new risk category: the “rogue AI agent”.
The rise of AI agents
The global market for AI agents is expected to experience 
substantial growth over the coming decade (Figure 1). With 
a projected compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of over 
43%, the market is forecast to expand from USD 3.66 
billion in 2023 to approximately USD 139.12 billion by 2033. 
Within this market, ready-to-deploy AI agents are expected 
to maintain their leading position. Their continued 
dominance is primarily driven by ease of implementation 
and seamless integration into existing business 
environments, making them an attractive option for 
organizations seeking to leverage AI capabilities without 
the time, complexity and resource commitments associated 
with developing custom-built ("build-your-own") agent 
solutions.4 
In our view, the rise of AI agents is fundamentally reshaping 
the cybersecurity landscape. As AI agents become 
increasingly accessible to develop and deploy and as agents 
are able to create and orchestrate additional agents, 
organizations are facing a rapid proliferation of non-human 
identities in the form of AI agents. These agents interact 
across applications, application programming interfaces 
(APIs), software-as-a-service (SaaS) platforms and data 
environments, often operating with privileged access and at 
machine speed.5  Consequently, the attack surface has 
expanded considerably. IT security teams are no longer 
tasked solely with securing software and infrastructure; 
they must also govern and protect autonomous systems 
whose decision-making processes can be difficult to 
predict, monitor and control, particularly in the case of a 
rogue AI agent.

What is a “rogue AI agent”?
A rogue AI agent is an autonomous system that acts in 
ways its creators did not intend, whether by pursuing 
objectives too literally, exploiting loopholes in its 
instructions, operating beyond its authorized scope or 
being manipulated by a malicious actor.6,7  
Importantly, these systems do not become rogue because 
AI is inherently malicious or a “robot uprising” is underway. 
Rather, such outcomes typically emerge when highly 
autonomous agents are deployed without adequate 
governance, oversight and security guardrails. AI agents act 
on signals and lack the “cognitive filter” that prevents 
humans from following bad ideas. They are designed to 
optimize for a given objective and may pursue unintended 
ways of achieving it, which can make their behavior 
difficult to predict. In reinforcement learning, successful 
outcomes are rewarded during training.8  In our opinion, 
this makes them both powerful and potentially dangerous.

Notable incidents of rogue 
AI agents
Recent evaluations by major AI labs revealed that AI agents 
went rogue during cybersecurity tests. Below are several 
notable incidents involving rogue AI agents:
	– An Alibaba-affiliated AI agent unexpectedly escaped its 
sandbox by establishing a reverse SSH tunnel9  to an 
external IP address. Without human instruction or 
authorization, the AI agent autonomously diverted 
computing power to mine cryptocurrency in order to 
maximize its computational rewards.10 
	– An autonomous AI agent driven by OpenAI models 
escaped its evaluation sandbox, penetrated the Hugging 
Face infrastructure, moved laterally over several days, 
stole tokens and utilized stolen credentials before being 
contained.11  
	– Anthropic’s Claude models gained unauthorized access to 
the real systems of three different organizations during a 
test after mistaking the targets for a fictional challenge.12
	– The UK's AI Security Institute reported that AI models 
from Anthropic and OpenAI took unsanctioned actions 
on the live internet. In one case, an agent created 
multiple fake online identities to trick a human open-
source project maintainer into approving malicious code.13 
How can organizations 
mitigate rogue AI agent risk?
When an AI agent goes rogue, it is rarely the result of a 
single dramatic failure. Typically, the problem develops 
through a gradual accumulation of small missteps, such as 
unreviewed access, missing audit trails, outdated credentials 
or unmonitored actions.14  
The following measures can help reduce the risk of 
rogue behavior:15 
Maintain a complete inventory of AI 
agents: Establish and maintain an 
inventory of all AI agents, documenting 
each agent’s purpose, owner, 
permissions and access to data, 
applications and systems.
Enforce least privilege principles and 
continuous governance: Grant each AI 
agent only the minimum permissions 
required to perform its tasks. Permissions 
must be tested, reviewed and logged 
regularly. Introduce a staging 
environment for AI agent actions before 
they reach production. Every AI agent 
needs a full audit trail.
Use continuous monitoring to observe 
agent behavior in real time: Centralize 
oversight through a unified checkpoint 
that inspects all agent traffic and blocks 
prompt-injection attempts, data 
exfiltration and suspicious tool calls 
before they cause harm.

Implications and outlook
AI agents are no longer a science-fiction abstraction; 
they are powerful optimization engines operating in 
complex, imperfectly controlled environments. The real 
risk lies in misaligned objectives, insecure integrations, 
opaque behavior and the speed and scale at which AI 
agents can act. 
As AI agents move deeper into our daily lives, treating 
rogue behavior as an operational risk becomes essential. 
Addressing this challenge requires a combination of 
technical safeguards, thoughtful system design and 
organizational discipline about how much autonomy to 
grant and when. 
For long-term investors, this structural shift is creating 
compelling opportunities. The UBS security equity strategy 
seeks to capture this trend by investing in companies at the 
forefront of AI security. By allocating capital to innovators 
in areas such as anomaly detection, continuous 
observability, dynamic agent governance or agent recovery, 
rewind and remediation, the strategy aims to benefit from 
the growing demand for solutions that enhance the 
reliability and trustworthiness of AI systems.
