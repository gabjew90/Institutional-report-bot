Euro Area Sovereign Risk: Now vs Then 
 
 
With European fiscal issues back in focus, investor questions about the possibility of 
renewed Euro area debt tensions have returned. So far, the bond market pressures have 
focused on France, with the OAT-Bund spread recently widening to decade highs (Exhibit 
1). But we have also seen tentative signs of spillovers into other markets, including Italy. 
 
We thus explore the similarities and differences between the current situation and the 
2011-15 European debt crisis. 
Similarities 
We see several similarities to the 2011-15 episode. 
First, fiscal imbalances are elevated in several Euro area economies (Exhibit 2). While 
public deficits are now notably narrower than in 2011-15 in some countries (Greece, 
Spain, Portugal), deficits are similar (France, Italy) or significantly higher in others 
(Belgium, Austria, Finland). Moreover, public spending needs have intensified further 
given pressures in health, pensions, climate and defence. The IMF, for example, 
estimates that these pressures will increase expenditures above current levels by about 
4½pp of GDP by 2040 in Europe.

Second, interest rates are high and rising (Exhibit 3). The ECB is hiking policy rates to lean

yields has not coincided with higher growth expectations in Europe, implying a more 
challenging backdrop for fiscal sustainability. 
 
Third, public debt dynamics look concerning in several countries (Exhibit 4). Given 
borrowing costs are now likely to surpass nominal growth over the medium term (with 
“r-g” turning positive in several countries), governments will likely need to run primary 
surpluses to stabilise their debt ratios. Our estimates suggest that France, Belgium, 
Austria and Finland would need to deliver very large fiscal adjustments to prevent 
further increases in their debt-to-GDP ratios. Debt ratios are therefore on course to rise

Conclusions 
Taken together, the fundamental fiscal challenges look harder now than in 2011-15. In 
most countries, the starting level of debt is higher, structural spending pressures are 
more pronounced, bond yields are rising quickly and political fragmentation complicates 
the required fiscal adjustment. These challenges are particularly pronounced in France. 
That said, the threat of disorderly market escalation and real economy contagion looks 
lower. Nominal growth is notably stronger, external imbalances are lower, banks are in 
much better shape and the EMU institutional framework has been clarified. 
The risk of a disorderly bond market crisis (with multiple market equilibria) has therefore 
been notably reduced. But the issue of how to deal with fundamental sovereign risk in 
the monetary union remains unresolved without a fiscal union and ultimately requires 
credible fiscal consolidation.
