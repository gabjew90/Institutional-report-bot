Equity perspectives
Opportunity demands selectivity
Introduction
Across sectors, the fourth quarter equity outlook is shaped by resilient 
structural investment and a more demanding macro backdrop. AI 
infrastructure, scarce power capacity, defense spending, metals demand, 
and healthcare innovation continue to create long-term opportunities. 
But higher rates, sticky inflation, and elevated capital needs are raising the 
bar for companies seeking to turn those themes into durable earnings.
At the same time, conditions vary widely across sectors and companies. 
Banks are benefiting from a favorable earnings cycle, parts of the 
healthcare sector are recovering, and select industrial and energy 
businesses are seeing visible demand. Consumer-facing sectors face 
greater pressure from financing costs, inflation, and weaker spending. In 
this environment, strong balance sheets, clear pricing power, disciplined 
capital allocation, and reliable cash flow are likely to be most important.
Structural investment is broadening, but returns must follow
The AI-led capital cycle now reaches well beyond technology. It supports 
semiconductor equipment, data centers, utilities, natural-gas 
infrastructure, industrial machinery, and materials used in electrification 
and construction. Healthcare research spending and defense investment 
add further long-term demand. Yet the investment case, especially for AI,
increasingly depends on monetization, contracted revenue, recurring service income, and disciplined financing. 
Structural growth remains compelling, but companies must show that their spending can produce sustainable cash flow.
Sector and company dispersion is widening
Broad sector labels conceal meaningful differences. Large banks have stronger earnings momentum than many other 
financial firms. In consumer discretionary, branded lodging, and value-oriented retailers look more resilient than categories 
tied to a broad spending rebound. In staples, emerging market exposure and brand strength matter as pricing power fades. 
Healthcare opportunities depend on research recovery, cost trends, and company-specific catalysts. Even among 
industrials, energy, and materials, asset quality and capital discipline separate outperformers from weaker companies.
Selectivity is essential in a concentrated market
Higher short-term rates, narrow market leadership, and crowded thematic trades can amplify risk even where long-term 
fundamentals remain sound. The answer is not to dismiss AI, power, infrastructure, or other durable themes, but to 
distinguish transformation from investment merit. Active research can identify companies with visible demand, defensible 
margins, conservative balance sheets, and management teams capable of allocating capital through volatility. In this 
environment, security selection is central to converting structural opportunity into lasting value.

Perspectives from the CIO
Markets are adjusting to recurring volatility rather than a single turning point. 
Higher yields may partly reflect resilient growth, but the sharper rise in short-
term rates also reflects concern about persistent inflation, the Federal 
Reserve’s September policy response,  and the possibility of further tightening.  
Tighter financial conditions raise the bar for equity valuations and could 
eventually pressure growth and earnings.
Inflation risks extend beyond the latest energy or commodity shock. Labor 
constraints, deglobalization, and supply-chain friction can reinforce wage and 
price pressures. The greater risk is that temporary shocks become embedded in 
expectations, forcing policymakers to cool demand more aggressively.
For investors, this environment favors durable cash flows, pricing power, 
strong balance sheets, and management teams able to adapt. Headlines may 
drive short-term swings, but earnings quality and valuation remain the anchors 
for long-term capital allocation.
Separating transformation from investment merit
AI illustrates the distinction between a powerful technology and an attractive 
investment. AI may eventually improve productivity and reduce costs, but the 
buildout is already consuming enormous amounts of capital, chips, raw 
materials, power, and data center capacity. Those demands can add to near-
term inflation even if the technology proves disinflationary over time.
Spending on AI remains strong, but monetization is now the test. Not every 
participant will convert AI demand into durable revenue and free cash flow. 
The economics remain uncertain. If competing AI tools prove difficult to 
differentiate, pricing may move closer to delivery costs, limiting returns on 
today’s enormous infrastructure investment. Meanwhile, market-cap-weighted 
flows can reinforce concentration by directing additional capital toward 
companies as their valuations rise, amplifying momentum on the way up and 
potentially magnifying reversals.
Investment discipline and selectivity apply across the market. Energy and 
materials may benefit from constrained supply. Consumer weakness can 
create opportunities where employment, spending, and fundamentals remain 
resilient. Small-cap industrial and power-related companies may gain from 
capital investment, although higher financing costs create a clear divide 
between stronger and weaker businesses. In other words, opportunity extends 
past the largest tech companies to power infrastructure, utilities in supportive 
jurisdictions, semiconductor equipment, and select software and cybersecurity 
businesses with essential capabilities and defensible pricing.
Looking beyond the apparent safety of the index
Index concentration deserves particular scrutiny. Market-cap-weighted 
benchmarks offer efficient exposure, but passive should not be confused with 
diversified. A narrow group of companies can leave portfolios more exposed 
than headline index labels suggest. What appears safe can become risky when 
positioning is crowded, and expectations are elevated. Active research across 
sectors can distinguish durable growth from momentum, recalibrate thematic 
exposure, and uncover opportunities beyond the benchmark’s largest weights.
The perspectives that follow examine where fundamentals are strengthening, 
where expectations have moved too far, and where disciplined research can 
identify durable value. In a concentrated and uncertain market, selectivity 
across and within sectors is essential.

Summary of investment implications
TECHNOLOGY AND COMMUNICATIONS
AI adoption is broadening across cloud, software, and infrastructure, supporting sustained investment into the next 
decade. Yet higher financing costs, complex funding structures, and uncertain monetization raise the threshold for 
investors. Companies with visible demand, strong balance sheets, and the ability to convert usage into durable cash 
flow appear best positioned. Selectivity is key in communications amid intense competition and advertising disruption.
ENERGY AND UTILITIES
Supply constraints support energy cash flows and select utilities. Disciplined oil producers, advantaged natural-gas 
infrastructure, and power providers with long-term hyperscaler contracts offer opportunities. Risks include weaker 
demand if oil prices remain elevated and lower power expectations if AI investment slows or regulation and 
technology shift. Strong balance sheets, disciplined capital allocation, scarce infrastructure, and predictable 
contracted revenue are especially attractive.
FINANCIALS
Financials remain in a favorable earnings cycle, supported by moderately higher rates, steady lending, resilient credit, 
and healthy capital markets activity. Large banks appear best positioned to expand margins, corporate lending, 
technology-related financing, and capacity for dividends and buybacks. Key risks include an aggressive rise in short-
term rates, a flatter yield curve, credit deterioration, and digital disruption across the market structure.
CONSUMER DISCRETIONARY
Higher financing costs, sticky inflation, and weaker demand are constraining discretionary spending and pressuring 
revenue expectations. Companies offering clear value, structural share gains, and growth independent of a broad 
consumer rebound stand out. Branded lodging and select value-oriented retailers fit that profile. Depressed areas 
may offer opportunity, but improving demand and earnings revisions are needed before taking broader exposure.
CONSUMER STAPLES
Consumer staples retain defensive characteristics, but weak demand in developed markets, private-label competition, 
rising input costs, and higher refinancing expenses have reduced the sector’s dependability. Pricing power is reaching 
its limit as consumers trade down. Differentiated brands with resilient emerging-market demand, flexible cost 
structures, credible productivity programs, and evidence that volumes can hold up through another difficult cost-and-
pricing cycle are better positioned.
INDUSTRIALS
Long-duration investment in data centers, power, defense, mining, and agriculture supports industrial demand despite 
cautious sentiment. Companies with visible backlogs, integrated offerings, and recurring service revenue look 
compelling, alongside select commercial services and rate-sensitive businesses where perceptions lag fundamentals. 
Risks include paying for peak expectations, treating one-time equipment sales as permanent growth, and pursuing 
expensive acquisitions late in the cycle.
HEALTHCARE
Healthcare fundamentals are improving as research spending recovers and managed-care pricing catches up with 
medical costs. The strongest opportunities include life science tools, managed-care companies with improving cost 
ratios, and pharma or biotech businesses backed by differentiated innovation and multiple catalysts. Medtech 
valuations are compelling, but visibility into procedure volume remains limited, and higher rates and clinical setbacks 
reinforce the need for careful company selection.
MATERIALS
Durable metals demand and constrained supply support copper and aluminum, while gold offers diversification amid 
fiscal and monetary uncertainty. AI-related construction adds incremental demand, though broader economic activity 
remains the primary driver. Construction materials face elevated energy costs and slower infrastructure spending. 
Companies with scarce assets, resilient margins, advantaged cost positions, manageable supply chain exposure, and 
discipline amid policy uncertainty appear well positioned

Technology and Communications
AI demand broadens as financial risk rises
Technology and communications enter the fourth quarter with growing  evidence that AI adoption is broadening. 
Hyperscalers are reporting more diverse customer demand, growing contracted workloads, and greater use of both 
proprietary tools and third-party models. Software companies are also expanding usage-based pricing, suggesting AI is 
moving from promise toward monetization.
Still, the investment cycle is becoming more sensitive to capital costs. Rates have moved higher, cheap funding is less 
assured, and more AI infrastructure spending is being financed externally. That raises concerns about risk tolerance, 
especially where leverage is elevated. 
Spending remains strong; monetization is the test
AI capital spending still appears likely to remain higher for longer. Expectations for 2027 and 2028 investments have 
continued to rise as semiconductor manufacturers, cloud platforms, and infrastructure providers expand capacity. However, 
infrastructure names may see multiple compression well before demand slows if investors view the cycle as near peak.
The more encouraging development is diffusion. Lower model costs are driving sharp increases in usage, and broader 
enterprise adoption is creating demand beyond frontier model developers. Yet the key question is whether rising volumes 
can generate sufficient revenue and cash flow to justify the scale of the investment underway. Price declines may create a 
mismatch between lower monetization and the sheer size of capital intensity. 
Selectivity rises across the AI value chain
This backdrop creates a more selective set of opportunities. Some AI-linked software names have already rallied, leaving less 
valuation support. By contrast, portions of semiconductor capital equipment and hardware have sold off despite secular 
drivers such as more complex chips, rising content, and expanding production needs. Vertical software also remains 
attractive where AI can deepen customer value.
Geopolitics and regulation add uncertainty. Export restrictions, the U.S.-China AI competition, and supply chain bottlenecks 
could affect customers and critical components. Although supply chains have been resilient, specialized wafers, memory, 
substrates, and materials remain vulnerable. Sentiment around AI is also shifting quickly, reinforcing the need to own 
companies with durable demand, strong balance sheets, and less exposure to technological displacement and financing risk.
Communications offers fewer clear winners
Within communications, opportunities remain more limited. Telecom and cable face intense competition and fading 
differentiation, while traditional media remains crowded. Live events offer a more constructive outlook as vertically 
integrated operators benefit from durable demand for experiences. Digital advertising is undergoing a shift as AI agents 
generate an increasing share of internet traffic, potentially changing how engagement is measured and monetized.
Our perspective: Tech and Communications
Technology fundamentals remain constructive, with AI infrastructure spending extending further into the decade 
and adoption broadening. However, higher rates, complex financing structures, and unresolved monetization 
questions have raised the investment threshold.
We favor companies with visible demand, strong balance sheets, and business models that can convert higher 
usage into durable cash flow. Volatility may create opportunities where fundamentals remain intact, while 
communications requires greater selectivity given competitive pressure and disruption to advertising models.

Energy and Utilities
Supply disruption strengthens the oil backdrop
Energy and utilities enter the quarter from different starting points but share a common source of opportunity: 
constrained supply. Commodity disruptions are supporting energy cash flows, while scarce power capacity is 
strengthening the outlook for select utilities.
Oil markets continue to be shaped by disruption in the Middle East and reduced Russian refining capacity. While refined-
product shortages, particularly in diesel, are feeding back into crude prices, a steeply backwardated futures curve implies 
that the market expects prices to moderate next year, arguing for disciplined underwriting rather than reliance on today’s 
spot market.
Utilities face a slightly different disconnect. Investor concerns about the durability of AI capital spending compressed 
power-company valuations over the summer, even as data center demand remained robust and available power remained 
scarce. As such, the divide between sentiment and fundamentals has created selective entry points
Cash flow and contracted demand define the opportunity
Among upstream producers, oil appears better positioned than natural gas, although the futures curve suggests markets 
doubt today’s strength will last. The best-positioned operators should generate attractive returns using conservative 
midcycle assumptions; if prices stay too high, fuel costs and elevated interest rates could ultimately weaken demand.
Capital allocation is therefore critical as strong commodity prices are creating a cash-flow windfall that can fund debt 
reduction, share repurchases, dividends, or selective growth. Management teams that deploy this cash countercyclically 
can strengthen balance sheets and boost shareholder returns before prices normalize.
Natural gas is an infrastructure narrative
Natural gas presents a supply-heavy near-term outlook but a compelling infrastructure story. New Permian pipelines are 
bringing constrained volumes to market, limiting price upside. Regional disparities, including negative prices where gas 
cannot reach buyers, underscore the value of pipelines and processing assets. Whether supply or demand grows, more 
volume requires more infrastructure.
Hyperscaler contracts bear watching in utilities
Companies that secure new power agreements with large cloud and technology companies, or hyperscalers, may convert 
scarcity into durable earnings. These contracts can run from 10 to 15+ years, creating cumulative, recurring revenue 
rather than dependence on one-time equipment sales. The market’s broad sell-off may therefore overlook meaningful 
differences among power providers.
Data center demand is also reshaping the generation mix. Renewables and storage continue to expand, but intermittency 
limits around-the-clock reliability. Nuclear provides dependable power, yet remains costly and slow to permit, keeping gas-
fired generation and other available solutions central to the near-term energy buildout
Our perspective: Energy and Utilities
Energy and utilities offer complementary opportunities across disciplined oil producers, advantaged natural-gas 
infrastructure, and utilities capable of converting scarce power capacity into contracted earnings.
Risks remain distinct: sustained oil strength could destroy demand, while a technology shift, punitive regulation, 
or slower AI investment could reset power expectations. We favor high-quality companies with conservative 
balance sheets, disciplined capital allocation, advantaged infrastructure, and visible contractual revenue.

Financials
Banks settle into a favorable earnings environment
The backdrop for financials remains constructive heading into year-end, supported by moderately higher rates, steady 
loan growth, and resilient credit quality. Bank earnings expectations continue to rise as margins expand, while consumer 
repayment trends show little deterioration. Capital markets activity also remains healthy after its typical summer 
slowdown.
Much of that favorable backdrop rests on how rates are moving, not simply whether they are rising. A gradual increase, 
particularly when longer-term yields keep a healthy spread between funding costs and lending rates, can support bank 
profitability. The picture would become less constructive if short-term rates rose sharply and flattened the curve, pushing 
up borrowing costs, squeezing margins, and eventually pressuring consumer credit. For now, however, those strains have 
yet to emerge.
Investment activity and capital returns support the outlook
Looking ahead, banks appear to offer the clearest earnings momentum within the Financials sector. Deposit costs have 
remained relatively contained even as market rates have risen, supporting liability margins, while inflation and economic 
growth are sustaining loan balances. This combination has produced some of the sector’s strongest upward earnings 
revisions, particularly among large banks with broad lending and capital-markets franchises. 
Capital markets broaden the opportunity
AI-related infrastructure investment supports debt issuance, syndicated lending, equity issuance, and M&A. Yet M&A 
remains low relative to GDP despite strong headline volumes, suggesting the cycle is not broadly excessive. Borrowing has 
so far been concentrated among large technology companies, leaving room for broader activity.
Exchanges also present a constructive outlook. Higher volatility supports transaction revenue, while data and AI-enabled 
platforms expand subscriptions. These cyclical and structural drivers point to modest earnings gains, although 
digitalization adds uncertainty about future market structure and which platforms ultimately capture trading activity. .
That momentum is less consistent elsewhere in the sector. Life insurers remain stable, but property and casualty insurers 
face softer pricing as benign losses add to industry capital and intensify competition. Private asset managers present a less
immediate opportunity, with limited realizations continuing to cloud valuations. A pickup in asset sales and distributions 
would clarify liquidity conditions and support renewed investment in the group.
Capital strength creates room for shareholder returns
One of the most promising fourth-quarter developments may be greater capital return. As banks generate earnings, 
complete balance-sheet rebuilding, and move above required capital thresholds, more institutions should be positioned to 
increase dividends and repurchase shares. Full-year results could bring positive payout surprises, particularly among 
European banks whose capital positions have improved considerably. . 
Our perspective: Financials
Financials remain in a favorable earnings cycle, but leadership is concentrated among large banks with exposure to 
capital markets activity, corporate lending, and technology-related financing. While we favor institutions with 
expanding margins, strong credit quality, and the capacity to translate higher earnings into shareholder 
distributions, key risks still include aggressive rate increases, credit deterioration, and digital disruption.

Consumer Discretionary
Consumer resilience is beginning to fade
Consumer discretionary is under mounting pressure as spending data begins to validate weak sentiment. U.S. credit card 
spending across discretionary categories has turned moderately negative, Europe remains subdued, and China’s brief 
economic stabilization has faded, weighing on areas such as luxury. Japan and Korea are relative bright spots, supported 
by currency and wealth effects, but those tailwinds may prove less durable as rates rise.
Higher mortgage costs, persistent inflation, and geopolitical uncertainty are shrinking the income available for 
nonessential purchases, especially for middle-income households. Restraint is now visible among higher-income 
consumers as well. Meanwhile, higher spending among lower-income cohorts may reflect rising costs of necessities rather 
than improved financial health.
Consumers grow more value-conscious
Top-line expectations remain vulnerable through year-end and into 2027, with negative revisions across luxury, sporting 
goods, and homebuilding sectors. Cost discipline may allow earnings to hold up somewhat better than revenue, but 
companies cannot cut their way to durable growth. The sector’s few resilient businesses are attracting investor attention, 
pushing valuations higher and underscoring the importance of selectivity.
The clearest opportunities are businesses offering tangible value: accessible prices, dependable quality, and products 
consumers do not need to replace frequently. That favors select apparel and off-price retailers in the U.S. and abroad. 
Japanese retailers have also benefited from value-focused demand and a weaker yen, although currency support could 
diminish as domestic rates normalize.
Lodging is another area of relative strength as consumers continue to favor experiences over goods. Global hotel 
operators are supported not only by steady travel demand, but also by a structural shift toward branded properties. 
Independent hotels increasingly lack the larger operators’ reservation systems, loyalty programs, marketing, and revenue 
management capabilities. With industry supply growing slowly, established brands can capture an outsized share of new 
construction and conversions.
Still, beaten-down areas may become more interesting as valuations and earnings expectations approach a trough. Luxury 
goods remain challenged by weak Chinese demand, while gaming, restaurants, and caterers may offer selective 
opportunities after recent underperformance. 
Our perspective: Consumer Discretionary
Consumer discretionary faces a difficult backdrop as structurally higher financing costs and persistent inflation 
constrain household budgets. Revenue risks remain tilted downward, and the market is rewarding the limited 
pockets of earnings visibility.
We favor companies that deliver clear value, gain share structurally, and can grow without a broad spending 
rebound. Branded lodging and select value-oriented retailers fit that profile. Elsewhere, depressed valuations may 
create opportunities, but timing will depend on clearer evidence of improving demand and upward earnings 
revisions.

Consumer Staples
Defensive demand is becoming less dependable
Consumer staples are under pressure heading into the final quarter of the year, with weak demand persisting across 
developed markets. A summer rebound failed to materialize, leaving spending on staples categories subdued in the U.S. and 
Europe. Emerging markets remain an important counterweight, generating much of the sector’s growth and supporting 
multinational companies.
At the same time, cost pressures are broadening beyond oil to derivatives and agricultural commodities, with fertilizer, 
diesel, and weather risks pushing up prices for crops such as wheat and sugar. The larger earnings impact is likely to hit next 
year, forcing companies to absorb costs, raise prices, or accelerate productivity savings. Fourth-quarter performance may 
increasingly reflect which companies investors believe can manage those pressures most effectively.
Pricing power is reaching its limit
For several years, consumer staples companies relied on price increases to protect margins. That strategy is becoming 
harder to sustain. Lower-income consumers are trading down, buying less, or leaving some categories altogether. Spending 
by wealthier households remains strong, but it cannot entirely offset volume losses across everyday products.
Stronger private-label offerings are giving retailers greater leverage over established brands as consumers become more 
willing to trade down. Manufacturers, therefore, have less room to raise prices without losing volume or shelf space, 
particularly when competitors hold prices steady to gain share. Demand resilience, brand strength, and product innovation 
will increasingly determine which companies can sustain consumer relevance.
Defensive, but no longer the default
Consumer staples have historically commanded a premium for delivering predictable earnings amid an increasingly 
unpredictable economic backdrop. That case is harder to make today, as higher bond yields have made the sector’s 
defensive earnings stream less attractive relative to fixed income.
Furthermore, the sector’s earnings profile may also be less defensive than headline demand suggests. Another headwind for 
earnings will emerge as debt taken on when rates were near historic lows that will need to be refinanced in 2027-2028. For 
some companies, the higher interest burden alone could reduce earnings growth by 100 to 200 basis points.
There are still opportunities, but they are becoming more company-specific. Businesses with meaningful emerging-market 
exposure, credible productivity programs, and brands that can hold demand should be better positioned. Beauty and 
personal care, including cosmetics, fragrance, and skin care, have also remained more resilient than expected.
Our perspective: Consumer Staples
Consumer staples retain defensive qualities, but the sector is no longer a uniform haven. Pressure on lower-
income households, stronger private-label competition, , and higher financing costs have weakened the 
dependable earnings profile historically associated with the group.
The fourth-quarter opportunity set is therefore selective. We favor differentiated brands with resilient demand 
in emerging markets, flexible cost structures, and identifiable savings. Evidence that volumes can hold as 
companies respond to higher costs will be essential, particularly if emerging-market consumer spending 
weakens or commodity inflation forces another difficult pricing cycle in 2027.

Industrials
Strong fundamentals meet more cautious sentiment
In what is proving to be a continuation from last quarter’s momentum, Industrials are seeing durable demand across data 
center infrastructure, aerospace, and defense, as AI-related capital spending is spreading to more parts of the machinery and 
capital goods value chain. Agricultural commodity prices have also improved, supporting expectations for a recovery in farm-
equipment spending. Meanwhile, record copper prices suggest mining capital expenditure may be entering an upcycle, with 
projects advancing in Chile, the U.S., and Argentina. Stock performance has lagged these fundamentals, as higher rates have 
pressured construction, building materials, and other perceived rate-sensitive stocks. Despite this, companies generally report 
little direct effect on data center or defense demand. Margins remain strong across much of the sector; housing-linked 
building products are the clearest exception, with weak activity affecting both earnings and valuations.
Longer-cycle demand offers greater visibility
Data center investment remains a powerful driver, with alternative financing expanding and suppliers discussing orders 
extending into 2032. That visibility favors companies with strong backlogs, integrated offerings, and recurring service or 
aftermarket revenue. It also argues for selectivity: one-time equipment sales can lift near-term results, but extrapolating them
into a permanent earnings run rate may prove risky if spending eventually normalizes.
Defense presents another disconnect between sentiment and operating reality. Rising rates have raised concerns about 
government budget capacity, especially in Europe, yet spending trajectories and multi-year commitments remain intact. 
Recent valuation compression may therefore offer an opportunity, although any change in the geopolitical backdrop could 
weigh on European defense valuations.
Commodity strength creates selective machinery opportunities
Machinery demands a sharper distinction between long- and short-cycle exposure. Mining and agricultural equipment should 
benefit from stronger commodity economics. At the same time, Japanese construction companies may be less vulnerable to 
higher rates than their share prices suggest because many carry substantial cash and rely less on debt. By contrast, demand 
for factory automation, particularly in Japan, appears closer to a cyclical peak.
Resilient businesses beyond the dominant themes
Opportunities are also emerging outside the market’s dominant themes. Commercial and professional services, including 
waste companies, government contractors, credit bureaus, and employment services providers, have generally maintained 
resilient fundamentals despite prolonged weakness in share prices. Their recent rebound still leaves a sizable performance 
gap, creating room for further catch-up if investors rotate toward earnings durability and company-specific improvement.
Our perspective: Industrials
We favor companies supported by long-duration investment in data centers, power, defense, and mining, but 
broad exposure is less attractive as expectations rise. In a slower-growth environment, visible backlogs and 
recurring revenue should matter more than macro sensitivity. Commercial services and select rate-sensitive 
businesses may reward investors where market perceptions have moved further than fundamentals.
The principal risks in industrials are paying for peak expectations, confusing one-time sales with sustainable 
demand, and deploying capital aggressively late in the cycle. Acquisitions intended to expand data center 
exposure could destroy value at elevated prices. Housing-linked building products remain challenged, while 
shorter-cycle machinery may offer less resilience than longer-cycle infrastructure businesses. Capital-allocation 
discipline will be as important as growth.

Healthcare
Earnings momentum begins to broaden
Healthcare enters the fourth quarter with improving performance, having served as a source of funds for the AI trade 
through much of the year. Volatility in AI-related technology shares helped the sector outperform during the third quarter, 
with capital flowing particularly into life science tools. This rotation may continue: large pharmaceutical companies can offer 
defensive earnings when technology volatility or rates rise. At the same time, biotech and other long-duration healthcare 
businesses are generally more sensitive to discount rates.
Recovering research spending creates a clearer growth path
Life science tools offer one of the sector’s clearest recovery stories. Pharmaceutical and biotech research spending is 
improving after several subdued years, supporting upward earnings revisions. A return from flat or low-single-digit growth 
toward the industry’s historical mid- to high-single-digit range would strengthen the outlook for 2027 and 2028. AI may add 
longer-term demand for instruments, diagnostics, and research capabilities, although its near-term earnings impact remains 
limited.
Managed care is also improving. Premium increases have begun to catch up with elevated medical costs, producing more 
favorable medical loss ratios and the potential for further earnings revisions. Healthcare costs are not declining; rather, 
annual repricing is restoring margins. Medicare quality ratings and the durability of that improvement remain important 
watch points.
Other subsectors complicate the picture
Medtech presents a more complicated opportunity. Valuations are near multiyear lows, but investors remain concerned that 
procedure growth could slow as Affordable Care Act exchange enrollment declines and the post-pandemic rise in utilization 
moderates. Lower expectations may create attractive entry points, but confidence in procedure volumes is needed before 
taking a broader view. Trends are also likely to differ meaningfully across markets.
GLP-1 adoption adds a longer-term consideration. These therapies have not yet materially reduced procedure volumes, 
partly because many patients discontinue treatment within a year. Expanded Medicare access could improve affordability 
and treatment persistence among older patients, potentially affecting utilization over several years rather than the next few
quarters.
Pharma and biotech remain highly company-specific. Upcoming clinical readouts may reward differentiated pipelines, but 
recent trial disappointments illustrate the downside risk. Positioning around multiple catalysts and proven innovation 
remains essential.
Our perspective: Healthcare
Healthcare’s fundamentals are improving, but selectivity remains critical. We favor life science tools benefiting 
from renewed research spending, managed care companies with improving cost ratios, and pharma or biotech 
businesses supported by differentiated innovation and multiple catalysts. Medtech valuations are increasingly 
compelling, though greater visibility into procedure volumes is still needed.

Materials
Resilient demand meets a more complicated cost backdrop
Heading into the final quarter of the year, the Materials sector is seeing support from stronger-than-expected U.S. growth, 
resilient metals demand, and a complicated geopolitical backdrop. Copper and aluminum prices remain elevated, while 
disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz are affecting commodity markets unevenly. Gold has also remained resilient despite 
higher real rates, helped by unusually strong central bank buying amid efforts by some countries to reduce exposure to U.S. 
Treasurys.
AI infrastructure remains a tailwind, but it is best understood as incremental demand. Data centers require cement, steel, 
copper, and aluminum, yet the copper used in AI facilities still represents only a small share of global consumption. Broader
economic growth, factory construction, and everyday end-use remain the primary drivers of global demand in the sector.
Supply constraints support metals, while costs pressure other segments
Copper remains one of the more attractive areas within materials, supported by electrification, reshoring-related factory 
construction, and data center investment. At the same time, new supply remains difficult to develop since mines and 
smelters can take a decade or more to build, limiting the industry’s ability to respond quickly. In the U.S., policy uncertainty is 
also slowing decisions, as companies wait for greater clarity on potential tariffs before committing capital. Copper 
stockpiling in the U.S. ahead of potential trade restrictions is contributing to higher prices, even though the country accounts
for a relatively small share of global copper demand.
Construction materials face a tougher cost backdrop
Conditions are less favorable for construction materials. Cement and aggregates are highly energy-intensive, leaving 
producers exposed to elevated fuel costs just as infrastructure demand is losing momentum. In some emerging markets, 
higher oil subsidies are diverting fiscal resources that might otherwise support road and construction projects. 
Hormuz disruptions ripple through fertilizer markets
The Strait of Hormuz has also created less-visible supply chain pressures. Sulfur, primarily produced as a byproduct of oil and 
gas processing, is essential for the leaching of certain metals and for phosphate fertilizer production. With a large portion of
seaborne sulfur moving through the region, disruption has pushed fertilizer costs higher. That pressure is not necessarily 
improving fertilizer producer margins; rather, stronger crop prices and rising biofuel demand are helping support farm 
profitability and fertilizer demand
Our perspective: Materials
Materials remain supported by durable metals demand, but the opportunity set is increasingly differentiated. 
Copper and aluminum benefit from economic growth, electrification, AI-related construction, and supply 
constraints, while gold continues to offer diversification amid fiscal and monetary uncertainty. Construction 
materials face a tougher mix of elevated energy costs and slower infrastructure spending.
Company selection should emphasize cost position, supply-chain exposure, and the ability to manage policy 
uncertainty. Businesses with scarce assets and resilient margins appear better positioned than companies relying 
mainly on higher commodity prices or a broad acceleration in construction activity.
