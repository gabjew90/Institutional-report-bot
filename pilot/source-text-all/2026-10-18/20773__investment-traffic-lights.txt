IN A NUTSHELL 
— 
After a strong August, September proved more challenging for investors as a sharp rise in bond 
yields weighed on most risk assets. 
— 
Despite the magnitude of the move in yields, equity markets held up relatively well, although 
performance was driven by only a handful of sectors. 
— 
Investors enter the fourth quarter against a backdrop of persistently unsettled bond markets. 
Nevertheless, resilient corporate earnings, solid economic growth and the continued strength of 
the AI-driven capex cycle suggest that it may take a more fundamental regime shift in bond 
markets before equities come under sustained pressure. 
1 / Market overview 
1.1 Geopolitical tensions and record high bond yields 
What shaped markets in September? The month presented investors with a mixed picture. On the negative side, continued 
tensions in the Middle East pushed oil and even more so diesel prices higher, fueling inflation concerns and contributing to 
a sharp rise in government bond yields. Markets grappled with growing anxiety over the scope of further monetary tightening, 
renewed concerns about Europe’s fiscal outlook, particularly in France
. At the same time a high-profile meeting between 
Trump and Xi failed to deliver any meaningful progress. 
 
Against this backdrop, however, economic fundamentals remained surprisingly resilient. Sentiment indicators continued to 
improve, with the U.S. flash composite PMI reaching a five-year high, the Eurozone Purchasing Managers’ Index (
PMI) a three-
year high, and Japan’s 
Tankan survey showing its strongest reading since the early 1990s. 2Q26 gross domestic product 
(GDP) data for both Europe and the U.S. have been revised upwards. In addition, corporate earnings proved resilient and the 
artificial intelligence (AI) investment cycle continued unabated. 
 
Given the sharp rise in bond yields, equities’ relatively modest decline is noteworthy, although performance was driven by a 
narrow group of sectors. Only 27% of stocks outperformed the MSCI AC World Index in September, and just two of ten sectors 
delivered positive returns, both linked to the AI ecosystem. In light of tightening financial conditions, the scale and structure 
of AI financing — record convertible issuance, jumbo junk bond deals and multi-trillion-dollar private valuations — alongside a 
growing debate about whether the capital being deployed can ultimately be justified by AI revenues was a dominant theme 
in September. Anthropic’s upcoming 
initial public offering (IPO) (the prospectus has already made the rounds) might help 
investors to better understand the monetization dynamics of this space.

and renewed central-bank tightening expectations pushed bond yields sharply higher. Government bonds suffered their 
worst quarterly performance since 2024, with the Bloomberg Global Aggregate Index falling more than 2.5% in September 
alone. U.S. Treasury yields surged across the curve, led by maturities higher than 7 years, with the 10-year yield moving

2 / Outlook and changes 
 
The fourth quarter in financial markets is likely to be shaped by two conflicting forces: high government bond yields on the 
one hand and robust economic momentum with continued earnings per share (EPS) growth on the other. While higher oil 
prices have often been cited as the main driver of higher bond yields, the increase has not been fueled by rising inflation 
expectations but by a rise in real yields. The coming month might shed more light on whether the yield rise has been triggered 
by improving economic expectations or doubts about fragile public debt profiles. As for profits, we are entering the third-
quarter reporting season with higher expectations than in the second quarter, slightly increasing the risk of disappointment 
even if the numbers are good. Equities will need to show sufficiently broad earnings momentum to offset higher bond yields, 
which are normally a drag on most sector valuations. 
 
2.1 Fixed Income 
Let’s try to see the positive side of September having lifted rates so drastically: higher 
yields have created more attractive 
entry points in fixed-income markets, while resilient fundamentals continue to support credit. In foreign-exchange markets, 
the dollar’s strength is increasingly being challenged by longer
-term trends towards global currency rebalancing. 
Nonetheless, the recent widening of European sovereign bond spreads warrants close attention. While waning confidence 
in France’s commitment to fiscal reform is unlikely to reverse in the near term, we believe fears of broader contagion across
 
Europe are exaggerated. 
 
Government Bonds 
U.S. Treasuries remain attractive following the recent yield repricing, as we continue to see market expectations for Fed 
tightening as overly aggressive relative to our central economic outlook. Higher yields have improved valuations, while a 
slowdown in growth and easing inflationary pressures could provide support for both the front and long ends of the Treasury 
curve.  
 
In Europe, government bond markets are experiencing heightened volatility. Alongside the global rise in yields, France’s fiscal 
situation has triggered a significant widening of spreads across many European government bonds relative to German Bunds, 
which have recently benefited further from safe-haven flows.1 With markets remaining highly fluid, we are either staying on 
the sidelines and waiting for momentum to subside or adapting quickly to market moves where our conviction is sufficiently 
strong.

Part of the current market dynamics reflects shifting expectations for the European Central Bank (ECB). While we believe 
inflation data would have provided the central bank with a strong rationale to continue its tightening cycle, the sharp moves 
in bond markets, which are already contributing to tighter financial conditions, make an October rate hike unlikely in our 
view. We held a positive view on two- and ten-year Bunds at the yield levels seen in late September.2 For Italian spreads, we 
have, for the time being, moved back to a Neutral, despite our constructive assessment of the underlying fundamentals, 
which appear broadly in line with those of its euro-area peers and continue to improve. Compared with the past, the Italian 
government has proven surprisingly stable. From a fiscal perspective, the debt-to-GDP ratio has stabilized at around 137%, 
growth is expected to remain below 1% in both 2026 and 2027, and inflation is projected to decline from 2.9% to 2.2% by 
2027. Recent rating upgrades reflect a favorable assessment by credit rating agencies. However, higher borrowing costs 
could put pressure on future budgets. Idiosyncratic risks remain relatively limited, suggesting that most of the recent 
widening in spreads is being driven by broader risk aversion, the persistence of which remains uncertain. 
 
As for French OATs, we remain Neutral. On the one hand, further spread widening cannot be ruled out given elevated political 
uncertainty. On the other hand, a significant amount of negative news now appears to be priced in. Moreover, French banks 
and insurers, traditionally viewed as natural domestic buyers of OATs, may see current yield levels as an attractive entry 
point. With risks and opportunities finely balanced, we see little justification for moving away from a neutral stance at this 
stage. 
 
UK gilts remain attractive, as markets continue to price in a significantly more aggressive Bank of England tightening path 
than our central scenario. Elevated real yields and compelling valuations support a constructive stance, while a moderation 
in energy prices or weaker economic momentum could provide scope for yields to decline, particularly at the front end of the 
curve. 
 
Corporate Bonds 
We upgraded European Investment Grade credit to +1, not least because new issuance has been much lower than feared. 
Furthermore, Euro IG credit has become a carry asset class for many investors, offering low volatility, strong fundamentals 
and predictable supply. The asset class differs significantly, both fundamentally and technically, from the increasingly heated 
debate around government debt sustainability. Absolute yields have become a non-negligible investment argument for many 
investors, while continued inflows into the asset class from new pools of capital or reallocation decisions provide further 
support for IG credit. We expect buying momentum to increase further as rates volatility eases. We have also raised European 
High Yield to Neutral, following a market correction in September that led spreads to widen by around 50bps to 292bps. 
Coupled with the rise in rates, this resulted in one of the few months of materially negative total returns for the market. Our 
more constructive stance reflects improved valuations, our more positive economic and rates outlook, and still-solid 
technicals, as we continue to see buyers stepping in whenever the market weakens. However, we would still not move to +1, 
as we view periods of global monetary tightening as risky. Technicals could also suffer if new issuance increases. Valuations 
are still not attractive enough to fully compensate for these risks. 
 
For both U.S. Investment Grade and High Yield credit, we remain Neutral, as resilient demand and still-healthy market 
fundamentals are balanced by headwinds from future supply by large technology issuers and still-relatively tight spreads.  
 
Emerging Markets 
Emerging Market sovereign debt continues to benefit from resilient growth, supportive commodity dynamics and positive 
rating trends across many countries. However, following a period of strong performance, valuations have become less 
compelling, prompting a more neutral stance despite the asset class’s still
-favorable fundamental backdrop.

2.2 Equities 
Based on the resilience of economic growth and corporate earnings, we remain constructive on equities, particularly as 
valuations have become more attractive during the third quarter. As Morgan Stanley noted, this has been the largest derating 
in a rising market in more than 30 years. In other words, although the MSCI AC World posted a modest gain during the 
quarter, its price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio declined by around 7%, as 12-month forward earnings estimates increased by more 
than 10%. In fact, every sector saw higher 12-month forward earnings estimates over the quarter for the first time in more 
than five years.3 
 
While we see few reasons to expect a sudden slowdown in earnings growth, markets still face several headwinds heading 
into the fourth quarter. Above all, higher bond yields remain a concern. With 10-year Treasury yields trading above 5%, valu-
ation pressure is increasingly affecting many parts of the market. September's performance illustrated this clearly: gains 
were driven almost entirely by technology-related sectors, with only Information Technology and Communication Services 
(which include several hyperscalers) delivering positive returns, while the remaining sectors ended the month in negative 
territory. Earnings growth must therefore continue to offset the drag from higher discount rates. Equally important, the AI 
investment cycle needs to remain largely intact, as it continues to be the dominant driver of market leadership. Consumer 
fatigue in the face of elevated energy prices is another risk we are monitoring closely. 
 
We have not changed any of our regional views in recent months,  Japan and European small- and mid-cap equities remain 
our only Outperform recommendations. Japan's recent strong Tankan survey, with business conditions across industries 
reaching their highest level since the early 1990s, combined with robust manufacturing activity supported by the AI-driven 
capital expenditures (capex) cycle, reinforces our positive stance. We also maintain our favorable view on European small 
and mid-caps, supported by the ongoing industrial recovery and historically attractive valuations. 
 
At the sector level, banks remain one of our preferred exposures, benefiting from higher interest rates even if the yield curve 
flattens further. We also continue to favor utilities, where AI-driven electricity demand remains a powerful structural tailwind 
despite the headwind from higher yields. Within technology, we now take a broader approach after having concentrated on 
semiconductors in 1H26. We now see AI driving companies in the hardware, software and semiconductor segments alike. 
The broader rollout of AI agents4 has provided another boost to the investment case. Finally, while we remain neutral on 
healthcare overall while appreciating its defensive characteristics within a diversified portfolio, biotechnology stands out as 
our preferred subsector, supported by sustained innovation and what we expect to be continued robust M&A activity. We 
have downgraded Consumer staples to Underweight as they are no longer the defensive haven they once were. Earnings 
revisions remain negative and pricing power continues to fade amid changing consumer preferences and tighter household 
budgets. With limited scope for margin expansion, demanding valuations, and only a handful of attractive stock opportunities, 
the sector's risk-reward profile has become less compelling. As a result, we downgrade staples to underweight, while con-
tinuing to favor select areas such as beverages and beauty, where pricing power and innovation remain supportive. We have 
upgraded global real estate from underweight to neutral as improving operating fundamentals across several property seg-
ments make a defensive underweight position less compelling. Limited new supply, driven by higher construction and fi-
nancing costs, is supporting occupancy and rental growth, particularly in senior housing, industrial properties, data centers, 
and commercial real estate services. Attractive valuations, above-average earnings growth, and meaningful AI exposure 
through data center Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) are supportive, although the sector remains sensitive to higher 
interest rates and energy-driven inflation risks.

2.3 Alternatives 
 
Gold 
Rising U.S. Treasury yields and expectations of additional Federal Reserve rate hikes put renewed pressure on gold prices. 
Despite the pullback, gold exchange-traded fund (ETF) holdings increased by roughly 400,000 ounces in the third week of 
September, suggesting continued institutional buying interest. Retail demand, however, remains subdued, with both Indian 
and Chinese investors largely staying on the sidelines. We believe the spot price of gold could test USD 4,000 again. We 
anticipate that central bank demand will pick up at this price level and view USD 4,000 as a potential lower trading boundary 
for gold. 
 
Oil 
We have reduced our stance on oil from +1 to Neutral. In early September, Iran held an advantage in disrupting traffic through 
the Strait of Hormuz, while the Houthis in Yemen expanded their conflict with Saudi Arabia by taking over coastal cities on 
the Red Sea. The Houthis also temporarily halted oil flows through the East-West Pipeline. However, momentum has shifted 
in recent days. Under U.S. protection, nearly all Middle Eastern oil production, except Iranian oil, is now believed to be moving 
through the Strait of Hormuz. Traffic through the East-West Pipeline has also been restored, and Saudi Arabia is now able to 
resume oil deliveries via the Suez Canal. With the U.S. continuing to tighten its economic and physical blockade and oil now 
seemingly flowing freely through these chokepoints, the Iranian government faces a challenging dilemma: either engage 
militarily with the U.S. to defend its control of the Strait of Hormuz or accept U.S. conditions to reach a deal. However, we 
do not believe that a deal will be reached before the midterm elections.

Glossary 
 
The Bank of England (BoE) is the central bank of the United Kingdom. 
The Bank of Japan (BoJ) is the central bank of Japan. 
One basis point equals 1/100 of a percentage point. 
The Bloomberg Commodity Index (BCOM) traces 23 commodities and reflects commodity futures price movements. 
The Bloomberg Global Aggregate Bond Index contains investment-grade bonds from 24 different local currency markets. 
Bunds is a commonly used term for bonds issued by the German federal government with a maturity of 10 years. 
Capital expenditures (capex) are funds used by a company to acquire, maintain, or upgrade long-term physical assets such as property, buildings, or 
equipment. 
A convertible bond is a fixed-income debt security that yields interest payments, but can be converted into a predetermined number of equity shares. 
The conversion from the bond to stock can be done at certain times during the bond's life and is usually at the discretion of the bondholder. 
DAX is Germany’s main blue-chip equity index, comprising 40 major companies listed on the Frankfurt Stock Exchange. 
Defensive stocks are stocks from companies whose sales are expected to fluctuate less than the market average as the demand for their products are 
less tied to business cycles. 
Earnings per share (EPS) is calculated as a company's net income minus dividends of preferred stock, all divided by the total number of shares out-
standing. 
The Euro Stoxx 50 is an index that tracks the performance of blue-chip stocks in the Eurozone. 
The European Central Bank (ECB) is the central bank for the Eurozone. 
EUR/USD is the abbreviation used in forex trading to denote the currency exchange rate for the euro and U.S. dollar. 
An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a security that tracks an index or asset like an index fund, but trades like a stock on an exchange. 
FX or foreign exchange is the currency — literally foreign money — used in the settlement of international trade between countries. 
The FTSE 100 is an index that tracks the performance of the 100 major companies trading on the London Stock Exchange. 
Gilts are bonds that are issued by the British Government. 
The gross domestic product (GDP) is the monetary value of all the finished goods and services produced within a country's borders in a specific time 
period. 
Hawk refers to a policymaker or investor who favors restrictive monetary policy, often to control inflation. 
High-yield bonds are issued by below-investment-grade-rated issuers and usually offer a relatively high yield. 
Hyperscalers are large cloud service providers, which can provide services such as computing and storage at enterprise scale. 
Idiosyncratic risk is a risk which is related solely to the individual company and has no correlation with the general market risk. 
Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services is rising and, subsequently, purchasing power is falling. 
Initial public offering (IPO) is the first public sale of a company’s shares on a securities exchange. 
Investment grade (IG) refers to a credit rating from a rating agency that indicates that a bond has a relatively low risk of default. 
The Japanese yen (JPY) is the official currency of Japan. 
The Japan Tankan Survey is a quarterly survey measuring business confidence among Japanese companies. 
Jumbo loans are loans above standard lending limits, typically used to finance higher-value assets. 
Junk bonds are high-yield bonds rated below investment grade. 
The final payment date of a financial instrument is its maturity. 
Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) are the two key methods of corporate consolidation. A merger is a combination of two companies to form a new 
company, while an acquisition is the purchase of one company by another in which no new company is formed. 
Firms referred to as mid cap generally have a market capitalization of between $2 billion and $10 billion.
