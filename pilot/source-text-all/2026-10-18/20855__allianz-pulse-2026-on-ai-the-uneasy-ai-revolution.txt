Summary
Executive
•	The Allianz Pulse Survey takes the temperature of European households 
each year on the economic, political and societal issues shaping their lives, 
with the 2026 edition focusing on artificial intelligence. As AI moves into 
everyday life and work, the survey explores how people use the technology, 
what they expect from it and how prepared they feel for the changes it will 
bring. For its eighth edition, we surveyed a representative sample of 1,000 
adults in each of Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Poland and Austria, adding the 
UK and US for the first time. This provides a transatlantic perspective on how 
people are experiencing and anticipating the AI transition. 
•	Europe is embracing AI, but adoption is less advanced and less work 
related than in the US, with large differences within Europe. Across all eight 
countries surveyed, 48% of respondents use AI at least weekly, with the US 
and Spain leading at around 55%, while Germany, France and Austria remain 
in the low 40s. The generational divide is particularly striking: 77% of Gen Z 
use AI weekly, compared with just 23% of Boomers. Moreover, adoption has 
yet to translate fully into workplace use: among AI users, 53% use it mainly for 
private purposes, 20% mainly for work or study and 27% across both contexts. 
Just under half therefore use AI at least partly for work or study, with the 
share highest in the US (52%), the UK (51%) and Spain (49%), but only 37% 
in Germany. Germany stands out as the country where AI adoption is most 
concentrated in private life, while Spain shows that high AI adoption and 
workplace use are not uniquely American. 
•	Europeans see the benefits of AI for themselves, but are considerably 
more skeptical about what it means for the economy and jobs, especially 
compared with Americans. Net sentiment towards AI is -34% overall, meaning 
negative views exceed positive views by 34pp. It deteriorates to -43% for the 
economic impact and -67% for jobs. The views about the job impact of AI are 
deeply negative, even as positive productivity expectations exceed negative 
expectations by 30% across all respondents, while 35% expect little or no 
impact. The US provides a clear contrast on employment: its jobs sentiment 
stands at -44%, still negative but considerably less so than the survey average. 
Within Europe, the picture varies strongly: Austria (-81%) and France (-75%) 
are the most pessimistic about the AI impact on jobs, while Spain combines 
high AI adoption with less negative attitudes towards AI’s expected job impact 
(-66%). Across countries, young, urban and high-income respondents are the 
demographic groups most positive about AI. More broadly, the observed AI 
skepticism is closely associated with an overall weak confidence in economic 
progress: respondents with negative views of AI also tend to hold gloomier 
views of both the economy and the future.

•	As AI adoption spreads, concerns about its impact on jobs are intensifying, even 
though most people expect task transformation rather than outright job destruction. 
Among countries surveyed in both years, net sentiment on AI’s impact on jobs fell by 
around 12pp, from -55% in 2025 to -67% in 2026. The deterioration was particularly 
pronounced in Europe: Austria’s jobs balance fell by around 19pps, while Germany and 
France also recorded double digit declines. Yet this is not primarily a story of expected 
mass unemployment. Only 7% expect their job to disappear entirely, while 21% expect 
significant parts to be replaced and 26% expect AI mainly to augment their work. 
First-hand experience changes expectations substantially: 41% of workplace AI users 
anticipate augmentation, compared with only 11% of non-users, although they are also 
more likely to expect task replacement (40% versus 17%). Exposure appears to make 
both the risks and opportunities more tangible, particularly the potential for AI to support 
rather than replace workers. 
•	Europe risks entering the AI transition with too little investment in workers, while 
training continues to favor those already ahead. Only 17% of all employed respondents 
across countries have received AI training at work, compared with 30% in the US. The 
lack of training opportunities is a major bottleneck: 44% report that their employer offers 
no AI training at all. The European range is wide, with participation falling to just 10% 
in Poland, while training is also significantly more common among tertiary educated 
workers. This suggests that access to training risks reinforcing existing divides in skills and 
opportunity. The transatlantic contrast is particularly concerning: The US combines more 
intensive AI use with greater employer-led preparation, while parts of Europe combine 
limited workplace adoption with stronger fears of labor-market disruption. 
•	People fear that AI will widen inequality and want stronger safeguards against 
its risks. Across the entire sample, 37% expect AI to increase inequality, 34% think it 
will stay the same, compared with only 9% who expect a reduction. Austria records 
the strongest expectation of widening inequality, while Poland is the least concerned, 
though the balance is positive everywhere. People worry less about what AI might 
become than about harms it already causes: misinformation (25%), data privacy (21%) 
and job losses (17%) top the list, ahead of loss of control over advanced AI (16%). The net 
support for stronger regulation, calculated as the share of respondents in favor minus 
the share opposed, increased by 17pp, from +61% in 2025 to +78% in 2026. This does not 
signal outright resistance to AI: Spain combines high adoption with strong support for 
regulation, showing that enthusiasm for the technology and demand for safeguards can 
coexist. 
•	For Europe, the central challenge is therefore to accelerate productive AI adoption 
while ensuring that its benefits are broadly shared. The survey points to five 
priorities. First, move AI from private experimentation into productive workplace use 
by lowering implementation costs, especially for SMEs. Second, close the training gap 
through employer-led, occupation-specific learning. Third, shift labor-market policy 
from protecting existing jobs to supporting transitions into new tasks and roles. Fourth, 
make sure the productivity dividend is broadly shared through skills, wider household 
participation in capital markets and tax systems that adapt to shifts in income from labor 
to capital. Fifth, strengthen trust through targeted, proportionate regulation addressing 
misinformation, data misuse and labor-market risks without stifling innovation. 
Ultimately, success will be measured by AI‘s ability to deliver broadly shared productivity 
gains, stronger employability and greater confidence in economic progress.

AI adoption is unfolding at historically unprecedented 
speed, but usage intensity remains uneven. Generative 
AI has reached mass adoption faster than earlier 
general-purpose technologies such as the personal 
computer or the internet, and our survey shows that 
more than three-quarters of respondents have already 
used AI tools. Yet only 21% use them daily and 22% 
have never used them at all (Figure 1). Weekly use 
reaches 48% across the sample, led by the US and Spain, 
at around 55%, while France, Germany and Austria 
remain in the low 40s. The transatlantic difference lies 
mainly in intensity rather than reach: the share that has 
never used AI is similar in Europe and the US, but daily 
use is materially higher in the US, at 30% compared 
with around 20% in Europe. Spain shows that high 
engagement is not uniquely American.
The largest divides are within societies rather than 
across the Atlantic. More than three-quarters, or 77%, 
of Gen Z use AI at least weekly, more than three times 
the 23% share among Boomers (Figure 2). Millennials 
are closest to Gen Z in AI adoption (65% weekly users), 
while Gen X (43%) occupy the middle ground. Large gaps 
also run through income, education and geography: 59% 
of high-income respondents use AI weekly compared 
with 40% of low-income respondents, while usage rises 
from 40% among the primary-educated to 58% among 
those with tertiary education. Urban respondents are 
also substantially more engaged than rural ones, at 57% 
versus 34%. The gender gap in weekly AI use is evident 
but smaller than other gaps: Weekly adoption is higher 
among men than women, at 51% versus 45%. Overall, 
AI use is already widespread, but the intensity and 
economic relevance of that use differs sharply across 
demographic groups.

AI is still used more in private life than at work. Among 
users, 53% employ AI primarily for private purposes, 
compared with 20% who use it mainly for work or study 
and 27% who use it for both (Figure 3). The private-use 
share ranges from 48% in the US to 63% in Germany, 
while exclusive work or study use varies only from 14% 
in Germany to 23% in France. Combining those who use 
AI mainly for work or study with those who use it across 
both professional and private contexts, just under half 
of users employ AI at least partly for work or study. The 
share is highest in the US (52%), the UK (51%) and Spain 
(49%), compared with only 37% in Germany. Germany 
therefore stands out as the clearest case where AI 
adoption remains concentrated in private life. The US 
and UK, by contrast, have the highest shares of users 
spanning both professional and private contexts, at 33% 
and 31%, respectively, suggesting somewhat deeper 
integration of AI across different parts of everyday life.
Age matters considerably more than country for how 
AI is used. Almost four in five Boomers (78%) use AI 
mainly privately, compared with only 8% mainly for work, 
although this partly reflects the fact that many in this 
age group are already entering or in retirement. Among 
Gen Z, the split is almost perfectly balanced: 35% use it 
mainly for work or study, 35% mainly privately and 30% 
for both. Millennials occupy the middle ground here, with 
44% using AI mainly privately, 22% mainly for work or 
study and 34% for both. Notably, the gender gap in how 
AI is used is surprisingly small: men and women use AI 
across private and professional purposes in a similar way, 
even though weekly adoption remains somewhat higher 
among men than women, at 51% versus 45%.

AI is already widely used, but most people view the 
new technology with anxiety rather than enthusiasm 
(Figure 4). To capture respondents’ overall attitude 
towards AI, we asked them to rate their general 
views about the technology on a scale from 0 (very 
negative) to 10 (very positive). Using the established 
net-promoter-score metric, we classify respondents 
into detractors (scores 0-6), passives (scores 7-8) and 
promoters (scores 9-10). Across all respondents in the 
eight countries surveyed, 52% classify as AI detractors, 
compared with just 18% promoters, while 29% fall in 
between as passives. In every country surveyed, the 
share of detractors far exceeds the share of promoters, 
underscoring people’s high levels of uncertainty and 
anxiety around AI. Spain stands out as the country with 
the lowest share of AI detractors at 40% and the second-
highest share of promotors at 24%. The most positive, 
or least negative, AI attitudes are found in the US with 
the highest promoter share at 30%. At the other end, the 
two countries with the most negative views about AI are 
Germany and Austria with only 14% and 11% promoters 
but 62% and 60% detractors, respectively. 
The divide in AI attitudes is just as striking across 
demographic groups, pointing to a broader gap in who 
feels comfortable with the AI transition. Promoters are 
disproportionately young, urban and high-income. Almost 
40% of high-income respondents are promoters, more than 
twice the share among low-income respondents (19%), 
while only 37% of the high-income group are detractors 
compared with 60% of the low-income group. Age shows an 
even sharper divide, with two-thirds of Boomers classified 
as detractors compared with 39% of Gen Z. Geography 
matters too: 66% of rural respondents are detractors, 
compared with 45% of urban respondents. Education, 
however, tells a more nuanced story. Tertiary-educated 
respondents are less likely to reject AI outright than those 
with only primary education, but they are not much more 
likely to embrace it: promoter shares are 22% and 20%, 
respectively. That may reflect the double-edged nature of 
AI for knowledge workers. They are better placed to use the 
technology, but many also work in occupations where tasks 
are especially exposed to the technology.

The central finding is a striking gradient in AI 
attitudes, which become steadily more negative as 
the lens widens from the individual to the economy 
and the labor market. Having established who falls 
into the promoter, passive and detractor camps, we 
use the same classification to compare attitudes across 
different dimensions of AI (Figure 5). For each question, 
net sentiment is the share of promoters minus the 
share of detractors; passives therefore do not affect 
the balance. On this measure, sentiment towards AI in 
general stands at -34% across the sample, underscoring 
the dominant share of detractors. When we widen the 
lens to AI’s effect on the economy, net sentiment falls 
to -43%. Widen it once more to AI’s effect on jobs and it 
drops to a stark -67%, the most negative reading in the 
entire survey. The latter number means that the 75% 
of respondents with negative assessments outnumber 
the 8% of respondents with positive ones by 67pp. This 
gradient shows that people are relaxed about the tool in 
their own hands but fearful of it in the labor market. No 
single country or demographic group escapes this pattern.

Every year, the Allianz Pulse tracks how households view the economy and their own prospects. In 2026, economic 
confidence fell back toward pandemic-era lows, raising a new question: Could growing fears about AI be adding 
to pessimism about both the near-term economic outlook and future generations’ prospects for prosperity? As 
AI becomes part of everyday life and work, its potential to transform jobs, incomes and living standards adds a new 
dimension to how people assess their economic future.
Economic sentiment has deteriorated sharply across most countries. Since 2019, the Allianz Pulse has tracked 
assessments of current economic conditions and future prospects. Our economic sentiment indicator averages the net 
balances of positive minus negative responses to both questions. In 2026, it stands at -67% in France, -56% in Germany, 
-50% in Italy and -46% in Austria (Figure 7). Germany recorded the sharpest reversal, falling from -17% in 2025 to -56%, 
below even its reading during the Covid pandemic. Poland remains the only positive outlier at +9%, while the UK (-38%) 
reflects the subdued mood across Western Europe. The US, included for the first time, is close to neutral at -2%.

promoter share from 14% to 24%. However, the direction of the relationship remains unclear. Economic pessimism may 
fuel AI skepticism, concerns about AI may weigh on confidence, or both may reflect broader anxieties about the future.

Beyond the immediate economic outlook, confidence in generational progress is also fading. Only 18% of 
respondents expect the next generation to be better off than today, while 50% expect it to be worse off, resulting in a 
net balance of -32%. Pessimism increases with age: The balance stands at -14% among Gen Z but falls to -43% among 
Boomers. The generation whose future is at stake is thus substantially more optimistic than older cohorts. The US stands 
out: Expectations for the next generation are close to neutral overall, and young Americans are the only group in the 
survey that expects future generations to be better off.
Doubts about generational progress go hand in hand with more negative views of AI. Only 18% of respondents 
expect the next generation to be better off than today, while half expect it to be worse off. Among respondents who 
expect the next generation to be worse off, 57% are AI detractors and only 13% promoters. Among those who expect 
an improvement, detractors and promoters are almost evenly matched, at 36% and 33%, respectively (Figure 9). This 
translates into a roughly 40pp difference in net AI sentiment, from -44% among generational pessimists to close to 
neutral (-4%) among optimists. The relationship holds across countries, although national differences remain significant. 
Among those expecting generational progress, net AI sentiment is positive in the US (+34%), the UK (+23%) and Spain 
(+3%), but remains negative in Germany, France and Austria.

AI is likely to reshape far more jobs than it destroys, but the scale of labor-market adjustment will still be 
substantial. Our estimates suggest that AI could affect 23% of jobs across the major advanced economies over the next 
one to three years³.  The estimate is built bottom-up from more than 800 occupations, which we classify according to 
four possible AI impacts: no exposure, automation, augmentation or reorganization. We aggregate these occupation-
level effects into sectoral AI-exposure scores and then to the country level, taking into account sectoral employment 
shares as well as structural factors such as AI adoption and preparedness, labor-market regulation and unit labor 
costs. Job reorganization is the largest channel, accounting for 10% of jobs, as workers remain in their roles but AI takes 
over or changes some of their tasks. A further 8% of jobs face a higher risk of automation, while 5% could benefit from 
augmentation. Exposure across countries differs markedly, largely because of differences in their sectoral composition 
as well as structural factors, such as labor market regulations and the institutional context. In the US, where finance, 
ICT and other knowledge-intensive services account for a larger share of employment, 29% of jobs could be affected, 
compared with 15% across the major European economies. Within Europe, the share ranges from 18% in the UK and 16% 
in Germany to 14% in France, 12% in Spain and just 9% in Italy. The US therefore faces greater near-term disruption, but is 
also better-positioned to capture the productivity and employment gains from AI. 
The first effects are likely to appear in hiring and job composition rather than in a sudden rise in aggregate 
unemployment. Early evidence already points in this direction: more AI-intensive sectors in the Eurozone have seen 
weaker employment growth since late 2022, while younger and less-experienced white-collar workers in routine 
cognitive occupations appear particularly exposed. AI may therefore initially show up through fewer entry-level 
positions, slower hiring and weaker wage growth in exposed occupations rather than large-scale layoffs. The main 
medium-term risk is a transition gap: firms can reorganize and automate tasks faster than workers can move into newly 
created roles. Around 25mn jobs across the US and the major European economies are estimated to be subject to 
automation risk, while new AI-related employment will only partly offset displacement in the near and medium term. 
Over the longer term, we expect both substantial productivity gains and a moderately negative impact on 
employment, but the gains are likely to be larger in the US than in Europe (Figure 12). Our ten-year estimates assume 
that the AI transition follows a so-called J-curve, with investments and costs upfront, while the gains will only be realized 
later⁴. AI-related investment already adds around 0.4pp to US GDP growth in 2026, but the immediate productivity gains 
are limited as they get absorbed by reorganization costs and labor-market adjustment. By 2030, US GDP is projected 
to grow  +0.6% faster annually, while employment will be 1.5% lower relative to a world without AI. Thereafter, the 
productivity dividend becomes more visible: by 2036, cumulative labor productivity is around +4.8% above the no-AI 
baseline in the US, while the employment effect largely unwinds to -0.1%, equivalent to roughly -170,000 jobs. In Europe, 
productivity gains are more muted and employment effects more persistent: employment remains below the no-AI 
baseline across the major economies, reaching around -2.9% in Spain (-650,000 jobs) and -3.1% in Italy (-740,000 jobs), 
while Italy records the smallest productivity gain at around +1.8%. Germany and France also remain below baseline 
on employment, although by less than Spain and Italy. However, these employment losses will unfold against a rapidly 
shrinking workforce in the ageing European societies: by 2036, labor supply is set to decline by 5.6mn workers in 
Germany, 4.2mn in Italy and 2.3mn in Spain. Europe therefore faces a double labor-market challenge from demographic 
change and AI. The silver lining is that AI may partly compensate for a shrinking workforce in aggregate, but not 
one-for-one, because AI-related displacement will not necessarily occur in the same occupations, sectors and regions 
where labor shortages emerge. Overall, the US combines the highest near-term AI exposure with the smallest long-run 
employment loss, reflecting faster adoption, stronger investment and more effective reallocation. Europe, by contrast, 
faces a more persistent employment drag and smaller productivity gains, but part of the AI-related displacement could 
help cushion the effects of ageing.

Governments have taken significantly different

more, but to regulate what matters without stifling

For Europe, the central challenge is not simply to accelerate AI adoption, but to turn widespread experimentation 
into productive, inclusive and trusted use Curiosity is not the main constraint: Europeans have embraced AI almost as 
widely as Americans, but the technology has made fewer inroads into the workplace, and workers are less prepared to 
use it effectively. The priority is therefore to remove the barriers that prevent AI from translating into productivity gains 
and broader economic benefits. This requires action on five fronts: 
	1. Move AI from private experimentation into productive workplace use. General-purpose technologies like 
AI generate productivity gains only after firms make large complementary investments. These investments 
show up as costs today, dragging down earnings and productivity in the short run, the so-called productivity 
J-curve. Because the costs are immediate while the benefits are delayed, private investment may fall short, 
in particular for SMEs, which face disproportionately high costs for the same implementation effort⁸. Europe 
should reduce these costs that are holding back implementation, especially for SMEs, through accelerated 
depreciation or temporary tax credits for identifiable complementary investments such as software integration, 
data infrastructure, cybersecurity and process redesign. Public support should be tied to actual deployment 
in business processes and, where possible, measurable productivity or efficiency gains rather than generic AI 
subscriptions.
	2. Close the training gap by starting with employers. This is primarily a provision problem: many workers 
receive no offer of AI training, while most of those offered it take it up or plan to do so. Employer-led training 
should therefore be supported through training tax credits, public co-financing and individual learning accounts, 
with a focus on occupation-specific skills and groups currently least served including women, mid-career workers, 
the secondary-educated and employees in smaller firms. Europe already has useful instruments: Germany’s 
“Qualifizierungschancengesetz” subsidizes training costs and, in some cases, wage costs for employees affected 
by technological change, while the EU  encourages member states to develop individual learning accounts that 
give workers portable training entitlements. Where firms receive public support for major AI investments, this 
could be linked to a workforce-training plan.
	3. Shift labor-market policy from protecting jobs to supporting transitions. Because AI is likely to change tasks 
and occupations more extensively than it eliminates jobs outright, governments need to identify adjustment 
pressures before they translate into unemployment. Combining occupation-level AI-exposure indicators 
with vacancy, wage and skill-demand data can help identify vulnerable workers and emerging employment 
opportunities. Workers in exposed occupations should have access to retraining while still employed, stronger 
job-matching services, mobility grants and temporary wage insurance where moving into a new role initially 
means lower pay. Such measures must go beyond traditional active labor-market programs, whose short-term 
effects are often limited and vary considerably across program types and participants. Better targeting is crucial:

	4. Make sure the productivity dividend is broadly shared. AI is likely to raise productivity but could also 
shift national income from labor to capital, and if automation outruns capital accumulation, wages could fall 
even while output rises¹¹. Policy should therefore address both labor income and household wealth. On the 
income side, broader access to skills can help translate productivity gains into better employment prospects 
and wages. . On the asset side, funded pensions, employee share plans and wider household capital-market 
participation can help more people benefit directly from AI-driven returns, particularly in Europe, where  equity 
ownership is heavily concentrated among wealthier households¹². At the same time, the tax system should 
avoid unnecessarily favoring capital over labor and adapt if a larger share of income shifts toward capital and 
economic rents. Europe is particularly exposed here, as its tax systems rely more heavily on labor income taxes 
and social contributions than the US, potentially putting greater pressure on public finances if the labor share of 
income declines¹³. 
	5. Strengthen trust by targeting the risks people actually identify. Misinformation, data misuse and labor-
market disruption point to different policy needs, so regulation should be targeted rather than generic: 
provenance and disclosure standards for synthetic content, clear rules and effective enforcement for data use, 
and human oversight and appeal rights for high-impact AI decisions. Rules should be credible and proportionate: 
safeguards that build trust can support adoption, while complex or inconsistent requirements can slow diffusion, 
especially for smaller firms. EU-wide templates, sector-specific guidance and regulatory sandboxes can help
