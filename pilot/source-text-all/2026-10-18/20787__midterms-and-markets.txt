Summary 
 The midterm elections could mean a shift from unified Republican government to divided 
government with a Democratic majority in the House of Representatives or/and the Senate. 
 In case of divided government, President Trump is likely to focus on ruling through his 
executive powers. He is also likely to test how far he can stretch these powers.  
 This means that in foreign policy, trade policy and immigration policy, Trump may continue 
his current course. In contrast, fiscal policy is likely to end up in gridlock. 
 Since markets have priced in Divided Government, a Republican sweep would have a major

fiscal policy measures. 
Introduction 
The midterm elections take place on November 3. The entire House of Representatives is on the 
ballot, 35 out of 100 Senate seats, and a range of state and local officials. Crucially, the President 
is not on the ballot. However, the party of the President tends to lose seats during midterm 
elections and given Trump’s declining approval rating this is also the most likely outcome this 
year. 
There are four possible outcomes for the midterm elections, depending on which party gets the 
majority in the Senate or the House of Representatives. However, since President Trump is not on 
the ballot, his veto power remains an obstacle to legislation from Congress that he disapproves 
of. This means that from the perspective of policy impact, the four election outcomes can be 
grouped into two: Divided Government (3 possible outcomes) and Unified Government (only if 
Republicans maintain control of both the Senate and the House of Representatives).  
More generally, Unified Government occurs if all three pillars of political power - the White 
House, the Senate and the House of Representatives - are controlled by the same party. Divided 
Government occurs if this is not the case.  
In this note we focus on the economic and market implications of the midterm elections. We look 
at the difference between a Divided Government election outcome and a Unified Government 
outcome for economic policy. We also address the nuances of the three versions of Divided 
Government. We try to establish what is currently priced in by the markets and what different 
election outcomes could mean for longer-term interest rates. 
Three forms of divided government 
If the Democrats manage to get a majority in the Senate or the House of Representatives, or both, 
the current balance of power will change when the new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027. 
This means that there will be a shift in the policies that will be proposed in Congress. However,

Faced with a (partially) unsupportive Congress, President Trump is likely to focus on ruling 
through his executive powers. He is also likely to test how far he can stretch these powers. This 
means that especially in foreign policy, trade policy and immigration policy, he may continue his 
current course. In contrast, since Congress has the power of the purse1, fiscal policy will become a 
major challenge. But this is true for both parties. Gridlock is the most likely outcome. At least this 
should limit additional government spending and tax cuts, but we don’t expect any serious effort 
to get the US public debt under control. This is also why we expect the term premium to remain a 
source of recurrent upward pressure on longer-term interest rates. For more details, please read 
our rates special Higher Ground.

Is there no difference between the three forms of Divided Government? When it comes to 
legislation, gridlock is the most likely outcome in all three cases. However, differences emerge 
when it comes to presidential appointments, congressional oversight and impeachment. 
The Senate and the House of Representatives have different non-legislative powers. The House of 
Representatives is tasked with congressional oversight and impeachment. The Senate is 
responsible for approving presidential appointments, such as cabinet members, judges and Fed 
governors. The Senate also judges whether impeachment by the House of Representatives should 
lead to the President’s removal from office.  
Therefore, it matters whether Democrats win a majority in the Senate or the House of 
Representatives. If they win the Senate, they can block Trump’s appointments. If they win the 
House, they can start investigations into the Trump administration and even impeachment 
proceedings. Needless to say that they can make President Trump’s life even more difficult in case 
of a Democratic sweep. Still, a two-thirds majority in the Senate is needed to remove the 
President from office after impeachment by the House (simple majority), so conviction is unlikely. 
For the Fed, a Democratic Senate would prevent Trump from getting loyalists into the Board of 
Governors if one of the current governors is forced out or steps down voluntarily. If the 
Republicans manage to hold on to their majority, this option is still open to the President because 
only a simple majority in the Senate is needed. 
To summarize, while fiscal policy legislation will be gridlocked in all three cases of Divided 
Government, the type of opposition available to the Democrats depends on which chamber of 
Congress they will control. Obviously, a Democratic sweep will give them the tools of both 
chambers.

Unified government 
Since there is no presidential election this year, Unified Government is only possible if the 
Republicans sweep both the Senate and the House of Representatives. This will extend the status 
quo. The two years of Republican control have led to the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) and 
with a continuation of Unified Government, the door to new fiscal policy measures remains 
open. We could see additional tax breaks, further deregulation, more defense spending and some 
minor spending cuts. Entitlement programs are likely to be left alone. The net result is that the 
public debt trajectory is likely to shift further upward. Combined with pro-growth policies this 
means higher longer-term rates. A low probability is currently attached to this outcome, but it 
would have a large upward impact on longer-term rates.

Which outcome is priced in? 
From a financial markets perspective, it is important to find out what is currently priced in. 
Polymarket provides a clear probability distribution of the midterm election outcomes. The most 
likely outcome is a Democratic sweep (62%), followed by the Democrats getting a majority in the 
House, while the Republicans hang on to the Senate (29%). Combined this means a 91% 
probability that the Democrats gain a majority in the House of Representatives. A Republican 
sweep is assigned a small probability (9%). The Democrats winning the Senate without overtaking 
the House seems highly unlikely (1%).

Conclusion 
According to the polls, the Republicans are likely to lose their majority in the House of 
Representatives and even the Senate is not safe. The financial markets are likely to have priced in 
this election outcome by now, so a major market move after Election Day is more likely if the 
Republicans unexpectedly keep both their majorities. Since this would prevent gridlock and open 
the door to additional government spending, concerns about the public debt would probably rise 
again. At the same time, it would open the door to additional pro-growth policies. This could give 
longer-term yields another boost in November. In contrast, the expected outcome, divided 
government and gridlock, would limit additional fiscal stimulus. Nevertheless, any major attempt 
at getting US public finances in order will also remain elusive. In fact, market concern about the 
public debt may become the most persistent driver of high longer-term interest rates in the 
coming years.
