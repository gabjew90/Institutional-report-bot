Plus ça change… 
France’s deteriorating fiscal outlook and persistent political instability have pushed the 
spread between French government bonds and German Bunds to around 150 basis 
points, levels rarely seen outside periods of crises. Neither Marine Le Pen nor Jean-Luc 
Mélenchon have presented a credible fiscal adjustment programme, while Édouard 
Philippe’s more orthodox proposals face limited public support. Without decisive action, 
rising borrowing costs risk reinforcing concerns about debt sustainability and will likely 
trigger further spread widening. Ultimately, market pressures will force whoever succeeds 
Mr. Macron at the Élysée Palace to enact fiscal adjustments. 
In the meantime, event risk will remain high and renewed spikes in the French government 
spread appear likely. Long-dated French government bonds remain exposed to substan-
tial volatility, making a spread widening beyond 200 basis points a more attractive entry 
point. Short- to intermediate-maturity bonds already offer more compelling relative value, 
yielding around 100 basis points more than Bunds with considerably less risk than their 
long-duration counterparts. In currency markets, uncertainty is likely to keep the euro vol-
atile. Historical experience suggests that EURCHF remains the cleanest expression of euro 
area sovereign risk, with further downside pressure until a credible fiscal solution 
emerges. 
US banks have kicked off Q3 earnings this week. The beat on headline numbers conceals 
weakness under the hood. In particular, fixed income-related business lines came in 
softer than expected, such as FICC trading and debt capital markets. While loan loss pro-
visions remained low, a sustained rise in high-yield spreads may start to weigh on loan 
quality and lending conditions in the quarters ahead.

Le “Spread” makes the headlines again 
The current policy proposals laid out by Le Pen and Mélenchon are not credible and 
consistent enough and are unlikely to become much more granular until the elections. 
Event risk will remain high and renewed spikes in the French government spread to 
Bunds appear likely. Markets will ultimately force fiscal consolidation measures. For 
now, long-term OATs carry significant mark-to-market risk, hence we would only start 
building up positions on a further meaningful spike beyond 200bp. Short- to interme-
diate bonds, though, look attractive on a total return basis.  
 
The spread of French government bonds (OAT) to German Bunds has widened substan-
tially over the past weeks to around 150bp. This is a level that is still below the highs 
marked during the euro crisis 2010-2012, but extraordinarily high outside periods of cri-
ses. The difficult fiscal position of France is not new. Yet the inability of numerous govern-
ments following the dissolution of the National Assembly in June 2024 to carry out any 
reforms because of political instability is new. What’s more, it is not clear how a potential 
win by Marine Le Pen would change that dynamic. The final trigger for the sell-off was the 
sharp global rise in long-term real yields that exacerbated France’s problems (Exhibit 1). 
 
The French fiscal situation is not sustainable. The debt/GDP ratio has moved well above 
100%. To keep the debt/GDP ratio stable, given expected growth and refinancing costs 
for its debt, France would need a positive annual primary surplus of roughly 1.8%of GDP 
over the next years. This is the fiscal balance before interest rate payments. Current IMF 
estimates for 2027 forecast a primary deficit of 2.7%, which gives an idea of the magni-
tude of the task any new government will be confronted with (Exhibit 2). 
 
 
Marine Le Pen from the “Rassemblement National (RN)” currently leads the polls by a 
large margin, followed by Jean-Luc Mélenchon from the left-wing “La France Insoumise 
(LFI)” and Édouard Philippe, centre-right and close to Macron. Le Pen has promised a bal-
anced primary budget within 18 months and to bring the overall deficit to below 3% by 
2030. Her main levers are restrictions to benefit eligibility for immigrants, capping 
France’s contributions to the EU, cutting public services expenditure and seeking savings 
from efficiency gains in the pension system. Details for many of these measures remain 
pending, with doubts about the attainability of most targets. Mélenchon doesn’t plan to

Without a credible attempt at consolidating the deficit, French bond spreads risk widening 
even more, which would be a headwind for economic growth, adding to concerns about 
debt sustainability. Ultimately, this could degenerate into a vicious cycle with systemic 
risks that only central bank intervention, or the prospect for it, would be able to stop. The 
current policy proposals laid out by Le Pen and Mélenchon, so far at least, are neither 
credible nor consistent, and they are unlikely to become much more granular until the 
elections. Polls even put Le Pen against Jean-Luc Mélenchon in the second round of the 
presidential elections as the most likely outcome. Therefore, event risk and volatility will 
remain high and renewed spikes in the spread appear likely. 
 
Political instability and a lack of a coherent policy have historically caused multiple periods 
of sharp spread widening in the euro area. Central bank intervention, or the prospect for 
it, have ultimately stabilised the situation, but only in exchange for painful and forced 
structural reforms. The institutional framework in the euro area has evolved since then, 
but it also provides central bank support only in exchange for compliance with the EU fiscal 
framework. Markets and the ECB will ultimately force some form of fiscal consolidation 
from a new government as excessive spreads and yields are not economically viable.  
 
The good news is that the bar for positive surprises is relatively low. After an extended period 
of inaction, credible efforts to consolidate the fiscal situation will likely be rewarded by markets, 
even if they are not overly ambitious. France is unlikely to default and unlikely to exit the euro. 
Consequently, the spread to Bunds should settle at lower levels than today once we are past 
the current episode. However, it is not clear what degree of pain it will take to force the nec-
essary fiscal adjustments. Past episodes with Italian bonds during crisis times suggest 
that we may have to reach higher spread levels first (Exhibit 3). 
 
 
After the recent repricing, France is cheap compared to other euro area credits. It trades 
like a BBB credit (Exhibit 4). Consequently, the rating evaluations by Moody’s and S&P in 
October and November should not cause too much excitement, even if Moody’s were to 
downgrade France to A1. Nevertheless, long-term OATs carry significant mark-to-market 
risk. Hence, we would only start building up positions on a further meaningful spike be-
yond 200bp. Short- to intermediate bonds, though, are more attractive. They yield 100bp 
more than Bunds with much less volatility than their long-term counterparts.

France’s budget: A replay of Italy's 2018 crisis? 
France’s current fiscal difficulties resemble Italy's 2018 budget crisis. Yet the political 
dynamics differ considerably. While Italy’s government was in a dispute with the Eu-
ropean Commission, France needs to achieve a compromise in its fragmented political 
landscape. This may prove to be much harder. We expect the euro’s near-term path to 
remain volatile as a result and stick to our conviction that EURCHF represents the 
cleanest way to express fiscal risk in the euro area. 
 
France’s renewed budget turmoil has revived concerns about fiscal sustainability and po-
litical fragmentation within the euro area. The recent developments have weighed on the 
euro, which has already been exposed to significant cyclical headwinds. While we are cog-
nisant of the pitfalls and difficulties of historical comparisons, we believe that the 2018 
Italian budget crisis may provide some indication of where France’s current budget crisis 
may be headed and what it could imply for the euro’s broader dynamics. 
 
The outcome of Italy’s General Election in March 2018 saw the anti-establishment Five 
Star Movement and the right-wing League forming a coalition government under PM 
Conte. Both parties had campaigned on promises to reverse previous fiscal restraint, in-
cluding tax reductions, additional social transfers and more generous pension arrange-
ments. But with public debt exceeding 130% of GDP, the government’s fiscal ambitions 
quickly brought it into conflict with European budget rules. The 10-year BTP-Bund yield 
spread, i.e., the yield spread between 10-year Italian and German government bonds, wid-
ened substantially in May, with the euro following suit. In particular, EURCHF showed a 
very close correlation over the coming months. The widening of the BTP-Bund yield spread 
to around 300bp from around 130bp in the beginning of May coincided with a drop in 
EURCHF to around 1.14 from around 1.19 (Exhibit 1). 
 
 
With the Fed continuing on its monetary tightening path and the euro area’s cyclical mo-
mentum weakening relative to the US during the second half of 2018, broader macro driv-
ers reasserted themselves. As a result, the correlation between the 10-year BTP-Bund 
yield spread and EURCHF started to loosen already before the confrontation peaked in 
October 2018, when the European Commission rejected Rome’s draft 2019 budget with 
a deficit target of 2.4% of GDP (substantially above the previous government’s target of 
around 0.8%). In December, Rome eventually reduced its 2019 deficit target from 2.4% to

France’s current fiscal difficulties share several characteristics with the Italian episode. 
Elevated public debt, doubts about fiscal credibility and political uncertainty have contrib-
uted to a reassessment of sovereign risk. With French public debt above 119% of GDP and 
the deficit expected to remain substantially above the EU’s 3% threshold, investors are 
increasingly questioning the government’s ability to stabilise public finances. The widen-
ing of the 10-year OAT-Bund yield spread reflects these concerns, although the magnitude 
of the repricing remains smaller than during Italy’s 2018 crisis (Exhibit 2). 
 
In spite of some similarities, the underlying political dynamics differ considerably. Italy 
sought to expand fiscal spending in defiance of EU budget commitments, while the French 
government’s drive to reduce its deficit lacks a stable parliamentary majority to implement 
the necessary measures. The proposed 2027 budget envisages reducing the deficit from 
an estimated 5.4% of GDP in 2026 to 5.0% in 2027. Yet both fiscal restraint and higher 
taxes face fierce parliamentary opposition. The nearing 2027 Presidential Election repre-
sents an additional obstacle as it reduces incentives for compromise. 
 
These distinctions may be crucial to financial markets. While Italy’s confrontation could 
ultimately be resolved through a relatively narrow agreement between Italy’s parliamen-
tary majority and the European Commission, France’s challenge appears to be more com-
plex as the parliament itself appears to be the primary obstacle. Even if the government 
succeeds in adopting the budget through political concessions or by invoking Article 49.3, 
doubts about the credibility of its medium-term fiscal adjustment are likely to persist. 
 
In consequence, we expect French sovereign risk premia to remain elevated for somewhat 
longer. The fragmentation of the French political landscape complicates finding political 
compromise and hence the euro’s path is likely to remain volatile in the near term. Ulti-
mately, we expect increasing pressure from the bond market and EU institutions to force 
a solution and ease downward pressure on the currency in 2027. FX option-implied vola-
tility skew reflects that the market has positioned for further euro weakness and is partic-
ularly pronounced for the EURUSD, EURCHF and EURJPY pairs (Exhibits 3 and 4). Given 
historical sensitivities, we stick to our conviction that in FX, EURCHF likely represents the 
cleanest way of expressing fiscal risks in the euro area.

US equities 
Banks’ Q3 reporting is a mixed bag 
Banks kicked off US Q3 reporting this week. While headline numbers managed to beat 
consensus expectations, there was noticeable weakness beneath the surface. Fixed 
income trading and the capital markets business disappointed, while equity trading 
held up well and loan loss provisions remained contained. This may change in light of 
rising credit spreads and could eventually start to weigh on banks’ willingness to lend.  
 
The US earnings season kicked off this week. JP Morgan (JPM), Citigroup (Citi), Bank of 
America (BofA), Morgan Stanley (MS), Goldman Sachs (GS) and Wells Fargo (WF) all re-
leased their numbers, with a mixed reception by markets (Exhibit 1).  
 
 
Headline numbers appeared reasonably strong. Apart from BofA and JPM, all others beat 
consensus earnings expectations for the quarter. This helped to lift yoy EPS growth for the 
US financials sector to 6.3%, against a consensus of -3.3% ahead of the season. Yet this 
is largely a function of earnings downgrades ahead of the season. Sequentially, financials 
EPS have fallen from Q2 (Exhibit 2). While seasonality may be strong at times and quarterly 
growth numbers need to be taken with a pinch of salt, Q3 is the second quarter in a row 
with EPS coming off. Looking at the annual growth numbers, it has become evident that 
financials earnings growth has been slowing after a bumper start to the year.

The reason for the slowdown can be assigned to earnings components other than net in-

has continued to provide support for NII, the corresponding rise in credit spreads has

FICC trading revenues dropped across the board, with the exception of Citi. The rise in

while equity trading revenues grew strongly across all major banks (Exhibit 5). The same

only experienced a drop at MS, while other major banks still saw their ECM business per-

Germany’s medium-term fiscal stance: unsustainable 
• The German Stability Council forecasts that Germany will not comply with the Euro-
pean deficit and debt criteria in the medium term under current spending plans as 
its new report, published this week shows. 
• Often perceived as the fiscal stability anchor in the euro area, it is often overlooked 
that Germany’s debt ratio is increasing quickly as well. 
• While the EU accepts exceptions from its deficit criteria for higher defence spending 
until 2028, that is not the case from 2029 on. Apparently, Germany is relying on an 
extension of those exceptions by planning with deficits of 4.5% of GDP in 2029 and 
2030 which would bring the debt ratio above 78% of GDP from 62.8% in 2025.  
• Even with current spending plans it will be difficult to achieve a deficit of 4.5% as 
higher bond yields will increase debt service costs and the deficit projection relies 
on unspecified spending cuts of EUR 150 billion. 
• It is not only France that needs to consolidate its government finances, Germany 
and almost every other EU country have to reconsider spending priorities as well.
