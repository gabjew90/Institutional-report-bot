US Week In Review 
Consumer prices for September came in hotter than anticipated with the CPI headline reading 
0.6% m/m (3.7% y/y) and the core registering 0.33% m/m (2.6% y/y). This was the second straight 
month of higher inflation readings, and unlike in August, September can not be chalked up to just 
a few items that jumped. Price pressures were broad-based, with core goods and housing higher 
than in the prior month, and the supercore index a touch lower, but still too hot. Read out entire 
recap HERE.  
Inflation moved in the wrong direction in September  
 
Source: Natixis, Bloomberg 
We had said that anything near consensus would not likely shift the narrative for the Fed for 
October, but this is far enough from consensus to at least make it a debate. Wholesale prices 
for September were also hotter than expected. In recent weeks, influential policymakers 
(namely NY Fed President John Willliams and Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson) have suggested 
that there is no urgency to hike in consecutive meetings, which is a belief we think the broader 
FOMC still holds, and Chair Warsh did nothing to refute when he spoke at the IMF meetings. 
 
Combining CPI and PPI, we are tracking the PCE deflator at around 0.3% m/m, which would be 
disappointing, but not bad enough to warrant an immediate response via another hike from the 
Fed. September’s data gives the hawks more ammo to argue for a hike, but ultimately we think 
the Fed will pause when it meets in a few weeks 
 
Retail sales rose more than expected in September, rising 0.8% m/m, but was primarily driven 
by higher gas prices. The retail sales control group, which paints a clearer picture of underlying 
demand, fell short of expectations coming in with a 0.1% gain on the month. The Fed’s Beige 
Book suggested that higher-income consumer continue to drive spending as lower-income 
Americans have become more cautious with purchases.

The NFIB Small Business Optimism Index fell 0.8 points to 97.9 in September, below consensus 
expectations of 98.2 and below its 10-year average of 98.5. Of the 10 components, three rose, 
six fell, and one was unchanged, with the biggest drags coming from softer expectations as the 
net share expecting the economy to improve over the next six months fell 4 points, while the share

further price increases over the next three months, while the share citing inflation as their single

US Rates Strategy 
Treasuries staged an impressive rally this week in the face of bad inflation data and a silent 
Warsh, with the market trading well in the face of negative news for the first time in months.  We 
have written over the past two weeks that we were seeing signs the market was attempting to put 
in a floor, and that front end pricing approaching four hikes over the next year was acting as a 
soft ceiling and one that we wanted to lean against given: (a) our house call of one more hike, (b) 
that inflation is not re-accelerating merely not falling as fast as the Fed would prefer, (c) that a 
funds rate at or near 5% isn’t appropriate for the current backdrop, and (d) that historically 
“corrective” cycles tend to not to be 125bp adjustments. In that vein, last week we recommended 
scaling into 2yr notes starting at 4.83%, adding up to 4.95%.  Given the post-CPI selloff, these 
levels were hit and we now stand with an average entry of 4.88%. 
Clearly the market’s rebound is based primarily on flight to quality with concerns centered around 
AI financing.  What is interesting in this current environment is that the benefit is actually to the 
long end of the Treasury market, not the traditional front end flight to quality bid, because if 
investors are increasingly shy about accumulating AI-related debt (perhaps with their current 
bonds performing poorly), then the AI-supply related argument for higher Treasury yields due to 
crowding out loses some of its strength.  The idea that long end supply may not be as high in the 
near term as feared turbocharged the flattening trend that was already beginning with investors 
considering potential 20yr supply changes (more on this below), and in our view this is also why 
longer dated swap spreads outperformed this week. Lastly, if the AI buildout stalls, this could have 
growth implications, but it is probably premature to take this idea too far at this juncture.  
As noted, the flattening trend began even before the stronger inflation data and AI fears began, 
and we attributed much of this to expectations surrounding potential supply changes that could 
be signaled in Friday’s dealer survey from the US Treasury. While this piece will be published 
before the release of the survey, we felt it worthwhile weighing in on the debate on whether to 
reduce the size of the 20yr auction or eliminate it entirely.  
Our position is that when it comes to influencing the overall level of yields in the long end of the 
curve, any benefit to cutting or eliminating 20yr issuance will likely be fleeting at best.  Readers 
know that since the Treasury increased buybacks in that sector with Secretary Bessent expressly 
communicating it was to influence yields rather than to assist liquidity in that sector (the latter 
which is a reasonable and valid argument), we felt that any benefit to the long end would be 
outweighed by an increase in term premium with the Treasury becoming irregular and 
unpredictable.  We also expressed at the time that mathematically the Treasury’s action was 
influential regarding the flow of supply/demand (with near zero net issuance in the 20yr sector 
after the buybacks were increased), but with a stock of ~$5.5tn in 10yr+ maturities outstanding, 
the change in supply/demand dynamics was a drop in the bucket.  
Clearly cutting 20yr issuance entirely, or reducing it significantly, would have a much larger 
influence than the smaller action of raising buyback sizes (and it would have a cumulative impact, 
with net supply in that sector turning negative). We do not doubt that it would cause a rally in the 
long end, and a flattening of the yield curve.  Our concern is whether the market reaction will last.  
The best example of a similar action is the elimination of the bond in October of 2001.  The 
elimination was a surprise (we could argue that while a current elimination of the 20yr is not fully 
priced in, it would probably not be a “surprise”), and 30yr yields fell 40bps over the next two days.  
However, within two weeks, 30yr yields returned to their pre-announcement level.

LatAm Highlights 
Latin America – Super El Niño is not kidding around 
Latin America’s exposure to El Niño is highly uneven. The Pacific coast of South America is most 
exposed to flooding and fisheries disruption, Central America and the northern part of South 
America are more vulnerable to drought and heat, while the southern part may benefit from higher 
rainfall, despite higher flood risk. 
Overall, Peru emerges as the most exposed economy across Latin America combining direct 
Pacific coastal exposure with heavy dependence on exposed industries (notably fishing and 
agriculture), while Colombia is also highly exposed with drought affecting both agricultural output 
and energy production, and its hydropower dependence is likely to turn the climate shock into 
energy and food inflation. 
Argentina stands out as a beneficiary, as increased rainfall can support agricultural production 
and exports. The outlook for Brazil and Chile is more uncertain, with heterogeneous regional 
effects making the aggregate impact ambiguous. Mexico appears comparatively less exposed to 
El Niño given its geographical position. To read the full article, click HERE.

  Brazil – Long BRL still looks attractive 
After the first round of the presidential election on October 4, the BRL appreciated to 4.95 from 
5.22. Since then, it has been depreciating, and as of yesterday it closed at 5.09. What is going 
on? While we concede that the election might be getting a bit tighter, we still think that F. 
Bolsonaro is the favorite to win. Some polls suggest that Bolsonaro’s lead has been narrowing 
and is now around 2%, according to a recent Poder360 survey published on October 15. We 
argue that it is very unlikely, although not impossible, to see a huge increase in support for Lula 
from voters who backed a candidate other than Bolsonaro or Lula in the first round. The bulk of 
these votes should go to Bolsonaro unless something unexpected happens.

To assess the second-round outcome, we used Quaest’s poll on second-round vote intentions 
among voters who supported neither Lula nor F. Bolsonaro in the first round. According to Quaest, 
Augusto Cury’s voters would split 25% to Lula and 41% to F. Bolsonaro, with 27% indicating 
blank/null or abstention and 7% undecided. Renan Santos’s voters would split 18% to Lula and 
50% to F. Bolsonaro, with 26% blank/null or abstention and 6% undecided. Ronaldo Caiado’s 
voters would split 20% to Lula and 46% to F. Bolsonaro, with 32% blank/null or abstention and 
2% undecided. Romeu Zema’s voters are the only bloc in which Lula holds a lead, with 38% 
indicating Lula, 32% F. Bolsonaro, and 30% blank/null or abstention. 
The not-so-hidden ace up Lula’s sleeve — passing a law that would shorten the workweek to 40 
hours from 44 — actually materialized. However, Bolsonaro and the right-leaning bloc in 
Congress also supported it. Thus, it is not clear to us that it will shift voting preferences in favor 
of Lula before the second round on October 25. 
Then what explains the weak performance of the BRL? In part, we think there was some profit-
taking after the furious rally following October 4. Also, the dollar has been rallying. Since global 
yields started to rise, the DXY has increased by 3.80%. As a result, LatAm FX has traded poorly, 
and that needs to be reflected in the BRL, even if the market is being dominated by election risk. 
However, in recent sessions, rates have eased somewhat and the dollar has weakened a bit. We 
also believe that F. Bolsonaro remains the favorite to win. Our pre-election survey suggests that, 
in such a scenario, the BRL could appreciate to between 4.80 and 4.90.  
In summary, we still like the BRL and believe that F. Bolsonaro is the favorite to win, as continues 
to be reflected in the betting markets. However, not even the BRL is immune to the correction 
linked to the strengthening of the greenback, which is sinking all boats in LatAm.
