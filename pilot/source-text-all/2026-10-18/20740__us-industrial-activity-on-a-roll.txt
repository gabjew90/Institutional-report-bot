US industrial activity on a roll
US manufacturing is performing strongly with output gains led by
advanced technology production, pharmaceuticals, chemicals and
energy. However, more commoditised production, where it is difficult to
justify paying extra for a 'made-in-America' premium, is struggling
US manufacturing is gaining momentum, especially in terms of technology and artificial intelligence
Manufacturing is gaining momentum
Today has seen the release of another decent US industrial production report.
September output rose 0.7%MoM/2.2%YoY plus there was a 0.1ppt upward revision to August's
growth rate (consensus was looking for a 0.4% gain overall). Manufacturing did OK, rising 0.3%
month-on-month, especially when we consider that auto volumes fell 3%, while utilities
surged 3.5% MoM as output is ramped up to meet demand from data centres and expanding
manufacturers. Mining was up only 0.2% MoM.
The sector has struggled in recent years with stagnant output, but the business surveys have
all been pointing to an improving picture. The chart below shows the relationship between
manufacturing output and the ISM manufacturing series, with the latter suggesting further
decent gains are likely. The labour market data tells a similar story with 72,000 manufacturing
jobs added year to date, after three consecutive years of net job losses in 2023, 2024 and
2025. Average weekly hours worked in the sector are at a 3.5-year high of 40.6.

Tariffs, tech and energy security are all supporting
So why is US manufacturing doing so well? Tariffs on foreign-made products and energy
security do boost the attractiveness of manufacturing in the US. But so does US economic
growth continuing to outperform other key markets. Between 2023 and 2026, the US
economy has grown an average of 2.5% year-on-year in volume terms versus 0.9% in Europe.
At the same time, the US Administration believes maintaining the lead in the AI/technology
frontier is imperative for both economic and national security, given competition from China.
Rapid AI adoption is leading to robust construction of data centres, boosting electricity
demand and spurring investment across the power supply chain, from generation and
transmission to electrical equipment.
As we can see in the chart below, high value-added or advanced manufacturing, within which
we group tech, electronics, transport and power-related sectors, has significantly
outperformed other sectors. We expect this story to continue. We also see defence as a sector
that should continue to post strong growth. As more countries increase military spending, US
companies may benefit from strengthening order books.

Traditional, labour-intensive, commoditised manufacturing sectors continue to shrink under
pressure from foreign competitors. Here, tariffs are not enough to provide the competitiveness
boost needed to offset the high cost of production in the US. These US producers are also often
detrimentally impacted in foreign markets by retaliatory tariffs.
Bifurcation set to intensify
As we outlined in our latest manufacturing outlook , highly automated, high-value-added
sectors at the forefront of the AI/technology revolution will continue to perform strongly. The
desire to incorporate AI advances should, in theory, drive advances in productivity-enhancing
initiatives that further support innovation. As such, pharma, tech, transport & aerospace,
electrical and power-related sectors should continue to grow robustly. Lower value-added
production, where labour costs make up a greater share of the overall cost base of production,
will continue to struggle unless it can garner a 'made-in-America' premium.
