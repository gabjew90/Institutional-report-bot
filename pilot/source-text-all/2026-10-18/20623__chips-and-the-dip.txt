CITI'S TAKE 
We remain tactically neutral on duration in the near-term given that rates 
continue to flip flop between AI slowdown concerns and rising energy costs. 
But the post-CPI intraday risk-off rally in rates is supportive of our view that 
investors would still find Treasuries attractive as a “safe haven” asset in a 
meaningful growth slowdown. For now we continue to express our long 
duration bias by receiving in October OIS with the expectation of a pause at 
the next FOMC.
Inflation — We remain in 2y breakeven shorts as market pricing of ex-gasoline 
inflation over the next year remains too high unless the recent pickup in goods 
inflation continues.
Q4 refunding preview — Treasury asked dealers for feedback on long-end buyback 
operations and structural demand for coupons. We think these questions support 
our view that on November 4th Treasury will reduce bond auction sizes (20y and 30y 
UST) by $3bn each.

The intraday swings in rates after this week’s CPI upside surprise can be viewed as 
a fitting foreshadow of how rates and curve could reprice over the medium term.  
The initial post-CPI knee-jerk spike in yields (Tsy 2y sold off as much as 16bps and 
the curve bear flattened) quickly reversed as risky assets sold off, with 10y and 30y 
ultimately rallying on the day and the yield curve bull flattening (Figure 1). The 
growing concerns around the AI growth outlook also fueled the flight-to-quality 
long-end rally on the day, which was essentially a partial reversal of the AI-driven 
real yield sell-off from the prior month (Figure 2). But today’s rates sell-off amid 
higher oil price is a quick reminder that the market has not settled on a consistent 
macro driver yet. While it is difficult to tell if the ongoing AI de-risking is going to 
lead to a sustained rate rally, the post-CPI price action is at least supportive of our 
view that in a meaningful growth slowdown, investors would still find Treasuries 
attractive as a “safe haven” asset, especially at these elevated yields. At the same 
time, further Fed tightening would incrementally increase the risk of a slowdown, 
and this self-correcting dynamic should favor flatter front-end 2s5s and SOFR 
curves, as we previously discussed (US Rates Weekly - Only hawks outside the 
window).

Despite the upside surprise in CPI, we still believe that the Fed will opt to keep the 
policy rate unchanged at the October meeting and will look for affirmation in the 
next CPI report before possibly hiking again in December. This scenario would be 
consistent with our economists’ updated forecasts which now call for the next hike 
in December and Fed pause until a rate cut in June of next year (US Economics - 
December hike likely - core PCE 0.30%MoM). Hence, we are still receiving in 
October OIS with the market currently implying 15% chance of a rate hike (3.92% as 
of 1pm 10/16/2026, see US Rates Strategy - Widening the aperture). Barring a 
series of upside inflation surprises, we continue to believe that the Fed could afford 
to stay on hold again next January and stick with a cadence of quarterly rate hikes. 
If such a gradual pace of tightening is realized, then that should limit the bearish 
risk on belly rates and favor a flattening in the 2s5s curve.

Remain short breaks for now
At first glance, the core inflation data for September looked concerning as 
inflationary pressures seemed widespread. Inflation in all three major sub-
components of the core inflation basket has increased from last month. Both 
shelter and services ex-shelter inflation in September were greater than 0.3% and 
core goods prices increased by about 0.25% MoM. However, the details are much 
less alarming. While Owner’s equivalent rent (OER) inflation has increased to 
0.35% in September, we have seen such spikes in MoM OER inflation previously. 
There was hardly any stickiness to these spikes over the last year and 4m 
annualized inflation in both OER and Rent components has been very consistent 
and range bound over the past year (Figure 7). So, YoY shelter inflation is unlikely to 
increase much from its current levels of 3.2%. Having said that, the floor for shelter 
inflation might be higher than what was indicated by forward looking indicators 
like the New-tenant repeat rent index (NTRR). But historically, OER inflation has 
lagged changes in home prices by about 18 months. So, we might see some 
declines in shelter inflation in coming months despite plateauing over the last year.
Core services inflation was greater than 0.3% for the second straight month in 
September. While strength in August was due to one-time price increases in 
Wireless services, rental car prices spiked in September. It is unlikely that we’ll see 
continued strength in these components going forward and we expect services ex-
shelter inflation to return to 0.2% MoM pace in coming months.

In the years prior to the pandemic, neither shelter nor services ex-shelter inflation 
were below 2%. In fact, shelter inflation was consistently above 3% during that 
period. However, the key to below/at target core inflation back then was the lack of 
goods inflation. If core goods inflation remains below 1%, then overall core inflation 
can fall below 2.5% even with strong shelter and services inflation. That is what 
happened over the last year when ex-gasoline inflation declined 2.3%. That was 
the basis of our short 2y breakevens (hedged with gasoline futures) trade as market 
is currently pricing about 2.8% ex-gasoline inflation over the next year (2.6% as of 
9:15AM on 10/16/2026, see US Rates Strategy: Shorting 2y breakevens). However, 
core goods inflation was 0.25% in September and in the last 3 months, it averaged 
about 0.18% MoM. The increase in September was mainly driven by used car prices 
which will be declining based on Manheim used car price index. We remain in our 
short breakevens position for now as we think core goods inflation will revert back 
to <0.1% MoM pace dragging overall core inflation below 2.5% (Figure 8).

8:30am ET for the upcoming refunding meeting. The announcement provides 
guidance on coupon sizes over the next quarter (Nov/Dec/Jan). We expect Treasury

On TIPS we expect Treasury to maintain sizes unchanged for now. On the same 
day, TBAC, which is a group of buy- and sell-side participants, will also publish 
their statement and minutes of the discussion, we expand on the discussion 
questions below.

Dealers’ questionnaire: long-end buybacks and structural 
demand
The latest refunding questionnaire asked dealers two questions that we think 
could signal changes in coupon issuance ahead. First, Treasury asked for feedback 
on long-end buyback operations since the cap increase. Specifically, Treasury 
asked for feedback around the effect on liquidity and market functioning, as well 
as “changes in client interest in participating in buybacks” and whether Treasury 
should consider additional changes to the program. It has been our view that 
Treasury increased the caps to these operations to limit the long-end selloff (see  
Figure 10), and not for market functioning purposes (US Rates Strategy - Getting 
long 20y after today’s buyback announcement). For example, over the past year 
30y auctions have on average stopped through the WI level by -0.23bp, while 20y 
auctions have marginally tailed (0.02bp). At the same time, as we show in Figure 11, 
the first two buyback operations under the $6bn cap were not maxed out, likely 
because sellers were not willing to sell at a price that Treasury deemed cheap. 
The second question that we think supports our view of a potential shift in bond 
issuance asked dealers’ feedback on structural changes in investor demand and its 
near-term outlook as Treasury continues to “evaluate future changes to nominal 
coupon and FRN” sizes. Treasury also asked about “What futures changes to 
auction sizes should [they] consider, given expected borrowing needs and investor 
demand?”. In our view, the answers to this question together with the buyback 
feedback, will inform Treasury’s near-term coupon issuance decisions and 
potentially support a reduction in 20y and 30y auction sizes. 
Lastly, Treasury also asked for comments around the scheduling of bill and FRN 
auctions on FOMC days and whether these should be shifted to surrounding days. 
Treasury often requests comments on auction mechanics, for example at the 
February refunding they asked whether the 7y note should be converted from a 
monthly new issue to a quarterly new issue with two reopenings.

Updated deficit projections and T-bill supply
On Monday, November 2nd at 3pm, Treasury will release their financing estimates 
for this quarter (Oct, Nov, Dec) and next quarter (Jan, Feb, Mar) as well as their TGA 
balance targets. These financing estimates are for private funding needs. We 
continue to expect the TGA balance target to be between $850bn and $950bn, in 
line with what Treasury has previously stated in past refundings. On total financing 
needs we expect $594bn and $556bn for Q4-26 and Q1-27 respectively. In Figure 
12 we provide our monthly TGA and net T-bill issuance estimates. And in Figure 13 
we give our latest supply forecasts. 
Note that our 2027 estimates assume that the debt ceiling cap will be reached in 
H1-2027 and that Treasury will begin drawing down the TGA balance and 
implementing extraordinary measures by Q2-27 in order to fund the government. 
Nevertheless, there is still uncertainty around where the TGA will realize 
throughout 2027, for details around our views on debt ceiling please see US Rates 
Weekly - OAT of the blue. As a reminder, in previous debt ceiling episodes, Treasury 
has forecasted future quarters assuming Congress would increase or suspend the 
debt limit.
