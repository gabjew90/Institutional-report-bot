Drivers of G4 Term Premium 
 
 
Our estimates of term premium suggest that term premia have risen substantially over 
the last half decade, standing around 150-200bp above 2021 levels across the G41. With 
different drivers cited for the increase, we introduce a new modeling framework to 
identify what has pushed term premium so much higher (Exhibit 1). Our framework can 
explain around 70-80% of the recent increase, underlining the mostly fundamental 
nature of the selloff. That said, about 35-50bp remain unexplained in each market, 
pointing to room for long-end yields to move lower should the macro backdrop change 
and skew of risks shift. 
 
Theory suggests that greater macro uncertainty, especially around the path of growth, 
inflation, and policy, raise the duration risks that investors bear when holding 
longer-term over shorter-term bonds. Consequently, they demand higher term premium 
to hold that risk. Since the pandemic, dispersion across macro forecasts (which we use to 
measure uncertainty) has risen notably across the G4, reflecting the effects of higher 
inflation, shifting central bank approaches, geopolitical risks, and the implications of the 
AI buildout. 
At the same time, greater bond supply erodes the convenience value investors typically 
assign to sovereign bonds given the increased availability, in turn raising term premium. 
Persistently high deficits on top of already high debt stocks thus contributed to a 
supply-driven cheapening of bonds, while the unwinding of the pandemic- and 
QE-related central bank bond holdings exacerbated that effect.

Our model is intentionally sparse and aims to allocate shifts in term premium to macro 
drivers. But there are other, harder-to-measure factors that could help explain the 
residual in our model. 
First, our model does not account for global spillovers. This is a modeling choice, but 
when we include an (instrumented) global term premium variable, the fit of the model 
improves. 
Second, the shift in global savings and investment due to AI capex could be putting 
upward pressure on term premium by raising the sensitivity of longer-term yields to 
sovereign debt supply and raising overall debt supply. 
Third, the succession of supply shocks since the pandemic has weakened the (positive) 
correlation between growth and inflation. In turn, this has eroded the hedge value of 
duration in a balanced portfolio, visible in the increase in the equity-bond price 
correlation, for which investors should require higher term premium to compensate 
(Exhibit 5).  
 
Under our baseline of inflation moderation, amidst so far limited second-round effects, 
we expect macro uncertainty to gradually decline as a source of term premium. 
However, greater certainty on the path for energy prices seems to be a prerequisite for 
this, and so far it remains elusive. 
The increase in bond supply from central bank QT has likely passed its peak. The Fed has 
moved to stabilize its balance sheet, BoE switched to a more market-friendly approach, 
and risks of an ECB QT slowdown have also emerged. This suggests less upside risk to 
term premium from central bank balance sheet policy. 
Fiscal pressures are likely to remain a key source of upside risk to term premium in the 
future. The projected rise in debt-to-GDP in the US is the main reason our long-term rate 
forecasts drift higher over time. This risk may waver between acute and chronic phases, 
but this positive link between term premium and the level of rates will only moderate 
once deficit trajectories change the long-run fiscal outlook.
