How deep is your moat? 
 
Riding the AI capex wave: 12-month forward consensus hyperscaler capex expectations have risen from $330bn in 
early 2025 to $1tn, equivalent to 3% of US GDP. This leaves the ratio comfortably above the levels seen in previous 
major capex cycles (1870s railroad boom: 2.2%; 1990s tech boom: 1.1%). This surge in spending has had a profound 
impact on the macro and market environment: (a) booming global growth (with the global composite PMI new orders 
index at a five-year high of 54.2); (b) a surge in corporate profits, with 12-month forward global EPS momentum at a 
five-year high of 8% and global consensus margin expectations at an all-time high; (c) a powerful equity rally, with 
global equities doubling since 2022 and European equities rising by 66%, supported by strong global growth and 
continued earnings upgrades; and (d) more recently, a spike in bond yields, as global growth has shifted from merely 
resilient to outright boom-like. Against this backdrop, the US 10-year real bond yield has risen by 100bps since February 
to 2.7%, the highest level since 2002.  
How deep is your moat? So far, the current episode is following the classic capital cycle playbook: an exciting new 
technology triggers an investment boom, which then leads to an economic boom, an earnings boom (due to the timing 
mismatch between revenue recognition, which is immediate, and cost recognition, which is delayed as capex goes 
through the balance sheet and shows up on the P&L only gradually as depreciation) and a financial boom. The key 
question is whether it will continue to track this narrative, as most major capital cycles of the past have ended in 
disappointment, even though the underlying technology often lived up to expectations.  
The reason for these boom-and-bust dynamics is that the high profit margins on offer at the start of the cycle lead to 
overinvestment and excess capacity, which then weigh on pricing power and profitability, especially once depreciation 
charges start to kick in. Once these headwinds to profitability push the return on investment below the cost of capital, 
companies typically start to cut back on investment spending, which in turn feeds into a growth slowdown. Weakening 
corporate profits and slowing growth then typically translate into a sharp correction in the equity market. The one 
capital cycle that stands out as a positive exception, albeit on a smaller scale, is the cloud business of the 2010s, when 
the rise of software as a service led to a data-centre investment boom that was never followed by a bust. The main 
reason was not that the economics of this business were more favourable. Natural barriers to entry (including the 
complicated logistics involved and high switching costs for customers) meant that the field was left to a small number 
of participants. This prevented the build-up of excess competition that led to the loss of pricing power in the other 
cycles, thus ensuring strong profitability and more sustainable investment spending.  
The key lesson from historical experience, in our view, is that what matters for the sustainability of large-scale capex 
cycles is not the brilliance of the new technology that triggers them, but the economics of the new industry that 
emerges. More specifically, what matters are the barriers to entry that determine whether or not the industry ultimately 
suffers from deflationary pricing dynamics caused by excess competition, which lay at the root of the boom-and-bust 
dynamics seen in past large-scale capex cycles.  
Stress-testing Jevons Paradox: the main concern with the current AI boom is that investors initially believed that the 
emerging industry would evolve into a duopoly made up of the two leading US frontier labs. However, the DeepSeek 
moment early last year shook these expectations, as it showed that it was relatively easy and cheap to introduce near-

frontier rival models. Since then, the competitive pressures at the model layer of the AI stack have increased 
meaningfully, with a number of high-performing models entering the arena, especially Chinese open-weight models.  
The experience of past cycles suggests that this build-up of competitive pressures should lead to increased price 
competition. And this is indeed what we are seeing, with average token prices declining by around 50% since the 
summer. The bullish pushback invokes a version of Jevons Paradox, namely that lower token prices will stimulate 
demand, such that total spending on AI services can continue to rise, if the increase in token volumes outweighs the 
decline in prices. The problem is that the evidence suggests that this is not happening, with data from Ramp AI on US 
business spending on AI showing that token volumes continue to rise sharply, but that total spending has faded slightly 
since August, as the volume response has failed offset the decline in token prices. This monetization disappointment in 
the face of intense competition is precisely the failure mode seen in past capital cycles.  
Combined with the funding pressures induced by higher bond yields, growing monetization concerns amid signs of 
stagnating revenue growth have translated into renewed pressure on AI-related assets. The Korean KOSPI has declined 
by 20% since early October, leaving it below the trough levels reached during the momentum reversal in July, while the 
Philadelphia Semiconductor Index is down 13% and the Nasdaq down 5% from recent highs.  
How much risk is priced in? The benign macro backdrop created by the AI spending surge, marked by strong growth 
and elevated corporate earnings, helped to push risk premia in financial markets to multi-decade lows over the summer, 
with the global equity risk premium (ERP) reaching 2.7%, the lowest level since 2002, while the European ERP also 
reached a 24-year low of 4.2%, and the US high-yield credit spread fell to 260bps, the lowest level since 2007.  
The combination of increased AI jitters and rising rates has led to a re-pricing of risk in some parts of the market, most 
notably in: (a) French sovereign debt, with OAT spreads spiking to 140bps as rising rates exacerbate France’s fiscal 
challenges (see: European Economic Weekly, Oct 9); (b) CCC-rated US credit, where spreads have reached a four-year 
high of 1,250bps; and (c) highly leveraged balance sheets in the AI ecosystem, where spending has become increasingly 
debt-fuelled, with the CDS spread for CoreWeave reaching a new record high of 1,100bps. There has also been a 
moderate increase in spreads in the overall credit market, with the US high-yield credit spread reaching a six-month 
high of 320bps, leaving it up 60bps from the recent low.  
Yet, this re-pricing of risk has not yet meaningfully impacted the equity market, where ERPs remain close to recent 
lows. It is true that equities have de-rated, from a peak of 20.5x in January to 17.5x now for global equities, and from 
15.8x to 13.8x for European equities, but this has almost entirely been due to the drag from a higher risk-free discount 
rate (i.e. the mechanical effect of higher real bond yields) rather than to the increase in the risk premium. In other 
words, equity valuations have absorbed the risk-free-rate shock but have not yet responded to the re-pricing of risk 
premia. Thus, while pockets of financial markets have started to discount increased macro risks in response to rising 
rates volatility and a fragile AI capex cycle, very little of this risk appears to be priced into the equity market at the index 
level.  
We remain negative on European equities and underweight cyclicals versus defensives: despite rising concerns 
around the sustainability of the AI capex cycle, the Stoxx 600 remains within 3% of the recent all-time high, as 
continued earnings upgrades have largely offset the drag from rising real bond yields, while risk premia have remained 
close to recent lows. We see a further 10% downside for the market to 580 by Q2, as mounting AI monetisation 
concerns increasingly challenge the sustainability of the benign macro backdrop, weighing on earnings expectations and 
pushing up risk premia. On bond yields, our rates strategists argue that the path of least resistance in the near term
