Healthcare’s technology 
	 inflection point
Healthcare has long been driven 
by scientific breakthroughs, but a 
growing number of advances are 
now emerging from the intersection 
of medicine and technology. 
Artificial intelligence is helping 
researchers identify potential drug 
targets, genetic data is enabling 
more personalised treatments, 
and digital tools are changing how 
healthcare professionals diagnose 
and treat disease. At the same time, 
technologies originally developed for 
consumer applications are beginning 
to find their way into hospitals and 
operating rooms. 
What makes this moment significant 
is that many of these innovations are 
now moving beyond the experimental 
stage. Rather than remaining confined 
to laboratories or pilot projects, they 
are increasingly appearing in clinical 
trials, regulated medical products and 
real-world healthcare workflows. The 
result is not the replacement of doctors 
or scientists, but the augmentation 
of their capabilities through data, 
software and advanced computing. 
For investors, this creates opportunities 
across a much broader range of 
companies than traditional healthcare 
investing has historically offered. 
Personalised medicine 
reaches an important 
milestone
One of the clearest examples 
of technology’s growing role in 
healthcare is personalised cancer 
treatment. In August 2026, a leading 
pharmaceutical company and an 
mRNA biotechnology specialist 
reported positive Phase 3 results 
for an investigational personalised 
cancer therapy used alongside 
an established immunotherapy in 
patients with advanced melanoma 
following surgery. 
The treatment is notable because 
it is designed specifically for each 
patient. Genetic analysis of an 
individual’s tumour is combined 
with computational tools to identify 
potential targets for the immune 
system. This information is then used 
to create a personalised mRNA 
treatment tailored to the patient.

The approach represents a move 
away from the traditional model 
in which every patient receives the 
same treatment. Instead, advances 
in genetic sequencing, data analysis, 
and manufacturing are creating the 
possibility of therapies designed 
around individual biology. Important 
questions remain, including 
long-term survival outcomes 
and regulatory approval, but the 
positive Phase 3 data nevertheless 
mark a significant step forward for 
personalised medicine. 
AI’s next test:  
drug discovery
Drug development has traditionally 
been a slow, expensive and uncertain 
process. AI developers hope that 
advanced computing can help 
researchers identify biological targets, 
analyse vast datasets, and design 
potential medicines more efficiently. In 
July 2026, an AI-focused biotechnology 
company began a Phase 3 trial for an 
experimental treatment for idiopathic 
pulmonary fibrosis, with AI playing a 
key role in both identifying the target 
and designing the molecule. The 
programme progressed from target 
identification to drug-candidate 
selection in around eighteen months, 
an unusually rapid timeframe. 
Yet speed alone is not enough. The 
central question is whether AI-
designed molecules ultimately become 
safe, effective, and commercially 
successful medicines. While more 
than one hundred AI-enabled drug 
candidates have now entered clinical 
trials, most remain in the earlier stages 
of development. Encouragingly, the 
pipeline is becoming real, but definitive 
clinical proof is still emerging. 
The operating room 
becomes connected
Technology is also beginning to 
reshape healthcare delivery itself. 
In September 2026, a medical-
technology company announced 
the first surgical procedure using a 
mixed-reality visualisation system 
developed in partnership with 
a major consumer-technology 
company. The platform allows 
surgeons to view imaging data, 
surgical video and other information 
within their field of vision while 
maintaining awareness of the 
operating room around them. 
The concept is simple but potentially 
powerful. Instead of dividing attention 
between multiple screens, surgeons 
can access relevant information in 
a single integrated environment. 
Although it remains early in its 
development cycle, the technology 
represents one of the first examples 
of spatial computing entering a 
regulated healthcare setting.
More broadly, it highlights an 
important trend: the line between 
healthcare and the technology sector 
is becoming increasingly blurred. 
Future innovation may depend on 
partnerships that combine medical 
expertise and regulatory know-
how with advances in computing, 
software and hardware. 
A broader technological 
transformation
Beyond these headline examples, 
evidence of healthcare’s technological 
transformation can be seen across 
multiple areas. Studies of AI-supported 
mammography have shown both 
reduced radiologist workloads and 
improved cancer-detection rates. Gene 
therapy is demonstrating the potential 
to address the underlying causes 
of conditions previously managed 
through supportive care. Meanwhile, 
robotic blood-draw technology 
illustrates how automation could help 
healthcare providers address repetitive 
tasks and workforce shortages.

Technology is moving from promise to proof
Recent developments across healthcare show how broad this 
transformation is becoming:
63.6% lower radiologist workload
A prospective study of AI-supported mammography showed that 
technology can potentially reduce the amount of routine work 
required from radiologists.
15% higher cancer detection
The same study reported higher cancer detection compared with 
standard reading.1 
80% of evaluable patients improved their hearing
A gene therapy for inherited hearing loss demonstrated how genetic 
medicine may potentially treat the underlying cause of diseases 
previously managed mainly through devices or supportive care.2 
Robotics reaches routine healthcare tasks
FDA-authorized robotic blood-draw technology illustrates how 
automation could eventually help address repetitive tasks and 
shortages of healthcare workers. 3
Taken together, these developments 
suggest that healthcare is undergoing 
a broad digital transformation 
rather than experiencing a single 
breakthrough. Technology is 
increasingly touching every stage 
of the healthcare pathway, from 
drug discovery and diagnostics to 
treatment, workflow management 
and ongoing patient monitoring. 
What this means for investors
The investment implications extend 
well beyond a narrow group of “AI 
healthcare” companies. Potential 
beneficiaries include pharmaceutical 
and biotechnology firms, diagnostics 
providers, healthcare software 
companies, medical-device 
manufacturers and businesses 
supplying the infrastructure that 
supports them. 
Importantly, innovation is not 
confined to smaller biotechnology 
firms. Large pharmaceutical and 
medical-technology companies 
may also be well positioned 
as they can combine new 
technologies with extensive scientific 
expertise, regulatory experience, 
manufacturing capabilities, and 
established healthcare relationships. 
In many cases, the value of a 
technological breakthrough 
increases when it is paired with these 
existing strengths.
While investors must distinguish 
carefully between technological 
possibility and validated clinical 
value, the direction of travel is 
becoming increasingly clear. AI 
may help generate drug candidates 
and new computing platforms 
may improve clinical workflows, 
but success will still depend on 
regulatory approval, manufacturing, 
reimbursement, and real-world 
adoption. For companies that 
can successfully navigate these 
hurdles, however, technology has 
the potential to unlock meaningful 
competitive advantages and long-
term growth opportunities.
