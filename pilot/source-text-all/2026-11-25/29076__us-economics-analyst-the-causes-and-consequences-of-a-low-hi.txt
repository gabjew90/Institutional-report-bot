The Causes and Consequences of a Low-Hire, Low-Fire Labor Market 
 
 
The labor market has settled into a softer, low-hire, low-fire form of full employment. 
The hiring, quits, and layoff rates are all running below their pre-pandemic levels, a sign 
that both firms and workers are reluctant to make moves (Exhibit 1, left panel). 
This low turnover is the main reason that our slack tracker—a composite of ten 
measures of labor market tightness—signals a labor market that is somewhat softer 
than the unemployment rate alone suggests (Exhibit 1, right panel). Some Fed officials 
have also recently highlighted the unusually low level of labor market dynamism, raising 
the question of whether subdued turnover points to a less healthy labor market than the 
unemployment rate and payroll growth currently indicate.1 In this week’s Analyst, we 
examine what is driving the decline in worker turnover and assess how the decline in 
dynamism is affecting workers’ job-finding prospects and wages. 
 
Job Reallocation Continues to Edge Down but Is Only Modestly Below Pre-Pandemic 
Levels 
Worker turnover can be decomposed into job reallocation, the pace at which jobs are 
created and destroyed, and churn, the pace at which workers move between existing 
jobs. 
Exhibit 2 shows that job reallocation—the sum of the gross job gain and gross job loss 
rates in the Business Employment Dynamics (BED) data—has declined since the late 
1990s, a long-term trend we first wrote about over a decade ago. But the decline has 
slowed in recent years. Now that pandemic-era distortions have faded, the reallocation 
rate sits just 0.2pp below its 2015-2019 average, suggesting that lower reallocation has 
contributed only slightly to the recent slowdown in worker turnover.

Churn Is Now Responsible for Most of the Variation in Total Worker Turnover 
Churn measures worker flows between existing jobs, rather than hiring to expand or 
firing to shrink a firm’s workforce. The left panel of Exhibit 4 shows that it has driven 
almost all the variation in turnover since the early 2010s. While churn was extremely high 
in 2022, it is now almost 1pp below its 2015-2019 average, accounting for most of the 
decline in total worker turnover since the pre-pandemic period. 
Churn typically tracks the overall health of the labor market closely. But the right panel 
of Exhibit 4 shows that it has run unusually low since last year.

What can explain this depressed pace of churn? It likely reflects, in part, the continuation 
of two structural forces that have pushed churn lower in recent decades. The first is an 
aging workforce, as older workers switch jobs less often. The second, as our global 
economics team noted recently, is an improvement in the quality of worker-firm 
matches. Better matches mean fewer quits and layoffs shortly after the start of a job. 
Consistent with this, data from the Current Population Survey’s Displaced Worker 
Supplement show that jobs held for less than one year have accounted for a steadily 
declining share of employer-initiated separations from full-time positions since the 
1990s (Exhibit 5). 
 
But even accounting for these long-run trends, churn looks unusually low today, and we 
suspect this largely reflects two temporary factors.

Reduced Turnover Is Starting to Affect Labor Market Outcomes, Though Modestly So 
Far 
Why should we worry about low labor market dynamism in an otherwise stable labor 
market? We see two key reasons. 
First, a low-turnover labor market risks locking out the unemployed. We find that 
reduced turnover is starting to weigh on transitions back into employment, but only 
modestly so far. Using individual-level data from the Current Population Survey, we 
estimate that the job-finding rate of unemployed workers has fallen further below its 
pre-pandemic level in industries and states where churn has fallen more sharply (Exhibit 
8, left panel). As a result, unemployment spells have lengthened roughly twice as much 
in these lower-churn industries and states (Exhibit 8, right panel). 
While lower turnover typically hits younger workers hardest, we find little evidence that 
early-career workers have fared disproportionately worse recently.

Second, lower dynamism tends to hold back wage growth, as workers switch to 
better-paid jobs less frequently and accept smaller pay raises in their current jobs amid 
greater job insecurity. 
Using industry- and state-level labor market data over the last three decades, we find 
that churn adds explanatory power to a wage Phillips curve model that already controls 
for the unemployment rate. This suggests that depressed turnover is contributing, albeit 
modestly, to the subdued pace of nominal wage growth, which our wage tracker puts 
slightly below 3.5%. As Exhibit 9 shows, we estimate that low churn is currently weighing 
on year-over-year wage growth by about 0.3pp. 
 
Looking ahead, we expect turnover to pick up somewhat as the temporary drags from 
post-pandemic normalization in job-switching and uncertainty about the labor market 
impact of AI fade.
