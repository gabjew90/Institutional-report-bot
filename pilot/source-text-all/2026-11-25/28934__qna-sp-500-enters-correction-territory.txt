VOLATILITY MAY FEEL UNSETTLING, BUT PERIODIC PULLBACKS ARE NORMAL
Market narratives can shift quickly, and the sharp decline in equities over the past week is the latest
reminder. The pendulum has swung from optimism, fueled by resilient economic growth, strong
earnings, and AI enthusiasm, to growing anxiety over the sustainability of AI capital investment and its
potential ripple effects on the economy and earnings. This abrupt shift in sentiment and positioning has
pushed the S&P 500 down 11.8% from its October 6 all-time high, marking its first correction of 2026.
For long-term investors, the critical question is whether the fundamental pillars supporting the bull
market remain intact. We believe they do. Economic growth remains positive, corporate earnings are
healthy, and a recession is not our base case over the next 12 months. While volatility may persist,
market corrections are a normal and healthy part of investing, and we would view further weakness as
an opportunity to selectively add exposure. Below, we examine the forces driving the latest sell-off and
explore the implications for the economy, Fed policy, and our outlook across financial markets.

Higher-for-Longer Interest Rate Concerns Resurface. Recent CPI and PPI reports have reinforced 
concerns about stubborn inflation, particularly at the core level. Meanwhile, increasingly hawkish 
commentary from Fed officials has prompted markets to price in two to three additional rate hikes by 
September 2027. While tighter monetary policy may help contain inflation, it also risks slowing growth.
• Our take: Markets may be overestimating how much additional tightening the Fed will need to deliver. We 
expect one final 25 bps rate hike in December, followed by an extended pause. The recent pullback in 
commodity prices should help ease inflationary pressures, while tighter financial conditions may reduce the 
need for further Fed action. 
Cyberattacks Add To Market Anxiety. A wave of high-profile cyberattacks, most recently targeting 
JPMorgan and EU agencies, adds another layer of uncertainty. Meanwhile, software disruptions affecting 
Microsoft Outlook and air traffic control, while not appearing nefarious, highlight the vulnerabilities of an 
increasingly technology-dependent economy. Together, these incidents have amplified investor unease.
• Our take: While these headlines are unsettling, it is important to keep the risks in perspective. Cyberthreats 
are an unfortunate reality of the digital age, with nearly 950 million incidents* observed in 2026 alone. 
Importantly, bank depositors should not assume that such attacks put their money at risk. Meanwhile, 
rising cybersecurity threats are accelerating spending on digital protection by governments and critical 
infrastructure operators, creating opportunities for companies providing these essential services.
ARE WE TURNING MORE CAUTIOUS ON THE ECONOMY?
Not at this stage. We continue to expect GDP growth of 2.3% in 2027, only modestly below this year’s 2.4% 
pace. However, the recent market repricing has brought two important downside risks into sharper focus: a 
potential slowdown in data center capital investment and a reversal of the wealth effect.
According to Jason Furman, former chair of the Council of Economic Advisers, data center capital 
investment has accounted for roughly three-tenths of US economic growth. Stricter state-level regulations, 
coupled with higher borrowing costs for developers, could slow the pace of new construction, weakening 
an important engine of economic activity. At the same time, falling asset prices could begin to weigh on 
consumer spending through the wealth effect. Rising home and equity values have helped fuel spending 
among affluent households, a dynamic Furman estimates has contributed the remaining seven-tenths of 
economic growth. A prolonged decline in asset values, particularly alongside further Fed tightening, could 
turn this powerful tailwind into a headwind.
While these risks bear watching, they do not alter our constructive economic outlook. Data center capex 
and the wealth effect have provided meaningful boosts to growth, but the economy’s underlying 
foundations remain solid. Payrolls continue to expand, unemployment remains low, and Americans are 
generally confident in their job prospects, supporting healthy consumer spending. In short, the fading of 
these tailwinds could slow growth, but we do not expect them to push the economy into recession.
Importantly, one of the forces that fueled inflation concerns may now help stabilize the outlook. After 
surging through much of 2026 amid supply disruptions tied to the US-Iran conflict and Russia-Ukraine war, 
commodity prices have reversed course, with the Bloomberg Commodity Index retreating from its 
September peak, as shown on the next page. Lower commodity prices should provide welcome relief to 
consumers and businesses while easing inflationary pressures. That, in turn, could give the Fed greater 
flexibility and temper its recent hawkish rhetoric. We expect one more 25 bps rate hike in December, the 
second of 2026, followed by an extended pause.

DOES THE RECENT VOLATILITY CHANGE OUR LONGER-TERM EQUITY VIEWS?
Not at all. Investor sentiment can shift on a dime. At the start of 4Q, many strategists rushed to raise their 
equity targets as the S&P 500 approached 8,000, fueled by AI enthusiasm. Now, with volatility rising and 
concerns surrounding the Technology sector mounting, many are reversing course and lowering their 
targets. Through these swings, we have remained anchored to the fundamentals rather than shifting 
market narratives. Our year-end and 12-month S&P 500 targets remain unchanged at 7,650 and 8,450, 
respectively, with our 12-month target implying upside of approximately 23% from current levels.
While market pullbacks are never comfortable, perspective is important. Despite a steady stream of 
challenges, including the Iran conflict, tariffs, hawkish Fed rhetoric, and concerns about AI-driven job 
displacement, equity volatility had remained relatively subdued until recently. Historically, the S&P 500 
experiences one correction of 10% or more annually, with an average maximum drawdown of 
approximately 13%. Until this week, the market had managed to avoid correction territory altogether.
Looking ahead, while near-term volatility may persist, four fundamental and technical factors reinforce our 
constructive equity outlook:
•
First, recession is not our base case. We expect the US economy to remain on solid footing, with no 
recession over the next 12 months. Historically, prolonged equity bear markets have often coincided 
with recessions, a scenario we do not anticipate.
•
Second, earnings remain a source of strength. The latest earnings season underscored the strength of 
corporate fundamentals, with EPS surging 31% year over year. Looking ahead, upward revisions to 2027 
estimates have lifted consensus EPS growth estimates to 16%, more than double the historical average.
•
Third, valuations have reset meaningfully. At the start of the year, the S&P 500's forward P/E ratio 
stood at 22.2x, in the 96th percentile historically. Given robust earnings growth and the recent market 
pullback, the forward P/E has compressed sharply to 16.6x. As shown on the next page, this is the lowest 
multiple since November 2022.

WHAT ARE OUR LATEST THOUGHTS ON THE TECHNOLOGY SECTOR?
The Technology sector has borne the brunt of the recent sell-off, declining 24.7% from its October 6 record 
high and entering bear market territory. However, we believe the severity of the decline is disconnected 
from the sector’s underlying fundamentals. Three key factors reinforce our constructive outlook.
Stricter data center permitting rules should not derail the AI buildout. While some states may impose 
tighter restrictions or even moratoriums on new data center developments, these measures primarily affect 
proposed projects rather than those already under construction. In fact, the power capacity of data centers 
currently being built is more than 1.5 times that of existing facilities, highlighting the substantial runway for 
expansion over the coming years. Reinforcing this outlook, commentary from companies tied to the 
buildout, including Duke Energy, Micron, GE Vernova, and Seagate,* points to robust demand and strong 
backlogs extending through at least 2028. Admittedly, the industry must do a better job addressing 
community concerns surrounding water and electricity consumption, some of which stem from 
misconceptions. Greater transparency and engagement should help ease these concerns. As a result, we 
expect the data center buildout to continue, even if its geographic footprint shifts over time.
AI adoption is accelerating, reinforcing the case for continued capital investment. While headlines 
have focused heavily on the enormous capital spending behind AI, the tangible benefits of that investment 
are becoming increasingly evident. Third-quarter earnings offered compelling proof: cloud revenue at 
major hyperscalers surged 63% year over year, accelerating from 54% in 2Q26, while cloud backlogs 
jumped 141% to $1.8 trillion. These growing backlogs provide greater visibility into future demand and help 
support the economics of continued data center expansion. Importantly, AI adoption is broadening well 
beyond the Technology sector. According to Ramp, 57% of businesses now use AI-related solutions, up from 
44% a year ago. Together, accelerating adoption, swelling backlogs, and demand that continues to outstrip 
supply point to a healthy AI ecosystem and a durable capital investment cycle. With demand remaining 
robust, we expect financing challenges to remain manageable.

Robust earnings growth and more attractive valuations strengthen Technology’s appeal. Consensus 
forecasts call for Technology earnings growth to outpace the broader market every quarter through 2028. 
For 2027, sector earnings are projected to surge 43%, nearly three times the S&P 500’s expected 16% 
growth. Importantly, these forecasts are supported by strong demand visibility, particularly among 
semiconductor companies, which continue to highlight robust order trends extending into 2028. 
Meanwhile, the recent sell-off has dramatically reset valuations. Technology now trades at a discount to the 
S&P 500 for the first time since 2019, while semiconductor valuations have fallen to their lowest forward P/E 
since 2011. With earnings prospects remaining strong and valuations considerably more compelling, we 
believe much of the recent pessimism is already reflected in share prices, creating attractive opportunities 
for long-term investors.
Just as falling equity prices can create a negative wealth effect that weighs on consumer spending, 
declining Technology stock prices could trigger a similar self-reinforcing cycle that threatens the AI 
buildout. Lower share prices make equity financing more expensive and reduce the capital that companies 
can raise, potentially constraining investment in AI infrastructure. Importantly, we have seen no signs of 
funding stress thus far among major Technology companies, and Anthropic’s recent IPO suggests investor 
appetite for AI-related equity remains healthy. Still, this is an emerging risk worth monitoring.
WHAT ARE OUR LATEST THOUGHTS ON THE BOND MARKET?
The bond market’s swift reversal reinforces an important portfolio takeaway: higher yields have restored 
Treasuries’ effectiveness as a diversifier of equity risk. Despite stubborn inflation and hawkish Fed rhetoric, 
rising equity volatility has sparked a classic flight to quality, driving yields lower across the curve. As market 
concerns shift from inflation to slowing growth, safe-haven demand can quickly take precedence over the 
prevailing economic narrative.
The broader risk-off environment has also tightened financial conditions, with the Goldman Sachs Financial 
Conditions Index climbing to its highest level since May 2025. This pressure has spilled into corporate credit 
markets, pushing high-yield spreads to 344 bps as growth concerns and deteriorating sentiment weigh on 
risk assets. Our up-in-quality credit bias remains well suited to this environment, and we continue to favor 
higher-quality bonds, where investors can capture attractive yields without assuming excessive credit risk.
Looking ahead, interest rates will remain sensitive to evolving inflation trends, growth expectations, and 
Fed policy. In the near term, continued de-risking and concerns about slowing growth should support 
Treasuries. We reaffirm our 12-month target of 4.50%–4.75% for the 10-year Treasury yield, supported by 
cooling inflation, moderating growth, and less Fed tightening than markets currently anticipate.
BOTTOM LINE
A wave of macroeconomic and sector-specific concerns has pushed the S&P 500 into its first correction of 
the year, with Technology leading the decline. While calling a market bottom is difficult, we believe 
sentiment and stock prices have fallen further than fundamentals warrant. With the economy on solid 
footing and corporate earnings remaining healthy, our longer-term outlook remains positive. Volatility may 
test investors’ patience, but it should not shake their discipline. Rather than reacting to short-term market 
swings, we would use periods of weakness to selectively pursue attractive long-term opportunities.
