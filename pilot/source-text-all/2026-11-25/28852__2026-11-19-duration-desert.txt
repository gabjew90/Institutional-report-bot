In Summary 
• Global gross duration supply, measured as the amount of interest rate risk added to 
markets by government bond issuance, peaked in 2021 when sovereigns placed USD6.5tn 
in 10-year equivalents (i.e. the volume of 10-year bonds carrying the same interest rate risk 
as the bonds issued). With USD6.5tn in 2025 and USD5.6tn year-to-date global gross duration 
supply is still elevated but is slowing down for all main issuers except China. Net supply after 
buybacks and roll-down of the debt stock has fallen strongly from USD3.3tn in 2021 to 
USD2.1tn in 2025. With USD1tn this year net supply will be the lowest since 2018. The UK, 
Japan and the euro area add no new duration this year; while the US and China each provide 
about half of the global net supply. Compared to 2025 the US share is declining, not least 
because buybacks have withdrawn duration around USD90bn while China’s share is growing 
and should surpass the US over the next years. With Chinese bond markets largely closed to 
global investors, net duration supply is shrinking not only in volume but also in accessibility.   
 
• Sovereigns have responded to the 2022 rate shock by shortening their issuance duration 
by 1 to 3 years. Only China has lengthened it being decoupled from the global rate cycle. The 
convenience yield, the premium embedded in government bonds for their special safety and 
liquidity, is under pressure since 2022 and turned negative for the US, France, and Japan in 
early 2025. That creates an incentive for issuing more bills and fewer bonds favoring rollover 
over duration risk. 
 
• Hedge funds with shorter investment horizons have become dominant buyers of duration. 
Traditional buyers such as pensions funds and central banks are stepping back. As hedge 
funds are leveraged and deployed in relative-value trades their demand for duration is 
shorter, more price sensitive and volatile. For central banks, this structural change of the 
duration demand adds a technical layer (funding conditions, collateral availability) to the 
monetary policy transmission. It requires flexible and reversible intervention tools (standing 
repo facilities), large bond purchase programs (QE) creating massive demand for duration 
become a tail scenario. 
 
• Corporate bonds are an imperfect substitute for sovereign duration. High-quality corporate 
bonds, hyperscaler included, account for maximum 20% of US Treasury issuance (6%-10% in 
the euro area). Despite the smaller market size, corporate bonds still have a lower 
convenience yield than government bonds lacking moneyness in repo and derivatives. This is 
creating an opportunity for EU Bonds issuance to position as a global safe asset. Having 
preserved their convenience yield through the recent turmoil EU Bonds could position Europe 
as a high-quality supplier of long duration and support the international role of the euro. 
 
• A duration desert is emerging  from supply and demand side, leaving the long end cheap 
and offering an extra term premium of 50-100bps. But we believe a duration revival will 
occur within 2-5 years as the need for safe long-dated assets for transition policies (green 
energy, infrastructure, defense) and ageing populations has not disappeared and will re-
emerge once the rate cycle is turning. We expect temporary duration scarcity as rebuilding 
supply takes years of issuance. Those willing and able to cross the desert today can be 
rewarded by 50-100bps of extra term premium (for a 30-year bond) that will vanish once 
duration scarcity hits.

Crossing the Duration Desert: Who will buy and who 
will supply the long end of the curve?

Global gross duration issuance remains elevated, increasingly driven by China due to its decoupling from the 
global rate cycle. Duration plays an essential role in modern financial markets. For issuers, it is the price of locking 
in funding costs over long time horizons and gaining planning certainty. For investors such as pension funds and 
insurers it is the price of hedging long-dated liabilities. At the same time, long and safe bonds serve the financial 
system as benchmark for pricing time value and risk, as a liquid reserve buffer for banks, and as collateral for 
creating liquidity in the repo and derivatives markets. In OECD countries at least two thirds of duration is supplied 
by governments, and for long-dated duration beyond 10 years their share is even higher. Gross duration supply has 
grown massively since 1970 with the liberalization of capital markets. Global government duration supply peaked 
in 2021, when sovereigns ramped up borrowing to finance the Covid-19 response. Combining issuance volume and 
duration expressed in 10-year equivalents, USD6tn of duration was placed in the market that year. Combining 
issuance volume and duration, expressed in 10-year equivalents, USD6tn of duration was placed in the market that 
year, of which USD3.3tn came from the US alone.1 Last year marked another high point in gross duration supply but 
this time it was mainly driven by China increasing its gross supply while other issuers slowed down. Being decoupled 
from the global rate cycle (China’s 10y government bond yield falling ~
150bps over the last 5 years while US 10y 
Treasuries and German 10y Bunds rose ~350bps) China is now the second largest global duration after the US. Its 
gross supply in 10-year equivalents, reached USD 1.5tn this year versus USD 2.5tn (Figure 1). 
 
Structural scarcity of long‑end duration 
issued by government will define the net supply for the coming years. In 
terms of net duration supply, i.e. the duration risk that new issuance adds to the existing debt stock losing otherwise 
lose duration with the passing of time and adjusted by buybacks, the decline is much stronger. The net change in 
the duration of the outstanding debt stock, in 10-year equivalents, also peaked in 2021 at USD3.3tn but has since 
fallen to USD2.1tn in 2025. In 2026, net issuance by the UK, Japan and the euro area will add no new duration risk, 
as the duration of their issuance is falling to or even below the weighted average duration of their debt stock. The 
composition of net global duration supply has shifted accordingly. In 2025 China injected close to USD1tn of net 
duration, more than the US, and in 2026 it again accounts for most of new duration risk (Figure 2). Since China's 
supply is largely inaccessible to international investors, new duration is becoming hard to source in developed 
markets as the long end of traditional developed issuers is less supplied. We expect this pattern to persist over the 
coming years. With policy rates settling well above their 2010s levels and term premia rebuilt, issuers have little 
incentive to add new duration risk, while the duration embedded in the outstanding stock is likely to shrink as the 
long bonds of earlier vintages roll down the curve. In addition, the US Treasury is stepping up buybacks of long-
dated securities, a further drag of USD94bn 10-year equivalents on net duration supply.

The global rise in interest rates in 2022 has substantially changed the incentives for shortening duration 
issuance. For governments as duration suppliers, the massive debt build-up during the Covid-19 crisis and the 
interest rate surge of 2022 have changed the incentives in debt management against longer duration. In contrast 
to the low-rate period of 2010 to 2021, the market rate for most sovereign issuers in the OECD now lies above the 
implicit interest rate on public debt (interest expense relative to the outstanding volume). This means that interest 
expense rises even if the debt ratio remain stable as old, low yielding debt is replaced by new debt at higher rates. 
The response of government duration supply moves within the tension between liquidity and safety. Together these 
two components form their convenience yield. This is the premium investors are willing to pay for government bonds 
because they can be traded at any time in large volume at low cost, can be used in repo as collateral to raise 
liquidity, and act as a safe haven in periods of stress. Safety and liquidity are opposed forces. If only few bonds are 
issued, they are very safe but hardly tradable. If many bonds are issued, they are easier to trade but the underlying 
debt promise becomes less safe. The composition of the convenience yield differs along the curve. At the short end 
the liquidity component dominates. At the long end the safety component dominates. When debt levels and fiscal 
uncertainty rise, the convenience yield erodes first at the long end, visible in rising term premia relative to the short 
end. Issuing longer maturities becomes more expensive. The difference in term premium between 2-year and 10-
year bonds is already back to pre- QE levels reaching 50 to 100bps for the main issuers (Figure 3). The result is the 
weighted duration of new issuances has declined markedly since 2021 from 7 years in the US and 11 years in the 
euro area to only 6 and 8 years respectively today. Again China is the outlier with an increase of 2 years from 
weighted issuance duration from 5 to 7 years (Figure 4).

The convenience yield is under pressure and even negative for some major issuers, creating an incentive to favor 
rollover risk over duration risk and to issue more short-term bills instead of long-term bonds. During the low-rate 
period, a consistently positive convenience yield acted as a buffer, allowing debt managers to focus on maintaining 
a full benchmark curve, broadening the investor base and swapping rollover risk for cheap duration risk. Since 2022 
the convenience yield has been under pressure. For the US, UK, France and Japan it has turned negative for both 
short and long Treasuries as a result of the massive expansion in government bond supply. Germany still maintains 
a positive convenience yield (Figure 5). Convenience yield management is moving back to the center of government 
debt management. Since we do not expect a major reversal in interest rates or in government funding needs in the 
coming years, the convenience yield management is likely to move back towards more rollover risk and less 
duration risk. This also implies a considerably more active and in part opportunistic debt management. In the US 
this trend is clearly communicated as “Active Treasury Issuance” (ATI) and shows up in the growing share of bills in 
the government funding structure, part of which is used to fund buybacks of long-dated securities(Figure 6). 
Buybacks subtracted USD94bn in 2026 of which USD75bn alone in the segments between 7 and 30 years. For other 
large sovereign issuers this trend has not yet taken hold. But the trend in issuance policy points to less  net duration 
supply in general.

Duration demand is shifting towards a more unstable and price-sensitive market structure that is less supportive 
of the convenience yield. The duration market is not driven by supply alone. It also responds to a structural demand 
for safe, long-dated assets. Traditionally this demand arises from asset-liability considerations (pension funds, life 
insurers), from the need for liquidity buffers, or from the use as collateral in the repo and derivatives markets. 
Globalization has reinforced this demand as trade surpluses in Asia and among commodity exporters have been 
recycled into developed market government bonds. These traditional buyers are relatively price-insensitive and thus 
support the convenience yield. Central banks temporarily created strong price-insensitive demand for duration 
through bond purchase programs (Quantitative Easing, QE). At the peak, QE purchases compressed 10-year 
government bond yields by 173bps in the euro area, by 100bps in the US and by 200bps in Japan. But the traditional 
demand becomes fragile. Reserve accumulation has slowed, while domestic demand is increasingly carried by 
price-sensitive levered investors who hold duration only for higher premia. Pension funds are shifting to defined 
contribution schemes with less maturity matching (i.e. Netherlands) and central banks are running off their holdings 
(Quantitative Tightening, QT), pushing duration back to the private sector. We estimate QT has raised 10-year 
government bond yields by 125bps in the euro area, by 40bps in the US and by 110bps in Japan. QT will add a again 
around 50bps to long-end duration over until 2033 (Figure 7).

Banks have become the “private duration buyer of last resort“ adding their warehousing premium on long-dated 
duration. As traditional demand weakens, primary dealer banks have to act as the "private duration buyer of last 
resort". But this comes with a price for the issuer as under Basel III every bond they warehouse ties up scarce, capital-
charged balance sheet, a cost they pass on to issuers in the form of higher funding costs. This warehousing premium 
penalizes issuance of long-dated duration in particular (Figure 8).

Sovereign debt markets are becoming increasingly reliant on domestic price-sensitive investors. Hedge funds 
have emerged as some of the most important marginal buyers of government bonds. Their positions are typically 
financed through repo transactions and deployed in relative-value strategies, most notably Treasury basis trades 
(long treasury / short futures) and swap spread trades (long treasury / swap receiver). Around 60% of the increase 
in hedge fund Treasury positions can be linked to basis-trading activity2 and hedge funds are now holding around 
USD6tn of sovereign debt exposure, funded with repo with a sharp increase since 2022 (Figure 9). Hedge funds have 
also become dominant in euro area government bond markets. ECB data show 56% of secondary-market Euro 
government bond trading was already hedge fund related in 20233, more than double than in 2018.

The importance of hedge funds in duration demand is changing the nature of monetary policy transmission as 
they add a technical liquidity cycle to the economy. A shorter government funding structure tightens the link 
between fiscal and monetary policy, as more public debt is priced closer to policy rates. But the presence of hedge 
funds adds frictions to this relationship. Since their duration demand rests on leveraged relative value trades, every 
position also absorbs collateral to fund and margin it. In Europe 75% of money market transactions are backed by 
such “special collateral”. 
This increases the demand for individual securities that must be borrowed in repo 
potentially creating a scarcity or “specialness” premium. This means their repo rates decouple from the policy rate. 
The wider this gap the weaker the pass-through of policy rate changes into sovereign yields. Financial conditions 
become increasingly influenced by collateral availability. So central banks have to respond not to fundamentals 
alone but to a technical liquidity cycle that is emerging from the change in duration demand. The paradox is that 
this demand structure needs at the same time ample aggregate bond supply for liquidity and security specific price 
dislocations to conduct basis and relative-value trades. If the number of issues declines, especially at the long end 
as issuance policy shortens, these trades concentrate on fewer bonds and smaller price dislocations, requiring more 
leverage to keep returns constant. This makes the demand structure more volatile and more fragile. This shift in 
duration demand will push central banks towards temporary rather than structural tools that can be reversed as 
fast as liquidity conditions change4. Instead of creating permanent reserves via outright purchases like during QE, 
central banks will rely on temporary facilities that provide reserves on demand and roll off quickly. For duration 
demand this removes a major potential buyer. Central bank QE turns from a standing bid into a far out-of-the-
money put. 
 
The importance of hedge funds is altering the stability of sovereign bond markets and increases cross-asset 
correlations. The growing importance of hedge funds amplifies rates volatility for government bonds. As their 
positions are leveraged, sovereign bonds increasingly serve as a “leverage ticket” to raise funding rather than as 
long-term investments. Their positions expand rapidly during periods of stability and unwind quickly when 
conditions tighten. More broadly, the rise of non-bank financial intermediaries (NBFI) is a signal for the evolution 
towards “money market funding of capital market lending
”5. By linking funding markets, sovereign bonds, 
derivatives and other asset classes this process contributes to stronger cross-asset correlations and increases the 
risk of deleveraging spirals. Moreover, because higher yields mechanically increase government borrowing costs, 
each stress episode risks triggering additional supply, reinforcing the very dynamic that caused the disruption. On 
the other hands, if the governments are reducing the supply because of austerity policies, they will also increase the 
specialness premium and increase instability of money market that could create a balance sheet recession.

Abrupt deleveraging and elevated specialness can destabilize government bond markets as it erodes the 
liquidity component of their convenience yield. The 2022 UK Gilt crisis demonstrated how leveraged positioning 
can amplify market stress and force rapid liquidations and force the central bank to intervene. More recently, 
episodes of tension in European sovereign markets have highlighted the risk that periods of investor retrenchment 
can resemble a temporary "buyer strike", where the marginal demand for duration suddenly disappears and yields 
adjust sharply to restore market-clearing conditions. This price adjustment can be understood as the loss of the 
liquidity part of their convenience yield. As sovereign debt becomes increasingly held by levered investors requiring 
both funding and risk compensation, we are seeing a growing sensitivity between these phenomenon and the 
higher term premia U.S. data illustrates that during QT 2022-2025, the share of Treasuries held by price-sensitive 
investors rose by ~17pp, while the 10-year term premium increased by approximately 110bps6 (Figure 10). Greater 
volatility and stronger procyclicality become structural features of duration markets.  
 
Private debt will not fill the duration supply left by governments. In the US the AAA/AA corporate segment makes 
up only around 10% of the US investment-grade universe representing 5-10% of Treasury issuance. In Europe and 
Japan where the corporate bonds market is less developed, the share is even lower. If the AI capex financing 
continues at current pace, they could hardly reach a level to compete with sovereign supply. Hyperscaler 
investment-grade bond issuance reached roughly USD108bn in 2025 and should reach USD250bn in 2026 and close 
to USD400bn in 2027. Hyperscaler issuance will thus amount to roughly 10-20% of net Treasury supply. In the euro 
area, hyperscaler reverse Yankee issuance has reached roughly EUR24bn so far in 2026 representing around 3% of 
gross government bond issuance. Even the EUR60bn of reverse Yankee supply that investment banks expect for 
2027 would lift this share to around 6% only.  
 
Hyperscaler supply is an imperfect substitution for sovereign duration lacking liquidity and moneyness of 
Treasuries in repo and derivatives markets. In convenience yield terms, hyperscaler should therefore trade at a 
clear penalty to sovereign duration with very small liquidity component and an elevated safety component. That 
this penalty is partly visible today as for instance Meta's 30-year issue is priced at 75bps over the 30-year Treasury 
today. However, hyperscaler duration is heavily concentrated in the very long segment, exactly where governments 
are retreating to shorten their maturity profiles. Rather than competing with sovereign supply, corporate duration 
could also become its complement at the long end.  
 
EU Bonds could position Europe as high-quality supplier of long duration and support the international role of 
the euro. Unlike the US long-term bonds, EU Bonds have preserved their convenience yield even during the latest 
market turmoil holding even better than the GDP weighted yield would have suggested (Figure 5). So, even if one 
member is in trouble the institutional framework holds and is creates a safety pattern stronger an aggregation of 
GDP weighted risk profiles. Eurobonds therefore offer a new type of safe asset based on institutional credibility, not 
on ratings only. 
 
With less supply and demand the long end of the curve risks desertification, leaving the long end cheap and 
offering a 50-100bos extra term premium. Within 2-5 years, with the interest rate cycle turning and private debt 
alternatives lose their risk-return edge, the need for safe long-dated assets will resurface as financing needs for 
transition policies (green energy, infrastructure, defense) and ageing populations have not disappeared. There will 
be a temporary supply mismatch as rebuilding a stock of long duration takes years of issuance. Those investors 
willing and able to deploy capital at the long end of the yield curve when markets desert it will be able to secure a 
50-100bos additional term premium on top of the safe and stable annuity of long government bonds. This extra 
premium will vanish once the duration supply and demand mismatch hits.
