2027 Year Ahead: Domestic demand still 
waiting for a spark 
     
   
 
 
Near term policy support unlikely to alter growth outlook 
We raise our 2026 GDP growth forecast to 4.6% yoy from 4.5%, largely reflecting a 
mark-to-market adjustment for growth in the first three quarters. Recent policy 
measures, including the policy financing tool and higher local government debt quotas, 
could help stabilize investment demand in 4Q, though October data showed little 
evidence of an improvement so far. We maintain our below-consensus 2027 and 2028 
growth forecasts at 4.2% and 4.0%, respectively. Without more substantial consumption 
support, underlying household demand should remain subdued, but retail sales growth is 
likely to improve as unfavorable base effects from earlier trade-in subsidies roll off. 
Exports to remain a bright spot, but growth to slow  
Exports should remain a relative bright spot in 2027, although growth is likely to 
moderate to a high-single-digit pace from more than 20% in 2026. The slowdown 
reflects slower demand growth for Chinese technology products as global hyperscaler 
capex growth normalizes, alongside continued trade frictions and an increasingly higher 
base, particularly from 2Q onwards. A sharper export slowdown would add to the 
pressure on already-weak domestic demand but could also prompt a stronger policy 
easing response. 
Subdued demand keeps inflation pressure modest  
We revise our CPI inflation forecasts to 0.9%, 0.7% and 1.0% for 2026, 2027 and 2028, 
respectively. Weak domestic demand should keep core inflation muted. Even if crude oil 
prices remain elevated more persistently, China’s refined-oil price smoothing mechanism 
and weak demand should limit the pass-through to fuel prices, transportation costs and 
headline inflation, while a higher base should further moderate the impact in 2027. Food 
prices remain a watchpoint, as the potential El Nino effect on supply has yet to become 
visible and warrants close monitoring after winter.  
A policy cushion, not a stimulus rescue 
We expect policy support in 2027 to remain targeted rather than broad-based. We do 
not anticipate policy rate or RRR cuts, while the official fiscal deficit is likely to remain 
at 4.0% unless growth deteriorates materially and capital-market stress intensifies. 
Instead, support is likely to come through greater policy-backed tools for infrastructure, 
particularly the Six Networks, and a potential expansion of the PSL. We expect special 
treasury bond issuance to remain at RMB1.3tn and the LGSB quota to edge up to 
RMB4.5tn from RMB4.4tn, with RMB800bn still earmarked for debt resolution. 
Policy inaction remains the biggest downside risk 
The key downside risk is that policy support remains incremental even if external 
demand weakens and domestic activity deteriorates further. A sharper export slowdown 
could prompt more forceful easing and partially offset the shock through stronger 
domestic demand, but an insufficient policy response would leave growth increasingly 
exposed. Other risks include weaker global AI capex, less effective near-term fiscal 
support and renewed trade frictions, while inflation risks remain broadly balanced.

GDP growth remained resilient in 3Q, supported by a larger contribution from net

However, beyond this near-term lift, we keep our 2027 and 2028 below-consensus

Will policy remain active in boosting domestic demand? 
How is policy likely to respond to the current macro backdrop? While we expect 
continued fiscal expansion and monetary easing, the overall scale of policy support is 
likely to remain broadly on par with 2026.  
In addition, the RMB2tn annual special refinancing bond program supporting local 
government debt swaps is scheduled to end this year, with no further issuance under 
this program from 2027 onwards (Exhibit 3).  
Although debt swaps do not directly generate additional fiscal demand, they reduce 
interest costs and ease debt-servicing pressures, thereby freeing up local government 
resources for other spending. The program’s expiry will therefore remove a source of 
incremental fiscal relief for local governments, although we expect the LGSB quota to be 
raised modestly to accommodate some continued debt-swap issuance.  
We also do not expect further special central government bond issuance for bank 
recapitalization. Overall, the anticipated policy mix is unlikely to provide a meaningful 
boost to domestic demand. 
On fiscal policy, we expect the following headline fiscal numbers for 2027: 
• 
Budget fiscal deficit to remain high at 4.0% (same as 2025-26), with a higher fiscal 
expenditure growth and stable fiscal revenue;  
• 
Continued issuance of special treasury bonds, with overall size remain at RMB1.3tn, 
potentially comprising RMB850bn for investment, RMB250bn for consumption 
support (possibly extended to services), and RMB200bn for equipment upgrades; 
• 
LGSB issuance is likely to edge up to RMB4.5tn in 2027, from RMB4.4tn in the 2026 
budget, with RMB800bn continuing to be earmarked for debt resolution.

On monetary policy, we do not expect any policy rate or RRR cuts in 2027 unless 
export growth deteriorates materially. In our view, the recent shift from the 7-day to the 
overnight reverse repo rate represents a change in the monetary policy framework 
rather than a signal of rate cuts, particularly in lending rates for the real economy. The 
persistently weak loan growth also suggests that modest interest rate cuts alone are 
unlikely to stimulate credit demand sufficiently to provide meaningful support to 
domestic growth (Exhibit 4). That said, we expect an expansion in pledged 
supplementary lending (PSL) to support policy banks in deploying additional policy 
financing tools in 2027, potentially exceeding this year’s RMB800bn allocation.   
On property policy, as noted above, the mortgage interest subsidy of one percentage 
point could stimulate borrowing among lower- and middle-income households and 
support housing demand. However, the new property sales policy could significantly 
constrain developers’ operating cash flow, putting additional pressure on land sales, 
property investment, and new home sales. Without meaningful policy adjustments later 
in the year, we expect the property sector to remain a drag on growth in the near term.

Exports to hold up, but growth to moderate meaningfully 
In contrast to subdued domestic demand, exports are likely to remain relatively resilient 
in 2027. Fundamentally, we expect demand growth for Chinese technology products, 
including electronic integrated circuits and automatic data-processing machines, to 
normalize as hyperscaler capex growth moderates. At the same time, persistent trade 
frictions are likely to constrain exports of certain products, such as EVs.  
Base effects will also become less favorable. The price-driven acceleration in technology 
exports began in 1Q 2026 and strengthened over the course of the year, meaning that a 
higher comparison base will start to weigh on year-on-year growth from early 2027, with 
a more pronounced slowdown likely from 2Q onwards. Taken together, we expect export 
growth to moderate to a high-single-digit pace in 2027, remaining resilient but slowing 
from more than 20% in 2026. 
A more pronounced slowdown than we currently expect could, however, increase the 
urgency for additional policy support, potentially catalyzing a more comprehensive 
easing package and a clearer pivot toward supporting domestic demand.

Policy inaction remains the biggest downside risk 
The key downside risk is that policy support remains incremental even if external 
demand weakens and domestic activity deteriorates further. A sharper export slowdown 
could prompt more forceful policy easing, partially offsetting the shock through stronger 
domestic demand. That said, an insufficient policy response would leave growth 
increasingly exposed. 
Other downside risks include a more pronounced moderation in the global AI capex cycle 
and less effective near-term fiscal support. Trade policy uncertainty could also remain 
elevated, weighing on longer-term corporate investment decisions. In addition, the policy 
financing tool and higher local government debt quota could deliver a smaller or slower 
near-term boost to activity than we currently assume. 
Inflation risks appear more balanced. Food-price pressures associated with a potential El 
Niño event represent the main upside risk, although there is not yet clear evidence of a 
material supply impact. By contrast, the pass-through of higher oil prices to headline 
inflation is likely to remain contained, limiting the upside risk from energy costs.
