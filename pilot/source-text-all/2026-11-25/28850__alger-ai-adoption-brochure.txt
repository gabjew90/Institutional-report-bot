From Investment to Results
Most companies are using artificial intelligence (AI) in some capacity, but many describe the benefit anecdotally, 
without quantification. That is partly a measurement problem. An hour saved across thousands of employees does 
not reach the income statement unless it changes how many people a company needs or how much it can sell. As 
a result, few companies today can say what exactly AI has contributed to revenue or profit margins. We believe that 
may change. Models are improving quickly, the number of uses is growing, and we expect more companies to begin 
reporting results that show up in their financial statements. 
ALGER’S AI INVESTMENT FRAMEWORK
In Alger’s AI investment framework, the adopters are the companies deploying AI, in the products they sell, in the way 
they operate, or both. We believe a growing share of the opportunity may belong to the adopters.

AI ADOPTION:
We characterize AI adoption as successful when it reaches the income statement. It arrives as faster revenue growth 
when AI improves what a company sells, or as margin expansion when AI enhances how it operates (see Figure 1). In 
both cases, the outcome is stronger earnings growth, which we believe is what ultimately drives shareholder value.

Axon Enterprise 
Axon makes equipment for police departments and now sells AI as a subscription 
upgrade to that gear: Draft One turns body-camera audio into the first draft of a police 
report, Axon Assistant translates and answers officers’ questions in the field, and 
Prepared helps 911 centers handle calls. The bundle that contains them, the AI Era Plan, 
took in roughly $750 million of orders in its first full year, about one-tenth of everything 
Axon sold in 2025. Those orders are multi-year contracts, so the revenue arrives 
gradually. By the third quarter of 2026, Axon’s AI products had passed $100 million in 
annual recurring revenue, with AI orders up more than 50% in the first nine months of 
the year. The appeal is time. Axon notes that Draft One saves an officer up to four hours 
a week. The payoff for shareholders is that existing customers spent 26% more with 
Axon than a year earlier, largely by adding AI to what they already owned.

Shopify
Shopify earns most of its revenue from a share of what a store sells, so more buyers 
for its merchants means more revenue for Shopify. AI is attracting those buyers in two 
ways. Shopify has connected its merchants’ products to AI assistants such as ChatGPT, 
Claude and Google Gemini, so shoppers can search and buy inside a conversation. 
In the third quarter of 2026, orders arriving this way rose 3.3 times year over year, 
shoppers who came through an AI assistant converted 64% more often than other 
visitors, and management said the economics of those sales “are exactly the same” as 
any other order. Sidekick, an AI assistant for merchants, saw daily active merchants 
rise 3.7 times, and new merchants who follow its guidance reach their first sales faster. 
The result is customers buying more, and Shopify’s revenue growing with them.

Unity Software
Unity makes software that developers use to build and run video games, and it 
sells advertising that helps those games find players. AI is now at work in both. 
Management says AI is making game creation “more approachable and more 
efficient,” drawing in more creators and more games. In August 2026, Unity opened 
its software to AI agents that write code, and on its November 2026 earnings call it 
said the number of people using its software each day to build games had risen 25% 
since that change. More games mean more choices for players to sort through, and 
that is where the second use of AI comes in. Vector, an AI model Unity introduced in 
2025, predicts which games a person is likely to enjoy and shows them ads to match. 
The model keeps retraining on more data, and as its predictions improve, game 
makers win more players for each advertising dollar, so they spend more. Vector 
revenue grew 126% year over year in the third quarter of 2026, and about 90% of the 
quarter’s growth came from existing advertisers.

Robinhood Markets
Robinhood is a financial platform where customers trade stocks, options, 
cryptocurrencies and prediction market contracts, and save for retirement. The 
company has used AI to grow revenue without growing its workforce in step, mostly 
in customer support and software engineering, and built much of the technology 
in-house. In early 2026, management reported that AI was resolving more than 75% 
of customer support cases, including complex ones that previously required licensed 
brokerage professionals. In engineering, Robinhood is using AI to automate coding, 
code review, testing, and deployment. The payoff shows up in revenue per employee, 
which has more than doubled since 2023, from about $850,000 to a record of nearly 
$2 million reported on its October 2026 earnings call.

Rocket Companies
Rocket is a homeownership platform whose businesses include Rocket Mortgage and 
the real estate brokerage Redfin, and since acquiring Mr. Cooper in 2025, it services 
roughly one in six U.S. mortgages. Rocket has used AI so that making more loans 
no longer requires hiring more people. In the fourth quarter of 2025, the company 
originated nearly $50 billion of loans, a level its CEO said it last reached in early 2022, 
but “with half the head count.” Management has said that once fixed costs are covered, 
50% to 70% of additional revenue flows to EBITDA, a measure of earnings before 
interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization.

Amazon
Amazon is the world’s largest online retailer and cloud computing provider. The 
company sells AI through its cloud business, but some of its clearest results come 
from applying the technology to its own operations. AI forecasts demand, coordinates 
a fleet of more than one million robots, and helps decide where inventory is placed 
so products sit closer to customers. In the first quarter of 2026, the number of items 
Amazon sold grew 15% while fulfillment expense grew only 11%. The gains extend to 
software, where six engineers using Amazon’s AI coding tool built a new engine for a 
core cloud service in 76 days, work that CEO Andy Jassy wrote “might take a team of 
40 people about a year.”
