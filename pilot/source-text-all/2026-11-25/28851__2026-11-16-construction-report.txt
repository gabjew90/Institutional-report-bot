In summary 
• Without data centers, US construction would be shrinking nearly twice as fast. US private 
construction spending on data centers reached USD96.5bn in September, 90% above a year earlier and 
more than ten times the level of 2019. The segment is now 12.2% of private non-residential construction, 
accounting for roughly one dollar of every eight spent. Europe is far smaller: the five FLAP-D hubs 
(Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin) have about 1.4 GW under construction, against 66-
80 GW in the US, depending on sources, while just over 750 MW should be added across Europe in 2026, 
with power availability the main constraint. Total US construction spending is down -2.7% y/y as of 
September but would be -4.8% excluding data centers. That makes the segment both the main support 
of the cycle and a concentration risk, and there are three clouds looming: labor (79% of North American 
construction markets report shortages, and over 93% in Europe), power and politics, especially 
following the Democratic sweep in the US midterms. We do not expect the political shift to end the cycle 
but permit pauses in Texas and New York could be reinforced, turning a cost constraint into a volume 
constraint by 2028, and limiting how fast and how much AI capacity can further scale.  
• Meanwhile, the new tightening cycle is squeezing residential construction on both sides of the 
Atlantic. Rate-sensitive segments such as housing and conventional commercial property are stuck or 
even contracting. US housing starts are 7.4% below a year ago, builder sentiment is at a low point and 
unsold new homes equal 8.4 months of sales. Housing starts should be around 1.25-1.30mn in 2027–
2028, below the 2019–2022 average. In Europe, the residential picture is uneven. German permits are 
recovering but remain a third below 2019, French permits are falling again, while Spanish permits are 
more than twice their 2019 level. Order-book sentiment remains deeply negative in France and 
Germany; only Spain, the Netherlands and Ireland are in positive territory as acute housing shortages 
are driving up demand.  
• The rate sell-off is repricing the whole curve, and different parts of the curve matter on each side 
of the Atlantic. In the US, the 30-year mortgage rate is set off the 10-year Treasury yield plus a spread. 
The mortgage rate climbed from 6.05% in February to 7.33% in October and the 10-year yield rose from 
4.1% to 5.1%, while the upper bound of the Fed's target range stayed at 3.75% until September, 
suggesting long yields explain the entire increase. The rise is also a real-yield move, with breakeven 
inflation barely changed, so markets are pricing tighter money for longer rather than higher inflation. 
But in the Eurozone the link is looser. The cost of house-purchase borrowing rose 30bps between 
November 2025 and September 2026, against 50bps for the ECB deposit rate and 80-100bps for long 
yields. This suggests that European mortgage costs depend more on the mix of floating and fixed-rate 
loans, and on gradual bank pricing. Nevertheless, the pass-through may be slower but banks already 
report a sharp fall in housing-loan demand (net -15% in Q3, -27% expected in Q4). Bank surveys show 
US banks have tightened standards on construction and land loans but not on mortgages, where 
demand has dropped, while Eurozone banks also report weaker demand more than tighter supply. 
• For 2027-28 we expect residential to stay the weakest segment in both regions, with European 
construction output growing by less than the roughly 2% a year. Developers and contractors tied to 
housing and conventional commercial work face another year of weak demand, rising costs and, in 
Europe, elevated insolvencies. Infrastructure supports but does not boom: US public construction is 
growing +1%, while Eurozone public investment is 3.8% of GDP in 2026 and 2027. Data centers should 
remain the strongest segment, whereas US office construction is expected to fall another 4.5% in 2027. 
In our baseline, the US 10-year yield is 5.00% at end-2026 and 4.75% at end-2027, easing to 4.25% in 
2028 and a pause in hyperscaler capex would remove the one growing segment.

K-shaped construction: AI builds, housing stalls 

2 
 
Data centers and AI: a boom with hard limits 
AI is turning computing demand into a construction program. US private construction spending on data centers 
reached USD96.5bn annualized in September, 90% above a year earlier and more than ten times the level of 2019. 
The segment is now 12.2% of private non-residential construction, roughly one dollar in eight. Over the past 12 
months, data centers added about USD46bn of annual spending, while manufacturing construction lost USD37bn. 
The consensus forecast expects data-center construction to grow +33% in 2026 and +25% in 2027. Without data 
centers, US construction would be shrinking nearly twice as fast. Total construction spending is down -2.7% y/y as of 
September, but -4.8% excluding data centers. The segment is both the main support of the cycle and a concentration 
risk. If data-center spending merely flattened while the rest of private non-residential construction kept shrinking at 
about -4%, we estimate the whole category would decline by -3-3.5% a year.

with and without data centers (right, y/y %) 
 
 
Sources: US Census Bureau, Allianz Research 
Contractors and equipment makers with scale are capturing the gains. Some 60% of the capacity under 
construction is built by third-party developers rather than by the hyperscalers themselves, broadening the client 
base for contractors and engineering firms. Equipment makers lifted capex from USD4.5bn in 2019 to an estimated 
USD11.2bn in 2026, but capacity additions are capped at 25-30% a year and backlogs for large generators stretch 
late into the decade, shifting pricing power to firms that hold skilled labor and equipment slots. Backlogs at US 
contractors exposed to the segment have expanded sharply from +40% to +78% including unsigned work. Power 
equipment is another binding constraint. Gas-fired projects in PJM, the largest US power market, show delivery 
dates of 2030–2031. Spillovers lift power, industrial and logistics construction: Private power construction rose 
USD18.6bn in the year to September, and copper demand from grids and data centers adds to metals price 
pressure. 
The boom is running into hard limits: labor, power and politics. The first is labor. US electrician employment rose 
+15% between May 2020 and May 2025, while data center deliveries are set to rise several-fold; 81% of firms with 
electrician openings find them hard to fill, and US construction unemployment is 3.1%, a 26-year low. The second is 
power. In Ireland data centers used 23% of all metered electricity in 2025, up from 5% in 2015, and developers 
increasingly turn to on-site generation, mostly gas, because grid connections take years. The third is politics. 
Opposition to a nearby data center rose from 42% to 75% of Americans between August 2025 and August 2026 in 
one poll (other polls show 71% and 63%). Texas halted new data-center permits pending an audit, and New York 
has a one-year moratorium on facilities of 50 MW or more. So far this has raised costs, as developers pay for grid 
upgrades, rather than cut volumes.

After the midterms: what a Democratic sweep could change 
The 3 November midterms went the Democrats' way, with data centers a key issue. Democrats won in Texas and 
in the three states that follow it for new capacity (Ohio, Georgia and Arizona). Several winners ran on restricting 
data centers or shielding electricity customers from their costs; data-center advertising in the campaign totaled 
about USD173m, USD140m of it Democratic. Nothing changes overnight. Governors can stop approvals not yet 
SSissued, but lasting moratoria need legislatures. Republicans still hold the Texas Legislature (control of the Texas 
House was undecided on 4 November), which meets from 12 January to 31 May 2027. 
Policy is tightening state by state. In Texas, the new governor wants a moratorium until a law is passed and an 
end to tax breaks, on top of the 2025 large-load rules, the governor's audit of a 474 GW interconnection queue and 
the 21 September permit halt. Georgia's Public Service Commission turns 3-2 Democratic, pointing to tougher 
scrutiny of Georgia Power's large-load contracts and tax abatements. Ohio's new governor has proposed a 
conditional moratorium and appoints the utility commissioners but faces a Republican legislature. Virginia has 
added a USD600m data-center electricity tax. In Washington, the House passed a ratepayer-protection bill 417-3 
on 16 September; the Senate has not acted, and analysts do not expect a national moratorium.

2027 volumes look secure; the exposure is 2028 and later. About three-quarters of the 33.8 GW expected to be 
delivered is already under construction. About 30 GW of 2028 capacity sits in states where a candidate favoring a 
pause was running (5.7 GW if only candidates proposing conditions win), and about 20% of the US pipeline is 
affected by the Texas permit pause. The likelier effects are on costs and timing: upfront collateral and minimum 
bills, grid-upgrade payments, bring-your-own-power requirements, disclosure rules and tax-break rollbacks. 
Hyperscalers can absorb these; smaller developers and projects without secured power are most exposed. We 
would expect the political shift to push some capacity from 2028 into later years and towards friendlier states, not 
to end the cycle. 
An unsupportive rates and financial-conditions backdrop  
The post-2022 tightening cycle is not over, but it has changed shape. Construction emerged from the 2021–2022 
pandemic boom into an inflation shock triggered by the war in Ukraine and later the Red Sea disruptions. Easing in 
late 2024 (Fed) and through 2025 (ECB) supported a mild recovery, which is now under threat. Higher energy prices 
linked to the war in the Middle East have put monetary policy back on a tightening path. The ECB raised its deposit 
rate by 25bps on 17 June and again on 16 September, reaching 2.50%; the Fed raised its target range by 25bps in 
September to 3.75%-4%. We expect three more Fed hikes (December, January and March) to 4.50–4.75%, two more 
ECB hikes (December and March) to a deposit rate of 3.00%, and the Bank of England to reach 4.5% by March 2027, 
before all three ease by end-2028 (to 3.75%, 2.25% and 3.25%). The sell-off has shifted the whole curve and 
construction is exposed to that wider tightening in financial conditions. 
Different parts of the curve matter for different borrowers. The policy rate sets the cost of short-term and floating-
rate debt, such as development loans and variable-rate mortgages. Medium-term swap rates, which follow 
expected policy rates, price fixed-rate loans in much of Europe. Long yields set US mortgage rates, the discount rate 
applied to future rents and sale proceeds, and capitalization rates. Credit spreads and bank lending standards then 
decide whether finance is available at all. Policy rates and long yields have both risen and Eurozone banks have 
tightened standards on corporate loans (net +8.6%), so the squeeze on construction is larger than the 25-50bps of 
policy hikes alone suggests.

In the US, long yields rather than the policy rate set housing finance costs. The 30-year mortgage rate is set off 
the 10-year Treasury yield plus a spread. Since 2022, monthly changes in the mortgage rate correlate by 0.89 with 
changes in the 10-year yield and only 0.13 with changes in the Fed Funds target. The recent episode shows this: The 
mortgage rate climbed from 6.05% in February to 7.33% in October and the 10-year yield rose from 4.1% to 5.1%, 
while the upper bound of the Fed's target range stayed at 3.75% until September. The mortgage-to-Treasury spread, 
at about 2.2 points, is narrower than the 2.8 points of 2023, so long yields explain the entire increase. The Fed hike 
explains little of it: the gap between the 10-year yield and the Fed's upper bound widened from about 40bps to 
about 110 bps, so this is a repricing of the curve rather than a policy move.

Long yields hit developers on both sides of the same project. A higher mortgage rate cuts what households can 
borrow. A higher long-term yield raises the discount rate on future rents and sale proceeds, which lowers the value 
of a completed property and raises the return a new project must earn. A project viable at a 4% ten-year yield can 
fail at 5% even if construction costs are unchanged. Starts, permits and order books therefore respond quickly to 
long yields, so we use the 10-year yield as the central rate variable for the US and as one of two for Europe. We read 
the 10-year yield as a summary of wider financial conditions rather than as the only channel: it moves with the whole 
curve, while short rates and bank credit standards matter too. The same logic applies to commercial real estate, 
where capitalization rates tend to follow long yields. 
Turning to the comparison with Europe, mortgage rates track long yields tightly in the US but only loosely in 
Europe, where the short end of the curve, anchored by policy rates, matters more for households. In the US, the 
correlation of three-month changes has been mostly above 0.75 since 2021 and is 0.90 today, against 0.38 with the 
Fed Funds rate since 2015. In Europe the link differs by country. Germany is closest to the 10-year yield (0.67 since 
2015), then Italy (0.44) and Spain (0.33); France is the outlier (0.21), with a negative rolling correlation in about 70% 
of the months of 2023–2025. For the Eurozone as a whole, house-purchase borrowing costs correlate with the Bund 
by 0.52 but 0.78 with the ECB deposit rate. This suggests that European mortgage costs depend more on the 
national mix of floating and short-fixed-rate loans, and on gradual bank pricing, than US mortgages priced off the 
10-year yield. Long yields nevertheless remain the better guide to project financing and property valuations.

Residential: Building less, waiting more  
US: starts down, builders discounting 
The US housing recovery stalled in spring and has not resumed. Starts peaked at 1.52mn (annualized) in March, 
fell to 1.18mn in May, rebounded to 1.37mn in July and slipped to 1.22mn in September, 5.2% below August and 
7.4% below a year earlier. Single-family starts are holding up (859,000, +2.6% y/y), but starts in buildings with five 
or more units fell to 349,000, 26% below a year ago, as rental developers face higher financing costs. Permits of 
1.40mn are 3.2% below a year ago, so the pipeline is not building. The sequence fits the mortgage-rate path, which 
began to rise in March, well before the Fed's September move.

Higher mortgage rates cut affordability as much as a 14% price rise. Our calculations show that the rise in the 30-
year mortgage rate since February has lifted the monthly payment on a given loan by 14%, equivalent to about 
USD340 a month on a USD400,000 mortgage. That equals a 14% price increase at constant payments. Buyers have 
stepped back even though prices are barely rising. 
2027–2028 outlook: public forecasts show no US recovery in 2027. Both public forecasts that cover the period are 
cautious. One projects total starts of 1.34mn in 2026, 1.27mn in 2027 and 1.25mn in 2028. The other projects the 
same 1.34mn for 2026 and 1.30mn in 2027, and assumes the 30-year mortgage rate averages 7.5% in 2027. Both 
are below the 2019–2022 average. 
Builders are cutting prices to move homes; house prices are adjusting through volumes. New-home sales were 
676,000 annualized in September, 5% below a year earlier, and unsold inventory equals 8.4 months of supply. The 
median new-home price was USD385,400 in August, 7.8% lower y/y, and 43% of builders reported cutting prices in 
October. The builder sentiment index fell to 29 in October, its lowest since 2019. 
Existing-home sales are stuck because of mortgage lock-in. At around 4mn a year, sales are well below the 6mn 
of 2020-2021, because owners with low-rate legacy mortgages do not want to sell. Lock-in prevents forced selling, 
which supports prices, but keeps transactions and builder activity low. National house-price indices show gains of 
2.1% y/y in August on one measure and 2.4% on a purchase-only measure.  
Europe: an uneven pipeline 
Permits show recovery from a low base in some countries, weakness in others. Eurozone permits were about 97 
in May (2019 = 100), a few points below pre-pandemic levels and well below the 2022 peak of 120. A one-off June 
surge in Dutch registrations linked to grid-connection rules distorts the data, so we exclude the Netherlands and 
stop the Eurozone series in May. Spain is at 241, more than twice its 2019 level. Germany shows the clearest 
improvement: After a collapse to 50 in 2024, permits in January–August are 12-13% above the previous year, but at 
66 the index is still a third below 2019. France follows the same pattern, with permits up 13% in 2025 from a 2024 
low, but at 74 the situation is getting worse before it gets better. Permits are down 2% over January–September, the 
latest three months show a drop of about 10% and developers' new-home sales fell -23.6% in the second quarter. 
European house prices still rise almost everywhere, but momentum is fading. Q2 nominal prices were up 12.1% 
y/y in Spain, 6.0% in Ireland, 4.3% in the Netherlands and 4.0% in both the Eurozone and Italy, but only 0.6% in 
Germany and -0.8% in France. In France, existing-home prices are falling, with houses (-1.3%) weaker than flats (-
0.1%) after a muted recovery in 2025. Political and fiscal uncertainty ahead of the 2027 presidential election and a 
renewed energy-driven rise in inflation and rates have deepened the fall in real terms. Eurozone prices are up only

Weak order books show European demand is still waning. Order-book confidence is negative in almost all major 
European economies, so demand is too weak to rebuild project pipelines. Only Spain among the biggest markets is 
positive: +3.5 in September (+5.4 in October), against -17.4 for the EU27 in September. A structural housing shortage

volumes well into 2027. 
2027–2028 outlook: residential lags in both regions; stabilization depends on yields. We expect European 
construction output to grow by less than the roughly 2% a year (2.0% in 2026, 2.2% in 2027 and 1.9% in 2028). The 
country detail is weaker than the headline. French housing starts are expected to fall again in 2027 after a modest 
rebound in 2026. UK private housing output is projected down 10% in 2026 and flat in 2027, and Germany's 2026 
forecast for real construction turnover was cut to +1.0% from +2.5%. We expect residential to remain the weakest 
segment in both regions in 2027, stabilizing gradually in 2028 only if long yields retreat. 
Commercial, office and industrial: weak and increasingly bifurcated 
US: Data centers hide the weakness 
Private non-residential construction looks stable only because of data centers. Excluding them, spending is down 
-3.9% y/y in September, against +2.3% for the total. The weakness is broad. Offices excluding data centers are down 
-8.4% and sit 35% below their 2020 peak. Warehouses are down -8.0% and 39% below their 2023 peak. Commercial 
building is down -4.4%, education -7.4% and healthcare -1.7%. Power is the other winner: up +12.1% (USD18.6bn) as 
generation and grids are built out. 
Factory construction is falling because the chip-plant boom is ending, not because factory demand is. 
Manufacturing construction is down -17.9% y/y (USD36.5bn) and 33% below its September 2024 peak. Computer, 
electronic and electrical plants explain all of it: down -41.7% (USD 37.6bn) and 58% below their mid-2024 peak. 
Excluding them, factory building is up +1.0% and transportation-equipment plants are up +38%. Equipment demand 
is firm, with the manufacturing PMI at 55.5 in October and core capital goods orders up +13.7% y/y. Our reading is 
that the building phase of the chip wave is rolling off while equipment spending continues. Tariffs and the pace of 
reshoring announcements are the swing factors.

Property fundamentals point to a trough, not a rebound. Industrial real estate is turning: vacancy is 6.8%, 
absorption is up +69% y/y year to date and the pipeline has grown +23% to 324mn sq ft, the highest since mid-2024. 
That argues for warehouse construction bottoming in 2027. Offices are slower as vacancy is close to 20% and 2026 
completions are the lowest in more than 30 years, but the pipeline is up +20% from its Q1 low, so further declines 
should narrow. Retail has almost no pipeline (0.4% of inventory). 
Lenders are tightening on real estate construction. Banks report tighter standards for construction and land loans 
(net +15.5%, from -3.7% in July) and for non-farm non-residential property (+8.8%), while demand falls (-25.9% and 
-10.5%). Business lending holds up better, with loans up 10% y/y, but standards are tightening (net +1.7% for large 
firms, +5.1% for small) and demand is down about 12%. Costs add to the squeeze: steel mill prices are up 28% y/y, 
and new warehouse, industrial and office building costs are up 4.5%, 5.7% and 6.0%. 
Leading indicators and forecasts do not point to a quick recovery. The architecture billings index stood at 44.7 in 
September, well below the 50 that separates expansion from contraction. Contractor backlog is 8.7 months overall 
and 11.3 months for data-center contractors, while heavy-industrial backlog is at its lowest since March. Consensus 
forecasts see office construction spending falling -5.1% in 2026 and -4.5% in 2027, manufacturing falling -11.6% and 
-0.6%, and warehouses falling -1.7% before rising +1.4% in 2027. 
 
Europe: shrinking office pipeline, no pull from factories 
Permits show a commercial pipeline that is flat at best and shrinking in offices. Eurozone permits for non-office 
commercial and industrial buildings are at 99% of their 2019 floor area, up +1% y/y. Office permits are 41% below 
2019 and 5% below a year ago. The country split is wide, with Germany the weak spot: Non-office permits are 31% 
below 2019 and down -18% y/y. Office permits are more than half below 2019. France is flat for non-office (95% of 
2019, +0.4% y/y), with offices about half their 2019 level. Spain is the outlier: Non-office permits are about three 
times their 2019 level (+7% y/y) and office permits more than six times, which may partly reflect data-center and 
logistics projects, although the data do not separate them.

Financing is dearer and banks are cautious. Eurozone firms pay 3.90% on new loans, 40bps more than a year ago 
and more than double the 2019 average of 1.58% (Germany 4.05%, France 3.81%, Spain 3.72%). Banks report tighter 
standards on enterprise loans (net +8.6%) and weaker loan demand (net -6.4%), with fixed investment a drag. Credit 
growth is accelerating nevertheless: Loans to non-financial companies are up +4.3% y/y (Spain +5.6%, Italy +4.3%, 
Germany +2.3%), which is mildly supportive. 
Factories offer no demand pull for new buildings. Capacity utilization is 78.6% against 82.2% on average in 2019 
(Germany 78.7% against 84.6%), and manufacturing output is down -1.7% y/y. Industrial confidence and order books 
are improving from low levels, with Eurozone industrial order books at -14.8 against -23.1 a year ago and Germany's 
at -19.7 against -38.8. Firms have little pressure to add capacity. As of early 2026, logistics take-up was 5-6mn sq m 
a quarter against a 9.6mn peak in late 2021, and net absorption was not expected to recover before 2027. New 
office supply is the lowest since 2020, and investment volumes are 40-45% below their peak. 
2027–2028 outlook: commercial and industrial construction stays weak but resilient. In both regions, tighter 
credit, structurally lower office demand and spare factory capacity keep conventional projects on hold. Growth 
comes only where a structural driver exists: power, data centers and, at the margin, logistics. US factory construction 
should stabilize in 2027 after a steep fall, and warehouse building should bottom while vacancy stays low. European 
offices keep shrinking, with Spain the exception. The main risks are tariffs, the pace of reshoring announcements 
and a further rise in long yields, which raises the return commercial projects must earn.

Infrastructure and public investment: a buffer, not a boom 
Europe: Germany accelerates, France retrenches, EU money ends 
Europe's infrastructure looks strong in aggregate and is uneven underneath. Public investment is forecast at 3.8% 
of GDP in 2026 and 2027, up from 3.6% in 2025. Germany rises from 3.3% to 3.7%, France eases from 4.4% to 4.3% 
and Spain peaks in 2026. Civil engineering output is flat y/y at the Eurozone level, with Germany up +7%, France 
down -5% and Spain down -4%. Output confirms the divergence between civil engineering and building as Eurozone 
construction output was 4.8% above its 2021 level in August, driven by civil engineering (14.5% above) while building 
activity is 10% below. Italy stands out at 35% above 2021, helped by public works, while France is 12% below. 
Germany is recovering (+10% y/y) but is at its 2021 level overall, with building activity 20% below.

Germany is the engine, but money flows more slowly than the headlines suggest. The EUR500bn infrastructure 
fund (EUR300bn federal, EUR100bn Länder, EUR100bn climate) plans outlays of EUR58bn in 2026, EUR55bn in 
2027 and EUR57bn in 2028, and transport spending falls from EUR22.0bn to EUR17.7bn in 2027, partly as projects 
move under the defense exemption. Only EUR14bn of the federal pillar was spent in 2025, 74% of what was 
planned. Civil engineering orders jumped +36% y/y in August, but on a few large rail and Bavarian contracts, not a 
broad upswing. Defense adds a new leg: EUR4.6bn of barracks contracts (more than 300 buildings at about 130 
sites) were signed in August, and the 2027 defense budget of EUR109.7bn is set to reach 3.5% of GDP by 2029. 
France is retrenching while EU recovery money ending this year is a headwind for Italy and Spain in 2027. Public 
works volumes are forecast to fall -8% in 2026 and a further -7% in 2027, about 15% cumulatively, with local 
authorities down -12% and the state down -10%. Public tenders are down -26% and contracts signed down -27% in 
January–August. Colas reports its French roads order book down -12%. EUR450bn of the EUR573bn EU recovery 
fund has been paid. Milestones were due on 31 August and all payments must be completed by 31 December 2026. 
Investment funded by the facility is expected to fall in 2027. Italy has received EUR166bn, but its public works under 
the plan are only 6.2% complete by value, with more than EUR75bn still to execute. About EUR35bn of cohesion 
funds have been reprogrammed, EUR11.9bn of it to defense and military mobility.

Pipeline indicators are mixed. Transport contract awards rose +16% in the 12 months to July (USD166bn), but 
August fell to USD8.8bn from USD13.8bn. Non-building starts are up +18% year to date, driven by electric power 
and utilities (+70%), while highway and bridge starts are up just +0.9% and environmental works are down -4.1%. 
Municipal bond issuance is 4% higher year to date (USD511bn, all purposes), although it fell -15% in October. Utilities 
plan capital spending of USD239bn in 2026, rising to USD276bn in 2028 and USD1.36trn over 2026–2030. 
Global infrastructure should hold up in 2027–2028, but 2027 is a transition year. Demand is secure in networks, 
defense and German rail, and contractor backlogs are at records. The risks are about funding and volumes. In 
Europe, the end of EU recovery money and French local-authority cuts weigh on 2027, while Germany's fund ramps 
up more slowly than planned. In the US, a federal extension that expires on 11 December, or a reauthorization 
below current levels, would hit new awards, and input-cost inflation above 10% erodes real spending. Capacity is 
the common constraint: contractors compete for the same trades as data centers and grids. 
 
Developers and contractors: hope for the best, but expect the worst 
Developers and contractors face lower sales and higher costs, most acutely in Europe. Weak demand means 
lower sales, slower launches and margin pressure for developers competing on price. More importantly, input-cost 
pressure is intensifying this year, raising the risk of delays, cancellations and higher working-capital needs, and 
ultimately of more insolvencies among leveraged players.

Materials add a second wave of cost pressure. Copper (LME cash) is at USD14,154 a ton, about 60% above its 
January 2025 level, and aluminum is about 23% higher. Strong demand from grids and data centers, disruption to 
energy and aluminum supply from the Middle East war and US tariffs have all contributed. In the US, steel mill 
products are up 49% since January 2025 (28% in the last twelve months), inputs to construction are up 11% y/y and 
overall construction costs are up 4.5% y/y. Cement and concrete reset to a much higher level after the 2022–2023 
energy shock triggered by Russia's invasion of Ukraine, with Eurozone cement producer prices up 44% between 
January 2021 and January 2023. Prices have not come down since, and US cement prices show the same step. 
Carbon-cost pass-through (via the EU Emissions Trading Scheme and Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism) will 
keep them elevated for longer. Weak demand prevents firms from passing on this cumulative cost shock.

Labor costs add to the squeeze. US average hourly earnings in construction are up +4.2% y/y and the employment 
cost index +3.0%; industry estimates put wage inflation at about 5%. Eurozone hourly labor costs in construction are 
up +3.9% y/y, from +1.9% in Italy and +2.0% in France to +5.1% in Germany and Spain. Labor shortages affect 78% 
of markets worldwide and more than 93% in Europe. Cost consultancies expect US bid-price escalation of +4.25% in 
2026 and +4.0% in 2027, German tender prices up about +5% in 2026 and about +3% a year in 2027–2028 and UK 
tender prices up +3.25% in 2026. They flag tariffs and fuel prices as upside risks. 
Weak demand stops contractors passing costs on, and insolvencies are rising. Large-company insolvencies are at 
record levels. Of the 376 cases in the first nine months of 2026, 51 were in construction, the third most-affected sector 
after retail (69) and services (62); these three sectors account for 48% of the total. The construction bankruptcy index 
is well above its 2021 base in every major economy, at 237 in France, 166 In Germany and 193 in the Eurozone, 
though Spain's is 22 points lower than a year earlier. The pressure is most acute for leveraged developers and 
contractors serving housing, squeezed between selling prices and input costs. 
Margins depend on contract structure and on how long the cost shock lasts. Fixed-price contractors absorb cost 
increases until they can reprice; cost-plus or index-linked contracts pass them through with a lag. Pass-through is 
easiest in segments with long order books and scarce capacity, such as data centers and power, and hardest in 
housing and conventional commercial work, where weak demand limits pricing. 
Looking ahead, insolvencies stay high. We expect global insolvencies to rise by 1% in 2027 and dip about 1% in 
2028, still 22% above pre-pandemic levels, with construction accounting for 15-20% of insolvency counts in most 
countries. Pressure should stay concentrated in leveraged developers and contractors serving housing and 
conventional commercial work, where rates and input costs remain high into 2027, while contractors in data centers, 
power and networks are better placed to reprice.
