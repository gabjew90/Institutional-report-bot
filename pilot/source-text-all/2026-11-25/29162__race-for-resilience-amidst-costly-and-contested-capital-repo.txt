Introduction 
The era of globalisation led to the largest trade imbalance in modern history. Previous imbalances were resolved by 
accord or by conflict; this time an accord looks unlikely, so the shift towards national interests and resilience 
continues to accelerate. That shift requires sponsorship from governments whose balance sheets are already 
stretched, just as artificial intelligence (AI) drives the largest capital cycle in technology history. We take the race 
for resilience, the emerging risk of a fiscal crisis and AI in turn and argue that an in-depth understanding of each 
individually and collectively will be key to navigating the period ahead.

1. National resilience 
China’s 2025 surplus reached a record $US1.2 trillion, the largest ever recorded by any country, and is on course to 
be surpassed in 2026 as overproduction and weak domestic consumption flood global markets with low-priced 
goods. Germany shows what this ‘China Shock 2.0’ 1, a second wave of the Chinese import competition that 
hollowed out Western manufacturing in the 2000s, can do: its long-running trade balance of manufactured goods 
has reduced notably. 
Imbalances this large do eventually unwind. Even if China succeeds with Shock 2.0 and its next phase 3.0, it will 
eventually find it is consuming its own demand. That is unlikely, however, as trading partners, watching their 
industries hollow out, are forcing the adjustment much sooner, as they have been since the mid-2010s. This is the 
geopolitical abrasion now under way.

In the past, global imbalances were resolved by multilateral accords (e.g. the Plaza Accord) or by conflict followed by 
accords. An accord this time, at least in the near term, appears unlikely. China is unwilling to surrender its 
ascendancy. It is pursuing a 2.0 and then a 3.0 phase of industrial upgrading and will experience internal disruption if 
it concedes. The US is equally unwilling to concede its former upper hand, though it no longer holds all the cards. It is 
now a substantial net debtor. Its credibility has also been eroded as it has signalled on numerous occasions differing 
rules for it as hegemon, including through global financial crisis (GFC) bailouts and money printing, that debased 
assets that foreign holders had treated as safe; the 2022 seizure of Russian USD reserves, that confiscated another 
nation’s funds; the abrasive ‘Liberation Day’ for friend and foe; the Iran war; and, more recently, the threat of using 
the dollar as a weapon. As US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent put it, “either you are with us or against us”.

The acceleration towards national resilience 
When Nixon ended USD convertibility to gold in 1971, the dollar became a 
fiat currency backed by the word and trust of the US. That trust rested on 
US military strength: protection of allies and naval supremacy. The Iran 
war has raised questions about that strength, including the depletion of US 
missile stockpiles. On display for all to see, many nations are now prudently 
reassessing how much they can rely on both US military protection and the 
US dollar. Unintentionally, the US’s isolated ‘excursions’, and the 
weaknesses they have exposed, could have profound longer-term 
implications. 
Before the Middle East conflict, national security referred to a nation’s 
development of its own large military or fostering alliances with nations 
that have them. Drones striking critical infrastructure and the closure of 
the Strait of Hormuz have dealt that mindset a blow. Japan, South Korea, 
arguably Taiwan, much of Europe and the Gulf states, all reliant on US 
security guarantees, are now reassessing them with haste. 
The reassessment reaches the US-China rivalry itself: President Xi Jinping has twice raised the risk of a “Thucydides 
Trap” 2, in which a rising China displaces the incumbent US, while openly asking whether the two can avoid it and 
establish a new paradigm for relations between them. 
Until a new balance is agreed, global abrasion and the race for national resilience will only gather pace. 
The national interest and resilience framework 
In ‘An erosion of trust in an era of national policy musts?’ (2025), we set out our framework for the shift from 
globalisation to resilience (or national interests – NI) and the investment thesis embedded therein. NI means 
individual nations working to remove vulnerabilities in five areas: 
• National security: military strength, adjusted for modern techniques (drones and supply chokepoints) 
• Energy security: oil, gas and renewables through to nuclear 
• Essential inputs: food, health and all critical components, from medical essentials to rare earths and magnets 
• Technology: leading, or at least not falling behind, in the AI ‘space race’ 
• Infrastructure: roads, ports, energy, power grids, data centres…everything 
Following more than three decades of outsourcing, almost every developed nation is chronically underinvested in at 
least one if not all five. We emphasise that these are ‘need to’, not ‘want to’, priorities and, now known, are very 
unlikely to be reversed. 
Provided it can be funded, the emerging era of NI or resilience will be a powerful influence in the coming years, 
creating opportunities nation by nation right across the framework laid out above. 
 
2. The risk of a fiscal crisis

US government debt has exceeded $US40 trillion 3, much of it short term, so the US must refinance a substantial

approximately $US2 trillion 4 and do so while accelerating the reduction of its national-interest dependencies, 
funding and replenishing weaponry for the wars involving Iran and Russia/Ukraine and encouraging the build-out of 
AI in order to win the global AI race.

The US fiscal position commands most of the attention, although many Western nations, including Japan, France 
and the United Kingdom, face similar challenges. As 2026 draws to a close, geopolitical and trade friction, stickier 
inflation and higher bond yields have brought the risk of a ‘fiscal crisis’ into the market lexicon. We wish to be 
clear: by a crisis we mean a market event of real consequence, not a prediction of a sovereign default. 
The concern is increasingly not whether such a crisis will flare, but when. Said another way, the bond market is the 
Achilles heel of the evolving new economic environment, and everyone – friends, foe, adversaries and policymakers 
themselves – knows this. 
Before assessing this emerging risk, it is worth understanding how Western sovereign balance sheets arrived at 
this point. 
How we got here 
Demographics: the cheque that was always coming 
The story begins with demographics, a long-known and slow-moving challenge that has steadily moved closer to the 
fore. As the baby-boomer cohort approached retirement, pension and healthcare entitlements that had never been 
properly planned for began to rise. With inadequate private saving, the burden fell increasingly on government 
finances. The cheque that was always coming is increasingly due, producing persistent fiscal deficits and 
ever-higher government indebtedness.  
Yet this was only the beginning. A series of other trends, events and unintended consequences would go on to 
compound the problem considerably. 
Globalisation and the financialisation of everything 
During the era broadly known as globalisation, it became common practice for large multinational companies to 
draw on the world’s resources, producing high-quality goods in low-cost locations in order to maximise returns for 
themselves and their shareholders (some of them went on to be known as ‘magnificent’). Little consideration was 
given to the structural side effects on individual nations, or to the fragilities and vulnerabilities that would 
accumulate, unchecked, over more than three decades. 
Nor was it widely understood that a model of outsourced production, concentrated primarily in China, with its 
enormous population, cheap labour and a managed, low-valued currency, would produce ever-larger Chinese trade 
surpluses. Those surpluses were recycled into Western, and particularly US, financial markets as foreign-currency 
reserves, largely invested in ‘safe’ bonds such as US Treasuries. The effect was to suppress the cost of capital quietly 
and artificially. This was Greenspan’s conundrum: long-term yields that stayed low even as the Federal Reserve 
raised rates, and over many years it was an important influence in encouraging the ‘financialisation of everything’.

The conundrum ultimately manifested in a global credit bubble and the GFC, that nearly collapsed the Western 
financial system and required government-funded bailouts. That experience laid the groundwork for the playbook 
used in the pandemic response. Between them, near-zero interest rates and trillions of dollars of quantitative easing 
(QE), utilised for more than a decade, supercharged an already heavily financially engineered global economy and 
placed an extraordinary burden on sovereign balance sheets. 
Inequality, populism and the collapse of the centre 
Inadvertently, this system also drove inequality in many Western economies to acute levels. The resulting social 
backlash contributed to the collapse of the political centre, a shift towards polarisation and the rise of populism. 
That rise, in turn, set off a chaotic and abrasive pushback against the many extreme imbalances that globalisation 
had created. 
Widespread sovereign indebtedness 
Demographic pressure, the post-GFC bailouts and the vast monetary financing programs of the pandemic combined 
to inflate government debt. They were underpinned by entrenched, and false, confidence that inflation was dormant, 
and by a belief that once the Rubicon of QE had been crossed, policymakers could ‘just do more’, as though the bill 
would never fall due. Countries such as the US also fought a succession of costly military campaigns, adding to 
the strain. 
By the mid-2020s, the scale of government debt is now common knowledge. So too is the reality that, in several 
large economies, annual fiscal deficits remain substantial, with outlays far exceeding receipts even in a reasonably 
robust economic environment. Few wish to consider what those deficits would look like in an economic downturn. 
Global imbalances of this degree cannot persist 
Questions about a fiscal crisis, and how unsustainable trends might be arrested, have been building for some time. 
More recently, a succession of events, set out in Figure 3, has made the task both more difficult and more pressing. 
Each of them is part of a pushback against the imbalances of globalisation, which by the late 2010s had grown too 
large to sustain. Each traces the abrasive unwinding of the system so far. 
Additionally, from the pandemic onwards, the return of inflation as a by-product of the evolving situation meant 
that a) the primary source of government funding since the GFC, central bank QE (money printing), was no 
longer available, and b) increased the cost of capital, exacerbating the fiscal challenges with much higher 
interest costs.

In this note and in earlier writing, we have sought to bring clarity to this process. Much of our focus has been on 
trade imbalances; on the fragilities created by decades of unchecked outsourcing; on the challenge of shifting, out of 
necessity rather than choice, towards national resilience; and the abrasive nature of how that may occur. We have 
also asked how it will all be funded – hint: mandate, legislate, confiscate… (and then, eventually, print) – arguing 
that existing rules and norms will need to be broken if national-interest initiatives are to succeed. 
The financial implications of the reversal of globalisation 
Any system left unchecked for three decades is not merely imbalanced; it also contains complex interdependencies. 
Reversing the imbalances of globalisation is far more complicated than it appears, as the financial system also 
adapted and thrived in tandem. The era of globalisation also meant globalised financial markets and the free flow of 
capital across borders. We suspect that only a few appreciate the intricacies involved, although we expect awareness 
to rise notably. The implications are threefold. 
1. If trade surpluses and deficits narrow, capital accounts will also narrow. If the US reshores production and 
corrects its trade imbalances, its capital inflows will reverse too, as they have begun to do, due to the series of 
abrasive events outlined above. The conditions that quietly enabled the financialisation of everything and the 
incredible level of government borrowing will unwind with them. Funding the current system is becoming more 
difficult, implying a stubbornly higher cost of capital. Yet the stock of financialised debt and accumulated 
government debt remains and continues to grow at a persistent rate. Many former holders of US debt have 
already stopped buying (funding). 
2. Nations repatriate savings to fund national imperatives. Governments now acutely aware of their national 
‘needs’ are each scrambling to reduce their own vulnerabilities and have resilience initiatives to finance. They will 
increasingly be tempted to encourage domestic savings pools to return from globalised, cross-border locations. 
Concentrated holdings in the largest bond and equity markets are likely to be repatriated, and it need only happen 
at the margin. How much money is invested passively in global indices with little or no due diligence, and does that 
serve national interests, or merely the largest companies in the largest economies? 
3. The banking system is globally interlinked. In a globalised financial system, banks, insurers and pension funds all 
own everyone else’s debt. As governments increasingly draw on the balance sheets of their national pension 
funds, insurers and national banks, pressure is likely to build within the financial system itself, whether through 
credit contraction or via rising sovereign risk. Fiscal stress in even one significant country would not remain 
confined to its own banking system. It would spread quickly to international banks as questions about who holds 
what erodes trust. 
The summary is that fiscal positions are deteriorating and more funding is needed, and yet funders are either already 
in retreat or may increasingly withdraw due to national-interest initiatives and/or fiscal risk concerns. The resultant 
risk is that yields are pushed ever higher, exacerbating the challenge through higher interest costs and larger 
deficits, and increasing the talk of a potential fiscal crisis. 
Japan: the recent coordinated intervention and the interdependencies 
Japan illustrates the point well. A world-renowned producer of high-end goods, it usually generates a healthy trade 
surplus. As part of the US-led Western alliance, it has enjoyed national security through its relationship with the US 
and, until recently, spent less than 2% of GDP on defence. Over many decades it has accumulated large savings 
pools, including significant pension assets, many of them invested around the world and considerably in US assets. 
Following the ‘Liberation Day’ tariffs of April 2025, Japan was criticised, despite being a close ally, for its trade 
surplus with the US and for underinvesting in defence. More recently, a confluence of factors, including persistent 
currency weakness, has lifted Japanese bond yields out of their multi-decade slumber. The war in Iran has added 
pressure on one of Japan’s best-known vulnerabilities, its reliance on imported energy, while the changing nature of 
modern warfare, on display in both the Middle East and Ukraine/Russia, accelerates the urgency of upgrading 
its defences.

In the name of national interest and resilience, Japan has pressing priorities at home and needs to fund them. This is 
precisely what the US asked for. Yet doing so will likely require repatriating assets from overseas, and Japan is a large 
holder of US Treasuries. The US needs more holders of its debt, not fewer, but its efforts to correct trade imbalances 
(not to mention the complications created by Iran) expose the interdependence. For allies such as Japan to spend 
more on defence and invest more in national priorities may ultimately require selling US bonds, placing further 
pressure on the bond yield levels and the US funding position. Japan is only one example: the Gulf countries are 
similarly large global investors with ever more pressing priorities now at home. Similar linkages run across the global 
banking, insurance and pension systems. 
The recent coordinated US/Japan intervention in USD/JPY to strengthen the yen suggests the US is aware of this 
unintended consequence. The operation was notable in three respects. It was unusually coordinated; it used repo 
facilities, borrowing against US Treasuries rather than selling them; and it involved selling euros rather than dollars, 
notably without notifying European counterparts. At first glance, it appears a creative solution to what is likely to be 
a persistent and growing challenge: the need for capital to be repatriated for national purposes, globally. 
The recent approach towards Japan illustrates both the focus placed on Treasury yields and the willingness to use 
unconventional policy tools. These measures sit within a broader effort to manage the cost of US borrowing: 
unusually large, long-end buybacks, a greater reliance on bill issuance, and support for stablecoins (dollar-pegged 
digital tokens backed largely by Treasury bills) as a new source of demand for short-dated Treasuries. In isolation, 
each of these measures may appear relatively minor. Together, they form a clear pattern of a Treasury willing to use 
inventive tools in pursuit of lower yields. It also carries an embedded signal: the US does not want current holders or 
potential buyers to leave US markets.  
However, in doing so, the pressure was effectively transferred to another market, in this case through the selling of 
euros, and most likely therefore of euro-denominated bonds. Japan is one of the largest holders of European debt, 
and French government bonds, as a somewhat higher-yielding market, are a significant holding. This may be one 
reason French government bond yields have been a topical story in recent weeks. While most attention is on the US 
fiscal position, if a fiscal crisis does occur, it is far more likely to originate in a smaller but still significant economy 
such as France. As if on cue, France has been experiencing a heightened level of social unrest relating to its fiscal 
situation. This is adding to the pressure on French bond yields.

France: where a crisis could start 
France faces a fiscal and sovereign debt situation similar to that of the US, but because it sits within the European 
Union and uses a common currency, it cannot fund itself through its own central bank, and markets can test it in a 
way they cannot test the US. Its political situation is fluid, and it is wrestling with difficult fiscal dynamics; a 
weakening economy; and threats to national resilience, both in security and in energy, from Russia and Iran. Its 
national needs are pressing, and so is the need to fund them. France is also a large participant in global finance 
through its major banks, insurers and pension system, whose assets reside in global markets, much of them in the 
US. As the need to address national interests escalates, so will the pressure to repatriate. And that means from the 
largest markets, i.e. the US. 
The risk of a repatriation domino effect 
Recent abrasive trade negotiations with allies also do not bode well. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney’s recent 
comment about agreements written in pencil underscores the point. An interconnected system calls for a 
coordinated and collaborative approach. Berating allies encourages an each-to-their-own mentality and with it 
withdrawal, when there are genuine needs requiring urgent funding at home. Continued friction risks setting off a 
repatriation domino effect across financial markets, one that would ultimately undermine the very foundations of 
the financialisation of everything. Policymakers will need to tread carefully and seek a coordinated approach to what 
is an exceptionally complex rebalancing. 
The era of globalisation was defined by the suppression of volatility. The era we are now entering, with the risk of 
such a domino effect, seems likely to be anything but. The need to “mandate, legislate, confiscate… (and then 
later, eventually, print)” to fund national resilience is likely to be a significant source of volatility in 2027 and the 
years ahead.

AI is an incredible transformational technology that is anticipated to have a profound impact on the global economy. 
AI has become a matter of national importance: to lose the AI super-race is to lose Cold War II, so it must 
be won.

AI is the second structural force bearing on public finances: its build-out needs vast capital, and its disruption 
could weaken the cash flows that service the world’s debt. 
Where did all the value come from? 
Since the release of ChatGPT in late 2022, well over $US20 trillion 5 of value has been attributed to the companies 
involved in this new transformational technology. The number is much greater if recent borrowing (debt) to aid the 
build-out of the essential infrastructure is included, greater again if investments in future energy supply are also 
included. These numbers are enormous.

A podcast conversation between Luke Gromen and Peter Alexander highlights how differently the two AI

“They’re not trying to create God in a box like they are at [leading AI companies]. They’re saying, Hey, can I use this

The contrast is stark: the US is playing ‘who wants to be a trillionaire’, while China is focused on how AI can help it 
expand market share. 
As such, the revenue ramp-up (demand) the AI companies need is racing against rapidly rising competition (supply), 
increasing model capability and efficiency, better token usage and collapsing token prices. It seems that bridging the 
gap to profitability will require the Jevons paradox: cheaper AI driving token usage far beyond anything anticipated. 
As a result, many observers in financial markets are increasingly realising that the largest capital cycle in the 
history of technology is currently underwritten by two private companies that may be challenged to achieve 
profitability. And given the lack of moat and the competitive dynamics with China, they may not do so. In other 
words, the justification for the valuation surge that has been ascribed to AI business models for their ‘new products’ 
looks increasingly problematic. 
A second argument holds that the (enormous) valuation ascribed to the AI stack reflects new revenue opportunities: 
with their large language models (LLMs) and the knowledge gathered from our use of AI, these companies will eat 
everyone else’s business models, and that is why the current valuations are justified. The cash flows of others will be 
redirected to these companies. On this view, the AI value creation will come from the soon-to-be disrupted. 
Valuations of this size will always be questioned. Nonetheless, they are priced into markets, as are the second-, third- 
and fourth-order beneficiaries, and cannot simply be taken back out. Either the valuations adjust to more 
appropriate levels, or the value must be delivered.  
If current valuations are correct, rapid disruption of the rest of the economy is coming, with a redistribution of 
cash flows that the system and governments will find hard to handle, especially at the pace it will arrive. The 
by-product of the prior system, a massive accumulation of debt, may then become a considerable problem: the 
indebted disrupted will not have the cash flow to service the indebted system. 
Recall that the previous globalisation regime, with ultra-low cost of capital and abundant liquidity, financialised 
everything and created a debt super-cycle. Governments were required to support it in the GFC and again in COVID 
and became indebted themselves. If broad segments are considerably disrupted, the ability to service outstanding 
debt would increasingly come under pressure: households (unemployment), corporates (default as business models 
are disrupted), banks (mortgage and corporate loan defaults) and governments (lower tax receipts, higher 
benefit outlays). 
If current valuations are not correct, and the value of AI will ultimately accrue to its users, the non-AI real economy, 
rather than to its creators and builders (as with the railway boom and the technology, media and 
telecommunications (TMT) bubble), the incredible valuation surge since the release of ChatGPT will correct. With a 
handful of mega stocks making up massive components of indices, passive investment means almost everyone 
is exposed. 
The outlook is never black or white, only shades of grey, and the AI outcome will probably be some combination of 
both. If pushed, the evidence leans notably towards the second scenario. Either way, AI is an unprecedented and 
rapid force of societal and economic disruption, and governments and policymakers will increasingly be called on 
to help manage the transition.

Conclusion 
Two structural trends, the reversal of globalisation and the rapidly approaching AI transformation, are increasingly 
disrupting the global economy and are central to the outlook for financial markets in 2027. Each is abrasive on its 
own; together they are ever more so. 
Markets are already registering the strain. Resilient growth, geopolitical abrasion, energy and inflation constraints, 
falling trust and concern about government financing are showing up in bond yields, which are climbing to levels that 
have previously proved troublesome. 
Greater emphasis on national interest and resilience likely means stronger nominal economic growth, although with 
it, higher inflation risk as geopolitical and supply-chain friction continues. Realistically, it would be naive to treat the 
Iran war as the last shock rather than the latest iteration in a more volatile and competitive world. 
The defining feature of this period is an abrasive, competitive environment where policymakers will lean towards 
using every available tool: large fiscal programs as well as accommodative monetary settings (including yield curve 
control) where possible. We anticipate policymakers will, as time passes, need to break rules and norms to facilitate 
funding, increasingly seeking to mandate, legislate, confiscate… (and yes, eventually when conditions permit, print). 
Previous imbalances were resolved by accord or conflict. Given the interdependencies set out here, an accord cannot 
be ruled out, even if it looks unlikely. Either way, this time the price seems likely to be paid in trust, the foundation on 
which the dollar system was built. As in life, trust is everything.

Investment implications 
What does this mean for… 
Bond markets 
The forces set out in this note, from geopolitical abrasion and resilience spending to AI capex and stretched 
sovereign balance sheets, all point to more supply and stickier inflation. We expect upward pressure on yields to 
persist in the near term, particularly in the US. We remain somewhat cautious on duration, with our strongest 
conviction in regional positioning: we favour Australia and New Zealand, where hiking cycles are more mature, and 
remain wary of the very long end. However, yields are approaching levels that have previously proved troublesome 
for growth, and we think pressure is building towards an inflection where the cost of capital bites. We aim to use 
moves to the top of our forecast ranges to add duration and are prepared to pivot to a longer stance should growth 
risks start to eventuate. 
Investment grade credit markets 
Investment grade fundamentals remain resilient and all-in yields continue to attract buyers, but dispersion beneath 
tight index spreads has risen materially. Widening has been concentrated in technology and communication, with 
stress coming from a historically large (and increasingly debt-funded) capital cycle, and rapid technological 
disruption. We expect US spreads to be more volatile, but ultimately remain largely range bound. As a result, we 
advocate for shorter, carry-focused positioning. We also believe this market will reward selection rather than beta 
and will create ‘baby with the bathwater’ situations, with sound credits being sold alongside the genuinely exposed. 
Active management will be key.  
High yield credit markets 
High yield spreads have widened from their lows in mid-2026. With all-in yields above 8% against a long-run average 
below 7% 6, we think valuations better reflect a more balanced risk outlook. Fundamentals have held up better than 
expected and should continue into 2027, but stress indicators are rising and a refinancing wall is approaching, likely 
leading to defaults in certain industries. Recoveries are likely to matter more as AI disruption threatens the cash 
flows of the soon-to-be disrupted, notably in the software sector. We are overall neutral on high yield credit, but 
within it, we favour higher-quality names and would avoid CCC-rated credit. 
Currency markets 
The US dollar has been supported this year by rate differentials, relative growth, AI-led equity outperformance and 
safe-haven demand. In the near term, we expect the USD to remain range bound, but the risks are asymmetric to 
the downside. A durable resolution in the Middle East would remove the safe-haven bid to the USD, while an AI-led 
equity correction would place the USD at the centre of the shock given the record levels of offshore ownership. 
Additionally, the Treasury’s inventive pursuit of lower yields could come at the expense of a weaker dollar. Over a 
longer horizon, the erosion of trust and the repatriation of foreign savings described above point the same way. We 
therefore do not favour outright long dollar exposure, preferring asymmetric short USD option structures to protect 
against those tail-risk scenarios.

Fixed income securities are subject to credit risk, 
which is the risk of loss of principal or loss of a 
financial reward stemming from a borrower’s 
failure to repay a loan or otherwise meet a 
contractual obligation. Credit risk arises whenever 
a borrower expects to use future cash flows to 
pay a current debt. Investors are compensated for 
assuming credit risk by way of interest payments 
from the borrower or issuer of a debt obligation. 
Credit risk is closely tied to the potential return of 
an investment, the most notable being that the 
yields on bonds correlate strongly to their 
perceived credit risk. 
Fixed income securities are also subject to interest 
rate risk, which is the risk that the prices of fixed 
income securities will increase as interest rates 
fall and decrease as interest rates rise. Interest 
rate changes are influenced by a number of 
factors, such as government policy, monetary 
policy, inflation expectations, and the supply and 
demand of securities. Fixed income securities with 
longer maturities or duration generally are more 
sensitive to interest rate changes. 
Quantitative easing (QE) is a government 
monetary policy used to increase the money 
supply by buying government securities or other 
securities from the market. Quantitative easing 
increased the money supply by flooding financial 
institutions with capital in an effort to promote 
increased lending and liquidity. 
A Treasury yield refers to the effective yearly 
interest rate the US government pays on money it 
borrows to raise capital through selling Treasury 
bonds, also referred to as Treasury notes or 
Treasury bills depending on maturity length. 
The yield curve is a line that plots the interest 
rates, at a set point in time, of bonds having equal 
credit quality, but differing maturity dates. The 
most frequently reported yield curve compares 
the 3-month, 2-year, 5-year, and 30-year US 
Treasury debt. This yield curve is used as a 
benchmark for other debt in the market, such as 
mortgage rates or bank lending rates. It is also 
used to predict changes in economic output and 
growth. 
The shape of the yield curve is closely scrutinized 
because it helps to give an idea of future interest 
rate change and economic activity. There are 
three main types of yield curve shapes: normal, 
inverted and flat (or humped). A normal yield 
curve is one in which longer maturity bonds have a 
higher yield compared to shorter-term bonds due 
to the risks associated with time. An inverted yield 
curve is one in which the shorter-term yields are 
higher than the longer-term yields, which can be a 
sign of upcoming recession. A flat (or humped) 
yield curve is one in which the shorter and longer-
term yields are very close to each other, which is 
also a predictor of an economic transition. The 
slope of the yield curve is also seen as important: 
the greater the slope, the greater the gap 
between short- and long-term rates.
