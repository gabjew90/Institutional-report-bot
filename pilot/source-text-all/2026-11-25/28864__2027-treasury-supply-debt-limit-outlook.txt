Treasury likely to hold coupon supply steady in the near term 
We expect the Treasury to maintain current coupon auction sizes in the near term 
before implementing modest increases in H227, with most of the additional 
issuance concentrated in the front end of the curve (Figure 1). At the November 
Quarterly Refunding, Treasury officials indicated that current auction sizes remain 
sufficient to accommodate potential changes in fiscal conditions and shifts in the 
Federal Reserve's balance sheet.  
The Treasury also stated that it expects to maintain nominal coupon and Floating 
Rate Note (FRN) auction sizes for the foreseeable future based on projected 
borrowing needs. Officials continue to monitor two important developments: the 
Federal Reserve's Treasury bill purchases associated with mortgage-backed 
securities (MBS) reinvestments and the growing demand for Treasury bills from 
private-sector investors.

Long-end issuance remains a wildcard 
The outlook for long-dated coupon issuance remains less certain. The Treasury's 
Office of Debt Management continues to evaluate investor demand across 
maturities and the potential financing costs associated with alternative issuance 
strategies. As a result, any decision to increase issuance at the long end is likely 
to depend on market conditions, demand trends and the Treasury's assessment of 
the overall cost of funding government deficits. We expect TIPS auction sizes to 
remain unchanged in 2027. 
Under our base-case scenario, coupon auction sizes in the two- through seven-
year sectors begin increasing in August 2027. This would result in gross coupon 
issuance of approximately USD4.5trn during 2027, compared with USD4.4trn in 
2026 (Figure 2). 
Treasury bills: supply and demand 
Near-term Treasury bill supply will primarily reflect seasonal fluctuations in 
government cash flows. Strong corporate and non-withheld tax receipts in 
December should allow the Treasury to reduce issuance of shorter-dated bills. 
However, this seasonal trend typically reverses in January, when Treasury cash 
outflows increase and bill auction sizes are expanded across the maturity 
spectrum. 
Federal Reserve demand for bills remains supportive. Although the Federal 
Reserve announced on 13 November that it would not conduct Treasury bill 
purchases for reserve management purposes during the upcoming four-week 
period, a policy that has been in place since mid-August, Treasury bill demand

from the Fed has not disappeared entirely. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York 
continues to purchase Treasury bills as part of MBS reinvestment operations, 
which are expected to total c.USD14bn over the upcoming four-week period. 
In Fiscal Year (FY) 2026, Treasury net-issued c.USD721bn bills. Given the 
expected size of federal deficits, we estimate net bill issuance could increase to 
c.USD800-900bn in 2027. The ultimate amount will depend in part on the timing 
and resolution of future debt-limit negotiations. 
At roughly USD7.4trn, Treasury bills currently account for about 23% of total 
marketable debt (Figures 3 and 4). This share remains below historical peaks and 
leaves room for the Treasury to rely more heavily on bills if officials decide to 
shorten the weighted average maturity (WAM) of federal debt. Any indication that 
the Treasury intends to modestly shorten WAM would likely be supportive for 
longer-dated Treasuries by reducing pressure on long-end supply.

In a nutshell 
Our base case remains that the Treasury will maintain coupon auction sizes 
through at least the first half of 2027 before implementing modest increases in the 
two- to seven-year sector later in the year. Gross coupon issuance is likely to rise 
only modestly, while TIPS supply should remain unchanged. 
Treasury bills are expected to play an increasingly important role in deficit 
financing, supported by robust demand from money market funds and Fed MBS 
reinvestments. The main uncertainty remains the long end of the curve, where 
Treasury continues to evaluate the trade-off between investor demand, market 
functioning and taxpayer financing costs. 
Debt limit: here we go again 
Debt limit negotiations are expected to create significant volatility in Treasury cash 
balance and bill issuance. Treasury projects an end-2026 cash balance of 
USD850bn, rising to USD1trn by end-Q127. However, the Treasury General 
Account (TGA) will likely decline sharply in Q227 and Q327, mirroring previous 
debt limit episodes – most notably June 2023 and June 2025, when the TGA fell 
to USD23bn and USD261bn (Figure 6), respectively, as the Treasury paid down 
bills to manage funding needs under the debt limit. 
Treasury debt is projected to hit the USD41.1trn statutory ceiling in early 2027 
(Figure 7), triggering the deployment of extraordinary measures to sustain 
government operations for several months. These measures, valued at a few 
hundred USDbn, can temporarily extend the X-date by delaying the point at which 
cash reserves are exhausted. 
The debt limit X-date is likely to occur in Q427, when both extraordinary measures 
and remaining cash reserves are depleted and markets begin pricing in potential 
default risks. Consistent with prior episodes, the Treasury is expected to reduce 
bill issuance and draw down the TGA ahead of the X-date. As the TGA declines, 
bank reserves increase correspondingly. Simultaneously, as the Treasury cuts bill 
issuance, money market funds will shift allocations from bills to repo, creating 
downward pressure on repo rates. 
Once Congress raises or suspends the debt limit, the Treasury will aggressively 
rebuild the TGA through substantial bill issuance. This expansion in bill supply and 
ensuing increase in the TGA will drain bank reserves and redirect money market 
fund flows back toward bill purchases.
