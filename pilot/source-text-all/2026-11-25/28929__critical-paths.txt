INTEREST RATE OUTLOOK 
 
As the end of an eventful year comes into sight, we are left to wonder what lies ahead.  
The course of the U.S. economy will depend on the following questions: 
• 
Is the peak of policy uncertainty behind us? While the midterm elections reduced 
some funding risks, they will not change the executive-led policy priorities of the 
current administration. 
• 
Will the Middle East stabilize? Official statements from both sides have indicated 
willingness to escalate and openness to a ceasefire. Declining energy prices reflect 
market optimism for a calmer path ahead. 
• 
Has the bond market found a steady state? While yields are off their highs, the drivers 
of the repricing have not been durably settled. 
It is too soon to say the worst is over, but the U.S. economy has grown through these and 
many other challenges. Our base expectation of continued growth remains intact. 
Following are our thoughts on the outlook.

The U.S. economy grew at a robust 3.4% annualized pace in the third quarter. Consumer 
spending continued its streak of growth, but the largest contributor was resurgent business 
investment, led by data centers. The gain in nonresidential fixed investment of 12.7% was 
the strongest pace seen since 2011. However, the import dependence of computing 
hardware significantly widened the trade deficit, weighing on overall growth. 
• 
With inflation elevated, and no obstacles from the labor market or broader economic growth, 
the path is clear for the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) to raise rates in 
December. Above-target inflation will likely prompt the FOMC to hike again in the first 
quarter. If the current set of hikes ends at three, it will have undone the slight easing to 
support a soft labor market in 2025.  
• 
After an apprehensive month of October, Treasury bond markets appear to be stabilizing. 
The 10-year U.S. Treasury yield has settled below its recent peak of 5.3%. Absent further 
inflationary shocks, yields should hold steady from here, though some daily volatility is to be 
expected. Persistent indebtedness, stubborn inflation and a Fed tightening cycle will all limit 
the prospects of lower rates. 
o 
The 10-Year Treasury note is the pricing benchmark for 30-year fixed-rate mortgages, 
which now cost well over 7%. Higher rates will diminish housing affordability, leaving the 
sector stalled. The volume of existing home sales fell to a 16-year low in October. 
• 
The surprise in the results of the midterm elections was the popular pushback against AI, 
with referenda on data center development revealing widespread opposition. We track AI as 
the locus of business investment activity and a source of wealth effects driving spending. 
However, any local policy changes might only slow some future development, not unplug 
running data centers or halt projects underway. We expect AI investment to remain a source 
of growth.
