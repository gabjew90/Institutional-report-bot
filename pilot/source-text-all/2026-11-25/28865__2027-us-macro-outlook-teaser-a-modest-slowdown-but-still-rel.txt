2027 US macro outlook teaser: a modest slowdown 
but still relatively resilient as Fed hikes further 
◼ 
We will publish our full US economic outlook with detailed discussions 
on key topics in the coming weeks, though as we have done in past 
years, we begin by previewing a high-level overview of our US scenario 
in advance of the full documents and our 2027 outlook sessions. 
◼ 
The US economy has faced a series of seemingly never-ending shocks 
in recent years, and to this point at least has remained resilient through 
it all. For 2026 as a whole, for example, we are tracking growth of 2.3% 
on an annual average basis, matching the 2025 pace and actually 
slightly above our expectation of 2.1% heading into the year, despite 
the Iran war. 
◼ 
Moving forward, we expect growth of 2.1% in 2027 and 2.0% in 2028, a 
modest step down but remaining relatively resilient overall. The 
slowdown from 2026 to 2027 would be more noticeable on a Q4/Q4 
basis, as this measure would slow from 2.5% to 1.9%, though even in 
this case 1.9% is still a solid pace of growth overall. 
◼ 
A number of pillars of support remain in place, including very healthy 
household balance sheets and the ongoing AI investment boom, both 
of which we expect to continue supporting growth next year. That said, 
the relatively concentrated nature of growth does create some 
vulnerability, and any further correction in AI-related equities would 
pose a clear source of downside risk. 
◼ 
After some cooling in late 2025, the labour market has stabilised more 
recently and given our expectation of growth remaining decent, we look 
for this stabilisation to largely persist. Job gains have admittedly 
slowed, but a lower breakeven due to a shift in immigration policy 
means the economy does not need to create as many jobs to hold the 
unemployment rate steady. Thus, the continuation of a low-hire, low-
fire environment can keep the labour market close to balance, with the 
unemployment rate hovering in the low-4% range in our base case. 
◼ 
Though the labour market has not been a major source of inflationary 
pressures, other shocks have helped keep inflation elevated, with the 
Iran war playing a key role for energy costs. That said, we continue to 
expect relatively limited second-round effects, eventually leading to 
some improvement next year. Specifically, we have headline CPI briefly 
dipping below 2% on base effects at points next year before eventually 
stabilising around 2.3%, with core holding close to 2.8% through Q127 
before ticking down to 2.4-2.5% by end-2027. 
◼ 
For the Fed, the combination of sticky near-term inflation and a stable 
labour market leads to the FOMC delivering further tightening, with 
additional 25bp hikes in December 2026 and March 2027 before the Fed 
then pauses with the upper bound at 4.50%. We see risks as tilted to 
the upside and would not be overly surprised to see hikes at 
consecutive meetings and/or a higher terminal rate than we currently 
project. 
◼ 
Further down the line, the improvement in our inflation forecast by 
H227 opens up the door for some modest easing, with a 25bp cut in 
Q427 and another 25bp cut in Q128 to take the upper bound to 4.00% 
before the Fed then pauses again. However, inflation that is still 
modestly above target and a neutral rate that has risen compared to the 
pre-Covid period prevent the Fed from easing too aggressively.

On the policy front, our central scenario is that shifts coming from 
Washington will play less of a role than the last couple of years, in part due 
to the likelihood of legislative gridlock given the fact that a Democratic 
congress will face the filibuster and President Donald Trump’s veto. Our key 
assumptions include the following (we will expand upon these thoughts in our 
detailed outlook): 
⚫ 
Fiscal policy: legislative gridlock means that any major fiscal packages, 
whether on the spending or the tax side of the ledger, are unlikely. Even if 
Democrats pass a reconciliation bill to sidestep the filibuster, we do not think 
Trump would be particularly shy about using his veto. Thus, while the deficit 
will remain large, we expect it will be relatively stable, meaning less of a 
fiscal impulse for growth in 2027 compared to the OBBBA kicking in in 2026. 
⚫ 
AI regulation: while Democrats may want to enact stricter regulations with 
regards to the AI buildout, we do not expect much to come out of Washington 
given the existence of the very pro-AI president’s veto. There may very well 
be some regulations that come out of various state and local jurisdictions as 
the blue wave carried on down ballot, though we think these will slow the 
pace of the buildout, or result in some projects shifting location, rather than 
derailing it entirely. 
⚫ 
Tariffs and immigration policy: Trump would have more ability to act on 
these two issues without needing Congressional approval, though we largely 
expect a status quo, with the weighted average tariff rate holding roughly 
steady and restrictive immigration policies remaining in place that keep net 
immigration low. 
⚫ 
The Iran war: the outlook for the Iran war is a bit of a wild card, as 
Democrats will push to wind it down, but it may not be entirely straightforward 
to force an end to the conflict given the president’s powers with regards to 
foreign policy. Admittedly Trump’s tone has been a bit more conciliatory 
following elections and our base case does not involve any major military re-
escalation, though this remains a risk that cannot be entirely ruled out.

case of still solid growth. Among these we would highlight:

At the same time, we do acknowledge some factors that could act to slow 
the pace of growth and provide some downside risks to the outlook as well: 
⚫ 
The pace of AI investment: while we highlighted the level of AI investment 
above as a supportive factor, we would admit that it may be impossible to 
maintain the pace of growth seen throughout 2026, with non-residential 
investment having risen by an average of more than 10% in the first three 
quarters of 2026. This is especially true given the possibility of some shift in 
the regulatory environment. Thus, we think AI investment will continue to 
support growth but may contribute to a slower pace in 2027. 
⚫ 
AI equity valuations: lofty asset valuations, in particular for AI-linked 
equities, have been a key source of support for the economy, and any 
reversal would turn this tailwind into a headwind. We do not cover equities 
and therefore will not take a strong view as to whether equities are fairly 
valued or overvalued, but concerns have obviously grown in recent weeks as 
stock markets have dipped from recent highs. 
Any further correction could pose a risk by leading to both some pull back in 
investment and a negative wealth effect. Admittedly, most academic 
estimates would place the financial wealth effect in the neighbourhood of 
USD0.02-0.03 per USD1.00 change in financial wealth, so this may not drive 
the economy off a cliff (depending on the magnitude of any sell-off), but it 
could be a material headwind. This is particularly true given the concentrated 
nature of growth, with AI investment and upper income households having 
played a disproportionate role.

Moving away from the broader economy, the labour market has stabilised 
more recently after some cooling in late 2025 and given our expectation of 
growth remaining decent, we look for this stabilisation to largely persist. 
Admittedly, the pace of hiring has slowed compared to the pre-Covid period, with 
NFP averaging +74k over the past 12 months vs a five-year pre-Covid average of 
+191k. However, this should be taken in context of a major shift in immigration 
policy that has resulted in the breakeven pace dropping sharply (see Figure 4). 
Given this context, the economy does not need to create as many jobs to 
keep the unemployment rate steady, and thus the continuation of a low-
hire, low-fire environment can keep the labour market close to balance. As 
such, we expect the unemployment rate to hover in the low-4% range throughout 
our forecast horizon. 
On the inflation side of things, we have already added 2028 to our outlook 
so will not spend too much time reiterating what we outlined in that 
Inflation-linked Focus or more recent tweaks to the outlook, but while we 
look for relatively sticky inflation through Q127, we do expect some modest 
improvement as we move into H227. The key question right now centres 
around second-round effects to core inflation from the surge in energy prices due 
to the Iran war, which in our base case remain relatively limited even if we do 
admit some upside risks.

Specifically, we have headline CPI remaining above 3% into Q127 then 
briefly dipping below 2% on base effects at points next year before 
eventually stabilising around 2.3%. We look for core CPI to hold close to 2.8% 
through Q127, in part boosted by base effects to shelter that continue to linger 
from the late 2025 government shutdown, before ticking down to 2.4-2.5% by 
end-2027 (see Figure 5). 
For the Fed, the combination of sticky near-term inflation and a stable 
labour market leads to the FOMC delivering further tightening, with 
additional 25bp hikes in December 2026 and March 2027 before the Fed 
then pauses with the upper bound at 4.50%. That said, we see risks as tilted 
to the upside, and we would not be overly surprised to see hikes at consecutive 
meetings and/or a higher terminal rate than we currently project. 
For example, if the Fed does hike twice as in our base case, then we could easily 
see those coming in December and January instead of December and March. 
We would not be surprised by a scenario where the Fed hikes in each of 
December, January, and March to take the upper bound to 4.75% either, and an 
even higher terminal rate cannot be entirely ruled out. 
Farther down the line, however, the improvement in our inflation forecast 
by H227 opens the door for some modest easing, with a 25bp cut in Q427 
and another 25bp cut in Q128 to take the upper bound to 4.00% before the 
Fed then pauses again. However, inflation that is still modestly above target and

When compared to the market, we are in an unusual spot of being a bit 
more dovish. For most of the past three years we have been on the uber-
hawkish side of expectations (for example, entering 2024 we projected a first cut 
in July even when the market was pricing a full cut by March and entering 2026 
we projected that the Fed would not cut any further, even before the Iran war), 
but now the additional two hikes we forecast are slightly less than the just over 
three priced by the market (see Figure 6). That said, our forecast is contingent 
upon the inflation outlook beginning to improve as we project as we enter into the 
middle portion of 2027, and if this does not materialise then market pricing may 
very well win out vs our forecast.
