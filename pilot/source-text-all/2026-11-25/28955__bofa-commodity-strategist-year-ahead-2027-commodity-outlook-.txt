Commodities may post strong performance, but it’s binary 
Commodities have been supported by two dominant macro forces this year: geopolitical 
disruptions and the continued AI-driven investment cycle, both of which look set to 
remain central in 2027. The ICE BofA MLCX and BCOM total return indices are up 64% 
and 21% ytd, respectively, on supply disruptions from geopolitical conflicts and resilient 
demand linked to the continued data center build-out. Energy has led the gains, as the 
continuation of the US-Iran war kept the petroleum complex strained, compounded by 
infrastructure damage stemming from the Russia-Ukraine war. Industrial metals have also 
found support from persistent supply disruptions, trade and tariff policy, and resilient end-
demand, while grains have benefited from geopolitical dislocations and weather risks. 
Precious metals sunk after peaking in January as inflation concerns pushed rates higher. 
On balance, upside risks seem greater than downside risks 
Looking into 2027, four themes may dominate commodity markets: geopolitics, AI 
investment, supply fragmentation and low inventories. On geopolitics, the range of 
outcomes remains unusually wide. A durable peace deal could pressure down energy and 
grains, while escalation could bring further upside. AI-related investment should be an 
important source of commodity demand, as continued spending on data centers, power 
generation, or grids supports metals consumption. A meaningful downward revision in 
capex would represent an important downside risk, particularly for industrial metals. 
Trade barriers and the continued reorganization of global supply chains are key sources 
of regional dislocation, as geopolitical pressures redirect food and fuels while changes in 
tariff policy alter the geographic distribution of metals.  
Low stocks boost diversification, inflation hedge benefits 
Against this backdrop, we remain bullish industrial metals and grains, while maintaining 
neutral views on oil, precious metals, and softs. True, a rally of this year’s magnitude is 
unlikely in 2027, but commodities could well post double digit total returns again as spot, 
roll, and cash returns should all contribute positively. With easy fiscal policy and cautious 
central banks, commodity allocations are likely more important than ever for diversification 
and inflation hedging in a world of strong AI investments and low stocks. In fact, strategic 
inventory accumulation could not only support the asset class throughout the year but also 
fuel roll returns. With binary geopolitical outcomes ahead, we believe commodities could 
again provide a unique diversifier to 60/40 portfolios in 2027.

Key takeaways 
 
Views 
Macro outlook 
• Our economists see world GDP rising 3.4% in 2026 and 2027, and 3.3% in 2028. 
WTI and Brent crude 
oil 
• We project Brent and WTI to average $80/bbl and $70/bbl, respectively, in 2027 and 2028.  
• We expect the oil market to return to a ~1.5mn b/d surplus in 2027. 
• We forecast a rebound in global demand growth of 3mn b/d YoY in both 2027 and 2028.  
• Non-OPEC supply should grow roughly 1.5mn b/d in 2027 and 1mn b/d in 2028. 
• We project total US crude and NGL supply to rise around 300k b/d in 2027 and 2028. 
• OPEC crude oil supplies should rise 4mn b/d in 2027 and another 2mn b/d in 2028. 
Atlantic Basin 
petroleum products 
• Petroleum product supplies are set to stay tight on refinery damage, drone strikes, and uncertainty of flows. 
• A peace deal in the new year could uplift commerce, freight, and ultimately diesel and jet fuel demand. 
• Thus, diesel prices should remain well supported and likely outpace gasoline and we see ULSD-Brent cracks averaging $43/bbl in 2027 
US natural gas  
• US gas supply and demand growth should grow 3.8Bcf/d and 4.1Bcf/d YoY in 2027, with LNG exports rising 2.1Bcf/d. 
• We forecast US Henry Hub natural gas prices will average $3.35/mmbtu in 2027 
LNG 
• Low European natural gas stocks and Middle East LNG export disruptions put TTF at risk of spiking above €120/MWh if Europe has a cold winter, but we 
expect global gas flows to eventually normalize and TTF to average €55/MWh next year. 
• LNG supply is set to rise ~56mn mt YoY in 2027 with more than half coming from Qatar LNG exports and ~20% from US export growth YoY. 
• We forecast TTF prices to average €55/MWh in 2027, down from roughly €61/MWh in 2026. 
Thermal coal 
• Limited seaborne supply flexibility leaves the global coal market tight and increasingly exposed to further disruptions. 
• While conflict and higher gas prices we forecast Newcastle at $125/t for 2027 before falling with other energy prices. 
Aluminium 
• Competition for aluminium units is increasing between consumers in Europe and the US as both markets are short. 
• Consumers have reduced inventories in the past 12 months, so there is pent-up demand that should support prices in 1H27 
• Indonesia and the Middle East are boosting supply, so we expect a small surplus 2027. 
Copper 
• China’s metals demand has slowed, but grid spending looks unsustainably low, so should rebound. 
• Demand in Europe and the US should expand, which along supply constraints, is set to increase competition for copper units. 
• Inventory financing deals have kept the market tight. Headwinds to data centre construction are a risk, even if we expect a deficit for 2027. 
Lead 
• There are no immediate scrap or concentrates shortages, suggesting the market could flip back into surplus. 
• India’s recyclers are key and a slowdown in production there would help slow inventory increases on LME. 
Nickel 
• Indonesia is looking to slow the unfettered output growth seen in recent years. 
• Market balances will largely depend on where Indonesia sets mining permits in 2027, but there is scope for a deficit. 
Zinc  
• Mine supply is extremely tight and any spare units are purchased by China’s smelters. 
• China only exports metal, when LME prices rally above those on SHFE. Hence, LME squeezes likely remain a feature in 2027 
• The market should flip back into a deficit for 2027. 
Gold 
• Gold has come under pressure from higher oil prices, a stronger USD and a hawkish Fed. 
• The undercurrent, including an unorthodox economic policy and elevated deficits, remains bullish. 
• We see headwinds until the Fed hiking cycle ends and the risk of another Iran war escalation subsides, but gold should push above $5,000/oz beyond that. 
Silver 
• The silver market has rebalanced on production discipline and demand from new applications including solar panels. 
• Spending on solar panels has boosted silver demand, but a shift in technology is now reducing consumption. 
• We expect a small surplus for 2027, but a rebound of investor demand could flip the market back into a deficit. 
Platinum/ 
• Palladium has been moving into surplus, keeping pressure on prices.  
Palladium 
• More production discipline is necessary. Potential trade restrictions on Russian ounces may be worth following. 
 
• Platinum remains in deficit in 2027 and should continue to outperform palladium. 
Iron Ore 
• Global iron ore production keeps pushing higher, likely giving rise to a surplus next year. More production discipline is necessary. 
• That said, higher cost iron ore ore production is now shuttered, so if there is another leg lower in prices, this may clear the supply overhang. 
• China’s steel mills remain under pressure on overcapacities and pushback from countries around the world against steel exports. 
HCC Thermal coal 
• Thermal coal prices have stabilized but with a higher price floor as marginal costs have risen. 
• The met coal market is rebalancing as the supply overhang in China eases. 
Wheat 
• Black Sea developments remain the key swing factor for the wheat market.   
• We see wheat heading toward $7-8/bu if disruptions persist, versus $5.50/bu should de-escalation come in spring. 
Corn 
• We see upside risks for corn in the near term subject to demand, South America developments and Ukraine’s logistics. 
• We expect corn to recover to $5/bu. A strong Brazilian safrinha crop and de-escalation in the Black Sea could cap the rally and drive prices back to $4.50/bu. 
Soybeans 
• We expect soy prices to average about $11.80/bu in 2027E, but, if US soy production exceeds USDA projections and Chinese imports weaken yoy, market  
sentiment could quickly turn bearish, pushing prices towards $11/bu or potentially lower. 
Soybean oil 
• We remain bullish on soybean oil through 2027, supported by robust demand and supply concerns across competing vegetable oils. 
• Soybean oil prices are therefore expected to stay supported and average 63$c/lb in 2027E vs 61$c/lb forward price.  
Soybean meal 
• Solid feed demand in 2026/27 should continue to support soy meal prices, with our 2027 forecast at $333/t, up from $322/t in 2026E. 
Sugar 
• We expect production losses in the EU, Asia and CS Brazil to support sugar prices near 18-19$c/lb over the next six months.

1. Macro overview 
 
Commodities supported by war, AI, and supply, which remain central in 2027 
Commodities have been supported by two dominant macro forces this year: geopolitical 
disruption and the continued AI-driven investment cycle, both of which look set to 
remain central in 2027. The MLCX and BCOM total return indices are up 64% and 21%, 
respectively, reflecting a combination of supply losses and broader disruptions from 
geopolitical conflicts alongside resilient demand supported by the continued data center 
build-out and industrial activity (Exhibit 4). Energy has led the gains, as the continuation 
of the US-Iran war has kept the petroleum complex strained, compounded by 
infrastructure damage stemming from the Russia-Ukraine war (Exhibit 5). Industrial 
metals have also found support from persistent supply disruptions, trade and tariff 
policy, and resilient end-demand, while grains have benefited from geopolitical 
disruptions and weather risks. Precious metals, by contrast, started the year strongly but 
have since faced headwinds as inflation concerns pushed rates higher. 
Looking into 2027, we expect four themes to dominate commodity markets: war, AI and 
infrastructure investment, continued fragmentation in global trade and supply chains, 
and low inventories. On geopolitics, the range of outcomes remains unusually wide. A 
durable peace agreement could rapidly compress geopolitical risk premia across energy 
and grains, while continued or renewed escalation would keep markets tight and leave 
both complexes exposed to further upside. Meanwhile, AI-related investment should 
remain an important source of commodity demand, as continued spending on data 
centers, power generation, grids and associated infrastructure supports industrial 
activity and metals consumption. A meaningful slowdown or downward revision in capex 
would therefore represent an important downside risk, particularly for industrial metals. 
Finally, trade barriers and the continued reorganization of global supply chains are likely 
to remain important sources of regional dislocation, as geopolitical pressures redirect 
barrels and bushels while changes in tariff policy alter the geographic distribution of 
metals, especially as inventories remain low. 
Against this backdrop, we remain bullish industrial metals and grains, while maintaining 
neutral views on oil, precious metals and softs.

and progress on inflation. Major energy importers like the UK, Germany, and Japan are

breakevens have also moved higher in response to the energy shock (Exhibit 10).

90bps for the US (Exhibit 11). Higher inflation expectations and resilient activity have 
pushed expected policy paths higher, while persistent inflation and stronger growth have 
also contributed to higher expectations for neutral rates. Political developments have 
added further upside pressure, as fiscal deficits look set to persist or widen. While 
cyclical commodities have shown limited sensitivity to tighter financial conditions, 
higher yields have offset some of the support for gold from geopolitical uncertainty and 
fiscal concerns, creating a meaningful headwind for precious metals.

2. Energy outlook 
2.1 Crude oil: Oil turns blue 
Oil supply disruptions hit an all-time record in 2026… 
Looking at recent history, we note that global oil supply disruptions have never been as 
high as in 2026, underscoring the unprecedented scale of the current shock (Exhibit 28). 
At its peak earlier this year, we estimate that global supply offline amounted to ~18mn 
b/d due to a broad array of short-term and long-standing disruptions from the Middle 
East to Africa to Latin America. Indeed, a time series of global oil supply going back to 
1965 puts the latest production shortfall in historical perspective, with the scale of this 
year and next year’s forecasted oil production (see our report, A deepening geopolitical 
schism) swings exceeding previous geopolitical supply shocks (Exhibit 29). The 
disruption through Hormuz is central to this story, with the IEA estimating in September 
that more than 10mn b/d of Gulf production remained shut in during August.

across the Atlantic. NYMEX New York Harbor distillate cracks are now sitting well above

Europe will release more barrels from strategic storage 
Against this backdrop, Europe has reached for the most immediate tool available to it:

3.2 Copper: financing deals keep global market tight 
Overview  
The US has become the global sink for refined copper, with tariff expectations and 
attractive warehouse-financing returns pulling the metal away from other markets. 
Elevated imports have left the US holding more than 1.7mt of copper, while inventories 
in China and on the LME have tightened. With CME contangos continuing to reward 
storage, we believe copper will remain in the US unless stronger nearby demand flattens 
the CME curve or higher LME prices provide sufficient compensation for re-exports. We 
also believe that while tariff expectations were important at the outset to move copper 
to the US, storage economics are now sufficient to keep the metal tied up. As such, any 
tariff decision by President Trump may in the end cause some volatility, with prices 
potentially dropping temporarily if the tariff threat were removed, but this should then 
lock up the copper in warehouses again. Hence, we believe that a reacceleration of 
Chinese demand, especially through grid spending, may be more important, especially in 
2H27.

much copper? Initially, metal was sent to the US in anticipation of tariffs and traders 
saw the potential for a free lunch. Any metal that was on the ground before a duty hit 
could later be sold at a tariff-inclusive price. Yet, those shipments ultimately 
overwhelmed the US market, weighing on the CME curve, which has moved into a steep 
contango. Exhibit 117 takes this a step further, highlighting that the steep contangos 
have made warehouse financing deals immensely profitable. Of course, the back end of 
the curve has also been pulled higher by very one-sided liquidity. Consumers have been 
hedging, while producers have been much quieter. Either way, even China’s consumers 
have complained about the difficulties of sourcing copper units because warehouse 
financing deal terms are so appealing.

President Trump decided not to impose a refined tariff. This would in all likelihood 
flatten the curve, reducing the attractiveness of financing deals. As a result, some of the 
copper could come out of the warehouses. The question would then be: could a trickle of 
copper turn into a flood that might overwhelm the market? In our view, this is unlikely. If 
traders couldn’t roll over financing deals for a period of time and copper hit the market, 
nearby prices would likely decline. This would ultimately lead to a steeper CME curve, 
which would then make financing deals profitable again. As such, we believe that any 
short-term volatility in time spreads and less profitable warehouse financing deals would 
end up self-correcting.  
From a US government perspective, maintaining the status quo could be the most 
desirable option, and that could well mean either 1) no tariff is announced, but guidance 
remains that duties may be implemented further down the line or 2) a tariff is 
announced, with the threat of more tariffs later on. 
What could prompt copper re-exports from the US? We believe traders would want to be 
compensated for that, which could come in two forms:  
• 
CME curves flatten, when consumers place a bid on nearby contracts to source 
copper units or if there is certainty that no tariffs will be implemented (tariff 
expectations may have supported the back end of the curve); as more copper comes 
out of warehouses and the tightness eases, contangos should increase again, likely 
prompting a renewal of financing deals; or  
• 
LME prices keep squeezing higher, giving traders an incentive to ship material, a 
dynamic that has started to happen, with the nearby LME prices almost level with 
CME quotations at some stage; the squeeze in LME-CME differentials could, 
however, mean-revert when more metal arrives on LME and prices likely stop 
rallying, ultimately bringing a halt to metal shipments. 
As such, the financing deals look to have created a stable equilibrium and the market 
should work down stocks gradually over time in reaction to “real” demand. This is what 
has happened on the aluminium market in the past decade. 
Mine supply is tight 
Meanwhile, copper mine supply remains tight, reflected in a persistent decline of 
treatment and refining charges – see Exhibit 118. The structure of next year’s TC/RC 
deals is a focus and there is a chance that contracts may change, with miners potentially 
trying to complement the headline treatment and refining charge with additional 
payables, e.g. on sulphuric acid.

Purchases of physical gold have been patchy in recent months and a decline in ETF

5.2 Soybeans: a tale of two markets 
China’s demand growth fades, but does its market influence? 
For years, China has dominated the soy market narrative, with its import demand largely 
dictating price direction. However, as China’s growth slows and global demand for soy 
crush products strengthens, this traditional framework may increasingly overlook an 
important source of support for prices. 
Can soy crush demand become the main pillar of market support? 
We expect soy prices to average $11.80/bu in 2027E, but if US production exceeds 
USDA projections and Chinese imports weaken yoy, market sentiment could quickly turn 
bearish, pushing prices towards $11/bu or potentially lower. Under this scenario, soy 
crush would need to do much of the heavy lifting in supporting the market, a challenging 
task, particularly if softer energy markets weigh on soy oil demand. 
Soymeal and vegetable oil demand to sustain crush growth at major origins… 
Global soymeal use in feed rations should remain strong, while vegetable oil demand for 
biofuels, particularly soy oil in the US, is expected to build on its recent substantial gains. 
Weaker energy markets could moderate the pace of growth, but supply-side risks, 
notably El Niño-related threats to palm oil output and potential disruptions to Ukrainian 
sunflower oil supply, should keep the overall outlook constructive. 
…driving further global crush expansion, outpacing export growth in 2026/27 
Import demand growth remains vulnerable without additional support from China. We 
have greater conviction in the outlook for soy crush products in 2026/27, with combined 
crush volumes in the US, Brazil and Argentina expected to increase by around 6-7 mn t 
yoy, double the projected growth in their combined soy exports (Exhibit 169). 
Nevertheless, a normalization in crush margins from the record highs of 2H 2025/26 
could temper the pace of expansion (Exhibit 170).

5.6 Sugar: not out of the woods yet 
 
Global sugar deficit solidifies in 2026/27… 
Production losses in Europe, a disappointing Indian monsoon and excessive rainfall in 
Centre-South Brazil have strengthened the case for a global sugar deficit in 2026/27. 
While prices have already rallied in response, we believe further upside remains. We 
maintain our bullish near-term outlook, seeing a move towards 18-19$c/lb, although 
weaker energy markets and BRL depreciation could act as headwinds. 
…while the outlook for 2027/28 remains promising but fragile 
El Niño has already taken a significant toll on Brazil’s sugar production, while we believe 
its full impact on India and Thailand may not materialize until 2027/28, particularly as it 
is expected to persist until at least April. This increases the likelihood of Indian sugar 
imports in 2026/27, and even more so in 2027/28. While Brazil’s 2027/28 crop 
prospects remain promising, we estimate that the country would need to produce more 
than 42 mn t of sugar for the global market to return to balance or surplus. 
Europe may need to import more than twice as much sugar as currently forecast 
In our view, lower planted area (Exhibit 190) and severe summer weather, especially in 
France, could reduce Europe’s sugar beet harvest by over 25% yoy. Consequently, we 
expect 2026/27 production to be down about 25-30%, finishing close to 12 mn t or even 
below that (from 16.6 mn t in 2025/16). This would lift import needs to roughly 3-3.5 
mn t versus the European Commission’s current forecast of 1.5 mn t (Exhibit 191).

6. Portfolio strategy 
Commodities have been the dominant asset class in 2026 
Commodities, equities and the dollar are currently poised to end positive on the year as 
markets have gone through an oil and commodity price shock from the US-Iran war, AI 
euphoria coupled with volatility, rising rates, and stubborn inflation. Commodities have 
led the way on account of dual benefits coming from the positive demand shock from AI, 
data-center and infrastructure investment, and the supply shock coming from the US-
Iran war as well as Russia-Ukraine and idiosyncratic bottlenecks (Exhibit 198). While 
equities were initially challenged by the war shock, they quickly recovered as AI took the 
helm before becoming range bound as concerns around lofty valuations and financing 
surfaced. Fixed income has lagged as stubborn inflation and a return to monetary policy 
hiking has raised rates and weighed on duration, while supporting the dollar in the 
second half of the year (Exhibit 199). We would expect commodity leadership to 
moderate given that returns are largely driven by the onset and persistence of the war, 
but the return of inflation in a rate hike environment could continue to highlight the 
diversification benefits of the asset class in 2027.

…but BCOM weights do better during price shocks… 
Across the last 10 years, and in general, a weighting scheme that optimized for risk-
adjusted returns (MVO) yielded the strongest performance compared with other 
commodity beta schemes (Exhibit 219). In particular, adding an enhanced roll schedule, 
by rolling over a larger window, and more frequently boosted performance across all 
time periods. The rationale for this is likely because the roll return from backwardated 
commodities gets captured more often, while using a wider window allows us to capture 
benefits from starting to roll before the majority of passive commodity beta holders, 
and also when spreads normalize following that activity. However, despite the generally 
strong performance of MVO weighting schemes, a traditional BCOM weighting scheme 
has also had its moments. In particular, during energy shocks such as 2011-13, 2018-19, 
2021-22, and this year, a BCOM weighting scheme significantly outperforms the other 
weighting schemes, even on a risk-adjusted basis (Exhibit 220).

…by placing more weight in energy commodities 
BCOM weights typically place a higher emphasis on the energy sector (20-30% in total), 
while an equal-risk-contribution or MVO scheme by design reduces exposure to high 
volatility commodities, even if they are doing well (Exhibit 221). This year has been a 
perfect example, as energy curves have been backwardated as well as volatile. 
Commodity beta indices that have higher notional in energy commodities will capture 
more of the roll returns available, but the MVO scheme has not held any crude oil, 
meaning it has not received the same boost from crude that BCOM did, on the order of 
13% cumulative returns YTD (Exhibit 222). Instead, MVO and ERC drew performance 
from aggs and industrial metals. As a result, MVO, ERC, and other weighting schemes 
that try to control for volatility perform better as fair-weather strategies, and tactically 
switching to a scheme like BCOM can capture returns during price shocks when those 
opportunities arise.

Commodity beta with these weighting schemes can boost a traditional 60/40 
We can take these results and explore how to integrate commodity beta into a 
traditional 60-40 portfolio, and the best way to weight the commodity portion. Given the 
current inflation backdrop, we examine the performance of 60-40 versus a 40% equity-
40% bonds-20% commodities portfolio (with BCOM weights) during periods of high 
inflation. The 40-40-20 outperformed in 9 of 12 historic high inflation periods, 
highlighting a tangible benefit of commodities (Exhibit 223). In particular, since March 
2026 the 40-40-20 portfolio has exhibited an annualized information ratio of 1.39, 
nearly double that of a 60-40, a promising sign should the war and high energy prices 
continue into 2027. Within the 20% commodities allocation, we can use the logic from 
our earlier findings to determine a weighting scheme, as volatility-controlling schemes 
like MVO or ERC have performed better since 2023, but BCOM weighting would have 
been more fruitful when including 2021-22 (Exhibit 224). Regardless, integrating 
commodity exposure and tactically adjusting weights has largely provided a boost to 
cross-asset portfolio performance in the current geopolitical and macro environment.
