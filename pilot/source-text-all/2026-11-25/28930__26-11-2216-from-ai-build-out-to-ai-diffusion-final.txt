On 12th October, at a conference in Singapore, Broadcom's Hock Tan compared AI investors to casino 
gamblers, implying short termism and an incomplete understanding of the risks. The market has 
always swung between exuberance and despair; over
-extrapolating
 in both directions. Field Marshal 
Slim’s observation comes to mind. "In battle nothing is ever as good or as bad as the first reports of 
excited men would have it."
 
 
In the short run, investment performance is most often about location. If location is sector
, then the 
Tech neighbourhood was the wrong one this month. Within our Tech holdings in the Premier Miton 
Global Leaders Fund (the Fund)
, there were substantial earnings beats and guidance raises, including 
Arista, Coherent, Lumentum and, pick of the bunch, Aehr Test Systems, which beat on sales and 
margins, and upgraded revenue guide for 2027 to 200%.
 The houses are in good repair; the area was 
falling out of fashion. 
 
 
Through the summer we retained
 a chunky overweight to Semis and Tech Hardware, believing AI 
would continue to diffuse through the economy and that frontier model performance would continue 
to justify spending in the nearer term. The first assumption is intact, the second is under great
er 
scrutiny. 
 
Greater scrutiny of the AI build
-out 
 
Earnings season is well underway. Despite further earnings beats and raised guidance, tech stock 
valuations are falling sharply. The reasons are clear, if multi
-factorial. Snags to the data centre build
-out 
include an under
-supply of chips, power and (now, perhaps) permits. The latter issue came into 
greater focus with the Blue Wave in the US midterms, causing fears of moratoria as states representing 
~40% of the planned build out saw Democratic governors replace Republicans in Texas, Ohio and 
Georgia.  
 
The principal factor is the slowdown in revenue growth at the frontier labs. This is the best available 
gauge of “diffusion”, the rate at which AI use is proliferating across enterprises and the economy. 
Anthropic’s break
-neck growth reportedly slowed in S
eptember and may well not have sped up in 
October. Hence the free cash flows available to underpin data centre investments are less forthcoming 
than the markets assumed in the summer.
 
 
Is this a cause for concern? In a word, yes. 
 
 
Anthropic and OpenAI (OAI) are the principal purveyors of “intelligence”, derived largely from Nvidia’s 
accelerator chips (GPUs). While the distributors of this compute,
 Amazon, Microsoft and Google, 
continue to see accelerating revenues, the planned pace of data centre builds requires the labs sales 
to keep growing fast. Taking a step back, Anthropic has moved from $1bn ARR (annual recurring 
revenues) in December 2024, 
to more than $
80bn reported in 
October . Sure, they haven’t yet achieved 
one trillion dollars, but this pace of growth is astounding, unprecedented in economic history. At the 
start of 2026, its own year
-end ARR estimate was $25bn;
 it was already around $80bn at the end of 
October , with two months still to go.

-centre revenue almost doubled, 
Palantir again delivered triple digit growth, and a range of infrastructure companies reported clear

Testing the economies
 
 
This scrutiny is justified. It is precisely why we model out the returns (profits) required by the AI 
companies to justify further investment. 
 
 
We do this by modelling the GW (gigawatts) of data centre power used by companies that are the 
major users of AI and assessing this against the revenues and profits we expect them to generate. This 
isolates the required annualised return trajectory of the 
frontier labs and compares it with both the 
current run rate and market expectations. Run rate revenues that exceed market expectations would 
be a healthy sign for the AI compute builders, vendors and users. The next reported figures for 
Anthropic and OAI 
are therefore critical for the near and medium
-term trajectory of the AI build out. 
 
 
While the prize remains substantial across the AI value chain, the market has moved from optimism to 
scepticism, and valuations now price in a material slowdown in investment. This sits in tension with 
the messaging from the big spenders
 and the hyperscalers themselves, all of whom pointed to higher
-
than -expected CapEx in 2027, when they reported over the past fortnight.
 
 
Investors are now asking harder questions. How much does that growth cost? How visible is future 
demand? Who captures the profit? And, perhaps most importantly, will the enormous wave of capital 
being deployed into AI infrastructure ultimately generate acc
eptable returns? 
 
 
The difficulty of these questions is neatly displayed by Microsoft, the world’s 
second largest company. 
Since 2023 its forward P/E has ranged from 20x to 35x, even as quarterly sales growth remained within 
a 12% to 19% band. Its current twelve
-month forward PE multiple is 25x, with quarterly sales growth 
recently accelerating to the top of 
that range.
 
 
From building AI to using it
 
 
We have reduced our exposure to the builders and vendors of AI compute. At the same time
, we see 
huge opportunities for its users. Companies that can and have integrated AI effectively into their 
organisations are already benefitting. The companies doing so span the economic spectrum. 
Examples include software code generation, legal research a
nd the analysis of health records to 
identify drug candidates, (as with the Fund’s holding Tempus AI). Deployment is far broader still.

Consider the diversity of another holding, Palantir. Alongside insurers, militaries and the NHS, Airbus 
runs predictive maintenance across its fleet using data platform Skywise, built on Palantir software. A 
modern aircraft generates up to a terabyte of da
ta a day; Skywise gathers it so engineers can track 
individual components and predict failures before they happen. EasyJet avoided nearly 80 flight 
cancellations in two months using this platform, and engineers traced unexplained engine
-valve leaks 
across the fleet to a control
-software fault, catching a cascading failure risk. Independent analysis has 
estimated that Skywise creates a revenue opportunity exceeding $850m a year plus cost savings, with 
more than 100 airlines onboarded.
 
 
Just last week we were visited by FedEx Freight (company management, not a delivery driver) who are 
rewriting their internal tech systems in partnership with Palantir. The common thread is consistent: 
take an organisation's fragmented data, build decision
-making apps on top of it, embed engineers 
and harvest the efficiencies. 
 
 
Using the energy system as an analogy, if clean, structured, permissioned data were to become the 
21st century commodity of choice (or of increasing value at least), and the unstructured data swamp 
is unrefined crude, is Palantir positioned to be the leadi
ng refinery or monopolist utility?
 
 
Diversifying across the AI opportunity
 
 
We accept the pace of the build out remains in question and is heavily reliant on the trajectory of 
Anthropic and OpenAI’s revenues. At the same time, the growth in AI use can proceed independently 
of this, offering attractive diversification opportunities
 within equity portfolios. We are actively seeking 
to capture these opportunities.
 
 
As conviction has increased in a broader set of companies harnessing AI, we have been able to reduce 
exposure to the pure builders of the “compute” itself and diversify across multiple neighbourhoods, 
beyond semiconductors and technology hardware. We belie
ve the Fund is positioned to benefit from 
the diffusion of AI across multiple areas and is now less reliant on the trajectory of the AI labs.
