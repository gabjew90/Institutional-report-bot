US Pulse: monetary policy strategy under spotlight 
What we’re watching 
• 
The appropriate monetary policy strategy for the current tightening cycle is being keenly 
debated at the Fed, as confirmed by the minutes of the October Federal Open Market 
Committee (FOMC) meeting.  
• 
Recently, Governor Waller has raised the prospect of increasing interest rates by 50bp 
in the absence of a 25bp rate hike in December being supported by clear guidance that 
interest rates will rise by another 25bp in January. 
• 
We think it is necessary that the Fed signals its inflation fighting intent to ensure inflation 
expectations remain well anchored. In this regard the dot plot has an important role to 
play in communicating monetary policy strategy and the probable extent of tightening.  
• 
In the absence of forward guidance from Chair Warsh, the median dot plot will need to 
reflect additional interest rate rises next year. Recent speeches from FOMC members 
have indicated that additional tightening would come early in 2027.  
• 
October control group retail sales data were strong and implied a brisk start to private 
consumption growth in Q4. The FOMC needs to lean against the current strength in 
demand and associated rise in inflation. 
• 
This week’s main focus will be on Fed speakers ahead of the communications blackout 
prior to the December meeting. Personal consumption expenditure (PCE) inflation data 
for October will be published. We forecast a rise of 0.3% m/m in the core PCE deflator 
which would leave the annual rate at 3.2%.

Monetary policy strategy: live debate over 25bp or 50bp hikes  
One topic we find ourselves discussing a lot is US monetary policy strategy and whether the 
FOMC will raise rates by 25bp or 50bp when it meets on 8–9th December. Readers know 
we thought the FOMC should have raised the target for fed funds by 25bp at the October 
policy meeting followed by another 25bp in December. Given the strength of data since the 
last FOMC meeting, we understand arguments that the Fed is at risk of falling behind the 
curve and needs to tighten in a decisive fashion. A gap of six weeks between meetings isn’t 
a very long time in the context of policy implementation given the lags with which interest 
rate decisions affect the economy. However, a gap of three months between 25bp rate rises 
is a long time when inflation’s momentum is building. 
The minutes of the October FOMC meeting shed some light on the discussion over 
monetary policy strategy at the committee. 
A few participants who favored an increase in the target range indicated that they could have 
supported no change in the federal funds rate provided that it was clearly understood by 
market participants that further tightening was likely. 
This underlines the important role that forward guidance has in monetary policy strategy. 
Warsh did not provide forward guidance on interest rates at the press conference following 
the meeting and we doubt very much he will provide guidance on interest rates after the 
December meeting. 
Recently, Fed Governor Waller has said he would support a 50bp rise in interest rates in 
December in the absence of guidance that interest rates will be rising by 25bp again in 
January. Waller’s preferred strategy seems to be for sequential 25bp rate rises, or in the 
absence of that and forward guidance on rate hikes, for a 50bp increase. His concern is that 
the FOMC currently is not sending a strong enough message on its determination to return 
inflation to target. However, from the minutes it does not appear that there was widespread 
support to consider a 50bp rate rise in December. Several members noted that the expected 
path of fed funds over time is important rather than individual meetings.  
In recent weeks, many FOMC speakers have indicated a preference for a series of 
sequential 25bp rate rises. However, given the strength of the activity and inflation data, it is 
very plausible that a 50bp rate rise will be discussed. At the very least, an active discussion 
on monetary policy strategy is required. The Fed must underline its commitment to getting 
inflation down through action, with sequential 25bp rate rises, or 50bp rate rises if less 
frequent. 
As well as the activity, labour market and inflation data, the Fed is closely watching the 
evolution of financing conditions, the transmission of the September rate increase to the 
economy, geopolitical developments and the durability of AI-investment demand. Financing 
conditions have tightened in recent weeks as equity markets have fallen and credit spreads 
have widened. The cost of capital is rising which may slow the future pipeline of AI-
investment demand given how capital intensive it is. We think recent financial market 
weakness supports a series of 25bp rate rises over a 50bp hiking strategy. A 50 bp hike, if 
not priced by the financial markets, could precipitate a very sharp financial market sell-off.

Data Pulse: the week ahead 
October’s PCE inflation is the headline data release this week. Based on inputs from the 
October CPI and PPI reports, we forecast the headline PCE deflator rose 0.3% m/m 
(3.8% y/y), while the core deflator is expected to have increased 0.3% m/m (3.2% y/y). We 
will be closely monitoring market-based core PCE inflation and supercore services inflation 
for signals of underlying inflation breadth and momentum. 
Personal income is expected to have risen 0.4% m/m in October, following a 0.2% m/m 
increase in September. Personal spending is expected to have increased 0.4% m/m after 
rising 0.5% m/m previously. Consumer spending remained on a solid footing at the start of 
Q4, supported by strength in control-group retail sales. 
The preliminary November flash S&P PMI is out this week. The S&P Global manufacturing 
PMI is expected to have eased slightly to 54.5 from 54.9, while the services PMI is expected 
to have moderated to 55.5 from 56.1. October’s composite PMI declined 2.5pt to 55.9, a 
three-month low. Cost pressures remained elevated, despite easing from September.

Data Pulse: the week that was  
Headline retail sales rose 0.2% m/m in October, easing from September's gasoline-led 
0.8% m/m gain. The underlying picture was stronger, with control group sales rising 
0.6% m/m, the most since August. The three-month average growth of control group sales is 
0.9% m/m, the strongest since Q1 2022.  
Industrial production fell 0.4% m/m in October, partially reversing September’s strong 0.7% 
increase, as sharp declines in utilities (-4.3% m/m) and mining (-0.8% m/m) weighed on the 
headline. Manufacturing activity was more resilient, with output rising 0.3% m/m, largely 
reflecting stronger motor vehicle production (4.4% m/m). Business equipment output also 
strengthened, rising 1.0% m/m after falling 0.1% m/m in September. 
The NAHB Housing Market Index (HMI) fell 4pts to 25 in November, its lowest since April 
2012, following a 3pt decline in September. Weakness was broad-based, with current sales 
conditions falling 6pt to 28, six-month sales expectations declining 2pt to 30, and prospective 
buyer traffic slipping 2pt to 16. High mortgage rates continue to deter would-be buyers, 
leaving builders reliant on sales incentives to support demand.
