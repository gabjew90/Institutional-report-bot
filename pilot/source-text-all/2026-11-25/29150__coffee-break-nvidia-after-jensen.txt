THE CONTEXT 
 
Nvidia’s quarterly results and conference call were among the most anticipated events in the company’s history and, 
for that very reason, among those from which investors might have expected to learn the least. Jensen Huang’s 
sudden departure had dominated attention ahead of the release. The results and the call proved more revealing than 
expected. They reaffirmed Nvidia’s technological lead, backed by exceptional demand and a strong product roadmap. 
The open question is whether Nvidia can carry forward the strategy Huang was building around that advantage.

THE DATA 
Revenue reached USD 115.8bn, up 103% year on year, with data centre revenue rising 110% 
to USD 107.6bn. Management raised its FY2028 revenue growth outlook (broadly calendar 
2027) to 75% from 70% on a higher comparison base. Vera Rubin, Nvidia’s new computing 
platform, generated almost double the revenue of the previous Backwell generation in its first 
quarter of shipments. Demand continues to exceed supply, even as the US tightens restrictions 
on Chinese access to advanced computing. Nvidia plans to continue supporting the wider AI 
ecosystem through investments, financing and guarantees, with greater discipline in how it 
commits capital. 
OUR VIEW 
The quarter reinforces Nvidia’s substantial technological advantage. The market’s relatively 
negative reaction to such impressive results suggests investors are focused on a question that 
quarterly earnings alone cannot resolve: what are the strategic implications of losing Jensen 
Huang? 
Major hyperscalers such as Microsoft, Google, Amazon and Meta, as well as AI labs such as 
OpenAI and Anthropic, have the means and incentives to reduce their reliance on Nvidia’s chips. 
All six are pursuing custom AI chip designs. Huang’s response was to help build an alternative 
AI ecosystem around open-weight models and independent computing infrastructure. 
Open-weight models allow companies to customise and run AI systems on infrastructure of 
their choice. This gives them an alternative to relying entirely on services provided by OpenAI 
or Anthropic. 
Independent infrastructure operators, often called neoclouds, include companies such as 
CoreWeave and Nebius. They specialise in providing the computing power needed to develop 
and run AI applications. As they compete with the hyperscalers, they provide an additional 
source of demand for Nvidia’s processors. 
Huang put this strategy into practice by overseeing the development of Nvidia’s own open-
weight models and backing the companies that build, distribute and run them. He recognised 
that independent AI developers and neocloud operators needed financial support to compete 
with the largest hyperscalers and reach the scale needed to fund their own growth. Developing 
advanced models is expensive. Building the infrastructure to run them is even more capital-
intensive, requiring substantial upfront investment in chips, data centres, power and networking. 
According to the Financial Times, Nvidia and its venture arm had made more than 120 
investments in 2026 by mid-November. Nvidia’s equity investments and investment 
commitments had reached approximately USD 124bn by the end of July. 
Two developments now threaten this strategy. The first is geopolitical. Washington is 
tightening restrictions on Chinese access to advanced AI computing, including remote access 
to US-designed chips through data centres in countries such as Singapore and Malaysia. If 
Chinese companies lose access to capacity they previously leased in Southeast Asia, providers 
would need to find other customers. OpenAI, Anthropic and the hyperscalers could take up 
some of that capacity at lower rates. 
The second concerns capital allocation. The next CEO will face a fundamental choice. Should 
Nvidia continue financing independent AI developers and infrastructure operators to create 
future demand for its processors? Or should it give greater priority to near-term shareholder 
returns, including buybacks, while focusing on its core technology business? 
The quarter demonstrated that Nvidia’s technological advantage remains formidable. Whether 
the company can preserve the broader strategy Huang was building around it remains an open 
question. For investors, that distinction is crucial.

OTHER THINGS TO NOTE 
Eurozone PMIs expected to rebound 
We expect the euro area composite PMI to increase from 51.7 to 52.2 in November, partially 
reversing the previous month’s decline. The survey continues to point to an economy that 
remains broadly resilient despite persistent headwinds to GDP growth. This resilience is 
supported in part by firms’ willingness to absorb higher input costs, particularly for energy, 
through lower margins rather than passing them through to final prices.
