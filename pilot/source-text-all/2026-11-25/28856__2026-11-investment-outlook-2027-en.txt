The price of power
Our 2027 outlook, “The Price of Power”, focuses on the costs and trade-offs of a macro regime shaped by five
powerful forces and what they mean for investors. Energy power makes reliable access to energy and critical
resources a competitive advantage. Technology power is shifting the focus from AI investment to adoption,
productivity and monetisation, favouring companies able to turn innovation into earnings. Capital power
increases the value of strong balance sheets and sustainable financing, while policy power redirects capital
towards security, sovereignty and industrial transformation. Finally, pricing power will distinguish those able to
protect margins and deliver returns from those absorbing rising costs.
This creates a macro backdrop of rising investment needs at a time when fiscal constraints are increasing the cost
of financing. In 2027, we expect global GDP growth to reach 3.3%, with different regional drivers. US growth
should remain supported by productivity gains and lower exposure to energy shocks. Europe faces headwinds from
the energy shock, but German fiscal stimulus should provide support. China remains split between strong exports
and weak domestic demand, while India’s growth should stay above 7%. More orthodox policies should support
resilience across emerging markets, although country-specific risks persist.
Inflation is set to remain above central bank targets, but to ease over the course of the year, with synchronised
monetary tightening likely to peak in the first quarter.
With a combined 40% probability assigned to alternative scenarios, portfolio construction should take into account
different risks and drivers. A funding squeeze, supply disruptions and geopolitical escalation are key downside
risks, while stronger productivity gains or a ceasefire in Ukraine could define a stronger investment environment.

Power will shape how the cycle evolves. How investors allocate to it and protect against its costs will shape their
returns.

MACROECONOMICS
AI, Investment and the New Macro Regime
AI is already lifting labour productivity, but the broader productivity dividend is still to come. For
now, the gains are uneven, sector-specific and heavily concentrated. That matters because the next
phase of global growth is more likely to be driven by investment than by consumption, and
the current capex cycle is narrow, more leveraged and less supportive of wages than many markets
assume.
The result is a macro backdrop that is becoming more cost-intensive. Inflation is likely to
remain sticky, rates higher for longer, and policy more constrained than investors expect.
Will AI deliver a productivity dividend and who will capture it?
The key question for 2027 is not how much capital is being invested in AI, but who converts
adoption into profits and productivity.
AI is driving investment. But investment alone does not guarantee a productivity dividend.
Labour productivity can improve when workers are given better tools. Total factor productivity,
however, only rises when firms reorganise around the technology – and that takes time,
capital, managerial discipline and the right operating model.
That distinction matters. Productivity is not the same as profitability. AI can generate economic
gains without delivering attractive returns to the companies financing the build-out. The value may
accrue instead to the infrastructure providers, the AI developers and the firms that adopt the
technology effectively. Workers may benefit too, but unevenly and with a lag.

Can central banks contain inflation without choking growth?
Central banks face a familiar but increasingly uncomfortable trade-off.
The key distinction is between persistent inflation and accelerating inflation. Our base case is
persistence, not re-acceleration. Transporting goods now takes longer and costs more, with routes chosen
for security rather than efficiency. Longer supply chains, larger inventories, duplicate capacity, and higher
insurance costs all add pressure. Supply shocks are also coming from energy disruption, manufacturing
reshoring, critical mineral constraints, and shifts in farming that may reduce yields and lift crop prices in
2026–27. Together, these forces are raising the global cost floor. Inflation is likely to remain more
volatile and settle above central bank targets. Further acceleration would come from additional supply
shocks rather than the baseline outlook.
That means central banks may have less room to support growth than markets hope. Inflation need not
be rising rapidly to constrain policy. It only needs to stay high enough to prevent easing.
The broader challenge is the growing tension between monetary and fiscal constraints. In an
environment of sticky inflation, supply shocks and high debt, policy begins to look less like macro stabilisation
and more like financial repression – even if that is not the explicit goal.
Several risks deserve close monitoring:
Competition for capital between governments and private investors
Higher term premia and vulnerability at the long end of the curve
Rising leverage in non-bank financial institutions and private credit
Liquidity mismatches and dependence on repo and Treasury market intermediation
A data centre may employ a large workforce during construction, but once it is
operating, the headcount drops sharply – while returns accrue mainly to capital
owners. That makes the current cycle less supportive of broad-based household
spending.
In 2027, consumption is expected to remain weak without collapsing. In the
US, aggregate spending is being supported by households at the top of the income
distribution, leaving the consumer exposed to an equity correction. Meanwhile, the
income, savings and credit positions of many other households are deteriorating.
In Europe, consumption depends more on confidence, making it vulnerable to
energy prices and uncertainty. Europe does have a savings buffer that could be
released if confidence improves, while the US has little room to do so.
Inflation and higher rates are biting. The recovery in consumption assumes that
energy prices will fall. Goods spending should stay weak while services and
essentials hold up better. Existing homeowners remain partly cushioned by fixed-
rate mortgages, but younger and lower-income households are being priced out,
reinforcing wealth inequality. The housing market is stalling, as the time needed to
sell residential properties has lengthened and new mortgages carry higher rates.
The result is a rebalancing toward goods weakness and services resilience. That
makes a durable shift from investment-led growth back to consumption-led
growth unlikely in the near term.
Will debt and leverage become the next source of instability? Is financial repression the end game?
Financial repression is one possible policy response, but it is not the core macro question. The more
important issue is the interaction between sovereign borrowing, private investment demand and an
increasingly complex financial system.

This is where financial shocks may differ from physical shocks.
The transmission can be faster, more nonlinear and more difficult to contain.
The distinction between developed and emerging markets is also blurring.
High public debt is increasingly a developed market vulnerability, while many
emerging markets remain more heterogeneous – some exposed, some resilient,
some much improved in terms of central bank credibility and fiscal discipline.
High debt does not automatically imply financial repression. But when debt is
high, inflation is sticky and rates are elevated, the pressure for some form of
repression rises sharply.
The first risk to watch is regulation that creates captive demand – for example,
changes to bank capital rules, stablecoin reserve requirements or home-bias
schemes. The second is disorderly repricing amplified by leverage. A policy
mistake or an unfunded fiscal announcement could be enough to trigger it.
The long end of the curve would likely feel the strain first.
The gap between interest rates and nominal growth has also changed. For
much of the last decade, rates sat below growth, making debt easier to carry. That
gap has narrowed – and in many countries, it has closed. When rates exceed
growth, governments need primary surpluses simply to stabilise debt. Few can
deliver that politically.
For 2027, the risk is not necessarily a debt crisis in the classic sense. It is a
world in which inflation remains above central bank targets, policy stays restrictive
for longer than expected, and markets periodically test the limits of official
tolerance. Leverage will amplify every mistake.

The tail risk is a compound geopolitical rupture.

disruption to energy supply, critical infrastructure or

Guidelines to redesign the portfolio
A more fragmented world, with tighter supply and higher capital needs, calls for a new way of building
portfolios. The usual mix of equities and bonds may no longer provide enough protection, especially when

Three scarcities
The regime is shaped by three mutually reinforcing bottlenecks.
First, physical capacity is constrained. Supply will take years to meet structural demand in grids, data centres,
defence and logistics, creating opportunities but also crowding and overinvestment risks. Second, cheap
capital has disappeared. Public and private borrowers are competing for the same pool of funding, and credit
differentiation is increasing. This supports private lending but raises leverage and refinancing costs,
particularly for private equity and real estate. Third, intermediation capacity is shifting. Banks and the official
sector are retreating, Asian capital is being repatriated, and retail inflows are increasing, although the latter
remains largely untested under stress. This expands private credit’s role but does not guarantee better
underwriting or adequate compensation for risk.
These scarcities are the structural engine behind the changing investment landscape. The opportunity has
migrated: from beta to selection, from leverage to cash flow and from broad macro calls to dispersion.
Private assets and hedge funds are two instruments fit to exploit that migration, each in a different way.
For private assets, this means opportunity in infrastructure (fund/own scarce capacity), direct lending (fill the
bank gap), private equity operational transformation, and secondaries (liquidity providers). For hedge funds,
this means commodity dislocations, fiscal supply trades, capital-structure arbitrage, and divergence/relative
value trades. The themes are right – but the security, structure, entry price, or risks may not be.
The macro regime has shifted structurally, creating a differentiated opportunity for alternatives. They are not simply
diversifiers, but a key monetisation vehicle for the forces reshaping the economy. Capital is becoming more

Following the money
These bottlenecks are driving a major investment cycle across AI and digital infrastructure, electrification and

Two routes to monetisation
Private capital and hedge funds both benefit from the same macro regime, but they do so on different time

REGIONAL VIEWS
US: Capex to sustain growth, inflation to constrain Fed
The US outlook is shaped by the extension of its investment cycle, broadening beyond the AI buildout to
include growing capex in productivity-enhancing software, industrial capacity, supply-chain reshoring, and
energy-related investment from a wider set of sectors, marking a structural supply-side shift for the US
economy. Overall capex will continue to grow, though more moderately than the recent expectations.
Capex remains central to unlocking the productivity gains and substantial investments are required to
preserve US technological leadership. At the same time, with the labour market remaining broadly in
equilibrium, consumption should continue to provide an important backstop for growth. With inflation still
above target and activity holding up better than expected, the Fed should proceed cautiously.
Europe: Investment brightens clouded outlook
The euro area outlook remains caught between the lingering effects of the energy shock on households and
firms and its effort to gain greater strategic independence through increased public and private investment
aimed at boosting infrastructure, defence, and the adoption of new technologies, while closing the
competitiveness gap with major economic players and trading partners. This is particularly true for
Germany, where the fiscal impulse will depend on implementation, financing conditions, and how quickly
commitments are translated into actual spending, with somewhat more delayed effects on the economy
than previously hoped for.
In other countries, more limited fiscal capacity, compounded by tighter financing conditions, may constrain
the overall economic impact of the effort, leaving growth under pressure into 2027 as oil and gas prices
continue to weigh on activity; domestic political uncertainty linked to the electoral cycle will also add to an
already complex geopolitical environment. In this context, the ECB should remain cautious and data-
dependent, keeping policy restrictive until inflation risks are clearer.
Japan: The stars still align
Geopolitics, politics, policies and micro fundamentals are all pulling in the same direction for Japan’s macro
turnaround. The LDP’s lower-house supermajority keeps politics stable at least until Takaichi’s party term
ends in September. That majority secures an FY2027 budget supporting strategic sectors, plus a two-year cut
in food consumption tax from 8% to 1%.
Underlying inflation remains sticky. Another solid spring wage round should keep real wages positive, so we
expect the BoJ to pre-emptively manage upside inflation and demand risks with quarterly 25bp hikes to a 2%
terminal rate. Compared with other DMs, Japan has a smaller deficit, more contained inflation and a central
bank defending its credibility, albeit at its own pace.

China: Transition over stimulus
China’s economy remains divided: exports are strong, while domestic demand remains subdued. As
technology overtakes property as a main contributor to growth, the leadership is likely to view this structural
rebalancing favourably and become more tolerant of slower headline growth. We expect the official growth
target to be lowered from 4.5–5.0% in 2026 to around 4.5% in 2027, allowing the transition to continue while
keeping more decisive stimulus in reserve. Selective rate cuts are likely, but large-scale fiscal support for
consumers remains unlikely.
Fiscal policy effort instead goes into strategic infrastructure. The “Six Networks” programme, an estimated
RMB20–25trn over 2026–30, includes data centre buildouts to narrow the compute gap with the US. This
builds long-term capacity, but offers little near-term relief to households. Hence, we expect the main growth
engine to continue to be external demand in 2027, while soft consumer demand suppresses inflation.
India: Strong growth, rising external constraints
Policy has played a powerful role in shaping India’s current business environment, through micro-reforms,
trade agreements, and policy orthodoxy that has kept inflation expectations anchored, alongside a re-
prioritisation of public expenditure toward capex. GDP growth is expected to remain strong in 2027 at
around 7%, while inflation is likely to breach the target in the short term – a move the RBI appears willing to
contain. Inflation is expected to average around 5% in 2027, and the RBI may hike again toward the end of
2026 or in early 2027 in response to inflation risks and currency weakness.
The challenges posed by rising energy and food prices (El Nino and a fertiliser shortage) are likely to persist,
and India’s twin deficits are not expected to improve materially. The current account deficit is expected to
widen from the current 0.7%, while the fiscal deficit consolidation path is likely to remain intact thanks to
some reallocation of expenditure.
LatAm: Fiscal credibility driving policy divergence
In Brazil, the “no regime change” election outcome has hit sentiment hard. Together with tighter financial
conditions, this will likely weigh on investment and consumption, while inflation, already slightly above
target, is set to rise further in 2027. Stagflationary pressures will complicate the central bank’s task. Easing
may resume after a pause, provided the overdue fiscal consolidation makes some progress in repairing
public finances and preserving financial stability. That would in turn allow the country to take greater
advantage of its rich commodity endowment, which is increasingly in demand in a changing world with
advancing technology. Otherwise, rate hikes remain possible.
Mexico, meanwhile, appears to have passed its weakest point. The economy rebounded after a first-quarter
contraction, supported by robust AI-related manufacturing exports to the US, underscoring the importance
