A Grind From Here: 2027 update and 2028 rollout 
Current dynamics are weighing on the near-term outlook, but a super El Niño, now upgraded to ‘strong-to-very strong’ through January-March 
2027 (79% probability per NOAA), has set the tone for a mild withdrawal season, with November and December tracking below the 10-year average
in weather terms. That backdrop, however, sits alongside a supply-and-demand setup that is structurally tightening into 2027 and 2028, with 
demand-pull dynamics driven mainly by LNG export capacity growth as well as power demand growth (with the support of AI data center demand) 
calling on supply. Our updated forecasts call for 3 Bcf/d of y/y production growth in 2027 (led by Permian-associated gas and the Haynesville) but 
that supply surge is met and ultimately overtaken by 6.2 Bcf/d of new LNG export capacity coming online in 2027 and some more in 2028, plus
eventual power-burn demand potentially hitting new highs. With the balance starting from an oversupplied position and a likely weak withdrawal 
season dragging on near-term prices, we have marked Q4 2026 to market at $3.15/MMBtu, lowered 2027 to $3.45/MMBtu, and launched our 2028 
base case at $3.83/MMBtu. That said, this all still represents a grind higher driven by the demand-pull dynamic we see building through our forecast.
• 
Nearly nine months into the Iran war, prospects for a lasting ceasefire remain limited, with prolonged disruptions elevating global LNG prices 
and increasingly competing for American supply. US LNG exports were up 20% y/y through the first three quarters, with shipments to Asia 
surging 105% y/y as export economics have favored the region, shifting US LNG trade flows since the start of the war. This pull to Asia saw 
exports to Europe lag, down 6% y/y March through September, setting Europe up for a precarious winter with storage at historic lows.  
• 
The results of the US midterm election introduce downside risk to our data center gas burn forecast as candidates who campaigned on stricter 
policies for development won key state races in Texas, Ohio, and Georgia. In Washington, Democratic control of congress also introduces 
uncertainty to the administration's deregulatory agenda and the passage of bipartisan permitting reform, with political gridlock likely on both 
fronts.

US Production 
Overall US Production Trends and Outlook 
Exhibit 3 - US natural gas production outlook 
 
• 
Our updated 2027 and new 2028 production forecasts factor in similar levels 
of growth y/y in annual average terms compared to the 2024-25 and 2025-26 
period. This growth is of course led by associated gas with 2.3-2.6 Bcf/d of 
growth y/y consecutively, the biggest share of which is from the Permian. We 
also expect the Haynesville to add roughly 1.9 Bcf/d each year too, supported 
by prices, existing transportation capacity, and proximity to LNG export 
demand growth. This puts our new production forecasts above 3 Bcf/d in 2027 
and even higher in 2028 if our price outlook holds – necessary given demand 
growth. As the Permian matures, Tier 1 acreage is increasingly spent, and 
associated gas plays become gassier and gassier, it is higher productivity 
through growing rig efficiency, longer laterals, and more enabling 
transportation capacity that drives production growth. While on average total 
daily production receipts have indeed ground higher in 2026, it has been 
uneven recently. This year’s gains are mostly due to efficiency gains and 
increased oil activity leading to associated gas gains, as gas drilling activity in 
gassy basins outside of the Haynesville have not really contributed y/y. 
Source: EIA, Bloomberg, LSEG, company reports, RBC Capital Markets estimates

Production Trends in Major Basins 
Exhibit 6 - Production by basin 
 
• 
The standout producing basin y/y (as of October data) is of course the Permian, 
with dry gas up 3 Bcf/d. This makes sense as rig activity in oil basins has fared far 
better than gas directed activity in gassy basins in y/y terms recently. 
• 
Yet, the Haynesville and Marcellus are both up over 1 Bcf/d y/y. The Haynesville is 
indeed the standout gas basin, as on a net rig basis, its gains have offset losses 
elsewhere (+12 y/y in November, versus -+2 y/y overall). This underscores our view 
that Haynesville will continue to play an important and unique role in the natural 
gas balance going forward given its location and gassy economics. 
• 
Appalachian rig count fell y/y due to lower rig counts in the Utica (-3 y/y in 
November) and flat activity in the Marcellus. We foresee limited, marginal supply 
growth from the Northeast on a net basis in our balance through 2028. 
• 
Oil-directed rig counts grew in the Permian and Eagle Ford, supporting our 
associated gas-led view on production. Even if activity were to slow in relevant oil 
basins (seems unlikely in this price environment), those basins continue to get 
gassier and gassier over time due to both drilling location and vintage regardless.

Weather Impacts 
Exhibit 9 - Near-term forecast 
 
• 
A super El Niño presents the biggest weather risk this winter, broadly expected to 
bring warmer-than-normal conditions across the lower 48 which may ease some 
residential and commercial demand this winter (we have adjusted our forecasts as 
such). The latest NOAA synopsis increased the intensity of the current El Niño, with 
a strong-to-very strong system likely through January-March 2027 (79% chance).  
• 
Medium-term forecasts reflect this expectation for a mild start to the winter, with 
November, despite a looming cold snap, expected to end the month with 6% less 
HDDs than normal. December forecasts are in line with this trend, with HDDs 
expected to slightly undershoot the 10-year average.  
• 
The key 3mo NOAA outlook looks milder than previous outlooks at the onset of 
the winter season, especially in the northern half of the country, where colder 
baseline temperatures make winter heating demand particularly important. Still, 
the typical mild environment expected during El Niño can still give way to volatile 
weather events that may cause short duration volatility for gas.

Powerburn and Near-Term Demand 
Exhibit 12 - Natural gas demand from power 
 
• 
We have moved our 2027 power burn numbers higher, and have released our 2028 
power burn forecasts higher still, at over 38 Bcf/d. About 70% of the y/y growth in 
2028 can be attributed to data-center related demand albeit there’s now some 
fundamental downside risk (see policy section) and some classification/data 
related risk, as a shift to behind the meter may not show up in power data at all. 
Regardless, the power story is second only to LNG exports, in driving the demand-
pull dynamic we expect through at least 2030 for US natural gas balances. 
• 
Between new capacity additions and delayed retirements, the gas-fired generation 
fleet on the grid is poised to continue its upward growth trend through 2028. 
Developers plan to add nearly 30 GW of gas-fired generation capacity to the grid 
in the next two years, building on the y/y trend of increased gas additions since 
2024.  
• 
Even if data center related demand does surprise to the downside, we continue to 
view natural gas as a reliability backstop for the grid, especially during extreme 
weather events which could still emerge in an El Niño winter.

Residential, Commercial, Industrial and Total Consumption Outlook 
Exhibit 15 - Total gas demand outlook 
 
• 
Due to the likely El Niño induced weakness this withdrawal season, the 2026-27 
winter residential and commercial (rescomm) demand period impacts both our 
2026 and 2027 demand forecasts. These numbers are lower than the 22.2 Bcf/d 
annual average number we have seen since the pandemic. Our 2028 forecast is a 
more weather normalized 22 Bcf/d, adding to the y/y demand picture in 2028, 
after adjusting for mild efficiency gains. 
• 
Total demand does scale new highs on the back of power growth, stable industrial 
demand, and in 2028, weather normalized rescomm demand after a likely weak 
2026-27 winter. 
• 
Overall, without export demand, the outlook for gas would be much weaker as 
increasing efficiency and prolific resources, paired with a strong outlook for 
transportation capacity growth in key areas, would weigh on prices. Thus, while 
we are forecasting a new high into 2028 for total domestic demand, it is still the 
export narrative that drives the most strength in our balance.

Policy Outlook 
Exhibit 18 - Data center BTM gas power air permit status, GW 
 
• 
The policy landscape has shifted dramatically after US midterms, with the 
Democratic wave introducing downside risk to our data center gas burn 
forecast, as gubernatorial candidates supporting development moratoriums or 
stricter approval requirements secured victories in Texas, Ohio and Georgia. 
Most consequential is Texas, where Democrats swept statewide offices, 
including Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa and a seat on the Railroad Commission, 
the state’s primary oil and gas regulator. On-site gas plants intending to power 
data centers could face air permitting delays, with Hinojosa potentially 
extending outgoing Governor Abbott’s existing pause. In Texas alone, 57 GW of 
BTM gas generation has applied for air permits, with only 36% receiving final 
approval, leaving 36.4 GW in bureaucratic limbo, for the time being at least.  
• 
Democratic control of Congress also introduces uncertainty to the 
administration’s deregulatory agenda, including EPA’s proposed overhaul of 
Biden-era methane standards. The changes include eliminating the Super 
Emitter Program, easing requirements for marginal wells and reconsidering 
standards for associated gas from oil production. While Democratic majorities 
will gain enhanced oversight and appropriations leverage, the administration 
retains authority to advance regulatory revisions without congressional 
approval. Attempts to block the changes would likely confront a presidential 
veto, leaving congressional scrutiny, funding negotiations and legal challenges 
as the principal constraints on EPA’s deregulatory efforts.  
• 
Permitting reform faces a more uncertain path following Democratic midterm 
gains, despite the Senate passing the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs 
Act by a 62-33 vote. The legislation seeks to accelerate environmental reviews, 
limit litigation uncertainty and streamline approvals for interstate gas 
infrastructure. However, the House must still approve the Senate’s proposal or 
reconcile differences before year-end. The limited lame-duck window and
incoming Democratic Congress leaves the timing of final passage uncertain.  
• 
Federal oil and gas leasing continues to expand through 2026, led by a record 
$4bln May lease sale across 33,530 acres in New Mexico and Texas, underscoring 
strong industry interest in the Permian Basin. More recently September onshore 
sales in Wyoming and Colorado generated $87.3mln, following $82.7mln in high 
bids from an August offshore Gulf of Mexico sale. While expanded leasing 
supports longer-term production potential, the impact on near-term gas supply 
remains limited.

US Exports 
Exhibit 20 - US Feedgas volumes 
 
• 
US LNG exports are the primary driver of the demand-pull dynamic that leads to 
our constructive view on US natural gas prices. With our feedgas forecast now 
projected to reach 21.6 Bcf/d in 2027 and 24.9 Bcf/d in 2028 (note that we have 
adjusted for stronger utilization in 2027 given our teams view on conflict in the 
Middle East), new capacity coming online is the driver of this wave of demand. 
Specifically, new capacity totaling 6.2 Bcf/d in 2027 and 1.4 Bcf/d in 2028, will pull 
more molecules and incentivize supply growth. Our 2028 forecast sees our 
assumed utilization numbers fall, reflecting our view that as capacity grows, US 
export infrastructure utilization levels may not remain so much higher than global 
peers. Regardless, we view LNG export capacity growth as the clearest driver of 
our view in the 2027-28 period. We have tightened our model (see Figure 21) 
through updated utilization numbers and show capacity additions and feedgas 
expectations through 2030, with the latter trend towards 30 Bcf/d. 
• 
For pipeline trade, Canadian net imports have strengthened and driven the total 
net import number closer to positive territory ahead of the withdrawal season. 
Source: LSEG, RBC Capital Markets

Storage Dynamics 
Underground Storage Status and Outlook 
Exhibit 23 - End of season storage forecasts 
 
• 
While this update only moves our 2027 storage forecasts by about 0.1 Tcf for both 
end of withdrawal and end of injection, in 2028 we do forecast lower end-of-
season storage volumes closer to 2022 levels.  
• 
Despite ample production growth in both 2027 and 2028, the growth in power 
demand (even with the downside risk to data center growth) and of course, the 
planned LNG export capacity additions, we do not think 2028 will be in any danger 
of touching the highs seen in 2024 and 2025 in end of season terms.  
• 
These lower storage volumes y/y in 2028 align with stronger natural gas price 
forecasts across all scenarios. In 2028, the risk skew is incrementally higher than 
2027 as a result, and we note that our low scenarios also grind higher too. 
• 
We expect the recent, relatively normal, price to storage relationship to continue 
and not revisit the exaggerated outcomes seen after Russia invaded Ukraine. 
However, we do note that the new normal does still give room for a wider set of 
price outcomes for any given storage surplus/deficit to the five-year average.

Global Gas 
US LNG Exports and Global Trade 
Exhibit 26 - Global gas prices 
 
• 
Nearly nine months into the Iran war, prospects for a lasting ceasefire remain 
limited, prolonging disruptions to critical Middle East natural gas supply and 
leaving global LNG markets increasingly strained. TTF and Asian spot prices have 
surged 158% and 189% since the start of the conflict, respectively, while Henry 
Hub remains relatively unchanged. 
• 
In the first three quarters of this year, total US LNG exports were up 20% y/y as 
buyers increasingly turn to American supply to offset Middle East disruptions. 
October exports rose 12% y/y and 3% m/m, supported in part by the continued 
ramp-up of Golden Pass LNG. While incremental exports have been constrained by

US LNG Exports to Europe 
Exhibit 30 - US LNG exports to Europe by terminal* 
 
• 
Amid high prices and increased competition for cargos, European imports of US 
LNG have lagged since the start of the war, down 6% between March and 
September y/y. The loss of Qatari supply has seen a significant pull of US LNG to 
Asia, as USCG-to-Asia netbacks rose higher than European netbacks in March, 
maintaining that lead until September. The shift in regional mix was especially 
acute between May and July when US LNG exports to Europe fell to 45% of total 
exports versus 62% last year. However, the past few months have seen a recovery, 
with September imports up 8% m/m and 5% y/y, even as landed prices for US LNG 
cargos are 143% higher than before the war.  
• 
The summer slowdown of imports is reflected in historically low injection levels, 
with Europe entering withdrawal season at only 75% full compared with the 5-year 
average of 89%. This delta is driven by Germany, with inventories now at just 58% 
versus a 5-year average of 88%. Ultimately, Europe is a price taker in this 
environment and low storage levels could keep demand elevated even if Qatari 
supply begins to recover.

US LNG Exports to Asia 
Exhibit 33 - US LNG exports to Asia by terminal* 
 
• 
The continued disruption of Middle East supply has required Asia to significantly 
increase its demand for US LNG, with exports to Asia in the first three quarters of

•

seasonal trends, but volumes still remain elevated. September volumes are down 
5% m/m but 74% higher y/y. As October netbacks to Asia return to a premium, 
favorable export economics should sustain Asia's ability to outcompete for US LNG 
even if supply constraints persist and prices continue to climb.

Volatility and Seasonality 
Exhibit 39 - Historical volatility seasonal graph 
 
• 
Aside from the spike in volatility in early 2026, this year has bucked a multi-year 
trend of higher volatility in the latter handful of years than the prior handful (see 
Figure 39 where blue and yellow (latter) tend to be higher than grey (former years). 
• 
We have said previously that we think that higher intra-seasonal volatility is 
structural given the fundamental shifts in the balance, but this year is a lesson that 
that is not always the case, especially with volatility not living up to that theme 
currently. We eye this withdrawal season as a potential end for this scenario. 
• 
While seemingly lower, volatility is currently in line with historical average levels. 
Importantly, absent unexpected or large cold spells this winter, volatility may not 
live up to usual withdrawal season hype, making that the case for the foreseeable 
future. 
• 
Seasonally, natural gas is pricing around 2023 and 2024 levels for this time in the 
year, and is facing down either a 2024 or 2023 style end to the year. We lean 
towards 2024 despite the implications of El Niño, but only mildly so.

Balance and Price Outlook 
Supply-Demand Outlook and Price Forecasts 
Where our view has shifted is stronger production growth, led by the associated gas basins and the Permian specifically, with the Haynesville contributing as well as the star 
of the dry gas basins. This is because despite what is set up to be a weak winter overall in both demand and volatility terms, albeit one that can still have cold spells and buck 
that trend at times. With the massive amount of LNG export capacity coming online, and positive power demand growth expectations (although the midterms point to 
potential downside risk for some data center demand related forecasts), we see a demand-pull narrative that requires more supply growth. Our new supply forecasts now 
point to 3 Bcf/d of growth y/y in 2027, and more still in 2028. While this supply growth does not surpass the export and demand additions in our balance necessarily, the 
balance is starting from an oversupplied picture, and of course, a likely weaker than average withdrawal season. We have marked to market our Q4 forecast, lowered 2027, 
and rolled out 2028, although each should be read as a grind higher versus prior/current levels given the demand-pull dynamic we have described.
