Industry View
In-Line
AI is becoming more tangible in European Software: product 
roadmaps are maturing, with clearer  agentic functionality, 
monetisation models aligning towards customer value, and 
greater c-suite commentary around optimising cost structures 
for efficiency. 
Key Take 1 – Agentic AI: Product strategies are becoming clearer, supporting 
confidence in the medium-term growth opportunity. Compared with last year's 
conference, when AI product roadmaps remained relatively early and compelling use 
cases were still being established, management teams demonstrated a clear 
evolution in their strategies. Vendors are increasingly able to explain how 
embedding AI into their core applications can deliver additional customer value, 
particularly through agents capable of executing work across existing business 
processes. For example, SAP's vision of the autonomous enterprise illustrates this 
ambition, with agents operating across interconnected Finance, HR and other 
enterprise workflows, supported by its understanding of customer data and 
processes. Dassault Systemes  sees virtual companions drawing on scientific and 
engineering knowledge to perform work across Engineering and Life Sciences.  
Wolters Kluwer's integration of Libra extends its proprietary legal content into AI-
powered drafting and workflow tools, providing a more tangible example of how 
vendors can expand their role in customers' day-to-day activities. We see the greater 
clarity around product strategies and use cases as encouraging for the sector's 
medium-term growth potential. However, investors will increasingly want evidence 
that these opportunities are translating into incremental revenue. Investor focus will 
shift to monetisation timelines and proof-points of early adoption building, and our 
expectation is that associated revenue is more likely a 2H27 than 1H27 story, in 
general. SAP's planned introduction of a dedicated AI-related revenue KPI in January 
2027 is a positive step, with this type of disclosure likely to be important in building 
confidence in the tangibility of the AI growth opportunity.
Key Take 2 – Monetisation: Pricing models are evolving to better reflect 
customer value. With product strategies becoming clearer, attention is increasingly 
turning to how vendors charge for the additional work their AI products can 
perform. In Software, management teams are experimenting with different 
approaches that move beyond traditional seat-based subscriptions. Dassault's Blue 
tokens charge according to outputs generated, while Nemetschek expects to 
combine subscriptions with consumption-based credits as its AI-enabled Max 
products develop. SAP's committed-consumption model similarly aims to align 
charges with usage while providing customers with greater spending predictability. 
These approaches reflect an effort to capture more of the value created through 
automation, while recognising that customers remain sensitive to adding uncertainty

adoption, particularly as customers seek confidence in the returns from their AI 
investments.
Key Take 3 – AI productivity: A growing body of evidence points towards leaner 
operating models. We interpret there to be increasingly willingness from 
management teams to talk about  AI's implications for their own cost bases.  SAP 
expects headcount to decline as productivity gains outweigh incremental staffing 
requirements, while Amadeus believes it can drive future growth with its current 
employee base size. Dassault similarly expects AI to halve the incremental hiring 
required, although it has no plans to reduce its workforce as it focuses on enlarging 
growth opportunities. These comments suggest the potential for software vendors 
to sustain revenue growth with structurally lower incremental personnel costs. 
Aside from the conference on this, alongside its FY26 results this week, Sage 
announced a c. £100m restructuring charge associated with simplifying its 
organisation, reducing management layers and investing in AI-native engineering. 
Management expects a small net reduction in headcount, alongside continued hiring 
in areas such as engineering. This follows IONOS' September announcement 
targeting a c. 12% workforce reduction, with savings earmarked for reinvestment in 
AI development and cloud growth. While these programmes extend beyond AI-
driven automation alone, they suggest companies are beginning to rethink how their 
organisations should operate. Separately, we think CFO concerns around token 
costs have subsided compared to earlier in the year, as they have built up internal 
capability to manage token costs better. For investors, the emerging debate is 
whether greater operating-expense efficiency can drive sustained EBIT margin 
expansion while offsetting potential gross-margin headwinds from higher AI 
computing costs and partner payments. Given the consistency and specificity of 
management commentary, we see encouraging potential for AI productivity to 
support operating leverage, even allowing for continued reinvestment in growth.
A view from the privates – who captures the value of enterprise AI? Mistral and 
Databricks both presented at the conference, providing other insights on Software 
trends.  Both argued that model intelligence is becoming less of a constraint for 
many enterprise applications, with differentiation increasingly dependent on access 
to relevant data, customisation and cost-efficient deployment. Mistral sees growing 
demand for open models as customers seek greater control over sensitive data and 
AI costs, while Databricks highlighted open-source models' increasing share of token 
consumption on its platform. More significantly, Databricks is expanding beyond 
data infrastructure into agentic applications, arguing that agents need context
