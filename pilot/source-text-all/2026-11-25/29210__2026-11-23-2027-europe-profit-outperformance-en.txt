◼ External and domestic shocks have derailed Europe’s recovery twice in 2025 and 2026 
◼ With most rates downsides priced in, 2027 is looking up: third-time lucky?     
◼ Unlike US, Europe is not at “peak earnings”; in fact, margins are just about to expand  
Over the past two years we have heard a lot about “surprising European macro resilience” in 
the face of mighty shocks – from Liberation Day tariffs to a series of negative energy shocks 
culminating in the ongoing Iran conflict. But to the attentive observer of the European macro 
cycle there is nothing surprising about this resilience. The slow pendulum of underlying macro 
and policy forces in Europe, especially in the Euro Area, had shifted markedly despite the long 
fallout from the post-pandemic and Ukraine war shocks, leaving the economy and assets primed 
for a major rebound – other things constant. Of course, “other things” didn’t stay “constant”, with 
European markets experiencing a couple of false starts since. But looking forward, most 
downsides (albeit not all – see below) are now priced into European assets, while 2027 looks 
up, especially when considering the outlook for profits relative to the US.  
Europe can outperform. To see how, just cast your mind back to last November when European 
equities began outperforming relative to the US until the Iran war broke out. It’s not by chance 
that such outperformance coincided with a relatively shock-free period, as the impact of 
Liberation Day tariffs faded, further weakened by the Trump-Xi summit in Busan, South Korea, 
and by rising expectations that SCOTUS would strike down IEEPA tariffs, which happened early 
this year. A turn in the European manufacturing cycle, German stimulus getting into gear and US 
stocks wrestling with souring AI trades, owing to concerns about excessive AI capex and its 
impact on profitability, also helped. But it was the lack of offsetting negative shocks that allowed

present the manufacturing cycle and German fiscal stimulus remain supportive, while AI trades 
are facing even stronger headwinds than last year.   
But then, as we know, the fallout from the Iran war in Europe (spiking energy prices and higher 
interest rates) added to intensifying domestic political dramas. The result was higher 
precautionary savings, as well as lower consumption, investment and market sentiment, 
especially in France. Some factors such as strong AI-related exports and stockpiling of 
manufacturing goods after the Iran war started have helped earlier this year, but the main drivers 
of European growth and asset performance have been impaired by rising uncertainty, input 
costs and interest rates. 
But looking forward to 2027, the glass is half-full for European assets.  
Profit recovery. In a recent note, my colleague Dario listed three main reasons why US earnings 
have likely peaked: (i) fading energy profits’ impulse and no savings buffers to fund an offsetting 
boost to non-energy earnings; (ii) slower AI-related capex; and (iii) rising unit labour cost 
recoupling with core inflation from below – i.e. a margin squeeze – as the composition of US 
growth pivots back from productivity gains to employment. Each of these US-negative factors 
contain a benign mirror-image message for Europe. 
Energy and savings. Unlike the US, which is a net energy exporter, Europe is one of the largest 
importers of hydrocarbons in the world and a lot more sensitive to energy prices owing to its 
manufacturing-heavy sector composition. To be sure, Europe too has large energy companies 
that do benefit from higher energy prices and refinery margins, cushioning the overall negative 
impact on earnings across all other sectors. But there is no question that a resolution of the Iran 
conflict would lift European profits via lower input costs and rates (more on the latter below). In 
fact, even a stabilisation could deliver lower prices through lower risk premia, as we’ve seen 
recently. According to Kpler data, crude flows out of Hormuz are back to pre-war levels and 
product flows are improving too, while shipping rates have plunged. With demand adjusting 
lower, the latest EIA estimates of supply balance show a net surplus for the first time since the 
war started, which has pushed the Brent crack spread at the lowest level since the war started.  
Moreover, in response to the 2026 energy shock European consumers did the opposite of US 
consumers – as usual, they retrenched, cut spending and grew their savings. This is especially

AI-related profits. As we said, the AI-related capex boom has benefitted some European 
manufacturers that emerged as important links in the global AI infrastructure and compute 
supply chain – a fact we documented earlier this year, e.g. here and here. This has helped support 
factory orders and exports despite the energy shock to the point that AI has become one of the 
main pillars of the “surprising economic resilience” narrative now in vogue, including at the ECB 
(and a convenient fig leaf for those analysts that are forced to revise up overly pessimistic growth 
forecasts rushed out after the Iran war started…). With hyperscalers’ spending already cresting in 
YoY terms and a broader AI-related capex slowdown in the US likely in train, there are questions 
about negative spillovers on Europe. But, for all its welcome support, AI has a very small macro 
and market footprint in Europe compared to both US and Asia.  
Based on data recently presented by Philip Lane in a rather AI-bullish speech, most of the recent 
cumulative rise in EA total market production (manufacturing and services) boils down to the 
electronics and information & communication sectors (Chart 2a in the speech). But even 
assuming all this is thanks to AI only, numbers are small (0.6% over two years) and, crucially, total 
market production is not value added – i.e. it does not represent real growth, because it includes 
inputs. Indeed, like for the US, other data by Lane show that AI did boost goods exports but 
boosted imports more, while we know that the EA is a net importer of AI “services”. So, the net 
trade effect on growth has in fact been negative (see Chart 5 with data from Lane’s Table 4). 
In terms of market exposure, the weight of tech in European stock indices is a fraction of that in 
the US and Asia and the rolling correlations between Nasdaq100 and Stoxx600 log daily returns 
are around 50-75% lower than those of the S&P 500. There is no question that a full-fledged AI 
crash would be a global risk-off event with few places where to hide in the short run, but Europe is 
set up to suffer much less than other jurisdictions. Unquestionably European investors have been 
taking part to the AI stock boom in the US and globally. As TS Lombard clients know, we have an 
open debate about whether “wealth effects” in the US are a key driver of the macro outlook or not, 
but there is no such debate for Europe. Without a US 401K equivalent in Europe and a lot lower 
level of financial asset ownership across households – including because of the low prevalence

If anything, there is a case for being less negative on the outlook of European firms involved in the 
global AI supply chain than on that of many parts of the US AI complex, especially if one believes 
in the long-term AI fundamental story. As we know, Europe is lagging in the AI buildout cycle (see 
Chart 6 above). In the short run this implies that still stronger European domestic demand could 
offset some pullback from abroad, if it were to happen. In the longer run, structural forces such 
as binding security and sovereignty imperatives are likely to support a further buildout in Europe 
from the current low starting point, while giving local firms building and furnishing datacentres an 
industrial and regulatory advantage. Finally, in the case of an AI crash, several European firms 
involved in the AI supply chain are established large industrial companies with big lines of 
business beyond AI, which would significantly benefit from the inevitable decline in rates, helping 
offset losses.  
Unit labour cost, productivity and margins. The AI buildout cycle is not the only cycle in which 
Europe lags the US. Labour cost, output and productivity are also lagging. This is good news for 
Euro Area profit margins, which, contrary to the US, are now starting to re-expand, especially 
in Germany. Like in the US, the post-pandemic labour shortages sparked a surge in new hiring; 
labour tightness and inflation pushed up wages. But the infamous rigidity of European job 
markets implied that while US firms flipped to trimming their labour force and focusing on 
efficiency gains a couple of years ago, European firms hoarded workers. The series of 
overlapping negative shocks in the past two years froze new hiring, but that was not enough to 
push down unit labour cost as growth slowed too. The result was more persistent profit margin 
pressure.  
Now, employment always lags activity, and the flip side of Europe’s loose and slow-moving labour 
markets is that wage growth is likely to continue to hover around 2.5-3% next year. But output 
reacceleration – which we expect to continue next year, projecting an EA growth of ~1.5% – 
implies lower unit labour cost and higher labour productivity (Chart 7). This nascent trend is 
already visible in recent data, and especially so in Germany where output and employment have 
been most affected by negative shocks and are primed for a rebound (Chart 8 and 9, and today’s 
strong German PMIs) – too bad nobody talks about it... Finally, to the extent that profits also lead

Beyond these factors, the outlook for rates and euro is also a tailwind for European profits.  
ECB tightening is priced in, while high real rates are already transmitting to the economy, 
which caps the downside looking forward. Markets have long discounted a 3% ECB terminal 
rate, with Christine Lagarde at the latest press conference confirming another hike in December. 
Meanwhile, recent bond gyrations – also owing to the steep repricing of European political risk – 
have driven real rates and yields to levels prevailing before the GFC. Data from ECB’s Bank 
Lending Survey shows that higher rates since Q2 took just a few months to start transmitting to 
the economy, with demand for bank credit lower across the board mostly on account of higher 
cost of money. Some growth headwinds from ECB tightening – both delivered and priced – will 
continue into next year, but less than if the transmission lags were more in line with the traditional 
12-18 months. What’s more, the ECB has become more worried about the strong and rapid 
tightening in financing conditions, as made explicit in October. Similarly, the Bank 
acknowledged once again that indirect and second-round effects from higher energy prices 
are not showing up in consumer inflation, which puts the ECB in a very different position from 
the Fed (more below). Since then, real rates have further increased on our measures. The 
implication is that it wouldn’t take much of an improvement in commodity markets or much of a

Significantly weaker euro since summer is also a booster for the earnings of European 
exporters and multinationals (Chart 12). Indeed, the euro has not only depreciated a lot against 
the dollar, but also against the yuan – a meaningful improvement in the context of the ongoing 
competition with China in both Euro Area domestic market and in third markets abroad. 
We see three major risks to this relatively benign view for European profits and risk assets:  
1. The risk of an energy crunch has not passed yet, neither has that of another escalation 
in the Persian Gulf, especially until the new US Congress is sworn in in January, as 
Trump’s erratic policymaking is still unchecked. A new major US offensive would send 
energy prices sharply back to where they traded before the Midterms while reinflating 
risk premia that would add further pressure on both stocks and bonds, especially in 
Europe. With most of the heating season still ahead of us, further constraints on crude 
and product flows out of Hormuz would imply an even tighter global gas market and 
steeper prices for European and Asian customers.  
2. Stronger than expected spillovers from still not-fully-priced Fed tightening cycle on 
global financing conditions and equities. Global markets are just beginning to adjust to 
the prospects of the Fed tightening more than they have priced in so far. We have long 
expected the Fed to embark on a series of 25bp quarterly hikes until the end of next year 
on the back of stronger domestically generated core inflation and a reacceleration in job 
markets. The market has partially caught up with that forecast (~three extra hikes have 
now been priced back in after the latest payroll and CPI releases refocussed minds), but 
there is still room for higher US rates.  
3. Europe-US “decoupling” narratives have a bad historical track record. In fact, in the 
past they’ve often marked the top in markets before major downturns and… at TS 
Lombard we are quite superstitious! The risk is that current market jitters – for now 
mostly localised around AI – couple with a sharper Fed repricing and lower US earnings 
prospects to generate a much broader and persistent equity sell-off spilling over across 
sectors globally. That said, for such a broad sell-off to happen and remain persistent, i.e. 
without an form of ex-post investor discrimination across regions, we should be wrong 
about many other core forecasts we are making on the underlying strength of the US and 
European macroeconomy – perish the thought! In fact, as we discussed above and Dario 
argued in his latest “stress-test” of the “Perkini Report”, even assuming that what we are 
witnessing on our screens right now is indeed the much-dreaded, ultimate AI crash, there 
is a decent argument for expecting some form of European decoupling after the initial all-
engulfing risk-off move.
