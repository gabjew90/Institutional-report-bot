The market impact of higher ECB MRR 
 
 
Press reports suggest the ECB is again considering an increase in Minimum Reserve 
Requirements (MRR), from 1% to 2% of euro area banks’ short-term liabilities1. The 
ECB’s communication on the rationale for this has been relatively scarce. But it 
suggests the Governing Council views such an increase as a modest technical 
adjustment, aiming to lower National Central Banks’ interest spending. Below, we 
update and expand our prior analysis on the likely market impact. 
At the aggregate euro area level, increasing MRR from 1% to 2% would see excess 
reserves decline by about EUR 175bn. To put it into context, this corresponds to an 
~8% decline in the stock of excess reserves or about 4-5 months of quantitative 
tightening by the ECB at its current pace. Because of the starting level of reserves 
and the relatively healthy market functioning so far, we think this is likely to be 
digestible by euro area money markets. That being said, as we have shown in recent 
research, the ECB’s survey of bank treasurers suggests reserve availability is already 
close to the upper bound of the band for plausible bank reserve demand (Exhibit 1).

An important consideration is that the impact of higher MRR could be uneven across 
countries. This is because excess liquidity itself is unevenly distributed across the euro 
area, with the bulk located in “core” countries (Exhibit 3). We map where the impact 
could be most pronounced by sorting major euro area economies according to where an 
increase in MRR would be most significant and by the resulting level of excess reserves, 
comparing both to the size of the banking sector (Exhibit 4). This analysis suggests that 
excess liquidity would decline the most in Greece, Belgium and Portugal. Italy and Spain 
would stand out as economies where liquidity is poised to be the thinnest following an 
increase in MRR, making these banking systems the likeliest to resort to increased 
market or ECB borrowing. 
 
A key channel to keep in mind as a mediator of the impact of higher MRR on banks’ 
behavior is liquidity regulation. Through the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR), banks must 
have sufficient high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) to face sudden outflows. A EUR 175bn 
decline in excess reserves would, all else equal, take the aggregate LCR of euro area banks 
lower by 5ppt. LCR ratios are currently well above the levels prescribed by regulation

LCRs are uneven across business models and typically lower at banks that are most

Since the ECB started to shrink its balance sheet in 2022, euro area banks have raised 
market funding in a number of ways, with debt securities issuance (both short and 
long-term) and interbank lending being the most prevalent (Exhibit 5). Repo market 
turnover has increased too, but the increase in repo liabilities is less visible as a share of 
bank assets. With interbank lending growing much more than repo as a share of bank 
assets since 2022, it appears that banks have chosen this source of funding as the 
marginal source as excess liquidity declines. For this reason, we believe spreads between 
OIS and unsecured money markets are where an increase in MRR is most likely to have an 
impact. We also note that ESTR-BOR bases have been most volatile around MRR-related 
news in recent months. 
Our fair value framework for bases relies on excess liquidity, bank credit spreads and 
rates volatility as inputs. It typically finds that the impact of lower reserves on bases is 
about 1bp for each 1ppt decrease in reserves as a share of bank assets, with the impact 
both more important and more reliably significant at the front end of the curve. On this 
framework, front-end bases are screening fair while long-end are more dislocated, with 
flows2 driving a disconnect with fair value in recent years. This leaves 2y2y as the best 
curve segment to position for a decline in liquidity, in our view (Exhibit 8).

At its operational framework review in 2024, the ECB set a sequencing for balance sheet 
reduction. According to this, the ECB would first wait for bank borrowing through its 
conventional lending facilities to increase, then unveil longer-term structural refinancing 
operations, and finally embark on structural asset purchases – effectively halting QT. 
Accelerating the decline in excess liquidity would indicate the ECB is comfortable with 
money market functioning, and, within the operational framework above, may also 
suggest that the ECB views the QT process as closer to the start than the end. So while 
the ECB may view the increase in MRR as a small technical adjustment, making this 
adjustment at a time when sovereign credit is in focus may convey the signal that the bar 
is elevated to pause QT. Because of this, we think a higher MRR could, at the margin, be a 
negative for sovereign credit, too.
