Banks after the comeback:
 	 What comes next?
Dirk Becker
Senior Portfolio Manager, 
Global Financials
Carola Dorn
Product Specialist
Banks have made a remarkable comeback. After 
years of extremely low interest rates when the sector 
played only a supporting role for many investors, the 
operating environment and the perception of banks 
have changed markedly. Yet given this recent strong 
performance the investment question becomes more 
interesting. How much of the comeback is already 
behind us, and what could drive the next phase? The 
answer is likely to be more nuanced than at the start 
of the recovery. For investors, the question today is 
less about the return of the sector as a whole and 
more about where sustainable earnings growth and 
attractive returns on capital can still come from. 
Europe offers an interesting starting point in this 
respect. Earnings expectations for European banks 
have, since the start of 2024, seen significantly 
stronger upwards revisions than for most other major 
European sectors.
After strong share price performance, this is crucial. The 
sector no longer has to prove that it has recovered from the 
difficult years of the low-interest-rate period; the recovery 
story is increasingly turning into an earnings story.

Where earnings growth can come from
A global view reveals how different the earnings drivers 
within the banking sector can be. Interest rate cycles, loan 
growth, and competition for deposits are not moving in 
step across Europe, the US and Asia. At the same time, 
lending, wealth management, payments and capital 
markets activities play a very different role depending 
on the institution. A strong deposit base can become a 
competitive advantage just as much as growing assets 
under management or a revival in issuance and M&A. As 
the broad tailwind from rising interest rates fades, these 
different sources of earnings could become increasingly 
important in driving the next phase.
For European banks, earnings development has been 
particularly remarkable. In the years of the financial 
crisis, and of low and negative interest rates in 2008-
2022, earnings were under heavy pressure. It took until 
2023 for the banks to regain their earnings levels of 2007. 
In the coming years, analysts expect further jumps in 
this respect.

Capital allocation is another factor. After years of higher 
profitability, many banks have substantial capital which 
they can return via dividends and share buybacks or 
deploy for organic growth, technology development, and 
acquisitions. JPMorgan Chase shows what that financial 
flexibility can look like in practice. The bank posted a net 
income of USD 15.3 billion in the third quarter of 2026, 
supported by a broad business model with growth in 
investment banking, payments, securities services, and 
wealth management, among others.¹ For investors, it 
increasingly matters not only how much a bank earns, but 
also what it does with the capital it generates.
Is there still room after the re-rating?
The strong performance has not gone unnoticed in the 
equity markets and the valuations of European banks 
have now moved well away from their earlier lows. This 
raises an obvious question for investors: how much of the 
recovery is already priced in?
Higher valuations meet higher profitability
During the low-interest-rate period, European banks traded, 
on average, at a discount to book value. This was justified 
as the banks generated returns on equity of 4-7%, which did 
not cover their costs of capital. Now, however, returns on 
equity have recovered to their pre-financial-crisis level and 
are back at around 14%. The rally alone therefore does not 
mean that the investment opportunity has been exhausted. 
Rather, what matters is that the drivers of future returns 
are changing. While the first phase was strongly shaped 
by the re-rating of the sector as a whole, earnings growth 
and capital returns can move further into the foreground 
going forward. The investment case does not have to end 
simply because part of the re-rating has already taken 
place. Nevertheless, the stronger starting position does not, 
of course, make banks risk-free. The challenges differ by 
region, from commercial real estate and refinancing in the 
US, through loan growth and deposit competition in Europe, 
to credit quality and property markets in parts of Asia.
A longer-term earnings tailwind for 
European banks
In Europe, a further long-term earnings driver comes from 
balance sheet management. Banks invest part of their 
liquidity for the long term in order to stabilise their interest 
margins across the interest rate cycle. During the long 
low-interest-rate period, the yield on these investments fell, 
and the effect became visible in earnings. That process 
is now starting to reverse. As low-yielding investments 
mature, banks can gradually reinvest the proceeds at 
higher yields. The benefit will take time to come through, 
but it could support earnings for several years. This is 
particularly relevant for banks in Italy, Spain, Greece and 
Portugal,where  lending rates adjusted relatively quickly to 
higher interest rates, while the rates paid on deposits rose 
much more slowly. As a result, banks are able to benefit 
from attractive deposit margins for longer.

New opportunities beyond the recovery
A global approach makes it possible to exploit 
different opportunities across regions. The investment 
opportunity extends beyond banks. Insurers can 
benefit from more attractive reinvestment yields, 
while asset and wealth managers participate more 
directly in rising assets under management and the 
development of the capital markets. Investing across 
this global universe of financial companies allows the 
combination of different regional cycles and sources 
of earnings. The investment opportunity has not 
disappeared. It has changed. Earnings growth and 
capital allocation are likely to play a greater role in 
determining which companies lead the next phase. 
For investors, the question is no longer simply whether 
financials can recover, but which companies can 
translate their stronger starting position into attractive 
returns over the long term.
