The next phase of capital markets growth & the AI infra impact
In this report, we examine the drivers of the step change in capital markets revenue since 2020, as well as market share trends over the past 15+ 
years. Key highlights: 
While there have been a number of cyclical drivers, in our view, the single biggest structural driver of revenue growth has been the expansion in  the 
•
pool of tradable assets and the shift to financing those assets, vs. improved revenue margins. 
We expect these structural factors to drive further long-term growth from here, with more growth potential within investment banking and
•
equities. 
In the near term, however, AI infrastructure-related capital markets activity has been an important catalyst for both investment banking and
•
equity trading revenue, accounting for a meaningful part of 2026 revenue growth - we look to size this contribution.
US firms across bulge bracket banks, market makers, and independent IBanks have been disproportionate beneficiaries and share 
•
gainers from the growth in capital markets, with market share increasing 23pp to 72% from 2010-26E. We also believe that share gains by 
independent IBanks could slow, given more competition for talent, and the fact that a high percentage of AI investment banking activity has a financing
component, which favors large banks. 
Top beneficiaries: 
Bulge brackets: Buy-rated BAC and JPM 
IBanks: Buy-rated EVR, LCLN, PIPR and PJT.

Capital markets revenue growth acceleration and ROE expansion. There has been a step up in capital markets revenue 
n
growth since 2019, with a 2019-26E industry fee pool CAGR of 11%, vs. a 2010-19 CAGR of -1% (Exhibit 1, Exhibit 2, 
Exhibit 3, Exhibit 4). We forecast capital markets grow at a 4% 2026E-28E CAGR, i.e., a slower, but still healthy growth 
rate. We attribute the bulk of this growth to the expansion in the pool of tradable assets, as the increased scale of global 
debt and equity markets, rather than improved revenue margins. Large bank trading assets are up ~110% since 2019 
(Exhibit 7, Exhibit 10, Exhibit 16, Exhibit 17). Revenue margins have been largely flat over the same time period, with 
2Q26 capital markets revenue yields at ~550bps, ~25bps above the long-term average (Exhibit 7). Banks have benefited 
from greater balance sheet flexibility following Nov. 2025 SLR reform, with leverage (avg. assets/equity) rising ~5.0x to 
~20.0x from 2019-1H26 (Exhibit 19). Balance sheet growth has been accompanied by operating leverage, with capital 
markets business efficiency ratios falling 7pp to 53% from 2019-1H26, as higher fixed costs have translated into 
economies of scale (Exhibit 14). In aggregate, capital markets ROEs have risen ~8pp to 21% from 2019-1H26 (Exhibit 5). 
AI infrastructure-related (AI-related) activity has been the marginal driver of investment banking in 2026... The 
n
growth in both financing requirements and market capitalization of AI infrastructure companies has played a central role 
in recent IB revenue growth. We estimate that ~50-55% of the growth in combined M&A, ECM and DCM volumes and fees 
has been driven by AI-related transactions so far this year. 2026 TD industry IB volumes/fees are up 15%/14% YoY, but 
we estimate AI-related volumes/fees are up ~275%/310% YoY (Exhibit 24, Exhibit 26). Excluding AI-related activity, 
industry IB volumes and fees are up only 7% YTD YoY, indicating that underlying activity is less stretched than headline 
growth implies, and that IBanking (outside of tech / AI related sectors) remains below peak levels. 
...and a meaningful part of 1H26 Equities revenue growth. The impact has been evident across both Equities 
n
intermediation and financing. We estimate that ~40% of the $10.8bn YoY growth in 1H26 Equities revenue (or +47% YoY) 
has been AI-related. On intermediation, we use US market turnover of AI infrastructure as our proxy, which has 
comprised 72% of the YoY increase in total US market turnover, which we estimate has driven 42% of the intermediation 
revenue growth (Exhibit 22). On financing, we use market cap, with AI infrastructure stocks accounting for 77% of the YoY 
increase in total US equity market cap, resulting in 38% share of Equities financing revenue growth from AI activity 
(Exhibit 22). As a result, 1H26 equities revenue ex-AI would be up a more modest ~+30% YoY. 
Outside of AI, 2026E Equities growth remains strong, while FICC grows slower.

Capital markets fees continue to grow, and ROEs rise, driven by growth in tradable float of assets, and higher markets 
 
 
After remaining largely flat from 2010-19, the capital markets fee pool has seen significant growth, rising at an 11% 
CAGR from 2019-26E to $454bn (Exhibit 2). We expect this growth to continue, albeit at a slower pace, rising at 4% p.a. 
from 2026E-28E, with the estimated fee pool reaching $488bn. We see structural fee pool growth, driven by: 1) 
expansion in the pool of tradable assets contributing to growth in intermediation fees; 2) the growth in businesses 
financing this asset growth (Prime brokerage and NDFI lending in particular), which have contributed to significant 
growth in markets NII; 3) higher market cap driving increased M&A and underwriting fees; 4) the significant financing 
need from AI capex, which increasingly is being financed by capital markets (see subsequent section on AI impacts on 
cap markets businesses); and 5) the trading balance sheet flexibility created through additional SLR capacity post 
recent SLR reform (see report here). Structural growth has driven higher returns on equity for large banks’ capital 
markets businesses, which have risen ~8pp from 2019-1H26 to 21% (Exhibit 5). Notably, this ROE improvement in 
capital markets businesses has been driven by a combination of balance sheet growth and margin expansion, vs. an 
improvement in revenue margins as trading revenue yields and investment banking fee rates are largely unchanged 
over the past decade (Exhibit 7, Exhibit 11, Exhibit 14). Growth in fee pools has been structural across both trading and 
investment banking, with industry trading fees rising at a 13% 2019-26E CAGR, and we expect 4% per annum growth from 
2026E-28E (Exhibit 3). Investment banking industry fees have risen at a 6% 2019-26E CAGR, and we expect 4% per annum 
growth from 2026E-28E (Exhibit 4). Further detail on drivers underpinning structural growth in the fee pools: 
Growth in float of tradable assets and equity market cap: Large bank trading assets have grown ~110% from 
n
2019-2Q26, and capital markets’ lending has grown by ~90% from 1Q19-2Q26 (Exhibit 16, Exhibit 17). We believe that 
much of the growth in trading balance sheets has been driven by the increase in size of debt and equity market 
capitalization, which has increased the pool of tradable assets, and hence intermediation fees. Further, trading 
businesses have benefited from significant growth in non bank financing activity which has supported growth in NII in 
markets businesses over the last 5 years. The increase in large bank trading assets has been driven by Other trading 
assets (~+175%), and MBS (~+145% - Exhibit 15). Global equity market cap has risen by ~225% from 2010-2026 (Exhibit 
10). Market cap drives investment banking revenue directly in the case of M&A (fees are a % of market cap), and 
indirectly in the case of underwriting, given that larger markets can absorb larger issuance. 
Revenue yields / fee rates largely flat: Despite the significant growth in the float of tradable assets and trading revenue, 
n
large cap banks’ trading revenue yields have remained largely flat at 550bps,  or ~25bps above the long-term average, 
and investment banking fee rates have remained at +/-60bps - across both trading products, and all three trading 
businesses, fee pressure has only been evident in DCM, a small portion of the fee pool (Exhibit 7, Exhibit 8, Exhibit 9, 
Exhibit 11, Exhibit 12, Exhibit 13). This implies that the growth in capital markets revenue is more a function of the larger

Margin expansion: Large banks have delivered notable operating leverage, with efficiency ratios falling from ~60% in 
n
2019 to ~53% in 1H26 (Exhibit 14) as the largest firms have benefited from economies of scale. We note that the top 3 
firms have seen ~900bps of efficiency ratio improvements, which are ~350bps more than the industry average from 
2022-1H26 (Exhibit 14). 
Leverage has risen: The growth in balance sheets across the large banks’ capital markets businesses has corresponded to 
n
increased leverage across the group, with leverage (defined here as average segment assets / average segment equity) 
increasing from ~15.0x in 2019 to ~20.0x in 1H26 (Exhibit 19). We believe part of this increased leverage relates to SLR 
reform, which was finalized in November, 2025. This rulemaking allows banks to operate with a degree more balance 
sheet leverage. It would disproportionately impact certain trading businesses, which are low risk, but low-ROA, i.e., those 
that consume notable leverage capital, but limited risk-based capital. An example is the repo business, which has grown 
at a 7% CAGR on average across the group since 2019, but more recently, has grown 19% p.a. from 2024-2Q26 (Exhibit 
20).

AI a key driver of both Equities and investment banking in 2026 
 
 
We believe that the AI infrastructure theme has driven a meaningful part of the capital markets revenue growth in 
2026, specifically in Equities trading and the investment banking businesses. Our analysis suggests that AI-related 
equities trading and financing has driven ~$4.5bn of the $34bn of 1H26 equities trading, or ~40% of the $10.8bn YoY 
growth in 1H26 Equities trading (Exhibit 21). Furthermore, we estimate that AI-related investment banking activity 
has driven ~$6.5bn of the ~$80bn of industry YTD investment banking fees, or ~50% of the ~$10bn YoY (14% YoY) 
growth (Exhibit 23, Exhibit 24). Lower ex-AI-related growth in both Equities and investment banking suggest that there is 
further room for investment banking activity to pick up outside of the tech sector. 
AI-related impact on Equities: The Equities business has benefited from AI-related activity across both Equities 
n
intermediation and financing. Our estimates suggest that 42% of the intermediation revenue growth, and 38% of the 
financing revenue growth can be attributed to AI, after accounting for AI activity in the US and normalizing for the US 
portion of the equities wallet (Exhibit 21). Given that financing comprises an estimated 33-48% of total Equities revenue 
across the group, we estimate that ~40% of YoY Equities growth is AI-related (Exhibit 21). 
In the Equities intermediation business, AI infrastructure stocks have driven a meaningful percentage of the 
o
increase in US equity market volume in 2026 which in turn has supported growth in intermediation fees. AI 
infrastructure (based on GS Portfolio Strategy’s AI disruption categorization) have comprised 40% of 2026 YTD 
total US equity market turnover, up from 29% in 2025 (Exhibit 22). This implies that 72% of the total increase in US 
equity turnover YoY has been driven by AI infra stocks. We normalize this % by the fact that the US represents 58% 
of the Equities wallet, implying that AI infra has driven 42% of the increase in equity intermediation YoY. Stripping 
out the impact of higher volumes in AI stocks would imply that equity intermediation revenues would only be up 
~20% YoY vs the reported ~35%. 
In the Equities financing business, we look at the contribution of US equity market cap of AI infrastructure stocks. 
o
AI infrastructure stocks comprise 35% of 2026 YTD US equity market cap, up from 30% in 2025; this results in 77% 
of the US equity market cap increase YoY being driven by AI infra stocks only. Upon normalizing for the fact that the 
US represents ~50% of the global market cap, this implies that 38% of YoY Equities financing growth in the US has 
been driven by AI infrastructure stocks (Exhibit 21). 
AI-related impact on investment banking: Industry announced investment banking fees have risen by ~$10bn (or 14%) 
n
YoY to ~$80bn - this is comprised of ~$1.7bn of YoY M&A fees growth (6% YoY), $6.2bn of ECM fees growth (68% YoY), 
and $2.2bn of DCM fees growth (7% YoY) (Exhibit 23). However, growth ex-AI infrastructure activity is much lower. We 
estimate that AI-related investment banking fees have grown ~310% YoY, vs. non-AI-related investment banking fees 
+7% YoY (Exhibit 24). This suggests that ~50% of the YTD YoY investment banking fees growth is AI-related, or

Equities continues to grow on expansion of financing business, IPOs, and market vol 
 
 
The Equities fee pool has grown at a 13% CAGR from 2019-26E, after being range bound in the previous decade 
(Exhibit 28). We assume fee pool growth slows, but remains reasonably healthy at a 5-7% CAGR across large banks 
from 2026E-28E. While we see long-term Equities fee pool growth driven by the structural tailwinds of growth in 
tradable assets and thus trading volumes, as well as the expansion of the prime brokerage that finances the increased 
pool of tradable assets, we believe that the Equities fee pool growth from 2024-26E (27% p.a.) has been heavily 
impacted by positive trends across four cyclical factors, the durability of which will be driven by market volumes, levels 
and volatility. We view AI infrastructure stock-related activity as the most important of the four cyclical factors, and 
we estimate that this has driven ~40% of the 2026 YoY step up in Equities trading (further detail in previous section). In 
addition, we see positive cyclical effects on the business from: 1) Widening equity financing spreads as demand for 
leverage to equities has increased; 2) an improving equity capital markets backdrop; and 3) higher equity market volatility, 
and recently, single stock vol has risen more than equity index vol. 
Growth of tradable float has led to continued growth in trading volumes...: We have seen persistent growth in global 
n
single stock and ETF trading volumes, which have driven greater Equities intermediation revenue. Global single stock/ETF 
volumes have risen at 20%/22% 2019-26 annualized CAGRs (Exhibit 30). Of the single stock volumes, for which we have 
regional breakdowns, we have seen 19%/20% US/non-US 2019-26 annualized CAGRs (Exhibit 31). 
...and structural growth in prime brokerage to finance tradable float growth: As tradable float across asset classes 
n
increases, clients need a greater quantum of leverage to finance this asset growth, which is typically done through the 
prime brokerage business. We see this driving the >4x estimated growth of large bank Equity financing revenue from 
1Q19-2Q26, vs. only 3.3x growth of Equities intermediation (Exhibit 29). 
Aside from AI-related impacts, we see three other cyclical trends that have accelerated Equities growth over the 
n
past two years: 
Wider equity funding spreads: Over the past two years, equity funding spreads, which is the cost of financing to 
a.
acquire leveraged exposure to equities, have widened out materially, at ~3x the 3Q20 levels, as clients have been 
willing to pay more for leverage (Exhibit 32). We believe this is in part due to cyclical demand for equities exposure, 
with prime gross/net leverage up from 260%/67% at the end of 2023 to the 300%/78% levels now (Exhibit 33). 
Recovery in the IPO market drives secondary trading: Growth in primary IPO markets drives associated 
b.
secondary trading of both the IPO shares, as well as subsequent trading of those shares and secondary share sales. 
We see a 69% R-squared of IPO volumes and Equities revenue (Exhibit 34). GS Portfolio Strategy forecasts ~440% 
growth of IPO volumes in 2026E, after ~50% YoY growth in 2025 (Exhibit 63).

FICC fee pool appears likely to grow at roughly GDP growth levels 
 
 
We expect the growth rate in the FICC fee pool from 2019-26E of 7% to drop going forward to closer to a 2-4% CAGR 
(Exhibit 38). In our view, the continued, slow growth in the FICC fee pool is largely the result of a mix shift in FICC 
business from intermediation to financing. Specifically, we see the ongoing growth in FICC financing, vs. a persistently 
subdued vol backdrop in many FICC products, limiting intermediation revenue growth. Historically, FICC revenue has 
correlated with volatility across FICC products - like Equities, a volatile market backdrop prompts client activity (Exhibit 39). 
However, the correlation has broken down more recently, with FICC revenue notably outperforming normalizing fixed income 
product volatility metrics. We believe this is driven by the growth of FICC financing - alternative asset management AUM has 
grown at a 13% CAGR from 2014-2025 (Exhibit 40). Much of this AUM needs to be financed, and has led to growth in FICC 
financing businesses. We see this evidenced by large banks’ Markets businesses, which have seen their NII grow ~105% from 
1Q21-2Q26 (Exhibit 41).

a 4% 2026E-28E CAGR, as we see growth in each of the businesses, across M&A, ECM, and DCM, driven by broadening

potential improvement in sponsor activity. Supporting a constructive ECM outlook, in the near-term, GS Portfolio Strategy

hyperscaler CapEx is likely to drive activity - GS Credit Strategy forecasts ~$1.3tn of debt financing needs from the top 5

n

n

Market share accrues to US firms, although to different types of US player, depending on the product 
 
 
US firms, across bulge bracket banks, independent investment banks, and market makers, have continued to increase 
market share, with revenue market share increasing by 23pp to 72% from 2010-26E (Exhibit 72). However, across 
different capital markets products, share gains have accrued to different types of US firm. In trading, share has accrued 
to both US market makers and US bulge bracket banks, with Equities in particular consolidating among the top 3 players. 
Conversely, in investment banking, share has accrued to independent IBanks although recent market share gains have abated. 
Further detail on share trends across different capital markets products, and further information on trends in each product in 
subsequent sections: 
Within total trading, US market makers have gone from de minimis share in 2010 to 21% as of 2026E, and US bulge 
n
brackets have taken 2pp of share, with their share now at 50% of total (Exhibit 73). 
In Equities, we have seen US bulge bracket firms take 23pp of share from 2010-26E and now stands at 69%, with 
o
the top 3 players benefiting disproportionaly (adding 18pp of share gains to reach 52% - Exhibit 74, Exhibit 75). We 
attribute these structural share gains to the significant migration of ECM activity to the US, the retrenchment of a 
number of European and Asian capital markets firms, as well as the significant investments US firms have made in 
both prime and execution capabilities. Note that Equities trends exclude non bank market makers, given their 
disclosures lack granularity on different trading businesses. 
In FICC, the US bulge brackets have taken a more modest 14pp of share from 2010-26E, although the top 3 have 
o
seen 9pp of share gains (Exhibit 76, Exhibit 77). Note that FICC trends exclude market makers, as well as certain 
banks, given their disclosures lack granularity on different trading businesses. 
Finally, in investment banking, independent IBanks have been notable share gainers, adding 17pp of share from 
n
2010-26E, with their share rising to 25%. Unlike in trading, there has been little share gain by the top 3 players, who have 
added only ~15bps of share to 29% (Exhibit 78, Exhibit 79). The US IBanks’ share gains has been driven by significant 
investment in talent, and the ability to pay more for senior bankers, as IBanks have significantly higher relative 
compensation to revenue ratios (vs. those of bulge brackets). As we discuss in the subsequent section, we believe share 
gains may have slowed for IBanks, given an increased financing component and more and more expensive competition 
for talent.

Independent investment bank share gains could slow going forward 
 
 
From 2010-26E, independent investment banks have taken a meaningful 17pp of investment banking revenue market 
share, bringing their share to 25%, with share gains across Advisory and ECM, as well as modest gains in DCM, as the 
group has grown their MD bases at an 11% CAGR over the past decade (Exhibit 78, Exhibit 84). We expect only 3pp of 
IBank share gains from 2026E-28E, as we believe share gains from here by IBanks could be more challenging to come 
by, especially in the M&A business, driven by a combination of: 1) a more competitive backdrop for talent, as more 
independent and bulge bracket banks compete for talent, driving higher compensation costs for bankers; and 2) 
IBanks’ businesses are more fully built out, which reduces the attractiveness of leaving a bulge bracket platform and 
going to an independent firm, given fewer entrepreneurial opportunities to build new sectors out. Nearer-term, we 
have seen US bulge bracket share gains, as more M&A transactions across AI-related and adjacent businesses that are 
driving activity currently involve a financing component, which favors US bulge brackets, and given the fact that 
financial sponsor M&A, to which IBanks are more exposed, has seen less growth YoY, vs. strategic M&A. Indeed, in 
advisory, IBank market share has fallen 3pp YoY in 2026E, which we see related to the financing-related M&A 
outperformance. In ECM, we see share as somewhat cyclical, with IBanks’ businesses narrowly exposed to healthcare, 
which have improved in 2026 so far. We do not expect material DCM market share gains going forward, given IBanks’ 
smaller balance sheets limit their financing offerings. From 2010-26E, IBanks have taken: 21pp of M&A share, bringing 
share to 47%, 14pp of ECM share, bringing share to 21%, and 2pp of DCM share, bringing share to 5% (Exhibit 81, Exhibit 82, 
Exhibit 83). 
Advisory share trends: The competitive bid for investment banking talent has structurally mounted in recent years, 
n
driven both by more and larger IBanks competing for more talent to sustain growth, as well as the enhanced investment 
banking aspirations among bulge bracket banks (e.g., C and WFC). This has made hiring more competitive in absolute 
terms, which weighs on hiring, but also increased the cost of hiring. As a result, comp / MD in total across IBanks has risen 
by 22% from 2019-26E to $6.5mn, as we note in Exhibit 85, and IBanks’ deferred comp / MD has increased by ~15% from 
2020-2Q26 to $1.3mn (Exhibit 86). Separately, in our view, IBanks’ value proposition when hiring talent has become 
somewhat less compelling, given that they have broadened out their sector footprints, leaving less white space in which 
to expand. Specifically, there are fewer places to offer senior potential hires the opportunity to build out new sectors 
from scratch. We believe this entrepreneurial opportunity could be an important hiring perk, in certain cases. The top 3 
sector contributions by deal volume for each IBank has fallen from 53% of total in 2019 to 46% in 2026 TD, 
demonstrating less pronounced sectoral skew (Exhibit 88). More recently, cyclical considerations have impacted M&A 
market share across US IBanks and bulge brackets. Specifically, the % of M&A transactions involving an equity or debt 
financing component has increased each of the past 5 years, reaching 27% of M&A transactions in 2026 TD (Exhibit 89). 
This compares to a 25% average from 2017-26 TD, and the % has risen each year since 2022, up from only 17% in 2022.

US bulge brackets, which offer broader financing capabilities than IBanks’, given their business mix and balance sheets, 
have historically taken share when the % of M&A deals with a financing component increase. In 2021, 31% of M&A deals 
involved financing, up 3pp YoY, and US bulge brackets added 2pp of IB share - we believe the same trend is occurring in

banks, with the % of their M&A over the past three years from sponsors at 47%, vs. 45% for US bulge brackets on average 
(Exhibit 90). With sponsor M&A growth notably underperforming strategic M&A growth, this has more recently favored 
bulge bracket banks (sponsor M&A has comprised 27% of YTD announced volumes, down 9pp vs. 2025 - Exhibit 57). 
ECM share trends: IBanks are heavily exposed to healthcare ECM, as this is one of the sectors that involves the least debt 
n
financing intensity. With their smaller balance sheets and more limited financing capabilities, this has allowed IBanks to 
compete well and take share in this sector more than others. IBanks’ healthcare ECM has comprised ~45% of their volume 
over the past 3 years (Exhibit 91). As such, the cyclicality of healthcare ECM will drive IBanks’ ECM market share, in our 
view. 
DCM share trends: Only two covered IBanks (JEF and PIPR) offer DCM. The lack of DCM footprint across the group 
n
reflects the fact that the IBank model is typically a capital-light model. With many acquisition-related debt financings 
involving both a balance sheet and market-based component, IBanks’ small balance sheet capacity to support financing 
activity limits their ability to participate in DCM.

Glossary 
 
 
Capital Markets Businesses 
Capital Markets: The combined revenue pools from trading (FICC + Equities) and investment banking (M&A, ECM, DCM) 
n
through which companies, governments and investors raise capital and manage risk. 
Trading: The combined intermediation and financing revenue from FICC (Shorthand for Fixed Income, Currencies and 
n
Commodities - the trading business covering rates, credit, FX, and commodities products) and Equities businesses (The 
trading business covering equity intermediation (cash and derivatives trading) and equity financing (prime brokerage, 
securities lending). 
Intermediation: Revenue earned from facilitating client purchases and sales of securities (i.e., market-making). 
o
Financing: Revenue earned from lending balance sheet and providing leverage to clients to hold positions (e.g., 
o
prime brokerage, repo, NDFI lending). 
IBanking (Investment Banking): The advisory and underwriting business comprising three sub-products: M&A, ECM, 
n
and DCM. 
M&A (Advisory): Fees earned advising companies on mergers, acquisitions, and divestitures. 
o
ECM (Equity Capital Markets): Fees earned helping companies raise equity capital, including IPOs, follow-on 
o
offerings, and convertible issuances. 
DCM (Debt Capital Markets): Fees earned helping companies and governments raise debt capital through bond 
o
issuance and loan syndication. 
Market Participants 
Bulge Bracket Banks: The largest, full-service global investment banks offering the broadest range of capital markets 
n
and financing services. 
Independent IBanks: Shorthand for advisory-focused investment banks without large trading balance sheets, 
n
specializing in M&A and, in some cases, ECM advisory. 
Market Makers (Non-Bank): Trading firms that provide liquidity and execute trades without a traditional banking 
n
charter or balance sheet financing model.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted 
are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized 
ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may vary 
depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. 
Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based 
on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile indicating a 
stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future 
compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary across sectors and regions) to incorporate the 
potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of 
the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not factor into our price target, and may or may not be 
discussed in research.
