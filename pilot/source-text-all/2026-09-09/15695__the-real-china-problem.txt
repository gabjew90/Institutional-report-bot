◼ Local government is the big drag on activity 
◼ A quick fix to boost investment is already in place 
◼ Activity improvement coming in H2/26 
Activity in China weakened sharply in Q2/26, reigniting debate over the structural drivers of slower 
growth. Consumption and property are frequently cited as the primary causes of macroeconomic 
deceleration. But we think the importance of local governments has been overlooked. Their share 
of GDP has slumped from near 40% in the 2010s to just 30% last year. A dramatic decline in 
infrastructure investment is one of the main cause of cyclical weakness. This is a fixable problem 
– at least in the short term – as boosting investment is not difficult for China. We expect an 
infrastructure-led improvement in activity over H2/26.  
The summer has seen a rare outpouring of forthright economic criticism in China. Economists 
have even resorted to footballing analogies to get policymakers’ attention (Xi is known to be a 
football fan). A speech by Li Daokui, dean of the Academic Centre for Chinese Economic Practice 
and Thinking at Tsinghua University, grabbed our and local markets’ attention. While Professor 
Li’s focus on the lack of aggregate demand received the most commentary, the second part of 
his remarks, which described the weakness of sub-national governments, was more interesting. 
In 2018 local government spending accounted for 37% of GDP. Last year that number had fallen 
to 31%. This 6 ppt decline is double that of real estate (both property and government spending 
have large spillover effects not fully reflected in sectoral numbers), while the consumption share 
of GDP has remained solid. Although household spending has slowed, it has held up remarkably

Local government spending used to have exceptionally high multipliers. Alongside property 
development, the transmission from infrastructure construction to spending on building 
materials, migrant labour and land improvement (which, combined, transferred a big chunk of 
income to households and private business) acted as the primary Chinese growth locomotive for 
more than a decade. That mechanism is now broken. 
Evidently, property is linked to the slowdown in local government spending. Land sales revenue 
has collapsed, depriving development of a major source of funds. Overall revenue, however, has 
continued to grow, albeit more slowly, with increased bond issuance and central government 
transfers offsetting the shortfall from land financing.  
The problem is what officials are doing with that funding. Fully half of all local government bond 
issuance goes to refinancing debt. Even special purpose bonds, which typically financed specific 
projects, are now used for debt repayment, with just 60% of issuance targeting investment, down 
from 90% in 2020. Infrastructure capex is the most striking casualty. On TSL’s calculation, 
spending on infrastructure has dropped by 1.8% of nominal GDP in the past 12 months.  
Part of the slowdown is policy-driven. In the past three years, Beijing has put a real focus on 
cleaning up local government balance sheets, recognizing hidden debts and swapping high 
interest-rate funds for cheaper sources of financing. While positive in the long term, this has had 
little impact on the real economy. More recently, the emphasis on trimming excess capacity in 
legacy industry, the forthcoming round of political reshuffling in 2027 (these occur every five 
years) and uncertainty around the Iran conflict have all made local governments more cautious 
about investing.  
Short term, this is an easy fix. In contrast with consumption, it has never been a problem for 
Beijing to boost capex. In July, we forecast that officials would accelerate fiscal spending and 
target infrastructure spending to boost growth. That is now coming into play. Since the July and 
August Politburos and the NDRC meeting in early September, the political messaging to boost 
investment has sharply increased. Moreover, discussions by some prominent officials suggest 
local government hidden debt risks are almost “resolved”, potentially presaging a softening of 
leverage constraints. Financing is flowing and local governments are racing to utilize annual debt 
quotas. Special purpose bond issuance (for investment) jumped in August and early September.

Longer term, the problem is also solvable. Beijing could address financing issues by taking more 
debt on the central government balance sheet (currently just 30% of GDP). Those funds could go 
towards buying local government assets, such as land or property, and converting unsold housing 
units to subsidized rentals. Other possible measures include long overdue fiscal reform (which is 
coming, albeit slowly) or the use of central funds to capitalize local welfare funds, as a precursor 
to hukou reform. 
Beijing, however, is focused on “overtaking on the bend”. We have been saying for a while now 
that Xi Jinping is a tech bro: he views current technological developments as a significant 
opportunity for China to take a commanding geoeconomic position. The Middle Kingdom was left 
behind during the last industrial revolution, and Xi is determined not to miss the next one. Policy 
bandwidth to deal with thorny fiscal issues is limited; and although Beijing is clearly moving in the 
right direction, progress will be slow. 
Social discontent could catalyse both short- and long-term fiscal efforts. At present, pressure 
is building, but only enough to push forward stop-gap solutions. The accelerated bond issuance 
described above will be utilized and prove sufficient to stabilize and improve cyclical growth, 
keeping us positive equities and the RMB.
