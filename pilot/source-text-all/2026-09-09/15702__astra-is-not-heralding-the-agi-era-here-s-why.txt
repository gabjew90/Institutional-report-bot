People are people...and AI isn't. Amid the recent news humanizing AI and its capabilities, we 
are turning our focus to a vital perspective from Arizona State University’s (School of Computing 
and Augmented Intelligence) Professor Subbarao Kambhampati. In his compelling talk, 
"Anthropomorphization Sins in Modern AI," he does his best to dismantle what he sees as the 
myth of machine sentience. 
Because we think his points are super interesting, today's Picture Day note trades all imagery 
for pure text. 
Here is a brief summary of his discussion...and...we would like to thank AI for helping us 
summarize it. Lol. 
The discussion...  
When we use advanced AI (like ChatGPT or reasoning models), we love to use human words to 
describe what they are doing. We say they are "thinking," "planning," "reasoning," or "having 
aha moments." 
Prof. Subbarao Kambhampati argues that this is a trap. AI doesn't think like a human. When we 
pretend it does, we misunderstand how it actually works, and we end up trusting it way more 
than we should. 
AI Doesn't "Plan" - It Just Remembers. 
What people think: People think AI can look at a brand-new, complex problem and logically 
figure out step-by-step how to solve it from scratch. 
The reality: AI is basically an incredible memorizer. If you give it a problem that looks very 
similar to something it saw on the internet during training, it can "retell" you the answer. But if 
you change the names or disguise the puzzle just a little bit so it can't rely on memory, its ability 


[Page 3]
 
to solve it drops close to zero. It's great at spitting out actions it has seen before, but terrible at 
figuring out how those actions interact in a new situation. 
Giving AI Advice Doesn't Really Teach It 
If you give a human a general piece of advice ("Hey, whenever you run into this type of problem, 
use this strategy"), they can apply it to short problems, long problems, and everything in 
between. 
With AI, if a problem gets longer or slightly more complicated, it completely loses track of the 
advice you gave it. You can't truly teach an AI a concept the way you teach a person; you have 
to hold its hand through every single variation. 
AI is Terrible at Checking Its Own Work 
Humans are often good at spotting mistakes even if we aren't good at creating the original thing 
(like how anyone can critique a song even if they can't write a symphony). 
People assume AI can do this too. But research shows that AI is just as bad at checking 
answers as it is at making them. In fact, if you force an AI to "double-check" its own work, it will 
often overcorrect and turn a right answer into a wrong one. 
Those Long "Thinking" Notes Aren't Actually Thoughts 
Newer AI models sometimes show you a long, hidden "thought process" before giving you an 
answer—using phrases like "Let me rethink this" or "Wait, that's wrong, let me backtrack." 
Scientists ran tests to see if these notes are actually a faithful map of the AI's logic. They aren't. 
In fact, if you scramble or fake the AI's "thought notes" with completely wrong information, the AI 
can still arrive at the correct final answer. 
These notes aren't thoughts; they are just "mumbling"—random filler text the AI learned to 
generate because it helps grease the wheels to get to the right output. 
Why Does This Matter? 
False Trust: If an AI gives you a wrong answer, but attaches a long, professional-looking 
"thought process" before it, you are much more likely to believe it's right just because it looks 
like it did hard work. 


[Page 4]
 
The Takeaway: AI is an incredible tool, but it's essentially a giant library lookup system mixed 
with a powerful pattern-matcher. We need to stop treating it like a human mind, because 
pretending it thinks like us hides how it actually works. 
*  *  * 
So, with all that in mind, we want to be clear that none of this says Astra is useless. 
A pattern-matcher that can grind through a tax return, a PCB layout, and a court booking in one 
session is a product. It is not a person, and it is not proof that "general intelligence" arrived on a 
Thursday. 
Kambhampati's tests still pulled the curtain back at the launch party. 
 
Disguise the puzzle and the "planner" collapses. Hand it advice and it loses the thread when the 
problem gets longer. Force it to check its own work and it can walk a right answer into a wrong 
one. Scramble the hidden "thought notes" and the final answer can still appear - because those 
notes were never a map of a mind.  
False trust is the feature, not the bug.  
A wrong answer wrapped in a long, professional-looking trace is easier to believe than a wrong 
answer that admits it guessed. 
So: welcome to another quarter of verbosity sold as thought. Astra can look like AGI, but that is 
not the same thing as being it.
