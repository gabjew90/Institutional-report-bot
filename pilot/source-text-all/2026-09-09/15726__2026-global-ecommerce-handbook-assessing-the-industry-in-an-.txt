Handbook, where we analyze the key trends and debates that are impacting 
eCommerce around the world. We update our proprietary Goldman Sachs Global 
eCommerce industry model (available on request) and forecast that global 
eCommerce sales will grow at a +6% CAGR between 2026 and 2031. 
Key Takeaways 
1) $4.5tn Global eCommerce Opportunity: +6% CAGR, Modest Online 
n
Penetration Gains. We forecast +6% global eCommerce sales growth 
(2026-2031 CAGR), reflecting a world where online channels continue to 
outpace global GDP and total retail sales, but with a slower pace of migration 
than before, as eCommerce matures. We view less penetrated countries as 
having the biggest runway for growth, with India/LatAm growing above average 
at 18%/13% 2026-2031 CAGRs. 
2) Mature eCommerce players have evolved from a retail business into a 
n
consumer ecosystem business. We see the industry evolving from commerce 
1.0 to commerce 3.0 as it is increasingly driven by high-margin ancillary revenue 
streams including advertising, subscriptions, logistics, etc. As these businesses 
deepen engagement across the consumer and merchant journey, revenue mixes 
are becoming more diversified, margins more resilient, and growth less 
dependent on underlying merchandise volume. 
3) We remain in the early innings of agentic commerce. We see agentic 
n
commerce evolving from (1) traffic from LLMs and (2) on-platform agentic tools, 
and note we are in the early innings of both consumer adoption and trust. Over 
time, however, we believe improving model accuracy, greater personalization, 
stronger merchant integrations, and the growing convenience of delegated 
shopping tasks will accelerate adoption.

Outlining the $4.5tn+ Global eCommerce Opportunity  
 
 
 
We size global eCommerce sales at $4.5tn in 2025, which we expect to grow +8% 
YoY to reach $4.9tn in 2026. We model global eCommerce sales growing at a +6% 
CAGR between 2026 and 2031 towards $6.4tn in 2031, mostly driven by the growth in 
retail sales, and supported by a modest increase in Global eCommerce penetration rates 
as consumers continue to shift their spending online. We expect that Global 
eCommerce penetration will reach 27% of addressable retail sales (which exclude 
auto, gas and restaurant sales) in 2031, up from an estimated 24% in 2026. 
Our proprietary Goldman Sachs Global eCommerce industry model (available on request) aggregates 
our bottom-up estimates from 6 research teams across the globe and covers the following regions: 
the US, China, Korea, LatAm, Europe, India, Canada, Japan, and Australasia.

Evolution of the eCommerce Business Model 
 
 
eCommerce has evolved from a retail business into a consumer ecosystem business. 
The first generation of eCommerce was built around transaction volume and market 
share. As the industry progresses, it is increasingly driven by high-margin ancillary 
revenue streams including advertising, subscriptions, payments, logistics, financial 
services, and AI-driven commerce. As growth matures, investors view leading platforms 
less as retailers and more as consumer ecosystems that are increasingly disconnected 
from the underlying retail transaction. 
Commerce 1.0 was characterized by bringing 1P retail online. During this phase, 
companies focused on convincing consumers to shop online rather than in physical 
stores by offering a broader selection, lower prices, and greater convenience. Growth 
was driven primarily by online migration and increasing internet penetration, and 
success was largely determined by who could build the largest assortment and 
fulfillment network.  
Commerce 2.0 was characterized by bringing 3P inventory online and shifting 
towards marketplace models (improving economics) along with mobile web/app 
interface, which altered user interface designs for online commerce companies. This also 
impacted the consumer discovery process and ushered in the faster same day/next day 
delivery methods. Rather than owning inventory themselves, platforms began 
connecting buyers and sellers while providing payments, logistics, fulfillment, 
advertising, and customer acquisition services. Instead of owning merchandise, 
platforms could earn a fee on each transaction while outsourcing inventory risk to 
merchants. A marketplace could process more GMV without proportional increases in 
inventory, working capital, or fulfillment assets compared with a traditional 1P platform. 
Commerce 3.0 is characterized by ecosystem monetization and increasingly about 
the consumer relationship rather than the transaction itself. Revenue from this phase is 
from advertising, payments, subscriptions, logistics services, financial products, and 
increasingly AI-driven commerce experiences. 
 
1P online retail tends to give the company greater control over the consumer 
experience and product quality, but with business models that often increase the need 
for labor and infrastructure investments (e.g., logistics, warehouses). These additional 
services can allow managed marketplaces to set a higher take rate, as a reflection of the

Fulfillment Services - Platforms provide logistics capabilities to merchants (i.e., 
n
Amazon’s Supply Chain Services, Fulfillment by Amazon, Wayfair’s Castlegate, 
Mercado Envios, etc.) and charge fees for fulfillment, warehousing, shipping, freight, 
inventory management, and returns processing. Given the already established 
distribution networks of large eCommerce platforms, offering logistics services is 
often a way to improve network utilization, increase package density, and fill excess 
capacity (i.e., limiting existing empty hauls) rather than serving as a standalone 
growth engine. While logistics businesses typically carry lower margins than 
advertising or software offerings due to their asset-intensive nature, they strengthen 
merchant relationships, improve retention, and deepen platform ecosystems by 
making sellers increasingly reliant on the platform’s infrastructure. Over time, 
logistics services can become an important competitive moat by improving delivery 
speeds, lowering unit costs, and expanding addressable categories such as grocery, 
perishables, and everyday essentials. 
Retail Media & Advertising - As previously outlined, retail media networks serve as 
n
a high margin revenue stream that leverages retailers’ existing traffic and first-party 
data assets. This model is attractive to advertisers because it provides access to 
high-intent audiences and allows advertising dollars to be directly linked to purchase 
behavior and return on investment. Formats can take multiple forms including 
sponsored products, sponsored brands, display advertising, video advertising, 
off-site advertising, AI-generated recommendations, and in-store digital media. 
Importantly, retail media revenue is often highly incremental from a profit 
perspective, as it is layered on top of an already existing commerce transaction. 
While retail media typically represents only a small percentage of revenue for many 
large platforms, it frequently contributes a disproportionately large share of 
operating profit and remains one of the most important margin expansion 
opportunities across global eCommerce. 
Payments & Financial Services - Several established eCommerce platforms also 
n
offer payment processing services, built-in merchant disbursement solutions, digital 
wallets, buy-now-pay-later products, and merchant financing or cash advances 
designed to help sellers invest in inventory. Acting as a payment facilitator, the 
platform collects a percentage of each transaction that moves through its ecosystem 
while improving checkout conversion and reducing transaction friction. In emerging

Membership & Subscription Programs - Membership and subscription programs 
n

cashback rewards, exclusive discounts, streaming content, or other ecosystem perks 
in exchange for a recurring monthly or annual fee. Subscription revenue itself often

generally shop more frequently, spend more per year, and demonstrate significantly 
higher retention rates than non-members. As consumer spending becomes 
increasingly selective, loyalty programs are becoming a critical tool for locking in 
customer relationships and raising switching costs. 
Merchant Services - Beyond facilitating transactions, many eCommerce platforms 
n
now sell software, analytics, marketing, storefront management, and operational 
tools directly to merchants. These services can include website creation, advertising 
management, customer relationship management, inventory planning, fulfillment 
software, analytics dashboards, and merchant productivity tools. Shopify serves as a 
merchant-services-driven model, though most large marketplaces increasingly offer 
a similar suite of capabilities. Merchant services generate recurring revenue streams 
that are often less cyclical than retail transactions and typically carry attractive 
margin profiles and increase seller dependence on the platform, making merchant 
retention stronger over time. 
Agentic Services - Agentic services represent the newest layer of eCommerce 
n
monetization and are still in the early stages of development. These services 
leverage AI-powered shopping assistants to help consumers discover products, 
compare options, receive recommendations, automate routine purchases, and 
ultimately execute transactions (i.e., Amazon’s Alexa for Shopping). Revenue 
opportunities could include sponsored recommendations, conversational 
advertising, merchant participation fees, AI-enhanced product placement, and 
higher transaction conversion rates. While revenue contribution today remains 
relatively immaterial, agentic services could evolve into an important monetization 
layer built on top of existing commerce ecosystems.

Agentic Shopping: Opportunity for future growth 
 
 
We frame agentic commerce around a few key themes: (1) we’re still in the early 
innings, with potential for major disruption as agentic commerce is positioned to usher 
in the next wave of e-commerce, (2) consumer adoption remains in the research 
phase (with growing but slow automation adoption), and (3) traffic from LLMs remains 
low (albeit fast growing and high intent) as consumer behavior shifts are multi-year 
cycles. We believe the application of agentic AI within retail commerce has the potential 
to provide incremental pathways for consumers to interact with brands, which will drive 
incremental conversion while adding little incremental marketing dollars. However, we 
emphasize this dynamic is in the early phases and will, in our view, take years to 
materialize into real earnings growth.  
What is Agentic Commerce? 
Agentic Shopping is the use of AI-powered autonomous agents to act on behalf of a 
customer to compare, negotiate, research and ultimately purchase with minimal human 
or manual intervention. Agentic commerce represents the next evolution of the 
consumer shopping journey, where AI-powered assistants increasingly help consumers 
discover products, compare options, receive recommendations, manage recurring 
purchases, and ultimately execute transactions on their behalf. While adoption remains 
early, we believe agentic commerce has the potential to reshape how consumers interact 
with retailers, how merchants acquire customers, and how commerce platforms 
monetize traffic over the coming decade.

From Desktop to Mobile to Agents. Just as the mobile revolution forced a complete 
restructuring of digital design, search engine optimization, and consumer engagement, 
the agentic shift is similarly changing the structure of online retail. E-commerce moved 
from precise, multi-tab, mouse-click navigation on large screens to touch-based, 
simplified, single-column layouts on small screens. Commerce is moving from manual, 
screen-based scrolling and filtering to natural language, conversational, and background 
execution. Instead of navigating a visual interface, consumers provide high-level intent 
(e.g., “Find a carry-on bag under $200 that fits United Airlines’ size limits and arrives by 
Friday”). Mobile did not eliminate desktop shopping overnight, it was a multi-year trend 
that is still seen today; similarly, we expect agentic shopping to coexist with current 
commerce avenues and become a longer-term thematic shift in eCommerce (similar to 
the mobile revolution).  
Evolution of AI in commerce 
As we move through the AI automation curve, more responsibility and tasks will be 
pushed to AI. Over the past several years, we have seen Amazon’s subscribe & save and 
Chewy’s autoship become a more popular way to automate shopping. This doesn’t 
require an agent but is a programmed convenience targeted at repeat, every-day items 
that allows customers to delegate the shopping experience. The next phase (the 
research phase) is where the agent acts as an analyst and scans websites to create a list 
of recommendations with certain features, but a human still makes & executes the 
decision. The multi-product basket phase requires the agent to solve trade-offs and 
present a coherent solution for more than 1 product given the task. The execution phase 
is where you give the agent the authority to execute a purchase within certain 
parameters. As we look forward, AI agents could be given autonomy to handle difficult 
and complex tasks and treated to handle the solution and operational flow through,

Traffic from LLMs  
While traffic referred from AI platforms remains small relative to traditional channels 
such as search, direct traffic, and email, it is growing rapidly and appears to exhibit 
significantly higher purchase intent than traditional web traffic. Unlike conventional 
search, where consumers begin with broad informational queries, AI interfaces are 
increasingly used deeper in the purchase journey for product research, comparison 
shopping, review summarization, and purchase recommendations. As a result, users 
arriving from LLM interfaces tend to have already narrowed their purchase consideration 
and may be closer to conversion. Over time, we believe AI-sourced traffic could become 
increasingly valuable despite representing a relatively small share of total visits.  
We leverage Similarweb data to track web traffic and engagement across global 
eCommerce leaders and note that GenAI has accelerated meaningfully across all 
platforms as a traffic source. Although still a small percentage of total (with BABA 
representing the biggest share at ~1.3%), we note recent acceleration (particularly in the 
last 3 months).

User Adoption: Consumers Are Embracing AI Assistance But Not Yet AI Autonomy 
Consumer adoption of AI-powered shopping tools continues to accelerate as LLMs 
become increasingly integrated into everyday online behavior. However, data suggests 
consumers are far more comfortable using AI as a research and recommendation tool 
than as an autonomous purchasing agent. Recent survey data from Visa stated that 
~40% of Americans have made a purchase they otherwise would not have made due to 
recommendations from an AI tool or assistant, suggesting that AI is already influencing 
purchasing behavior. The survey also stated that 58% of Americans are comfortable with 
AI comparing prices but only 27% are comfortable allowing AI to execute autonomously 
on their behalf (this varies by survey and generation). Consumers are increasingly using 
AI because they believe it improves shopping outcomes and reduces the risk of making a 
bad purchase (this is even more pronounced in the Millennial and Gen Z cohorts). As 
product selection expands and online catalogs become larger, AI’s ability to summarize 
reviews, compare alternatives, surface relevant products, and personalize 
recommendations becomes increasingly valuable. 
Trust will continue to be a big factor to unlock the full potential of Agentic 
Commerce. As a result, we expect adoption to progress gradually from discovery and 
research toward low-risk automated purchases (i.e., recurring purchases and everyday 
essentials) before eventually expanding into broader transaction execution.

As eCommerce platforms have matured, revenue growth has increasingly shifted away 
from traditional merchandise sales toward higher-margin businesses. Among these

leverages their already existing traffic and 1P data assets. Formats can take a number of 
different shapes, including display ads (majority of market share), sponsored products, 
video ads, off-site advertising, and in-store digital.  
As previously noted (Link | Link | Link), this revenue stream has grown rapidly, faster than 
social and search, as advertisers look toward both onsite and offsite options, blurring 
the lines between eCommerce and advertising. It is often looked at as the third wave of 
digital advertising (followed by search and social) as it combines purchase intent and 
consumer data with advertising. Given the decline in 3P cookies and an increase in 
privacy regulation, 1P retailer data has become more valuable and retail media has 
become a structural beneficiary of the privacy-driven shift.  
Sizing Retail Media  
Historically, consumers discovered products through search engines, social media 
platforms, television advertising, and other media channels before ultimately completing 
transactions elsewhere. With retail media, consumers are able to discover products, 
evaluate alternatives, and complete purchases within the same ecosystem, allowing 
retailers to monetize traffic at multiple points throughout the shopping journey. This 
represents a large and growing opportunity, with Statistica estimating the size to be 
~$170b globally in 2025, up from $77b in 2020, representing a 17% 5-year CAGR.

Tech infrastructure & AI Disruption 
Retail media networks are built on an increasingly sophisticated advertising technology 
stack that allows retailers to monetize first-party consumer data and deliver highly 
targeted advertising experiences. At the foundation are supply-side platforms (SSPs), 
which allow retailers to manage and monetize advertising inventory across owned and 
operated properties, and demand-side platforms (DSPs), which enable advertisers to 
purchase inventory programmatically based on audience characteristics and campaign 
objectives. Meanwhile, data management platforms (DMPs) and customer data 
platforms (CDPs) collect and analyze consumer behavior, giving retailers deeper insight 
into customer behavior.  
While these technologies serve as the backbone of modern retail media, we believe 
artificial intelligence represents the next major evolution of the ecosystem. Similar to 
broader digital advertising, AI is increasingly being deployed across campaign 
management, audience targeting, attribution, creative generation, and product 
discovery. Over time, we expect AI to become deeply embedded throughout the retail 
media value chain and drive increased advertising efficiency, advertiser ROI, and 
monetization opportunities for retailers.

Regional Breakdown: US and China are the Dominant Players 
The retail media landscape remains highly concentrated, with the United States and 
China representing the largest markets globally. According to Statistica, the US and 
China accounted for ~35% and ~47% of global market share as of 2025, supporting 
their scale in eCommerce, concentration of digital advertising budgets, and advanced 
monetization capabilities. China remains the largest retail media market globally, 
benefiting from deeply integrated commerce ecosystems where advertising, payments, 
content, and transactions occur within a single platform. The United States follows 
closely behind, led by Amazon, Walmart Connect, and Target’s Roundel, with retail 
media accounting for an increasingly meaningful share of total digital advertising 
budgets. 
While the U.S. and China dominate today, we believe some of the most attractive 
growth opportunities exist outside these markets. Retail media remains significantly 
smaller across regions such as Latin America, Europe, and India, creating a substantial 
runway for future monetization. According to Statistica, these markets are expected to 
see double digit YoY growth in 2026 and 2027 (vs. the US at ~9% and China at ~6%), 
suggesting that these international markets remain in the earlier stages of development. 
As leading regional eCommerce platforms continue to scale traffic, improve first-party 
data capabilities, and build advertising infrastructure, we expect retail media 
monetization in these regions to scale over time.

dominate, and in LatAm MercadoLibre is the biggest player. This highlights that

What Is Implied by Our Forecast?   
Pace of online migration: We model US eCommerce sales growing by an average of 
n
~$80bn/yr through 2031. This is higher than ~$60bn/yr pre-COVID (2019 and prior) 
and much lower than ~$240bn/~$140bn in 2020/2021. As a way to frame a 
downside case for eCommerce growth, a return to the pre-COVID cadence of 
$60bn/yr would result in eCommerce growing at a +4% 2026-2031 CAGR, roughly 
-1pp slower than our base case of +5%. 
Incremental online penetration rate: Assuming that addressable US retail sales 
n
grow at a +4% 2026-2031 CAGR, our eCommerce forecasts imply a ~40bps/yr 
average increase in online penetration through 2031. This is below the nearly 90bps 
increase seen in 2024, significantly below prior historical levels of (~125bps/yr 
pre-COVID and ~500bps in 2020), and slightly ahead of the 30bps seen in 2025, as 
we expect that higher penetration levels will make gains more challenging.

Looking Ahead: Key Trends Shaping US eCommerce 
1. Resilient but Choiceful & Value Oriented US Consumer in 2026  
The US consumer remains resilient, and consumer spending has held up better than 
expected despite low sentiment. Data compiled by our US Economics team in Exhibit 
52 points to the US consumer being on solid footing, with real PCE growing at 2.1% YoY 
through May and robust household balance sheets despite weak income growth and 
confidence. Aggregate spending remains positive, but consumers are increasingly 
reallocating wallet share toward value, essentials, and high-utility purchases, while 
becoming more selective across discretionary categories. We have even seen Walmart 
and Kroger (link) recently announce price investments, which our consumer team sees as 
another value oriented indication.   
Demand remains strong to date - US eCommerce sales grew ~10% YoY in Q1’26 and 
most large eCommerce companies spoke to consistent demand. Most companies 
exceeded Street expectations in Q2 and spoke to a solid demand outlook in 2026 
despite macro uncertainty and soft data (e.g., relatively low consumer confidence and 
softer outlooks for cash flow).

3. Services > Goods 
US consumers continue to shift more of their spending toward services, extending a 
decades-long trend in the economy. Expenditures on services are growing faster than 
goods purchases (especially post pandemic), with services now back to their pre-COVID 
wallet share of ~70% (Exhibit 64) and spending on services continuing to outpace overall 
consumer expenditures in recent months (Exhibit 65) as consumers prioritize 
experiences. This trend is further amplified by high-income households prioritizing 
experiential spending—such as travel and live entertainment—over physical 
merchandise, even as lower-income cohorts face budget constraints from cumulative 
inflation. 
This is also playing out online - Internet companies exposed to service-oriented 
verticals (incl. media streaming, ridesharing, delivery, travel) are growing faster 
than eCommerce companies (Exhibit 66). We expect that services will continue to gain 
share in consumer wallets and this shift could be accelerated in the short-term by the 
rise in inflation expectations.

4. Large players continue win; seeing a shift towards loyalty/recurring purchases  
As companies compete for a larger share of consumer spending in an increasingly 
selective and value-oriented environment, many have shifted their focus away from pure 
customer acquisition and toward increasing customer lifetime value through loyalty 
programs, subscriptions, recurring purchases, and enhanced fulfillment capabilities. 
These initiatives aim to increase purchase frequency, improve retention, reduce 
customer acquisition costs, and strengthen customer relationships at a time when 
consumers are becoming more intentional about how and where they spend. 
 AMZN: Prime, Subscribe & Save, expanding selection, and Amazon’s increasing 
exposure to everyday essentials have made the company one of the largest beneficiaries 
of the consumer’s shift toward convenience and value. Everyday essentials now account 
for roughly one-third of units sold on the platform, creating a recurring purchase engine 
that drives higher shopping frequency and keeps consumers within the Amazon 
ecosystem. The combination of Prime benefits, recurring purchasing programs, broad 
selection, and faster delivery has strengthened customer loyalty and increased wallet 
share over time. 
eBay: Their focus on enthusiast buyers, recommerce, and trusted category-specific 
experiences has positioned the company as a beneficiary of consumers’ increasing 
preference for value, uniqueness, and secondhand goods. Focused Categories, including 
collectibles, motors, luxury fashion, and refurbished products, continue to attract highly 
engaged buyers. Although they do not have a distinct frequency program, eBay’s 
investments in trust features, authentication services, AI-powered discovery tools, and 
community-oriented experiences such as eBay Live, the company has strengthened 
buyer loyalty, increased engagement, and expanded wallet share within its core 
verticals. 
Chewy: Autoship has become the cornerstone of Chewy’s customer retention strategy, 
representing approximately 84% of total sales. The subscription-like nature of pet food, 
medication, and consumables creates highly recurring purchasing behavior, providing 
the company with predictable revenue and strong customer retention. More recently, 
Chewy introduced Chewy+, a paid membership program offering 5% rewards, free 
shipping, and exclusive benefits for an annual fee, further reinforcing customer loyalty 
and encouraging greater share of wallet among existing customers. 
Wayfair: Wayfair has increasingly focused on driving repeat engagement through its 
recently launched Wayfair Rewards program, which offers 5% rewards back, free 
shipping, and exclusive member benefits for an annual fee. Historically, home furnishings 
have been a low-frequency purchase category, making customer retention more 
challenging than in everyday essentials categories. Wayfair’s loyalty initiatives are 
designed to increase engagement across its family of brands, encourage repeat 
purchases, and improve customer LTV. 
Etsy: Etsy has similarly sought to deepen engagement through its Etsy Insider 
membership program, which provides members with benefits such as free shipping 
incentives, exclusive savings opportunities, and enhanced shopping experiences. While 
Etsy’s marketplace is more discovery-oriented and discretionary than Amazon or Chewy, 
loyalty programs can help improve purchase frequency and keep buyers engaged 
between major purchasing occasions.

Competition remains in the spotlight in Brazil. During the last year, leading 
ecommerce players in the region (particularly MELI, Amazon and Shopee) have 
continuously invested behind greater competitiveness in an effort to attract traffic and 
drive volumes on their platforms. While some of this has come in the form of incentives 
directed to consumers such as marketing spend, couponing and broadening free 
shipping policies, there has also been a notable step-up in seller support in the form of 
take-rate discounts. As a result, seller all-in cost has been brought down, driving 
additional selection online and enabling a continued online shift.  
Take-rate competition in focus, but stabilizing at the margin. MELI announced in 
mid-April 2026 (link) new incentives to ensure its Brazilian sellers maintain competitive 
pricing on the platform, including up to 5pp reduction to seller commission rates for 
products with competitive pricing and MELI-funded discounts for buyers using instant 
payment form PIX. We believe this was, at least in part, in response to Amazon having 
launched and consistently maintained promotional campaigns that waived FBA fees 
(link), as the company sought to attract more sellers into its fulfillment structure. We 
note, however, that recent data points have indicated a degree of stability in take-rate 
competition, with MELI raising shipping rates to pass through fuel price inflation (link) 
and Amazon letting part of its take-rate discounts expire as of July 31.  
MELI remains dominant, but Shopee and Amazon firmed as relevant forces in Brazil. 
We estimate that MELI will reach a market share of 47% in 2026E, up from 28% in 2020.

GS Factor Profile 
The Goldman Sachs Factor Profile provides investment context for a stock by comparing key attributes to the market (i.e. our universe of rated stocks) 
and its sector peers. The four key attributes depicted are: Growth, Financial Returns, Multiple (e.g. valuation) and Integrated (a composite of Growth, 
Financial Returns and Multiple). Growth, Financial Returns and Multiple are calculated by using normalized ranks for specific metrics for each stock. The 
normalized ranks for the metrics are then averaged and converted into percentiles for the relevant attribute. The precise calculation of each metric may 
vary depending on the fiscal year, industry and region, but the standard approach is as follows: 
Growth is based on a stock’s forward-looking sales growth, EBITDA growth and EPS growth (for financial stocks, only EPS and sales growth), with a 
higher percentile indicating a higher growth company. Financial Returns is based on a stock’s forward-looking ROE, ROCE and CROCI (for financial 
stocks, only ROE), with a higher percentile indicating a company with higher financial returns. Multiple is based on a stock’s forward-looking P/E, P/B, 
price/dividend (P/D), EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and EV/Debt Adjusted Cash Flow (DACF) (for financial stocks, only P/E, P/B and P/D), with a higher percentile 
indicating a stock trading at a higher multiple. The Integrated percentile is calculated as the average of the Growth percentile, Financial Returns 
percentile and (100% - Multiple percentile). 
Financial Returns and Multiple use the Goldman Sachs analyst forecasts at the fiscal year-end at least three quarters in the future. Growth uses inputs 
for the fiscal year at least seven quarters in the future compared with the year at least three quarters in the future (on a per-share basis for all metrics). 
For a more detailed description of how we calculate the GS Factor Profile, please contact your GS representative.  
M&A Rank 
Across our global coverage, we examine stocks using an M&A framework, considering both qualitative factors and quantitative factors (which may vary 
across sectors and regions) to incorporate the potential that certain companies could be acquired. We then assign a M&A rank as a means of scoring 
companies under our rated coverage from 1 to 3, with 1 representing high (30%-50%) probability of the company becoming an acquisition target, 2 
representing medium (15%-30%) probability and 3 representing low (0%-15%) probability. For companies ranked 1 or 2, in line with our standard 
departmental guidelines we incorporate an M&A component into our target price. M&A rank of 3 is considered immaterial and therefore does not 
factor into our price target, and may or may not be discussed in research.
