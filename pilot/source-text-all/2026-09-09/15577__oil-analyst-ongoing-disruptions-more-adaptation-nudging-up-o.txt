Ongoing Disruptions, More Adaptation: Nudging Up Our Price Forecast 
 
 
We raise our Brent/WTI price forecasts by $5 to $85/80 for Dec2026 and to $80/75 for 
2027 (Exhibit 1), now assuming that Mideast shipping disruptions continue into 2027.   
Markets are increasingly pricing a prolonged Mideast conflict. Brent spot futures have 
risen to $97, and the options-implied probability of Brent above $100 in March 2027 
has risen from around 6% a month ago to about 25% now (Exhibit 2, left). 2027Q2 
Persian Gulf-to-China crude tanker rates now price shipping disruptions lasting into then 
(Exhibit 2, right).

Modest Price Upgrade Despite Assumption Shipping Disruptions 
Continue  
The price upgrade is modest despite our new assumption that shipping disruptions 
continue because OECD commercial oil inventories have so far barely drawn since the 
war began, and because we assume Mideast supply adaptation continues.  
Reason 1: Resilient OECD Commercial Stocks 
OECD commercial land inventories—a key predictor of crude prices—have barely drawn 
so far since the war began (Exhibit 3), leaving our end-August OECD commercial oil 
stocks counter 119mb above our July balance estimate. The limited OECD commercial 
draw reflects a smaller-than-expected deficit amid market adaptation, and an unusually 
large concentration of global draws in OECD SPR, on water, and in China. 
 
Deficit narrows. We estimate that the global oil market deficit has narrowed from about 
7mb/d in March 2026 to 1mb/d in 2026Q3, as Gulf output partially recovered on higher 
pipeline use and dark flows, Asia demand remained weak, and ex Gulf output grew 
(Exhibit 4). Our 2026Q3 1mb/d deficit is 5.4mb/d smaller than the 6.4mb/d Q3 drop in 
Persian Gulf output vs. its 2025 average, reflecting a 1) 1.2mb/d surplus in 2025, 2) 
2.7mb/d weaker demand (vs. 2025), and 3) 1.4mb/d stronger ex Gulf production (vs. 
2025). The Americas drive the strength in ex Gulf liquids production with 2026Q3 
combined output from the US, Canada, Brazil, Venezuela, Argentina, and Guyana on 
track to be 2.3mb/d above its 2025 average with the largest contributions from the US 
(1mb/d) and Brazil (0.7mb/d).

Reason 2: We Assume Mideast Supply Recovery Continues  
We assume Mideast supply adaptation continues, with output recovering as dark flows 
rise, new pipeline capacity comes online in late 2027, and the UAE and Saudi Arabia 
eventually deploy some spare capacity.  
Gulf liquids output has already improved from 14.3mb/d below Feb26 levels in April to 
8.0mb/d below in July. We assume output gradually returns to pre-war levels by 2027H2, 
then overshoots as the UAE, now outside OPEC, and Saudi Arabia use some spare 
capacity to help rebuild inventories (Exhibit 6). 
 
We view gradual further increases in Gulf Exports as a reasonable base case for two 
reasons. 
First, exports have already trended higher, suggesting producers and specialized 
shippers are adapting to a risky shipping environment, including with rising dark flows 
(Exhibit 7), given strong economic and national incentives. 
Second, additional resources—especially pipelines, tanker fleet expansion, and 
potentially additional ship protection—could support further export gains. We estimate 
3.8mb/d of new effective pipeline capacity addition by end-2027, driven by the UAE’s 
West–East pipeline expansion (+1.8mb/d) and Saudi Arabia’s Yanbu port expansion 
(+2.0mb/d) (Exhibit 8).  
To be clear, the intensity and geographical breadth of tanker attacks—a highly uncertain 
variable—will remain a key driver of whether Gulf oil exports recover and how quickly.

Low Global and SPR Oil Stocks Don’t Imply Crude Prices Have to Jump 
Imminently 
Visible global oil stocks (Exhibit 10, left) and OECD SPR levels (Exhibit 9) are low by 
historical standards. Even so, they do not necessarily imply a crude price spike if Middle 
East supply continues to recover, for three reasons: history, our view that we aren’t 
running out of oil in the near-term, and China’s price-sensitive imports.  
 
Reason 1: History 
The historical relationship between prices and global visible oil stocks is weak. In fact, 
when visible global stocks hit their all time low in November 2024 (in a sample starting in 
February 2017), Brent was $76 (Exhibit 10, left).1 The latest OECD commercial stocks 
measured in days of demand—which predict oil prices better than global stocks—
remain 16% above their 2003 average all-time low. In 2003, Brent front-to-back 
1M/36M timespreads traded around a relatively high but not extreme 20% (and Brent 
spot prices around $30 (Exhibit 10, right)).2  
1
To be clear, late 2024 was a different environment from today with high OPEC spare capacity and limited 
supply disruptions. 
2
OECD commercial landed stocks were structurally lower before the US shale boom. The structural increase in 
OECD commercial stocks can be explained by a shift from storage on water (given long-haul voyages from the 
Middle East) to landed crude storage in the US (and short-haul voyages).

Reason 3: China Crude Imports Will Likely Remain Price-Sensitive 
Price-sensitive Chinese crude net imports, still down about 30% year-over-year, will 
likely moderate price upside in an escalation scenario and cushion price downside in a 
de-escalation scenario (Exhibit 12, left). We estimate Brent fair value would be $10-15 
higher if China had kept its imports stable in March-August 2026, triggering more 
significant OECD commercial draws (Exhibit 12, right).

We expect China’s crude imports to stay low if prices remain high because the two 
drivers—lower crude demand and crude draws—look largely sustainable in coming 
quarters. The 3.3mb/d drop in July 2026 net imports versus the 2025 average reflects a 
2mb/d decline in refinery runs and a 1.3mb/d swing from stockpiling in 2025 to draws 
since May (Exhibit 13, left).   
China crude demand should remain price-sensitive because investment in electric 
personal cars, electric taxis, electric trucks, public transportation, and coal-based 
petrochemical plants has increased flexibility to switch away from oil without much 
pressure on economic activity.  
China crude stock changes should also remain price-sensitive because China visible 
crude stocks remain high—above 1.1 billion barrels—and even higher when invisible 
stocks are included (Exhibit 13, right).

Net Upside Price Risks and Recommendations 
Risks to our price forecast remain significantly tilted to the upside on net, especially 
near-term. 
Key upside price risks include more intense and geographically broader shipping 
disruptions, and damage to crude production or export Infrastructure, which could 
weaken output in the Mideast or elsewhere for longer. We see the risk of larger or more 
persistent supply disruptions as especially high in and around Hormuz, the Red Sea and 
the Black Sea (Exhibit 14).  
The main downside price risks are a faster Mideast output recovery and stickier demand 
losses. Our base case assumes 0.7mb/d of demand scarring in 2027, or 12% of the 
6mb/d 2026Q2 loss versus a no war scenario.  
 
We illustrate the significant net upside price risks with two Gulf output and Brent 
scenarios (Exhibit 15):  
Price upside scenario: Brent might exceed $120/bbl if 2027 average Gulf output 
n
remains 4mb/d below pre-war levels, versus 0.5mb/d below in our base case. We 
view more intense shipping attacks in Hormuz and the Red Sea as the most likely 
driver of this lower-output, higher-price scenario.  
Price downside scenario: Brent might decline into the $60s in 2027 if 2027 average 
n
Gulf output rises 1mb/d above pre-war levels.  
We still recommend hedging geopolitical risk through deferred Mar27-Dec27 European 
diesel timespreads, which would rise over 100% if persistent Russia or Mideast refinery 
outages keep the nearby 9-month spread near current levels.
