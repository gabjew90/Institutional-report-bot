AI Funding Needs and Strong Demand Fuel Record Convertible Supply 
 
 
Convertible bonds have long been an important vehicle for funding early-stage, 
growth-oriented companies, giving investors exposure to equity returns on the upside 
while sitting higher in the capital structure and having the benefit of bond-like risk if the 
growth doesn’t materialize. Given this dynamic, the convertible bond market has long 
been dominated by biotech, pharmaceutical and technology names. It should come as 
no surprise then that over the last couple of years it has increasingly become a funding 
vehicle of choice for AI-focused companies; particularly for smaller, speculative-grade 
names that might be new to the space or making a pivot to AI from another business 
model. 
The proliferation of AI converts offerings has led to a surge in primary market issuance: 
year-to-date USD supply of $135 billion has already set a full-year record with still four 
months left in the year (Exhibit 1). Based on our estimates, 44% of this supply has come 
from AI-related names (Exhibit 2). While the vast majority of these offerings have come 
from the smaller issuers with high yield (HY) ratings, a key development in 2026 has been 
the selective entry of investment grade (IG) mega-cap technology issuers. For example, 
Google issued over $19 billion across two tranches in June and Oracle issued a $5 billion 
dollar tranche in February. 
 
In our view, this type of hyperscaler equity-linked issuance achieves two primary 
structural objectives: 1) diversification of financing sources given potential market 
capacity constraints and 2) balance sheet optimization. In one instance, the convertible 
offering was done in conjunction with a larger equity raise, which to us suggests a 
strategic intent to diversify financing across asset classes and potential acknowledgment 
of issuer concentration and market saturation limitations in the USD IG corporate credit 
market.  We also see an element of balance sheet optimization, as convertibles receive 
favorable equity treatment from rating agencies relative to senior unsecured straight 
debt, preserving balance sheet flexibility without triggering punitive leverage re-ratings.

cycle, is the strength of demand for the product driven by three core market dynamics. 
First, implied volatility on the convertibles index has surged to the highest levels in over 
20 years, making the embedded conversion option more valuable (Exhibit 3). Second, 
and related to point one, small and mid-cap equities have been performing extremely 
well, with the Russell 2000 outperforming the S&P 500 by roughly 15% since the 
beginning of the rally that started in April 2025 (Exhibit 4). Issuers in the converts index 
are dominated by small and mid-cap companies, so convertibles tend to attract more 
investor interest when that part of the market is performing well. Finally, with 
benchmark IG and HY spreads hovering near historical tights, credit investors face 
constrained capital appreciation potential in traditional fixed income. Convertibles offer 
a compelling alternative for credit and cross-asset allocators seeking asymmetric total 
return convexity. 
 
Strong demand leads to better borrowing terms and more supply 
This backdrop means that, to a certain extent, demand is creating its own supply. From a 
corporate treasurer’s perspective, terms have been unusually borrower-friendly, making 
the convertible bond market more compelling from a funding standpoint. The equity 
upside means that the coupon on the bond is lower than it would otherwise be for a 
similar maturity straight bond, lowering borrowing costs. 
Exhibit 5 shows that, similar to the environment that persisted in 2021, the share of new 
convertible bonds being issued with a zero coupon is elevated at nearly 30% this year, 
though down from nearly 40% last year. Similarly, the premium at which issuers are able 
to set the conversion price has also increased to the second highest level after 2021 
(Exhibit 6). When demand is weak, issuers need to set the conversion price closer to 
in-the-money to induce buyers. But when demand is strong, the premium increases thus 
minimizing equity dilution. Exhibit 7 shows that, despite the wider premium offered in 
the primary market, the average premium for converts trading in the secondary market 
has shrunk to just 30%, down from nearly 70% a couple of years ago. As conversion 
premiums compress, convertibles transition from more bond-like profiles into 
higher-convexity instruments. This shift increases portfolio delta sensitivity, allowing 
investors to participate more substantially in equity appreciation.
