Housing Starts Bounced Back in October, But 
Underlying Activity Remains Weak 
Headlines 
 
Total housing starts bounced back somewhat in October after falling for three 
straight months, up 10% to 1.309 million. On a year-over-year basis, starts were 
up 2.8%. 
 
 
Both multifamily and single-family housing starts increased in October. 
Regionally, starts rose in the Midwest and South, although multifamily 
construction drove the entire gain in the Midwest.  
 
Residential construction permits fell in the month, down 7.4% to 1.295 million 
units. 
 
Total housing completions rose 6.5% in October from September but were still 
10.4% down from a year earlier.  
 
PNC expects single-family homebuilding to remain weak into 2027. 
Details
Total privately owned U.S. housing starts rebounded 
in October after declining for three consecutive 
months, increasing 9.9% to an annualized rate of 
1.309 million units from a downwardly revised 1.191 
million in September (previously reported as 1.221 
million). October starts exceeded PNC's forecast of 
1.270 million units. Despite the monthly increase, the 
three-month moving average, which helps smooth 
monthly volatility, declined 1.6% to 1.260 million units. 
 
Housing construction surged during the post-
pandemic recovery, peaking at nearly 1.8 million 
units in April 2022. Since then, activity has trended 
lower as mortgage rates have climbed from below 
3% to above 7%, significantly reducing affordability. 
Housing starts have been particularly volatile since 
early 2025 amid uncertainty surrounding tariffs, the 
conflict in the Middle East, and higher mortgage 
rates over the summer. Nevertheless, the broader 
downward trend that began in 2022 remains intact. 
On a year-over-year basis, total housing starts were 
up 2.8% in October 2026. 
 
Both single-family and multifamily housing starts 
increased in October, rising approximately 4% and 
25%, respectively. Multifamily construction tends to 
be considerably more volatile on a month-to-month 
basis. Even with October's rebound, single-family 
starts remained 3.2% below their year-earlier level, 
while multifamily starts were more than 20% higher 
than a year ago. 
 
Regionally, housing starts rose in both the Midwest 
and South, increasing 49% and 11%, respectively. 
However, multifamily construction entirely drove 
the sharp gain in the Midwest. Housing starts

Residential building permits, a leading indicator of 
future construction activity, fell 7.4% in October to 
an annualized rate of 1.295 million units. Permit 
issuance declined for both single-family (down 8.7%) 
and multifamily (down 5.3%) projects and was down 
8.7% from a year earlier. 
 
Housing completions increased 6.5% in October from 
September but remained 10.4% below their year-
earlier level. Completions have generally trended 
lower since peaking in August 2024. After rising 
steadily from 2011 through the first half of 2024, 
completions began to moderate and are likely to 
drift lower over the near term as the pipeline of 
homes under construction continues to shrink and 
housing starts remain subdued. 
 
Permit data suggest that housing starts are likely to 
remain under pressure in coming months, following a 
sharp decline in permit activity during July and 
August. Single-family construction faces growing 
downside risks. The National Association of Home 
Builders' Housing Market Index remained near a 15-
year 
low 
in 
November, 
signaling 
continued 
weakness in builder sentiment. 
 
PNC expects homebuilding activity to remain 
subdued into 2027 as elevated mortgage rates and 
poor affordability continue to restrain housing 
demand. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate 
is currently around 7.1%. Both new-home sales and 
measures 
of 
prospective 
buyer 
traffic 
have 
softened in recent months and remain well below 
levels seen at the start of the year. 
 
The labor market has shown signs of stabilization in 
2026, which should provide some support to housing 
demand. Nevertheless, downside risks remain 
elevated. A prolonged conflict in the Middle East 
could keep energy prices elevated and place 
upward pressure on mortgage rates, while any 
renewed deterioration in labor market conditions 
would 
present 
an 
additional 
headwind 
to 
homebuyer demand and residential construction 
activity.
