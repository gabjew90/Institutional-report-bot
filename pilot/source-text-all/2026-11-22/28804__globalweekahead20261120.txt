Caught in the Moment 
Strong reliance upon near-term inflation data that may be particularly overheated in coming releases may lead the Federal Reserve to 
become even more hawkish over coming meetings. As US Thanksgiving beckons, the week’s main focus upon holiday sales and inflation 
readings could bolster this point. Seasonal discounting around turkey time is showing signs of being far less than normal which may mean 
that after applying seasonal adjustments there could be some really warm numbers ahead.  
This could be combined with other unusual seasonal factors. El Niño is probably already adding to inflationary pressures in the US with a 
reminder link to this piece from the San Francisco Fed. 
It’s feasible that the Fed should look through at least some of a potential coming wave of inflationary pressures as El Niño peaks and begins 
to wane by late year and once muted holiday shopping effects on discounting wane. The other side to more inflamed than usual price 
pressures could be quite different as seasonal adjustments could overcompensate against a softer price pattern afterward. This may be 
hard to do and could drive risk of an overreaction by the Committee. 
In an otherwise holiday-interrupted week, global markets will consider potentially important pre-blackout guidance from BoC Governor 
Macklem. I’ll argue that he shouldn’t be fussed by recent softness in the job market and remain laser-focused upon inflation risk. The Bank 
of Korea will be the only central bank to weigh in with a decision before December’s onslaught of major central banks begins. The price to 
pay for going on holiday will be for American market participants to cram in a wave of economic indicators on Tuesday and Wednesday. 
They may miss some of the action in terms of global inflation readings. 
THIS HOLIDAY SHOPPING SEASON MAY IGNITE MORE INFLATION 
American Thanksgiving is on Thursday. Stocks and bonds will be shut and then subject to an early 1pmET close for stocks and 2pmET for 
bonds on Friday. Unofficial early closes would surprise no one on Wednesday. The effects will be felt across other markets in the absence of 
American investors and suspension of trading in US dollar-denominated debt securities overseas. 
Eat and shop and not necessarily in that order, or perhaps any order whatsoever. So named because it’s traditionally the time of year when 
retailers start to turn a profit, Black Friday and then Cyber Monday may set the tone for the holiday shopping season. 
Yet the dates are not what they used to be in years past and perhaps particularly so this year. There used to be a cottage industry of folks 
tracking satellite images of parking lots at malls and monitoring shopper traffic for signs of how robust the season may be. There is some 
residual attempt at doing so, but the tracking is done much more electronically now with card payments, online traffic statistics and rapid 
company anecdotes.  
What may make this year even less relevant by comparison to prior years is that price discounting is expected to be much less prevalent. In 
many categories it could be entirely absent which I’ll come back to in a moment.

Charts 1–3 provide forecasts for the US holiday shopping season. They include forecasts from 
various groups for the percentage changes in the value of sales. Because of this, they embed 
assumptions about price discounting—if any—and the volume of sales. Both count for 
earnings. Only volumes and industry value-added count for tracking the economy.  
To help inform the price versus volumes breakdown of expectations, chart 4 seems optimistic. 
It shows the categories that could be subject to the deepest discounts this year. Don’t hold 
your breath in either the US or Canada.  
Charts 5–12 show our tracking of prices by sales category in the US so far this year. The only 
category where discounting is prevalent this year is if you’re in the market for, say, a bedroom 
set and fresh sheets! In every other category, the price pressures tend to be more acute than in 
prior years.  
This could mean that if seasonal discounting is less prevalent, seasonally adjusted CPI could be 
hotter into the holiday season. That’s because at this time of year, SA factors would 
compensate against seasonal discounting, but if there isn’t any, then SA factors could create 
some really hot numbers. Thereafter, when SA factors would compensate against post-
discounting price pressures, they may go too far in doing so. 
Charts 13–21 do the same thing for categories of Canadian consumer spending. Audio equipment, household appliances, maybe toys and 
cosmetics are witnessing significant discounting compared to prior years. Like the US, forget about any unusually great deals on electronics 
and computers partly due to the surging demand for components because of the AI investment craze. Ditto for clothing. While the 
evidence isn’t quite as strong in Canada, it also points to the possibility that seasonal adjustments may inadequately compensate for 
stronger than normal price pressures that may exert some upward influence upon Canadian CPI into year-end. 
BOC’S MACKLEM—BAH HUMBUG?  
Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem delivers one of two pre-holiday speeches on Tuesday. This one is billed as “an outlook for the 
Canadian economy and the challenges facing monetary policy,” so what could he be left to address in his more typical pre-holiday speech 
on December 15th? There will be a press conference during which media may ask impactful questions. 
The urgency to speak may reflect a desire to weigh in ahead of the blackout period that begins on December 2nd ahead of the decision 
seven days later. His speech on the 15th is then likely to rehash what he said in the communications around the fresh policy decision or 
something else. The only other times since 2021 when Macklem has inserted a speech before the December decision and his year-end 
speech were in 2022 and 2023 when policy changes were definitely in motion.  
These speeches can be impactful to markets especially when policy changes are afoot at inflection points (chart 22). I would expect this one 
to have a carefully hawkish tone that picks up on the communications around the October decision and subsequently. 
Here is a list of the ones during Macklem’s tenure so far. 
• 
Macklem, 2020: Focused on trade and investment, worried about a second COVID wave. Little market reaction. 
• 
Macklem, 2021: Hawkish slant, pointing to diminishing slack and pivoted toward concern about high inflation. CAD rallied a touch 
thereafter. 
• 
Macklem, 2022: Generally hawkish tone but shifted toward a tweaking tone after 400bps of hikes with another 75bps to be delivered 
later. Little market reaction. 
• 
Macklem 2023: Indicated a prolonged hold at 5%, noting it was premature to consider easing but that this could occur before inflation 
fell to 2%. Markets would subsequently push out expectations for rate cuts. 
• 
Macklem 2024: The speech had an apologetic tone, admitting that public trust in the BoC had been damaged by allowing inflation to 
surge but that rate cuts would be downsized after a half point reduction that December. The tone generally matched the fresh 
decision.

WHAT’S GOING ON WITH CANADA’S JOB MARKET? 
Would Canada’s recently soft jobs numbers hold the BoC back from hiking? Recall that about 115k jobs have been ‘lost’ over the past three 
months. This is a very common question across clients. I’ll offer several theories on why they shouldn’t be treated as a barrier and they’re 
not necessarily mutually incompatible. 
1. Youths have accounted for two-
thirds of the job losses in the past 
three months. Why? This summer 
job market from May through July 
was the hottest one on record 
excluding the pandemic rebound 
years of 2020 and 2021 (chart 23). 
Then youths lost more jobs over 
the August to October period 
marked by end of summer 
vacations and the return to school 
than any other year on record save 
one—1981 (chart 24). Simply put, 
what went up faster than ever, 
went down faster than ever as the 
student job market stabilized. 
Why? It was a hot summer for 
tourism activity that also included 
the FIFA World Cup and a lot of the workers in that area of the job market tend to be young. Standard seasonal adjustments—particularly 
given a recency bias to how they are computed—would not have adequately controlled for an outsized summer job market or its outsized 
unwinding. The BoC should look through this and it’s not under their control in any event. In fact, the folks at Indeed have already been 
flagging an unusually early rebound in seasonal hiring that could impact the November jobs print just before the December BoC decision 
(here). 
2. Lost jobs reflect a negative shock to labour supply. Canadian jobs are down by 45k ytd. The labour force is down by literally twice as 
much. You can’t hire if you can’t find workers. Tighter immigration policy is part of this. The pattern has shifted somewhat in the past three 
months during which the supply of workers shrank by 62k but jobs fell by 115k. We therefore have to consider other explanations for the 
more recent pattern, such as the first one on this list, but also the following ones. By the way, contrast this to Australia’s recently weak job

Business activity has remained close to a five-

bigger question for November will be whether

Canada Quiet 
Canada will mainly follow Governor Macklem’s speech and signs of momentum in holiday sales. Only SEPH payrolls for September are due 
out (Thursday). They’re a lagging indicator that is often subject to massive revisions each month and that often doesn’t line up with the 
Labour Force Survey’s component for payrolls. 
US—Cramming it All In 
Any US indicator that matters will be crammed into Tuesday and Wednesday before a big whooshing sound out the door into 
Thanksgiving. 
Tuesday’s main release will be consumer confidence. November’s reading could improve to the extent to which decent job growth and 
falling gasoline prices drive it. Confidence is hovering around pandemic-era lows so it’s hard not to imagine some improvement. 
Wednesday crams in several releases as follows: 
• 
Personal income (October): Incomes could grow at the fastest pace since May. Wages and salaries are posting decent growth, and 
incomes will be lifted by two special factors. One is a one-time Medicare premium rebate of $90 to about 21 million Americans. 
Another is because November 1st was on a Sunday this year, so some payments will be brought forward to October, such as Veterans’ 
and other military benefits. 
• 
Personal spending: The retail sales control group’s 0.6% m/m SA rise in October is expected to lift total personal spending by a similar 
to slightly slower pace given close historical correlations. 
• 
PCE: The problem facing spending, however, is that much of the gain is probably due to higher prices. The Fed’s preferred measures of 
inflation—the PCE and core PCE price deflators—are both expected to rise by about 0.3% m/m SA. November’s readings are informed 
by CPI, relevant producer price components and methodological differences including different weights on various types of spending. If 
the core PCE estimate is in the right ballpark, then it would reinforce FOMC communications that lean toward hiking again on 
December 9th. A lower than expected number could impact rate pricing after an expected December hike. 
• 
Q3 GDP revisions: I don’t think we have enough information to stray from the initial reading of 3.4% q/q SAAR. There may be slight 
upside potential from positive retail sales revisions. The next revision will be the one to incorporate fuller evidence on services. 
• 
Durable goods orders: October’s reading is expected to continue to be driven by core capital goods orders and especially from the 
tech-sector. Boeing’s plane orders were strong at an unchanged pace over the prior month.

Several regions other than the US also update inflation measures for November. 
Falling gasoline prices in Europe could motivate a bigger drop in Eurozone CPI in m/m terms 
but not just yet while core CPI is expected to remain sticky. Super 95 gasoline prices moved

Bank of Canada (BoC): Bank of Canada Governor Macklem will deliver a speech at the Economic Club of Canada on Tuesday at 11:30 am ET on the Canadian 
economic outlook and challenges facing monetary policy. The speech will be followed by a press conference, with remarks and webcasts available on the bank’s 
website. The communication will be closely watched for any signals on the bank’s December policy decision-with markets remaining divided over the prospect of a 
rate move-and beyond.
Bank of Korea (BoK): After pausing in October following back to back 25bp hikes in July and August, consensus expects the Bank of Korea to deliver another 25bp 
hike on Thursday. The bank paused in October to reassess the economic outlook, risks, and its policy stance after 50bp of cumulative tightening. However, the 
economy has continued to outperform expectations, led by exports and investment, with Q3 GDP expanding 1.0% q/q. Core inflation has also risen to 2.8% from 
2.6% in August, while housing prices in Seoul continue to increase. These developments, alongside the continued terms of trade shock, increase the risk of inflation 
remaining above target and support another 25bp hike, consistent with the bank’s August six-month policy rate projection for one further hike by year-end. That 
said, some caution remains warranted, as the broader economy has yet to see meaningful spillover from the AI-driven chip cycle, with gains largely concentrated in 
the export sector and private consumption remaining soft. As a result, the updated six-month policy rate projection, alongside new growth and inflation forecasts, 
will be key for markets in assessing the policy path ahead.
