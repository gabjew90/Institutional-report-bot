As the holiday season approaches, markets are beginning to grapple with the likelihood that the Federal
Reserve (Fed) will raise interest rates in December, arguing that monetary policy remains insufficiently
restrictive. We continue to believe that, after a December increase, the Fed should pause and allow time
for the cumulative effects of recent rate hikes to work their way through the economy. Nevertheless, some
Fed officials have discussed the possibility of a 50 basis-point increase in December, while others have
suggested rates may need to rise by as much as 100 basis points by the middle of next year. In our view,
such discussions are premature. We continue to believe that both markets and Fed officials will ultimately
move closer to our assessment of the appropriate policy path.
If that seems difficult to envision amid today's heightened rate expectations, consider how quickly the
narrative can change. In our October 16, 2026 Weekly Economics, we described Beth Hammack, President
of the Cleveland Federal Reserve Bank, as one of the more outspokenly hawkish members of the Federal
Open Market Committee. Today, she sounds considerably more moderate than many of her colleagues.
That shift underscores how rapidly expectations can evolve and suggests there is ample room for the
current market narrative to move toward a more balanced outlook on rates.

We have repeatedly argued that today's inflation environment bears little resemblance to the inflationary

Housing Market Struggles Continue
Recent housing data continue to depict a sector under significant strain. Existing home sales last week fell 
to levels not seen since the Great Recession, while the median price of existing homes was up just 0.4% 
from a year earlier. This week's National Association of Home Builders Housing Market Index (HMI) offered 
little encouragement, falling to a 14-year low as elevated mortgage rates, high construction costs, and 
persistent labor shortages continue to weigh on builder sentiment. Although housing starts posted an 
increase, activity remains subdued, while building permits dropped to a six-year low, signaling a weaker 
pipeline of future construction activity. 
The latest source of weakness within residential construction has been residential improvements. After 
growing strongly through the first quarter of this year, activity has reversed course and is now declining at 
the fastest year-over-year pace since January 2019. Although this category is typically more volatile than 
single-family or multifamily construction, it has grown to represent nearly 40% of total residential 
construction activity. Importantly, residential improvements had been offsetting weakness elsewhere in 
the sector, as single-family construction has generally declined while multifamily activity has remained 
largely flat throughout the year.

While we do not expect housing weakness to evolve into a broader crisis, the sector is likely to remain under

We expect these conditions to place additional downward pressure on home prices during 2027, 
particularly in the new-home market. Such an adjustment would further challenge homebuilders and 
reinforce the recession already underway in residential investment. 
Bottom Line: Markets appear increasingly convinced that the Fed will need to remain aggressive well into 
2027, but we believe those expectations are likely to moderate over time. At the same time, housing 
remains one of the clearest areas of economic weakness and should continue to act as a drag on economic 
growth and inflation going forward.

Retail Sales: Retail sales data showed a mixed picture in October. Although there were several outlier retail distribution 
sectors for the month, the overall picture was not more muted even though control group sales were up by more than the 
headline number suggests, 0.6% versus 0.2%. Weakness in automobile sales and furniture stores kept the headline retail 
and food services increase contained while a 1.5% increase in sales at gasoline service stations, a purely nominal effect due 
to higher gasoline prices, prevented a much weaker headline number. Still, the strong control group print as well as the 
upward revision to September’s control group sales continues to indicate that consumer demand remains relatively stable 
even if the overall picture from October’s sectorial view was uneven. Retail and food services sales were in line with 
estimates, up 0.2%, during the month of October after increasing by 0.8% in September, according to the US Census 
Bureau. On a year-over-year basis, retail and food services sales were up 6.8%, up from 6.6% in September. Excluding sales 
at motor vehicle and parts dealers, sales were up 0.6% while excluding sales at gasoline stations, sales were up just 0.1%. If 
we exclude sales at motor vehicle and parts dealers as well as sales at gas stations, sales were up 0.5%. Retail trade sales 
were also up 0.2%. Sales at motor vehicle and parts dealers declined 1.6% in October, while sales at furniture stores 
plunged 2.3%, month on month. The highest growth retail distribution channels during the month of October were 
miscellaneous store retailers surging 4.7%, month on month, health and personal health stores and sales at gasoline 
stations, both up 1.5%, and sales at nonstore retailers, up 1.2%. Finally, sales at food services and drinking places increased 
by 0.2%. Control group sales were up by 0.6% during the month of October. Retail and food services sales were revised 
higher in August, from 1.3% to 1.5% while control group sales were revised higher from 1.2% to 1.6%. Control group sales 
were revised up to 0.5% in September from an original increase of 0.1%.
Import Prices : Both import and export prices surprised to the upside in October, with higher nonfuel import prices driving 
the import price index higher. In fact, import prices excluding food and fuel rose 0.9% MoM, their fastest monthly pace in six 
months. Similar to last week's CPI and PPI releases, the report adds to evidence of broadening price pressures, reinforcing 
expectations for a 25-basis point Fed rate hike in December. Import prices increased by a more than expected 1.1% in 
October, while export prices surged by just as much, 1.1%, compared to September, according to the Bureau of Labor 
Statistics. Given the government shutdown this time last year, the report did not provide year-over-year growth rates. Fuel 
import prices increased 3.6%, month on month, after increasing by 6.3% in September. All import prices excluding fuel 
prices increased by 0.8% during the month, according to the release. The 1.1% increase in export prices was driven by a 
1.2% month-on-month increase in agricultural export prices, while non-agricultural export prices were up 1.1% month on 
month. According to the release, “BLS did not collect survey data for October 2025 because of a lapse in appropriations. For 
US Import and Export Price Indexes (MXPI) series that primarily utilize survey data, BLS could not publish October 2025 
indexes. As a result, BLS cannot publish 12-month percent changes for October 2026 for indexes where October 2025 data 
are unavailable.
Industrial Production: Industrial production was much weaker than expected in October, down 0.4%. However, the details 
are better than the headline number as a large contraction in the production of utilities was the reason for the weaker 
month-on-month number. Manufacturing production was strong, up 0.3%, underscoring the strength seen in the ISM 
Manufacturing index. Interestingly, this industrial release report did not include the typically large revisions to previous 
monthly numbers. The only revision was June’s print, which was revised higher from a 0.2% increase to a 0.3% increase on 
the back of a similar increase in manufacturing production. Also, after two consecutive months of weak business equipment 
output, the sector came back strongly, up 1.0% month on month and 7.8% compared to a year earlier. This sector has been 
driving the manufacturing sector during this year. Industrial production was much weaker than expected in October, down 
0.4% compared to an estimate for a 0.2% increase, after a 0.7% increase in September. By major industry group, industrial 
production posted another strong 0.3% increase in October and is now up 2.2% on a year-over-year basis, while mining 
output was down 0.8% during the month but up 2.4% compared to a year earlier. Finally, the production of utilities was 
down 4.3%, almost wiping out the 4.4% increase in this sector during the month of September. On a year-over-year basis, 
the production of utilities was up just 1.7%. By major market groups, the production of final products was down 0.2%, 
month on month, but up 2.3% compared to a year earlier, as the production of consumer goods was down 0.7% and also 
down, by 0.2% compared to a year earlier. The production of business equipment was up 1.0% after two consecutive 
negative months. Compared to a year earlier, the production of business equipment was up a strong 7.8%. Nonindustrial 
supplies production was down 0.3% with construction down by 0.4%. Finally, the production of materials was down 0.7% 
compared to September. Capacity utilization was down slightly, from 76.8% in September to 76.4% in October, driven down 
mostly by a large decline in capacity in the utilities sector.  The Federal Reserve revised industrial production in June, from a 
0.2% original print to a 0.3% on the back of an upward revision to manufacturing production, which was also revised up to a 
0.3% compared to a 0.2% original print.

NAHB Housing Market Index: The NAHB Housing Market Index remains on a downward trend and has fallen to a 14-year 
low as higher mortgage rates, elevated energy and material costs, and rising labor shortages hamper demand and dampen 
builder confidence. All three major components of the index moved lower in November: current sales conditions fell six 
points, future sales expectations fell two points, and prospective buyer traffic fell two points and reached its lowest level in 
15 years (ex-COVID). With mortgage rates remaining elevated (30-year average fixed mortgage rate: ~7.5%) and inflationary 
pressures broadening, we see little improvement this year and well into next year. The National Association of Home 
Builders (NAHB) Housing Market Index (HMI) was lower than expected in November, declining to 25 compared to a reading 
of 29 in October. This was the lowest reading for the NAHB HMI since April of 2012. The three components of the index were 
weaker in November compared to October. The Single Family Sales: Present index was 28 in November compared to 34 in 
October; the Single Family Sales: Next 6 Months was at 30 in November compared to 32 in October, while the Traffic of 
Prospective Buyers index declined to 16 from 18—its lowest reading in 15 years (ex-COVID). By region, all  four regions in the 
country were lower in November compared to October of this year. The Northeast saw the index drop to 31 compared to 37 
in October. In the Midwest, the index declined to 30 compared to 35 on October. In the South, it declined from 28 in October 
to 23 in September. Finally, the West showed the index at 19 compared to 20 in October. According to the release, 46% of 
builders cut prices in November. This was the highest share during the period 2020-2026. The average price cut was 6%, the 
same average cut reported during the last eight consecutive months.
Business Inventories: Business inventories were in line with expectations in September, up 0.5% month on month, and 
5.1% compared to a year earlier. Sales were up 1.8%, month on month. The strong sales performance for all of the different 
distribution channels meant that the inventories to sales ratio declined to 1.33 in September of this year, compared to 1.42 
during September of last year. The inventories to sales ratio for every distribution channel were down in September of this 
year compared to last year. Although this is bullish for production going forward, it is bearish for the trade deficit, as some of 
these sectors’ inventory replenishments needs to come from higher imports. Manufacturers’ and trade inventories were up 
0.5% during the month of September, month on month and up 5.1% compared to a year earlier, according to the US Census 
Bureau. Sales for every distribution channel, manufacturers, retailers, and merchant wholesalers, were up 1.8% during the 
same month and up 12.5% compared to a year ago, according to the report. The inventories to sales ratio at the end of 
September was 1.33. This is lower than the ration during September of 2025, which was at 1.42. The inventories to sales 
ratio for manufacturers was 1.46 in September of this year compared to 1.56 in September of last year. For the retail sector, 
the inventories to sales ratio was 1.35 in September of this year compared to 1.38 last year. Finally, for merchant 
wholesalers, the ratio was 1.21 this September compared to 1.34 last year.
Housing Starts: Housing starts were stronger than expected in October while building permits were lower than expected 
during the same period. Housing starts were boosted by a very strong increase in starts of multi-family units. In regional 
terms, housing starts in the Northeast were weak for multi-family units but strong for single-family units. The opposite was 
true for the rest of the regions. Building permits were weak for every type of homes, single-family units, 2 to 4 units, as well 
as 5 units or more. The weakness was also clear across the almost every region of the country, with the exception of the 
Midwest. The increase in housing starts, while positive, is not enough to bring housing starts out of their slump while the 
weaker than expected building permits is not a good omen for what is to come. Building permits recorded the lowest level 
since May of 2020, which was during the start of the pandemic recession. We remain negative on the new home market for 
the rest of this year and into next year. Housing starts were stronger than expected in October, at a seasonally adjusted 
annual rate of 1.309 million, according to the US Census Bureau and the US Department of Housing and Urban 
Development. This was a 9.9% increase compared to September, and a 2.8% increase compared to October of last year. 
Starts of single-family units were up 4.0% compared to September of this year but 3.2% lower than in October of last year. 
Starts of 5 units or more surged by 25.5% compared to September of this year and were 22.0% higher than in October of last 
year. In regional terms, the Northeast saw the largest decline in housing starts, down 20.8%, month on month, and down 
34.5% compared to last year. The largest percentage increase occurred in the Midwest, up 48.4%, month on month, and up 
17.2% compared to a year earlier. The South, which is the largest regional market in the US, saw starts up 10.5% compared 
to September and by 12.2% compared to a year earlier. Finally, the West recorded a month-on-month decline of 2.6% and a 
decline of 11.6% compared to a year earlier. Building permits were lower than expected, at a seasonally adjusted annual 
rate of 1.295 million or 7.4% lower than in September and 8.7% lower than October of 2025. Building permits for single-
family unit homes were down 8.7%, month on month, and down 12.9% compared to a year earlier. The Northeast saw 
building permits down by 6.9% compared to September of this year as well as compared to October of last year. The 
Midwest was the only region recording an increase in building permits, up 0.5% compared to September by 8.1% compared 
to October of last year. The South saw permits down 10.7%, month on month, and down 8.3% compared to a year ago.

Pending Home Sales: The Pending Home Sales Index fell to a record low in October, beating the low point recorded in April 
of 2020 at the start of the pandemic recession. The regional picture was as weak as the national picture, as no region of the 
country saw improvement in the index for the month of October, both month on month as well as year over year. The 
release tried to point to the glass half full, indicating that this could mean that there is a buildup of future potential demand 
that could help improve the future prospects for existing home sales. They added that mortgage rates have probably hit a 
cycle high and thus should come down and help home sales. We are less sanguine about the stance of the US housing 
market and expect further deterioration as long a mortgage rates remain at these levels or increase further. The Pending 
Home Sales Index was lower than expected in October, falling by 4.7% compared to September while also down, by 16.7%, 
compared to a year earlier, according to the National Association of Realtors. Pending home sales declined for every region 
of the country, both month on month as well as year over year, according to the release. The largest decline was recorded in 
the West region, down 6.9% month on month, and 20.3% year over year. The South showed a 6.3% decline month on 
month, and an 18.7% decline year over year. The Midwest saw a 2.7% month-on-month decline and 14.0% year-over-year 
decline. Finally, the Northeast saw the smallest decline, both month on month as well as year over year, at 0.8% and 11.1%, 
respectively. According to the Chief Economist for the NAR, Dr. Lawrence Yun, “October could mark the low point in contract 
signings for this housing cycle, as mortgage rates appear to have topped out.” He further added that “Two consecutive 
months of record-low pending contracts point to very sluggish fourth-quarter home sales closings, and annual existing-
home sales could be the lowest since the mid-1990s. The Pending Home Sales Index (PHS), a leading indicator of housing 
activity, measures contract signings for existing single-family homes, condominiums, and co-ops. Because homes typically 
close one to two months after a contract is signed, the PHSI generally leads Existing Home Sales by a similar timeframe.”
Leading Economic Index: The Leading Economic Index declined in October, driven by weak consumer expectations as well 
as lower building permits on the non-financial side while every financial component was positive during the month. The 
Conference Board indicated that economic growth is being fueled more and more by a strong investment sector and less by 
consumer demand. Still, the Conference Board estimates that the US economy will grow by 2.3% year over year in 2026 and 
by 2.1% in 2027. We have a slightly higher estimate for year-over-year economic growth in 2026, at 2.5% (2.4% for the whole 
of 2026), and 2.0% year over year for 2027 (or 2.3% for the whole of 2027). The Leading Economic Index (LEI), published by 
The Conference Board, was lower than expected in October, falling 0.3% after remaining unchanged in September, 
according to the release. Every one of the financial components was positive during the month while four of the non-
financial components were negative, one was flat and the other two increased slightly. Once again, the LEI was drawn down 
by a very large decline in consumer expectations as well as a large decline in building permits. The Coincident Economic 
Index (CEI) was flat in October after a downwardly revised 0.1% increase in September. Finally, the Lagging Economic Index 
(LAG) was also unchanged during the month of October after an upwardly revised increase of 0.7% in September. The 
Conference Board indicated that “…the LEI’s six-month growth rate remained positive, indicating continued economic 
expansion ahead, but increasingly investment-led with softer household demand. The Conference Board expects real GDP 
to grow by 2.3% y/y in 2026 and 2.1% y/y in 2027.
