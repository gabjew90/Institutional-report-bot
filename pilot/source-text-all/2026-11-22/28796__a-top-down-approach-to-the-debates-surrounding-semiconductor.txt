1. The “Semiageddon” Debate: A significant but Not Yet an Abnormal Drawdown
Since peaking in June 2026, the semiconductor sector has experienced a drawdown of approximately 31%, as measured by the S&P 500 
Semiconductors & Semiconductor Equipment Industry Group. Despite this correction, the sector is still up 3% year-to-date (excluding dividends), 
427% since 2022 (53% annualized) and 2,663% since 2009 (22% annualized). These figures compare with gains of 5%, 88% (18% annualized) and 
544% (12% annualized), respectively, for the S&P 500.
Importantly, drawdowns of this magnitude are not unusual for the sector. The current correction ranks as the ninth-largest in the historical series. The 
most severe drawdown (-86%) followed the bursting of the dot-com bubble, with the market eventually bottoming out during the early stages of the 
Global Financial Crisis. However, there is a significant concentration of historical drawdowns in the 30%-50% range, including those recorded in 2022 
(-49%), 1990 (-42%), 2026 following the outbreak of the Iran war (-39%), 1996 and 1998 (-38% and -37%, respectively), as well as the Covid-19-
related correction in 2020 (-35%) and the tariff-related sell-off in 2025 (-35%). A further correction of just a few percentage points would place the 
current drawdown among the most severe episodes in the sector's history.
Against this backdrop, the key question for investors is whether the recent correction represents an attractive entry point following a period of 
exceptional outperformance, or whether it signals the beginning of a more prolonged adjustment in semiconductor valuations.

2. The Fundamentals Debate: Not Everything that Glitters is Gold
The semiconductor cycle has massively strengthened lately, although growth in physical volumes remains far more moderate than the surge in 
revenues would suggest. According to Semi, global silicon wafer shipments increased by 13.4% YoY in 3Q26 (accelerating form 7.4% in 2Q26), driven 
primarily by AI-related demand. HBM (High Bandwidth Memory), other advanced DRAMs (Dynamic Random Access Memory) and AI accelerators are 
leading the expansion, while conventional memory products are recovering more gradually and analog, power and industrial semiconductors remain 
comparatively weak. The sharp increase in semiconductor revenues therefore reflects, not only higher shipments, but also rising memory prices and 
a shift towards higher-value products.
Global PC shipments contracted by 20.1% YoY in 3Q26, while smartphone shipments are expected to decline by 16.7% in 2026, with the contraction 
accelerating to 27.2% in the second half of the year, according to IDC. Rising memory costs, higher retail prices and inventory adjustments are 
increasingly weighing on consumer electronics demand, with lower-priced products particularly affected. In contrast, AI server shipments are 
expected to grow by approximately 31% in 2026. This divergence underscores the increasingly polarized nature of the semiconductor cycle, with AI

3. The Hyperscalers’ Capex Plans Debate: The Rate of Change is Turning
The “Blue Wave” in the recent US midterm elections has added to existing concerns about the execution of hyperscalers' capex plans related to the 
build-out of AI infrastructure. Although it is probably still too early for these concerns to have had a meaningful impact on consensus estimates, 
hyperscalers' capex expectations have continued to trend upwards.
Nevertheless, the rate of growth is expected to slow significantly in 2027, with capex growth moderating to 47% from the 93% expected for 2026. 
This sharp deceleration already appears to be reflected in consensus expectations for semiconductor EPS growth.
However, the combination of slowing capex growth, high operating leverage, substantial increases in semiconductor prices and ongoing capacity 
expansion raises questions about the sustainability of current earnings expectations. The recent change of CEO at Nvidia (N/R), together with concerns 
about potential violations of export controls and their legal implications, adds another layer of uncertainty to the sector's earnings outlook, given the 
company's pivotal role in AI infrastructure deployment. Against this backdrop, the risks to consensus earnings expectations may be more significant 
than the projected slowdown in capex growth alone would suggest.

4. The Expected EPS vs. Margins Debate
Historically, semiconductor sector price performance, as is often the case in highly cyclical industries, has been a leading indicator of future EPS growth. 
However, a significant divergence has recently emerged between sector price performance and consensus expectations for future earnings growth.
A key question is whether consensus estimates are overly optimistic regarding the sustainability of the substantial margin expansion experienced 
by the sector in recent years. Current expectations suggest that margins will remain close to their historically elevated levels at least until 2028, the 
final year in which hyperscalers' capex growth is projected to remain comfortably in double-digit territory.
The main uncertainty lies in what happens beyond 2028, particularly if demand growth slows significantly or turns negative while production capacity 
continues to expand. Under such a scenario, the combination of weaker demand and increased supply could put significant pressure on semiconductor 
margins, calling into question the sustainability of current earnings expectations.

5. The Earnings Revision Momentum Debate: Earnings Revisions Are Still Rising, but Momentum Is Fading
Earnings revisions are a key driver of equity performance, although their interpretation becomes more complex in highly cyclical sectors. In these 
industries, earnings revisions tend to become particularly relevant when they signal a meaningful change in the trajectory of the earnings cycle.
As Figure 7 suggests, given the highly cyclical nature of the semiconductor industry, earnings revisions need to be sufficiently significant to change 
the expected direction and pace of the earnings cycle in order to have a sustained impact on equity prices
Between 2H25 and 1H26, substantial upward revisions to EPS estimates translated into a significant improvement in expected earnings growth. Since 
then, however, positive revisions have continued at a more moderate pace, but have not materially changed the expected trajectory of the earnings 
cycle.

6. The Valuation Debate: When Are Low Valuations Attractive Enough?
In highly cyclical industries, the conventional investment approach is often to buy when earnings multiples are elevated and sell when they decline, 
reflecting the inverse relationship between valuations and the earnings cycle. This pattern is broadly illustrated by the upper-left chart, although the 
duration and magnitude of the current AI-driven semiconductor expansion introduce additional complexities.
Semiconductor valuations have now fallen to historically low levels. Current 12-month forward P/E multiples are approximately 23% below their 
2006–2019 average (excluding 2009) and 39% below their 2006–YTD average. The valuation discount is even more pronounced when considering 
24-month forward earnings, as current multiples (8.3x) are the lowest since 2006 having breached the previous lows seen in the summer of 2011. 
This represents a discount of approximately 37% to the 2006–2019 average (excluding 2009) and 45% to the 2006–YTD average.
These valuation levels suggest that the market is already discounting a significant deterioration in forward earnings expectations. The recent de-
rating has been further exacerbated by concerns about the implications of the US midterm election results for AI infrastructure deployment, alongside 
emerging corporate governance issues at Nvidia (N/R) and finally by a potential tightening of export restrictions on US-related semiconductors to 
China.
The key question for investors is whether current valuations adequately reflect the risks to future earnings, or whether further downward revisions 
could undermine the apparent attractiveness of the sector. There is clearly no straightforward answer, although current multiples appear to be 
increasingly discounting a return to the net margin levels that prevailed during 2018-2022.

7. The Positioning Debate: The Buy-the-Dip Mentality Still Holds
Figure 13 suggests that, judging by flows into semiconductor equity ETFs, investors have already taken a position in the debates discussed above. 
The buy-the-dip mentality, which has also been prevalent across broader equity markets, appears to remain firmly in place in certain segments of 
the market.
Almost three-quarters of year-to-date inflows (Figure 13) into semiconductor equity ETFs have occurred since the summer peak in semiconductor 
stock prices. Even during the current sell-off, which began in early October, investors have continued to allocate capital to these ETFs, broadly in line 
with the pattern observed across equity markets, as discussed in our latest ETF Flows monthly review.
However, the most recent daily flow data (Figure 14) suggest that this trend may be starting to weaken although in the October 19 sell-off there 
were still marginal inflows.

Investment implications
To own or not to own semiconductors? That is the question. Within our geographically-driven equity allocation framework, this translates into how 
we should position ourselves in markets with significant semiconductor exposure, particularly EM Asia, Japan and, to a lesser extent, the US, relative 
to regions with substantially lower exposure to the sector.
During the summer, we adopted a relatively cautious stance towards semiconductor-heavy markets, maintaining an underweight position in EM Asia 
while remaining overweight on the US, where the balance between semiconductor producers and companies investing heavily in AI infrastructure 
(hyperscalers) was more favorable to the latter. At the same time, we maintained a significantly higher relative exposure to those markets with limited 
semiconductor exposure, particularly Europe and LatAm.
In our recent Asset Allocation Review, we partially reversed our preference for US equities over EM Asia. Nevertheless, our positioning in Europe and 
LatAm means that we remain slightly underweight on the semiconductor complex on a relative basis.
Semiconductor prices are clearly becoming more attractive, with the magnitude of the current drawdown approaching levels consistent with previous 
major corrections, excluding the post-2000 collapse. Valuations also appear to be increasingly discounting a return to the margin levels that prevailed 
before the AI investment boom.
Meanwhile, earnings revisions remain positive, although their magnitude has diminished considerably relative to the summer. Given the highly 
cyclical nature of the sector, earnings revisions alone are probably no longer the main factor driving investment decisions at this stage of the cycle.
What appears to be missing before adopting a significantly more constructive stance towards the semiconductor complex is clearer evidence of an 
unwinding of investor positioning, which has so far been limited. A further correction in prices, potentially in the high-single-digit range, would also 
bring the current drawdown closer to those observed during previous major sell-offs.
4
 

 
In short, while the correction in prices is making valuations increasingly attractive, investor positioning has yet to show clear signs of capitulation. 
Taken together, we believe this argues for maintaining our Neutral stance on the semiconductor complex for now.
