Metals sell-off runs ahead of physical
markets
It has been a difficult week for metals. Copper is down more than 8%,
aluminium almost 6% and gold around 9% as a stronger dollar, higher
rate expectations and weaker risk appetite weigh on the complex
Metals have come under sharp pressure this week, despite continued tightness in physical markets
The speed of the sell-off looks more like a sharp repricing of investor exposure than a broad
weakening in physical markets . Copper has taken the biggest hit as investors unwind bullish
positions and reassess AI-related demand. Yet the sharp correction in prices contrasts with
still-tight physical markets.

Investor selling weighs on copper
Copper is on track for one of its steepest weekly declines in a decade.
We think the speed of the sell-off partly reflects an unwind of bullish investor positioning. Both
LME and COMEX net length have fallen sharply from recent highs, while positioning across the
wider metals complex has also become less bullish.
Copper has also become more closely correlated with equity markets, including AI-related
stocks. The AI link matters for investor sentiment, but it does not make copper's demand story
dependent on data centres alone. Grid investment and electrification remain important
sources of demand. But as investors cut exposure to AI-related assets, copper has become
more vulnerable to the same selling pressure.

That contrasts sharply with the physical market in China. Shanghai inventories are at their
lowest since late 2023, while the Yangshan premium reached a record $500/t this week.
Bloomberg reports that some US copper is now being offered to buyers in China, where
physical availability remains tight. Volumes are limited so far, but the shift raises the possibility
that some of the metal accumulated in the US ahead of potential tariffs could start returning
to the global market.

Copper could remain under pressure if funds continue to cut positions or the macro backdrop
deteriorates. But the price correction has moved much faster than physical conditions. Tight
availability in China should provide some support once the investor selling eases.
The risk is that more US metal returns to the wider market if tariff expectations fade further,
gradually easing some of the regional tightness now supporting the physical market.
Aluminium supply remains tight for now
Aluminium has fallen with the wider complex, but nearby supply remains tight. The LME cash-
to-three-month spread has moved into a steep backwardation, while LME stocks remain
historically low and largely of Russian origin, limiting what is readily available to some buyers.
This should help contain the near-term downside.
At the same time, investors have reduced bullish aluminium positions from recent highs, in line
with the broader move across metals.
The medium-term picture is less supportive. Middle Eastern production is recovering, and new
Indonesian capacity is coming online. We expect the market to move towards surplus next
year, which should limit the scope for a sustained price recovery.

Gold feels the pressure from higher rates
Gold has fallen for five straight sessions and is down around 9% this week, putting it on course
for its steepest weekly decline since March. Prices have also touched their lowest level since
September 2025.
The shift in the macro backdrop has weighed on gold, with higher rate expectations and a
stronger dollar reducing its appeal. The equity sell-off may also have prompted some investors
to sell gold to raise cash.
We expect gold to remain vulnerable while rate expectations and the dollar remain elevated.
Central bank demand remains an important longer-term support, with China adding 26 tonnes
to its reserves in October. But for now, macro forces are likely to set the direction.
