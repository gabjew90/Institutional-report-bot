Mind the gap 
Since 2007, the US economy has grown by 49%, compared with 22% for the euro area. 
Demographics explain part of that divergence, but the widening productivity gap raises 
more questions. During the pandemic, US income support cushioned the financial cost of 
job losses, while almost no effort was made to protect jobs through short-time work 
schemes. Unlike in the US, European furlough schemes kept workers in their jobs and 
preserved employment relationships. However, this may have unduly slowed hiring in ex-
panding sectors, likely compounded by less flexible labour market laws. The energy shock 
following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine has added to the growing divergence. Higher import 
prices eroded European purchasing power, while the US benefited from its position as a 
net energy exporter. Closing the productivity and growth gap will require tighter political 
coordination and painful reforms, however, Europe’s increasingly fragmented political 
landscape suggests the opposite. 
Meanwhile in China, the K-shaped economy is in full swing: robust tech exports offset 
weak domestic demand. Unlike in 2024, the growth momentum is strong enough to hit 
the 4.5% target without stimulus. Still, China has room to ramp up investment next year if 
and when external demand falters. Absent more policy support, subdued earnings are 
likely to persist—keeping our view on Chinese equities neutral for now.  
In the equity space, a heavy sector rotation has seen defensives outperform cyclicals to 
an extent that normally happens during a 20% correction, though the S&P 500 is down 
only 6%. As a result, defensives’ valuations are now stretched. In a relative allocation we 
would still prefer quality. We would also caution against dipping into the market too early 
as the cycle moves into its later stages.

Global Macro 
Multiple reasons for European – US growth divergence 
Economic growth in Europe and the US has been diverging since the global financial 
crisis (GFC), giving rise to the notion of US “exceptionalism”. The US corporate sector 
has been extremely dynamic in recent years, while European countries have also been 
exceptionally unlucky, with repeated energy shocks hitting them hard. Policy choices 
— particularly during the pandemic, but also the UK's decision to leave the EU — have 
likely played a role too. In this piece, we compare the economic performance of the 
US, UK, Switzerland and the euro area since the GFC and show some stylized facts 
that explain their divergent paths. Higher energy prices and less flexible labour mar-
kets appear to be key factors behind Europe’s underperformance. 
 
Since the beginning of the global financial crisis (GFC), the US has outgrown most other 
countries (Exhibit 1). Since 2007 its economy has expanded by 49%, while that of the euro 
area grew by 22%, Switzerland by 43% and the UK by 30%. Partly, this reflects different 
demographic patterns and higher immigration into the US. However, labour productivity – 
measured as GDP per total hours worked – also grew a lot faster in the US than in Europe, 
in particular since the pandemic (Exhibit 2). Yet, productivity within Europe was not homo-
geneous either—productivity in Switzerland grew at a similar rate to that of the US, clearly 
outperforming the euro area and UK. Interestingly, this happened during a period in which 
the US dollar and the Swiss franc appreciated versus the euro and the British pound in 
real terms, suggesting that the currency strength was a reflection of their relatively strong 
domestic economies (Exhibit 3). 
 
 
One obvious reason for the more recent economic divergence between the US and Europe 
has been the war in Ukraine. Besides weighing on consumer confidence, the war has in-
creased energy and in particular gas prices much more in Europe than in the US (Exhibit 
4). Even if the US economy is more energy intensive (1.36 kWh/1$ GDP in the US vs 0.87 
kWh/1$ GDP in Germany), it has been a net energy exporter since 2019. Europe, however, 
relies heavily on imported energy. This has weighed on its terms of trade – the ratio of a 
country's export to import prices – and so its real national income. Between January 2020 
and July 2026, Germany's terms of trade fell by 1%, while those of the US rose by 10%.

Another reason for the diverging productivity growth paths likely reflects the different pol-
icy reactions during Covid. All governments spent heavily over this period, but the way they 
supported their respective economies differed significantly. The US helped unemployed 
workers by initially adding USD 600 to their weekly benefits. Large stimulus checks to 
households were sent on top. However, almost no effort was made to protect jobs. As a 
result, the unemployment rate shot up from 3.5% in February 2020 to a peak of 14.8% in 
April 2020 (Exhibit 5). Hence, a large part of the workforce lost their jobs and had to find 
new ones. This reshuffled the labour market and might have built the base for the follow-
ing productivity boom. Unemployed workers eventually found employment in growing and 
more productive sectors of the economy, which also paid better, supporting stronger con-
sumption. The availability of workers was one factor supporting faster structural changes 
and the subsequent AI investment surge.  
 
 
European countries followed the script that was considered to have been successful dur-
ing the GFC – protecting jobs through short-time work schemes (Kurzarbeit). This pro-
tected existing jobs with the aim of preventing a loss of human capital. It also helped to 
limit and delay the rise in unemployment. In the UK, the ILO-unemployment rate rose from 
4.1% in February 2020 to a peak of 5.3% in November 2020, in Switzerland from 4.2% in 
Q1 2020 to 5.3% in Q1 2021 and in the euro area from 7.3% in February 2020 to 8.1% in 
April 2021. Furlough and short-time work measures were quickly unwound in Switzerland 
and the UK. As a result, unemployment peaked at an earlier date in these countries than 
in the euro area, where a mix of national and European measures provided support for 
non-working employees. This has had far-reaching consequences. Preserving existing

Other deeply entrenched factors are likely at play as well, such as the size of markets, the 
availability and type of financing, institutions, and risk culture. The good news is that many 
of these issues are well documented and have been presented to policymakers in com-
prehensive reports, such as the one directed by Mario Draghi and published in September 
2024. The problem is that the recommendations are not easy to implement: scaling them 
up requires coordination among like-minded policymakers. Fragmented parliaments and 
the rise of populist leaders only make the necessary reforms harder to push through – 
which suggests the productivity gap is more likely to widen further than to close.

China macro and equity 
Strong exports imply no urgency for stimulus 
China’s K-shaped economy remains intact in the latest data release: strong tech ex-
ports vs. weak domestic demand. Unlike in 2024, when the Chinese government 
launched a stimulus package at the end of Q3, the economy today has enough mo-
mentum to meet the annual growth target of 4.5%. With government bond issuance 
largely backloaded this year and the September lending measure remaining unused, 
China has room to ramp up investment next year if and when external demand falters. 
But no government urgency to support domestic demand implies a continuation of the 
subdued earnings outlook, keeping us neutral on Chinese equities. More stringent US 
export controls of advanced chips do not help. 
 
China’s K-shaped economy remains intact in the latest data release: strong tech exports 
vs. weak domestic demand. While industrial production growth at 4.8% in October slowed 
from 5.5% in September, hi-tech manufacturing accelerated to 17.6% yoy (Exhibit 1). The 
global AI boom remains an important pillar for China’s near-term growth, keeping export 
growth robust (Exhibit 2). Industrial profits also mirror the K-shaped economy with sectors 
that have benefited from high global prices (electronics, chemicals and mining) enjoying 
solid profit growth, while domestically oriented sectors continue to suffer (Exhibit 3). 
 
 
Despite some easing measures in late September, indicators in October look worse with 
falling fixed asset investment and barely growing retail sales. Service production growth 
held up at 4.6%, however, providing some relief on the consumption side. Only fixed asset 
investment in computer services, likely driven by the data centre drive, has shown robust 
5% growth year to date (Exhibit 4). A large part of the retail sales weakness comes from 
auto sales (Exhibit 5). 
 
Still, even without more government support, GDP growth this year should easily meet 
4.5% (target range 4.5%-5%). This is unlike in 2024, when year-to-date growth was at 4.8% 
by Q3 and the government launched a stimulus package to meet the 5% growth target 
(Exhibit 6). With the government bond quota largely backloaded and the availability of the 
People’s Bank of China’s lending instrument for infrastructure projects, China has policy 
room to accelerate investment next year. Ramping up investment will not be easy, how-
ever, as officials remain cautious ahead of the Party Congress in late 2027.

Consistent with industrial profits data, the earnings outlook for Chinese equities has been 
lacklustre. The vast majority of the domestic economy has hardly benefited from tech ex-
ports and continues to be subject to downward pressure from lower income growth and a 
lack of confidence. The earnings outlook for China remains the worst among Asia Pacific 
countries (Exhibit 7). In 2026, the MSCI China index was down by 20.7% (as of 18 Nov), 
making it the worst-performing major equity index year to date. Within Asia, China has 
underperformed the broad Asia Pacific index by 25.4 percentage points in USD terms. 
 
We continue to maintain a neutral stance on Chinese equities at the index level. To turn 
more positive, we need to see more decisive policy from Beijing, which could be in the 
form of a strong stimulus to support domestic consumption and/or to put a floor under 
the ailing housing market. Following the selloffs in July and October-November, the valu-
ations for Chinese equities have become much lower (Exhibit 8). At a forward price-to-
earnings ratio of 9.4x, China equities trade well below its historical average of about 11x, 
but they have not reached the extreme levels of previous downturns (at or below 8x).  
 
 
The recent significant tightening of the US export controls on advanced AI chips will likely 
weigh on Chinese AI labs in the near term due to further constraints on access to compute. 
At the same time, it may benefit some selected players in China’s domestic semiconductor 
industry, which will likely see stronger demand and continue to enjoy the government’s 
policy support.

US equities 
Dip buyers beware 
Equity markets have undergone some heavy rotation over recent weeks. Defensives 
have outperformed cyclicals to an extent typically only seen in 20%+ market correc-
tions, yet the S&P 500 is only down 6% from its peak. Defensives’ valuations are now 
trading at the absolute top of the range over past decades, making them tactically 
less attractive, despite significant headwinds to the cycle. They provide less portfolio 
protection than they did over recent weeks. The widening of credit spreads has broad-
ened, bank lending conditions are tightening for the first time since 2024 and the Fed 
has no leeway to provide support as inflationary pressures are likely to remain ele-
vated until the end of Q1 2027. 
 
The market has been in consolidation mode over recent weeks. This has been notable at 
the index level, with the S&P 500 more than 6% below its peak and 4% below pre-election 
levels, but even more so beneath the surface. Notably, cyclical sectors (industrials, mate-
rials, consumer discretionary) have underperformed defensive sectors (utilities, consumer 
staples, health care) by over 12% in the past three months. Over the past ten years, this 
has only happened twice, in March 2020 and in July 2022, which in both cases marked a 
market trough. Yet what makes the recent defensive outperformance different is the fact 
that index-level moves were much more muted and most of the relative performance was 
driven by defensives moving higher, rather than cyclicals moving lower (Exhibit 1). Defen-
sives have re-rated sharply and are now trading at the absolute top of their historical 
range, at 19x 12-month forward EPS. Cyclicals’ valuations at the same time have fallen 
somewhat, leaving them more attractive on a relative basis, yet not really priced for a 
slowdown (Exhibit 2). 
 
 
This leaves us with a situation in which defensives are no longer attractive, at least tacti-
cally, while cyclicals are not yet priced for the macro backdrop which may lie ahead. In 
terms of tactical positioning, a reduction of defensive exposure certainly makes sense. 
With regard to our preferences, this would mean less exposure to consumer staples, util-
ities and Swiss equities, which we all have on “most preferred”. Tech and all its sub-sec-
tors (semis, software and hardware) rank better on our EPS revisions/valuation matrix 
(Exhibit 3), yet we would only add to it on a relative basis and in the highest quality names.

Furthermore, we remain fairly cautious on banks despite their recent underperformance. 
Valuations are not compelling despite the softness in the sector (Exhibit 4). We also think 
that the best days for banks are behind us for the current cycle. We continue to expect the 
cycle to slow, with events and data over recent weeks reinforcing this view.  
 
 
There are various reasons to expect the cycle to slow and keep equity performance in 
check. One notable observation over the past month is that credit spreads have not only 
continued to widen in the CCC space, but in the broader high-yield market as well (Exhibit 
5). Back then it was the result of an idiosyncratic shock, while it comes on the back of 
deteriorating fundamentals and renewed monetary tightening today. A quick reversal in 
spreads thus appears less likely than it was in April 2025.  
 
 
There are two channels through which higher spreads affect equities: i) risk premia and ii) 
lending conditions. Risk premia in equities are still close to their lowest in years. Even 
though the equity risk premium over Treasury yields has risen from zero to 50bps over 
recent weeks, it remains at historically depressed levels (Exhibit 6). We would expect more 
of a risk premium to be priced into equity markets as credit spreads continue to rise. This 
could either happen via lower equity valuations or via lower Treasury yields. What it effec-
tively means is that a rise in credit spreads would reduce the attractiveness of equities 
relative to high-quality bonds.

The second channel through which credit spreads weigh on equities is the lending chan-
nel. The Fed’s senior loan officer survey shows that in Q3, US banks actually tightened 
lending conditions for businesses for the first time since 2024 (Exhibit 7). This followed 
the aforementioned rise in CCC spreads. Banks appear to see the same deterioration in 
credit quality as the credit market does. Anecdotal evidence also suggests that banks 
have become more prudent in their lending decisions and are increasingly trying to offload 
data centre risks from their balance sheets (LINK). This adds to our more cautious view 
on banks. High valuations, a tightening of lending conditions and some cracks in Q3 re-
porting all suggest that banks are likely to see some consolidation before performance 
returns. 
 
 
Lastly, what acted as a support factor from mid-2024 until late last year was easing mon-
etary policy. This has changed. The Fed started to raise rates again in September and will 
likely continue to do so over the coming months. If anything, inflationary pressures have 
risen and broadened in the October CPI data, which has been acknowledged by FOMC 
members. Given that core CPI data tends to lag the cycle by around 10 months, inflation-
ary pressures are unlikely to ease before the end of Q1 2027 (Exhibit 8). Tightening mon-
etary policy, higher credit spreads and tighter bank lending conditions are all factors which 
are typical for the later stages of a cycle. This does not mean that the cycle will come to a 
standstill, but it will most likely slow significantly over coming quarters. In this kind of en-
vironment, cyclical sectors and cyclical market segments may provide tactical opportuni-
ties but are unlikely to generate sustained performance until the cycle bottoms.
