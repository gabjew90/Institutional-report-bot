Our view
We continue to view AI as a Transformational Innovation 
Opportunity, supported by a solid 2027 capex outlook. 
Robust compute demand, tight capacity, and projects under 
construction should underpin spending next year, even 
as hyperscaler capex growth slows from an exceptionally 
high base. That said, visibility into 2028 is lower. As 
the cycle matures, spending will face higher monetization 
and return scrutiny, while financing and refinancing costs 
could pressure capital-intensive projects. Power availability, 
grid access, and data center permitting may also delay 
deployments.
Politics could add uncertainty. Geopolitical developments 
and growing US scrutiny of data center development, 
including potential policy challenges in states with 
Democratic gains and other local jurisdictions, could delay 
or reshape projects. We therefore believe it is prudent to 
manage the risk of disappointing AI capex growth beyond 
2027 rather than extrapolate the recent acceleration.
Lower visibility into 2028 points to slower forecast AI 
capex growth, though investment remains historically high. 
Deceleration could affect expectations across the AI value 
chain, especially enablers whose earnings and valuations are 
most tied to capacity additions. These span the compute 
semiconductor supply chain and broader data center 
ecosystem, including accelerators, networking, servers, 
power, cooling, and other infrastructure. Infrastructure 
operators and AI adopters, by contrast, may benefit 
more from improving utilization and monetization as the 
installed base expands. We remain constructive on AI's 
transformational opportunity, but favor greater selectivity 
and active management of downside risk from slower-than-

Rising uncertainty on 2028 global AI spending
AI capital spending remains resilient despite tech volatility 
and greater scrutiny of returns over past few quarters. 
Stronger hyperscaler spending commitments and robust 
AI compute demand, as indicated by NVIDIA in its latest 
earnings call, support continued expansion. In our ”AI 
quick pulse: Funding the next three trillion in AI capex“ 
publication, we forecast global AI capex of USD 1.0tr in 
2026—the first year above the USD 1tr threshold—rising 
to USD 1.4tr in 2027 and USD 1.5tr in 2028. Continued 
infrastructure buildout and strong compute demand should 
keep the investment pool substantial as the cycle matures.
Despite this scale and extended investment cycle, our 
forecasts imply AI capex growth slowing from approximately 
40% in 2027 to around 8% in 2028, shifting the 
debate from spending levels to the sustainability of growth 
and returns. Further downside could arise from greater 
monetization and return requirements, tighter financing or 
refinancing conditions, power and grid-access bottlenecks, 
data center delays, or rising geopolitical and policy 
uncertainty. These risks do not undermine our structural 
AI thesis but reinforce the need to manage a slower-than-
forecast capex growth path as the cycle progresses.

Higher monetization and return requests hurdles for 
AI spending
The scale of AI investment and its financing burden 
should sharpen investor and company scrutiny of adoption, 
monetization, and sustainable returns. As capex growth 
decelerates, we expect the AI trade to continue shifting in 
2027 from infrastructure investment toward monetization 
and adoption, rewarding companies that visibly convert AI 
investment into revenues and earnings. Hyper-scalers could 
be key beneficiaries, given their established platforms, large 
customer bases, and multiple monetization channels. That 
said, as the cycle matures and cumulative investment rises, 
further spending will face higher hurdles, increasing the 
emphasis on tangible adoption and credible returns rather 
than investment commitments alone.
Enterprise AI adoption and evidence of productivity gains are 
broadening, though meaningful earnings benefits remain 
nascent. During 3Q26 earnings season, 13% of S&P 500 
companies quantified AI-driven productivity benefits in use 
cases such as coding or customer support, but only 3% 
quantified earnings impacts. Accelerating enterprise AI 
spending suggests these benefits should become clearer 
in 2027. We remain constructive on the longer-term 
productivity opportunity, including in physically intensive 
businesses where AI can improve labor productivity, asset 
utilization, and operating efficiency with relatively lower 
disruption risk. To broaden and sustain the AI trade's 
momentum, investors will increasingly require proof that 
adoption and productivity gains translate into faster, more 
visible monetization, and earnings growth.

Financing conditions could tighten
The scale of AI investment is increasing reliance on external 
financing. Hyperscaler capex has exceeded operating cash 
flow, and free cash flow is expected to become more 
negative as investment remains elevated. After years of 
largely funding AI infrastructure internally, hyperscalers 
increasingly finance incremental spending through debt, 
equity, leases, and supplier-backed financing. Substantial 
lease and purchase commitments add sensitivity to financing 
costs and credit availability, reinforcing the importance 
of monetization and cash flow generation as the cycle 
progresses.

Neoclouds face greater refinancing risk because their 
capital-intensive business models and funding access are 
more sensitive to credit conditions and investor risk appetite. 
Tighter financing could delay projects, curb capacity 
expansion or increase stress on leveraged operators and 
suppliers. However, we see limited direct risk to aggregate AI 
capex: as noted earlier, the largest hyper-scalers and SpaceX 
account for roughly 80% of forecast 2027 spending, versus 
around 8% for neoclouds. We therefore view worsening 
neocloud financing mainly as a risk of selective project delays 
and tighter financial conditions across the AI ecosystem—
not, at this stage, a trigger for a broad global AI capex 
downturn. Still, investors should closely monitor AI project 
financing conditions over the next few years.
Political risks in approvals, grid access and geopolitics
Political and regulatory uncertainty could constrain US AI 
infrastructure expansion. Post-midterm Democratic gains 
in states with substantial planned data center capacity 
could increase scrutiny of permitting, grid connections, tax 
incentives, and water and energy use, risking delays and 
higher development costs. Already-approved projects are 
likely to proceed, limiting near-term disruption, but delays to 
planned capacity could shift some 2028 deployments into 
2029. We therefore see post-midterm policy uncertainty as a 
potential headwind to 2028 AI capex growth, extending the 
investment cycle and shifting spending's timing or location 
rather than undermining structural AI compute demand.

Greater scrutiny from Democrat-led local governments 
could shift the data center debate from capital availability 
toward resource availability, particularly power and water. 
In Texas, for example, tighter review of data center 
grid connections, proposed by Governor-elect Hinojosa, 
suggests that power infrastructure could become a 
meaningful constraint on AI project timelines. Developers 
and hyperscalers may therefore need to shoulder more of 
the investment required to upgrade grids and supporting 
utility infrastructure. More stringent government review 
of infrastructure spending, however, could slow these 
upgrades and ultimately constrain the pace of AI capex 
growth.
Geopolitical restrictions could weigh on global AI capex. If 
broadly implemented and enforced, limits on Chinese users' 
remote access to overseas data centers for AI training could 
curb China's contribution to global compute spending. 
Tighter controls on chips, cloud access and cross-border 
infrastructure could fragment markets, lower utilization in 
affected locations and increase investment uncertainty. US

Potential pressure on North Asian semiconductors and 
hardware names amid 2028 AI capex uncertainty
Moderating 2028 AI capex growth warrant managing 
downside risks in the AI spending cycle. We do not expect a 
reversal of the structural AI investment trend, and spending 
should remain substantial. That said, the risk balance is 
becoming less favorable as the cycle matures: expectations 
are elevated, incremental spending faces higher hurdles, and 
investment disappointments could disproportionately affect 
markets most exposed to AI infrastructure. We thus take 
a more neutral view at the current stage on North Asian 
markets dominated by AI-related semiconductor and data 
center exposure, particularly South Korea and Taiwan, while 
remaining selective on leaders with durable competitive 
advantages.
Strong memory pricing earlier this year supported Korean 
memory leaders' earnings, but could moderate or reverse as 
supply responds and AI capex growth normalizes. Shortage-
driven memory price hikes accounted for over 50% of the 
global AI capex increase in 2026.

Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix's dominant positions in 
global high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and conventional 
DDR memory supply strengthen their pricing power across 
both product lines amid shortages. Leading Korean memory 
makers' substantial capacity expansion in 2027 could ease 
shortages just as AI capex growth moderates, increasing the 
risk of a pricing reversal. Lower memory prices could, in 
turn, dampen nominal capex growth without an equivalent 
decline in compute demand. Korea's substantial memory-
cycle exposure means weaker pricing could amplify the 
earnings impact of slower AI infrastructure demand. We 
maintain our Neutral view on Korea but still favor leading 
memory maker SK Hynix, which holds the leading position 
in HBM .
Taiwan’s semiconductors and technology hardware account 
for more than 85% of its benchmark, leaving it highly 
sensitive to global AI capex trends. We still expect solid 
earnings growth of around 60% in 2027, but visibility into 
2028 has deteriorated. After four years of rapid expansion, 
moderating AI capex growth and the risks discussed above 
could weigh on spending and earnings expectations.

Slower AI investment, normalizing component prices or 
earnings downgrades could have an outsized market 
impact. With expectations elevated after strong earnings 
revisions, risk-reward looks less favorable beyond 2027. 
We therefore downgrade Taiwan from Attractive to 
Neutral, favoring selective, bottom-up opportunities in 
subsector leaders with durable competitive advantages, 
strong execution and pricing power.
Within Taiwanese semiconductors, we favor AI supply-
chain leaders with strong competitive positions: foundry 
leader TSMC, ASIC vendor MediaTek and OSAT supplier ASE 
Technology. Their leadership in their respective segments 
should provide greater resilience as AI spending growth 
moderates.

applies.
CIO Selected Designation and CIO Selection Lists
CIO Selected stocks are intended to support the UBS House View (see global asset class preferences definitions) and are 
chosen based on their overall risk-return profiles and other relevant considerations. The starting universe for the CIO Selected 
designation generally consists of securities covered by IB Global Research and rated Buy or Neutral. From that universe, CIO 
Equities applies its own investment judgment, considering a variety of factors such as the fundamental outlook, expected 
performance relative to an identified benchmark, market, sector and industry trends, risk and return characteristics, downside 
considerations, diversification benefits, and other relevant factors. No single factor is necessarily determinative. Rather, CIO 
considers these factors collectively and holistically in determining which securities warrant inclusion in a CIO Equity Selection 
List.
In certain circumstances, CIO may designate a security that is not covered by IB Global Research as CIO Selected where 
CIO determines that the security presents a relevant investment opportunity and applicable eligibility, reasonable-basis and 
approval requirements for such selections have been satisfied. A security will no longer be eligible for inclusion in any CIO 
Selection List if IB Global Research changes its rating to Sell. In such cases, CIO Equities will remove it from applicable 
Selection Lists within a reasonable timeframe.
