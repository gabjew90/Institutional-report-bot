Balanced By Design: AI optimism and market
caution
Tom Record, co-portfolio manager of Fidelity World and Global Special Situations,
considers the competing forces influencing markets, as investors weigh up the
extraordinary potential of AI against the uncertainties of the investment cycle. He
explains why a balanced investment approach is particularly relevant in today’s
uncertain environment.
PRO
Tom Record Global 19/11/2026 4 min read
Key points
For much of the past year, I have been making a similar point when speaking to clients: the range of
potential market outcomes is unusually wide, conviction in any single scenario is difficult, and
maintaining a balanced portfolio makes a great deal of sense. The market experience in 2026 has
reinforced that view. Extraordinary rotations between investment styles, sectors and sub-sectors have, at
times, overwhelmed the incremental gains generated through careful stock selection. But increasingly,
I've found myself asking whether the balance of risks is beginning to shift.
The optimist in me sees enormous opportunities emerging from AI, with rapid technological progress
and the potential to transform large parts of the global economy. The pessimist sees an investment
cycle increasingly dependent on extraordinary levels of spending, access to financing and the
availability of power.
While AI revenues are growing and successful use cases are broadening, the next phase of the
investment cycle presents challenges, such as funding, power availability and planning constraints.
Investors must navigate an increasingly complex economic backdrop, characterised by substantial
government deficits, rising bond yields, inflationary pressures and geopolitical uncertainty.
Balanced investing isn't about sitting on the fence, but rather recognising that conviction in
companies doesn't require certainty about markets.

AI: an extraordinary opportunity, but at what cost?
AI represents one of the most significant technological developments of our generation. However,
delivering that opportunity requires extraordinary investment, at levels that invite comparisons with
previous technology booms.
Of course, today's leading technology companies are substantially more profitable, consumers have the
means to access AI already, and adoption is occurring at a much faster rate. Perhaps we should expect
investment to be more concentrated as a result. But is this time genuinely different, or is the same
investment cycle simply playing out at greater speed? I think it’s important to challenge our perspectives
of the status quo, so that we can more easily identify changes that we have already anticipated.
History tells us that even transformative technologies can experience periods of overinvestment. The
internet ultimately fulfilled much of its early promise, but that didn't prevent a painful adjustment along
the way. AI could follow a similar path. The technology can be enormously successful without every
supporting investment delivering an attractive return.
Encouragingly, AI revenues are growing, the successful use cases of applications are broadening and
costs are falling. But the debate is shifting from whether demand will materialise to whether the
required infrastructure can be delivered at the anticipated pace.
While the largest technology companies generate enormous cash flows, other businesses involved in
building AI infrastructure depend heavily on external funding. As borrowing becomes more expensive,
or lenders’ concentration limits max out, some projects may become uneconomic, with consequences for
demand across the wider supply chain.
Power availability and planning constraints present further challenges. The brakes on AI investment may
ultimately be applied not by West Coast technology executives who decide to slow down, but by East
Coast lenders who decide that they have enough risk in datacentre buildout. Importantly, a slowdown in
infrastructure spending wouldn't necessarily undermine AI's long-term success. Lower costs could
ultimately make AI more affordable and accelerate adoption. However, this could also put pressure on
less competitive datacentre operators and create a more disinflationary environment for associated
equipment and services.
We can be optimistic about AI's long-term future while remaining cautious about the shorter-term
investment cycle. The two views are not contradictory.
A market of competing forces
Beyond AI, the wider economic backdrop is hardly straightforward. Government deficits remain
substantial, bond yields have risen from exceptionally low levels, and inflationary pressures persist.
Higher energy costs, geopolitical uncertainty and changing trade relationships create further
complexity.
The US midterm elections have added to this. Growing political opposition to datacentre development
could introduce further constraints on infrastructure expansion, adding another layer of uncertainty to an
already demanding investment cycle. Even if we could predict the direction of interest rates or

economic growth, translating those forecasts into successful investment decisions is another matter
entirely.
The sharp rotations we've witnessed throughout 2026 illustrate the challenge. Investors have moved
rapidly between growth and value, technology and traditional sectors, often with little change in the
underlying prospects of individual businesses. For fundamental stock pickers, these shifts can
temporarily overwhelm the benefits of getting individual company decisions right. Attempting to
anticipate the next rotation is tempting, but notoriously difficult.
Why balance still matters
In the current environment, I find myself cautious about sections of the AI investment cycle where growth
depends on continued access to financing. But that doesn't diminish our conviction in the long-term
opportunity. Instead, it reinforces the importance of maintaining selectivity and breadth across the
portfolio.
Our balanced approach brings together businesses with different investment characteristics, spanning
the spectrum of investment styles. Rather than attempting to predict which style or sector will outperform
next, we focus on identifying attractive individual companies without becoming overly dependent on any
single market outcome. Over my investment career, I have seen opportunities when taking a contrarian
pessimistic or optimistic view has paid off richly - but now is not one of those times.
We want sufficient exposure to benefit if AI continues to exceed expectations, while maintaining
resilience should the investment cycle become more challenging. And while AI is the focus of this article,
it is important to remember that the portfolio is much broader, with a diverse range of businesses and
investment opportunities whose prospects are driven by very different factors. Balanced investing does
not mean an absence of conviction. We can have considerable conviction in individual companies while
recognising that our ability to predict today’s markets and investment cycles is much more limited.
The optimist in me continues to see enormous potential in AI, while the more cautious part recognises
that even extraordinary technological progress cannot eliminate the realities of shorter-term investment
cycles. And perhaps that's precisely why balance remains so relevant. Being balanced isn't about sitting
on the fence, but rather recognising that conviction in companies doesn't require certainty about
markets.
