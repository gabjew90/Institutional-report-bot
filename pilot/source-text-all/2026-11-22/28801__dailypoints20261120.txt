Canadian trade pragmatism—what it could mean, and the opposing forces 
The ANT got squished took the whole AI trade down with it. That’s one possible 
interpretation of what transpired in the wake of the biggest IPO ever. Anthropic initially 
rallied well above the $83 issuance price yesterday morning before settling back toward a 
more modest gain by day’s end. It’s down sharply in today’s pre-market. At no point did 
broader equities share in any enthusiasm which is perhaps a stark signal on the durability 
of the tech trade; recall that when SpaceX debuted in June, it rallied for a few days and 
coincided with general risk-on sentiment including across tech. Not this time. As Jay 
notes, there is more interest in Grand Theft Auto than Anthropic (chart 1).  
After the S&P and Nasdaq both fell by 2%+ yesterday, futures are tracking a further 
decline in the vicinity of 1–2% declines this morning. TSX futures are also down by just 
under 1%. European cash markets are similarly lower. 
Sovereign bonds are picking up the safe haven flows. Yields are down across the board in 
the US, Canada, and all of Europe. Oil is a touch lower but Trump is deferring talk on what 
to do with Iran until next month; my guess is he doesn’t want to rile up the GOP during the 
lame duck session when he needs them for other things. Gold is down another $60/oz. 
The dollar isn’t really benefiting as most major currencies are up against it. 
CANADIAN RETAIL SALES MOMENTUM MAY BE STALLING, BUT IT’S EARLY 
Canadian retail sales were up by 3.9% q/q SAAR in Q4 but are tentatively tracking just shy 
of a -2% q/q SAAR contraction in Q4 (chart 2). A lot of Q4 data still lies ahead including 
the key holiday season. 
On a monthly basis, volumes were up by only 0.1% m/m SA in September. Nominal sales 
were up 0.8% (0.7% initial guidance) and revised up in August to 1.4% m/m (from 1.2%). 
October’s preliminary flash nominal guidance was -1.5% m/m SA; while we don’t get any 
details, our tracking of prices suggests this was driven by lower volumes. CPI-gas, for 
instance, was up 1.9% m/m SA in October.  
UK CONSUMERS ARE ALIVE AND KICKING 
UK retail sales volumes ex-gasoline beat expectations. October was up 0.9% m/m SA 
(0.1% consensus) with only a partial assist from a downward revision that presented a 
lower than previously understood starting point (-0.9% m/m instead of -0.7%). There has 
been positive momentum since 2024, but at a fairly tepid pace (chart 3).

TRUMP’S PRESSER UNLIKELY TO REVEAL MUCH 
Trump holds a presser at 12pmET today to sum up his Asia trip. Various reports indicate no 
breakthroughs in talks with Putin and Xi Jinping. Generalities about seeking to undermine the 
UN through reforms and doing likewise against the International Criminal Court reflect 
disturbingly common ground between the three. By all accounts, there was only rhetoric absent 
substantive agreement on key issues like Ukraine, Iran, US-China trade, etc. 
CANADIAN TRADE PRAGMATISM 
Dominic LeBlanc—the Canadian government’s lead on the trade file—floated a trial balloon in 
yesterday’s interview that sought acceptance for some reduced level of US tariffs against 
Canada. The broad tone had a feeling of moving to stage 5 in Maslow’s stages of grief—
acceptance. He seeks an interim deal that would achieve some relief on Section 232 and Section 
338 tariffs—ideally wiping out the latter and reducing the former—before then perhaps moving 
onto a broader CUSMA renegotiation. He did not expand upon what Canada would offer in 
terms of its retaliatory tariffs, investments, purchases of US goods such as defence companies or anything like that. The clear signal was to 
take what you can get now and keep trying. No firm timeline was set as guidance indicated negotiations may stretch into the new year. It’s 
difficult to fully judge what is being sought since there would be a lot of important bilateral details beneath just the tariff rates. The purpose 
of the interview was clearly to pave the way for an acceptance tone in what lies ahead while making it clear that a deal is not imminently at 
hand. 
Critics may say dig in, as Canadians won’t accept any American tariffs and you don’t want to codify acceptance of tariffs against the 
longstanding spirit of the trade deal since that could make it impossible to unwind in future. They may say you’ve got the US administration 
on the ropes after a long laundry list of failures resulted in being totally wiped out in the midterm elections and potentially on a path to lose 
the Presidency. They may also say hold firm, the cavalry is coming, and that notwithstanding the concentrated pain to a narrow portion of 
the Canadian economy, Canada should sit back and rely on the US courts, Congress and voters to reverse US protectionism. Tariffs are part 
of what is hurting America in terms of affordability and maintaining them could further work against the GOP into 2028. They may say that 
the current unelected US cabinet is in its dying days so fade the rhetoric. 
Others would say get some relief and keep pushing that rock up the hill in order to reduce the 
pain where feasible and keep negotiating. All or nothing has no place in trade negotiations. That 
you can die of old age before the US legal system gets around to doing anything in a system 
whereby the law you get is determined by money and politics. Ignore the insults and lack of 
professionalism from Trump and outsiders like Navarro’s angry tirades and who carries no 
influence. This camp may also argue against relying on the Democrats who had a chance to 
throw some tangible love at Canada but instead shot down Keystone XL and maintained Trump 
1.0 tariffs when Biden was in office. His VP and failed presidential candidate—Kamala Harris—
had voted against USMCA and opposed the TPP and was perhaps at best a more polite 
protectionist than the current administration. They could note that the ‘progressive,’ left-wing 
members of the faction that seems to be steering the Democrats are no friends of trade and 
commerce in general. This camp may also argue that time is of the essence in sectors most 
affected by tariffs and that playing the waiting game may be too costly. This camp could also say 
that Canada can still exact some revenge in its choices on what to buy, from whom, and who to 
include in its investment plans, such that Canada has plenty of negotiating leverage. It may also 
argue that Canada may pivot toward greater Chinese involvement in the Canadian economy as a 
continued evolution away from the US in terms of the falling US share of Canadian exports and falling US share of FDI into Canada as the 
rest of the world marches in.

A still further consideration is that some US tariff pressure on Canada may be necessary in a perverse way. Wiping them away could stall or 
reverse domestic progress in Canada toward changing its ways on many fronts including major projects, tearing down barriers to growth 
and revitalizing its armed forces. This country needs a boot in the tenders in order to exact change, or else nothing gets done. Witness the 
last time around when a collective sigh of relief after the USMCA deal resulted in lost momentum to change things. Canada is a messy 
combination of competing governments and special interest groups that repeatedly thwart change. 
Key is also how provincial governments respond, namely Ontario where much of the affected steel and auto industries are located. 
As for the potential impact of some tariff relief, have a look at chart 4. If wiping out the 338 tariffs can be achieved, then the effective tariff 
rate (ETR) would drop by a little over three percentage points. US tariffs on Canadian energy and potash obviously make no sense and 
penalize Americans perhaps the most of any tariffs so maybe they’re most likely to see a reversal but that’s unknown at this point. Key then 
becomes the 232 tariffs on metals and autos. The domestic blowback in the US against aluminum tariffs when Quebec is a dominant 
provider might do Canada’s work for it. With reductions on auto and steel tariffs, the overall ETR could be meaningfully dropped to within 
the low single digit percentage points. Some would say make the affected firms whole by writing a cheque. 
It’s a political calculus with concentrated significance to a handful of sectors but with modest macroeconomic consequences. Canada has 
performed just fine in the tariffs era. The C$ has adjusted, the rates spread to the US may narrow going forward but Canadian borrowing 
costs are likely to remain lower than in the US, and US growth can continue to create an income-driven pull effect on Canadian exports.
