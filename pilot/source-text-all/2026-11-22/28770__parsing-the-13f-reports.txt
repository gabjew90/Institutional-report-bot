Tech remains central, but positioning is far from uniform

Quarterly 13F filings from major institutional investors, published by the US Securities and Exchange Commission,
provide a retrospective view of long US equity holdings and offer useful insight into hedge fund positioning. While
the filings do not disclose short positions and are published with a delay, they can help identify sector allocation
changes, major portfolio adjustments, new positions and securities that have fallen out of favour. As ever, these
quarterly filings provide a backward-looking snapshot rather than a real-time view of positioning, but they can
nonetheless offer useful insight into how portfolios were evolving through Q3.
The latest filings point to continued differentiation in hedge fund positioning. Technology saw the largest increase
in reported sector allocation during the quarter, yet hedge funds remain substantially underweight the sector
relative to the S&P 500. Beneath the aggregate figures, individual manager activity also reveals considerable
dispersion, with significant buying and selling across some of the same names. At the end of the quarter, assets
under management across the hedge fund industry stood at approximately USD 3.8 trillion.
Today we review hedge fund holdings at the end of Q3 2026. We look at aggregate sector positioning, active weights
relative to the S&P 500, changes made by selected leading managers and screens of the largest holdings, additions
and exits. We also include an update on insider activity, another commonly monitored indicator of “smart money”
positioning.
Setting the scene: Q3 was all about Tech volatility, higher financing costs and market rotation
It was in Q3 that we saw markets really start to challenge the momentum behind the AI trade that has driven
markets in recent years. July’s sharp reversal in AI-linked stocks coincided with greater scrutiny of valuations, AI
capex, funding requirements and the returns expected from the build-out. At the same time, Middle East conflict
and disruption to energy flows pushed oil prices higher, adding to inflation concerns and upward pressure on
interest rates and bond yields. Higher financing costs weighed particularly on rate-sensitive and smaller companies,
contributing to increasingly uneven market leadership.
Markets nevertheless avoided a broad retreat from risk. The Tech trade recovered in August as resilient activity and
earnings revived interest in the sector, before higher energy costs and yields drove another rotation in September.
The result was considerable dispersion across regions and sectors, with AI-exposed markets and companies
remaining particularly sensitive to changing expectations around valuations and the returns from investment.
For hedge funds, the quarter was therefore characterised more by rotation and selectivity than by a uniform risk-off
move. Macro strategies, particularly trend-following CTAs and commodity-oriented funds, benefited from the
volatility, while long-short technology strategies also remained among the stronger equity-hedge sub-strategies. 
So which strategies performed best and worst in Q3 2026?
Hedge funds lagged US equities in Q3, with the HFRX Global Hedge Fund Index down 0.8% versus a 2.0% gain for
the S&P 500. Beneath the aggregate, however, performance varied considerably, with the strongest gains
concentrated in a relatively narrow group of strategies.
As illustrated below, Cryptocurrency strategies led by a wide margin, returning 25.6% over the quarter, as Bitcoin
gained around 44% - and despite the rise in Treasury yields. Commodity Hedge (+7.2%), Activist (+7.1%) and CTAs
(+6.0%) also performed strongly. Overall, the quarter favoured strategies able to capture clear directional moves,
including the sharp repricing in oil and rates, while activist strategies benefited from company-specific catalysts.
At the other end, Distressed Debt was the weakest strategy, falling 7.1%, while Fixed Income Arbitrage (−1.2%) and
Equity L/S (−0.6%) also lost ground. Rising yields challenged rate-sensitive positions, while sharp reversals in
Technology and AI-related stocks complicated long/short equity positioning. Most other strategies were modestly

positive or close to flat, reinforcing the quarter’s key message: performance was selective, with clear market trends
creating opportunities while distressed and rate-sensitive strategies struggled:

Hedge funds increased Technology allocations during Q3
In terms of reported long US equity holdings, by far the largest increase in sector allocation over the quarter was in
Tech, at 0.9 percentage points. Healthcare and Energy followed at 0.3 points, while Communication Services and
Financials each gained 0.2 points.
The largest reductions were in Industrials, down 0.8 percentage points, and Consumer Discretionary, down 0.5
points. Materials declined by 0.2 points, while Real Estate, Utilities and Consumer Staples were broadly unchanged.
At first sight, this points to a clear increase in Tech exposure. However, changes in sector weights reflect both
portfolio transactions and changes in the relative market value of existing holdings. With the S&P 500 Information
Technology index gaining 7% in Q3, part of the 0.9-point increase is likely to reflect market performance rather than
net purchases alone.
The important distinction, therefore, is that while Technology may have gained weight within reported long
portfolios during the quarter, but the data do not tell us how much of that increase resulted from active buying. This
becomes particularly relevant when we compare hedge fund positioning with Technology’s growing weight in the
S&P 500.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 19/11/2026.

Overall sector differentiation moderated slightly in Q3
Aggregate active weight measures how far hedge fund sector allocations differ overall from those of the S&P 500, and
as illustrated below, after rising from 27.0% at the end of 2025 to 31.1% in Q1 and 33.6% in Q2, it eased to 32.3% in Q3.
In other words, reported hedge fund long allocations moved slightly closer to the benchmark during the quarter, but
the change was modest. Aggregate active weight also remains close to its highest level since 2012 and well above the
levels seen between 2017 and 2025. Moreover, the modest convergence masks important differences at the sector
level, with Technology a notable exception to the broader trend as the gap between hedge fund and S&P 500
Technology weights remained particularly large.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 19/11/2026.
Technology allocations rose, but remained well below benchmark weight
Technology may have gained weight in hedge fund long portfolios during Q3, but at 22.4% the allocation remained
well below the sector’s significant 39.7% weight in the S&P 500, resulting in a 17.3 percentage-point underweight, by
far the largest relative sector position in either direction as illustrated below. Consumer Staples and Communication
Services were also underweight, but by much smaller amounts of 1.4 and 0.9 points respectively.
On the other side, Healthcare recorded the largest overweight at 3.1 percentage points, followed by Industrials at 2.3
points, Materials at 2.0 points and Financials at 1.6 points. Consumer Discretionary, Energy and Real Estate were
also moderately overweight.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 19/11/2026.
The Technology underweight has become increasingly structural
The gap between hedge fund Technology allocations and the benchmark weight has been widening since 2022, and
while Tech recorded the largest increase in reported hedge fund sector allocation in Q3, the gap with the S&P 500
widened further.
As illustrated by the line in the chart below, before 2022, hedge fund allocations to the Tech sector generally ranged
from neutral to around 6 percentage points below the S&P 500. Since the release of ChatGPT in November 2022
however, and the subsequent acceleration of the AI investment cycle, the gap has widened materially as AI has
reshaped the US equity market and Technology’s weight in the index has risen sharply. 
Hedge fund long allocations have adjusted over this period, but not at the same pace. The widening underweight
therefore appears less a reflection of an outright withdrawal from Technology than of a structural shift in the
benchmark that hedge fund allocations have not matched.

Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 19/11/2026.
Insider buying is returning in Tech as the sector comes under pressure
Corporate insider transactions disclosed through regulatory filings, including those by company executives and
directors, can provide a complementary indicator of market positioning.

The charts below show the ratio of insiders buying shares to those selling across the S&P 500 (top) and its Tech
sector (bottom). Insiders are known for their tendency to act as dip buyers, with buying vs selling typically increasing
during periods of market weakness. This was particularly visible around the tariff-driven ‘Liberation Day’ volatility in
Q2 2025, when buyer-to-seller ratios increased across both the broader market and Tech, remaining elevated into
2026 before moderating as equity markets reached new highs.
Importantly, this moderation was still underway at the end of September, the reporting date for the Q3 13F filings.
Since then, however, the two indicators have diverged. The insider buyer/seller ratio has continued to decline across
the broader S&P 500, while the Tech ratio shows a marked resurgence in insider buying during November alongside
the recent decline in the sector. While still a relatively recent development, the move is noteworthy and consistent
with the dip-buying behaviour seen during previous periods of market weakness: as Tech has come under pressure,
sector insiders have started buying again:

Hedge fund beta to global equities remained below its early-2026 peak
The chart below tracks the beta of the HFRX Global Hedge Fund Index relative to the MSCI World Index, providing a
useful gauge of how closely hedge fund returns are moving with global equities.
After rising to around 0.58 in early 2026, beta fell sharply and has since recovered only partially. It remained below its
earlier peak throughout Q3 despite continued strength in global equities, while the latest observations beyond
quarter-end remain around the middle of the range seen in recent years.
While not a direct measure of net equity exposure, the indicator shows that the unusually high degree of co-
movement with global equities seen earlier in 2026 has not returned. This sits alongside the 13F evidence of
continued differentiation in hedge fund sector positioning relative to the broader market.
Source:Bloomberg Finance L.P.; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 19/11/2026.
Star manager moves in Q3 2026
Selected manager disclosures reinforce Technology’s central role in Q3 portfolio activity, but point to substantial
disagreement rather than a uniform sector view. Point72, for example, added Microsoft, Celestica and Intel while
reducing Credo Technology, ASML and Taiwan Semiconductor. Citadel increased Cloudflare while reducing Nvidia
and Microsoft, while Renaissance added Micron and AMD but reduced Palantir and Taiwan Semiconductor.
The broader pattern is one of active rotation and stock selection rather than a common directional move. Tech-
related positions appear prominently on both sides of the table, with some managers buying securities that others
reduced. Outside the sector, positioning was similarly selective, while several managers also reduced reported broad-
market ETF holdings. The ETF reductions may point to greater use of individual securities relative to passive vehicles

within reported long portfolios, although the filings alone cannot tell us whether overall market exposure was
reduced or expressed through other instruments.
Overall, the manager-level disclosures reinforce the aggregate message: Technology remained central to portfolio
activity, but the Q3 data show selective repositioning and substantial disagreement rather than a broad-based
increase in Technology exposure across the hedge fund universe.
Source: FactSet financial data and analytics; Pictet Trading Strategy; as of 19/11/2026.
So what was hot and what was not? Screening the 13F detail
1. Hedge fund favourites: Tech still on top
The 25 largest reported hedge fund long holdings by market value at the end of Q3 remain dominated by Technology
and technology-related companies. Nvidia, Amazon and Microsoft occupy the top three positions, followed by
SpaceX and Apple, with several other major semiconductor and Technology names featuring prominently.
However, changes in market value should not be confused with active position changes. Microsoft provides a clear
example: the aggregate market value held increased by 35% during Q3, largely reflecting the stock’s strong (37.5%)
performance: the underlying reported position actually fell by 2%. Nvidia shows a similar, though less pronounced,
effect, with market value rising 16% while the reported position increased by only 2%.
Elsewhere, there is clearer evidence of active repositioning. Intel recorded by far the largest position increase among
the top 25, up 83%, while Meta rose 11%. In the other direction, managers reduced positions in SanDisk by 24%, AMD
by 15%, Micron by 10% and Apple by 9%, reinforcing the considerable selectivity within the Tech sector itself.
Broad-market ETFs also saw sizeable moves in both directions. Reported holdings in the iShares Core S&P 500 ETF
fell 47%, while the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF increased 38% and the SPDR S&P 500 ETF rose just 2%. This points more
towards rotation between vehicles than a uniform increase or reduction in benchmark exposure.
Overall, the screen reinforces the broader conclusion: Technology remained dominant within reported long
portfolios, but managers were highly selective beneath the aggregate allocation, with substantial increases in some
names alongside reductions in others.

2. Biggest movers: buying extended well beyond Tech
The screening below ranks the 25 largest increases in reported hedge fund long positions during Q3, focusing on
holdings with a remaining market value above USD 1 billion. 
Unlike the largest-holdings screen, the changes here capture increases in the underlying positions rather than top
holdings by market value. ETFs occupy the top two positions, led by a sharp increase in the ARK Innovation ETF,
while the Utilities Select Sector SPDR ETF position rose 210%. The contrast is notable, with managers adding
exposure to both innovation-oriented growth and the traditionally more defensive Utilities sector.
Among individual companies, Industrials feature particularly prominently, including Honeywell Aerospace (+158%),
GFL Environmental (+156%), Cummins and Curtiss-Wright (both +103%) and Sunbelt Rentals (+100%). Technology is
also represented, with increases in NXP Semiconductors (+116%), Shopify (+89%) and Intel (+83%), but does not

Overall, the screen broadens the picture from the aggregate sector analysis: while Technology remained an
important area of activity, the largest position increases extended across Industrials, Consumer Discretionary,
Healthcare and other sectors, pointing to selective accumulation across a diverse range of exposures.

3. What fell out of favour?
The next table highlights the 25 largest reductions in reported hedge fund long positions during Q3, focusing those
with at least USD 1 billion in remaining market value. As in the previous screen, the ranking captures changes in
reported holdings, offering a clearer view of active repositioning than changes in market value alone.
Reductions were spread across sectors and investment styles. Medline recorded the largest position decline at 68%,
while several ETFs also saw substantial reductions. The iShares Core S&P 500 ETF position fell 47%, although
simultaneous increases in other S&P 500 vehicles highlighted above point more towards rotation between ETFs than
a broad reduction in market exposure. Positions in the VanEck Gold Miners ETF, SPDR Portfolio S&P 500 ETF and
Health Care Select Sector SPDR ETF were also materially reduced.
At the company level, Industrials feature prominently, including Lockheed Martin (−38%), Bloom Energy (−37%),
Republic Services (−35%), Otis Worldwide (−34%) and 3M (−32%). Consumer Staples, Energy and Healthcare also
feature among the larger reductions.
Technology was also present on the sell side, including Nebius (−41%), NetApp (−37%), STMicroelectronics (−34%),
Qualcomm (−33%) and Zoom Communications (−31%). 
Together with the most-bought screen, this reinforces the picture of selective repositioning within Technology rather
than a uniform sector-wide move.

4. What was new?
The screening below ranks securities by the number of managers initiating new reported long positions during Q3,
rather than by the size of the aggregate position change shown in the previous screens.
Medtronic and Meta Platforms top the ranking, with seven managers initiating positions in each, representing
approximately USD 3.7 billion and USD 2.7 billion respectively. Intel followed, with six managers establishing new
positions worth around USD 2.7 billion in aggregate. Overall, Healthcare and Technology accounted for much of the
activity.
Importantly, however, new Technology positions were not evidence of a common sector view. As the full-exit screen
on the next slide shows, other managers were simultaneously exiting Technology names, reinforcing the broader
picture of substantial disagreement and selective positioning within the sector.

5. And what was sold off ?
The final screen ranks securities by the number of managers fully exiting reported long positions during Q3, rather
than partially reducing existing holdings.
SpaceX led the ranking, with six managers closing positions representing around USD 2.4 billion in aggregate
reported value. The SPDR S&P 500 ETF followed, with four managers exiting positions worth around USD 2.0
billion. Applied Materials and Microsoft were also fully exited by four managers, representing approximately USD 1.9
billion and USD 1.2 billion respectively.
Technology and technology-related companies feature prominently, but the comparison with the new-picks screen is
more revealing. Microsoft was newly added by five managers while being fully exited by four others; SpaceX was
initiated by three managers and exited by six.
Rather than signalling a collective retreat from Technology, the two screens highlight substantial disagreement and
portfolio turnover beneath the aggregate data. Technology remained a key battleground for manager views, with
significant activity in both directions.

The Equity quantitative grades
Our equity quant process is one by which we rank and score
the stocks within our equity universe according to various
short-term (i.e. EPS growth) long-term (i.e. quality) and
positioning (i.e. technical) characteristics, and on relative
and/or historical basis as appropriate. The various scores
make up a stock’s total or ‘global' grade’:Growth Grade: The
Growth Grade is a proprietary formula made up of earnings
revision momentum, past earnings growth, earnings stability,
and current and long-term earnings growth.  A grade above 55
is considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45 and
neutral between 55 and 45.
EPS Grade: The EPS Grade is a proprietary formula consisting of
current and forward EPS growth, change and surprise data.  An
EPS grade above 60 or below 40 is considered predictive for
future out/under performance.Sales Grade: The Sales Grade is a
proprietary formula made up of current and next year’s sales
momentum, past sales growth, sales stability, and current and
long-term sales growth. A grade above 55 is considered bullish on
a 3-month basis, bearish below 45, and neutral between 55 and
45.
Value Grade: The Value Grade is a proprietary formula made up
of estimated P/E, P/B, P/S and P/CF ratios. 40% of the grade is
based on historical values and 60% on current market data.  A
grade above 55 suggests a stock is cheap, below 45 expensive,
and neutral between 55 and 45.
Quality Grade: The Quality Grade is a proprietary formula that
focuses on the balance sheet (i.e. change in accruals, change in
free cash flows and profitability). A grade above 55 suggests a
healthy balance sheet.Credit Grade: The Credit Grade focusses
on the passive side of the balance sheet.  It is divided into three
sub-components to assess both short and long-term solvency.  A
grade above 55 suggests a strong capital structure, while a grade
below 45 suggests a weak one.
Money Flow Grade: The Money Flow Grade is a proprietary
formula that gives the accumulation/distribution based on the
volume flows of a stock. A grade above 55 indicates good money
flow and a grade below 45 suggests weak money flow.Smart
Sentiment Grade: The Smart Sentiment grade is a contrarian
indicator based on investor positioning measures such as the
days-to-cover ratio, the put-call ratio, and the short-interest
ratio.  A weak grade suggests ‘too much’ optimism.

Relative Strength (RS) Grade: The RS grade measures the price
momentum of a stock over its 1-year price performance.
Global Grade: The Global Grade is a weighted average of the
Growth, EPS Sales, Value, Quality, Credit, Money Flow and Smart
Sentiment Grades.

The Exposure Optimisation Model
The PTS Exposure Optimisation Model (EOM) is a proprietary
model designed to give tactical signals on the prevailing
short-term regional equity market regime. The model is
applied to each market on a mutually-exclusive basis, with
output expressed by way of exposure scores (US; Europe;
Japan and EM); in each case an average of underlying factors
scores with dynamic weighting applied according to the
prevailing structural market volatility regime. Exposure score
ratings are: very bullish (80-100%); bullish (60-80%); neutral
(40-60%); bearish (20-40%); and very bearish (20-0%). Factors
are as follows:
Regime: A combination of technical indicators, moving averages
and a Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance (CPPI) strategy,
the regime score is designed to reflect short-term market
momentum in the region;
Valuation: Built around the forward price-to-sales ratio, the
dynamic valuation score is based on a calculation that is re-set on
bull/bear market regime changes and calibrated to facilitate
regional comparison;
Economics: The economics score draws on real economy activity
data (i.e. heavy truck sales and shipping traffic), economic signals
from financial markets (i.e. yield-curves) and medium-term
economic indicators (i.e. PMIs and unemployment data); 
Flows: The flows score draws upon both active and passive
weekly EPFR fund flows data giving us an indication of risk
appetite going into the week ahead; 
Market Sentiment: The market sentiment score is built around
classic regional market risk indices (i.e. VIX; ARMS) as well as soft
survey data (i.e. U.of Michigan Consumer Sentiment). The scoring
system is applied in a similar fashion at both regional and market
level, while at sector level we refer only to the Regime, Flows and
Valuation factors.

Technical Analysis
The technical analysis used in this presentation combines
traditional technical tools: graphical analysis (trend lines,
support lines, continuation and reversal patterns) which
determines the tendency, mathematical indicators (moving
averages, RSI, MACD) used as numeric filters and Elliot wave
theory which allows us to build a scenario with target levels
and invalidation points. We also occasionally refer to
Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis. 
Elliott Wave Theory: According to Elliott Wave Theory, markets
move in impulse waves – with five sub-waves (numbered 1-5 or I-
V) following the direction of the main trend, followed by three
corrective sub-waves (A-B-C) (example below).  These waves
follow a set of specific rules and are linked to each other by target
and retracement ratios based on the Fibonacci sequence, and the
characteristics of each wave form an integral part of the
reflection of the mass psychology it embodies.
Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator that
measures the speed and change of price movements. It ranges
from 0 to 100 and is typically used to identify overbought or
oversold conditions in a market. An RSI above 70 suggests that a
stock may be overbought, while an RSI below 30 indicates that it
may be oversold.
The Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD): A
trend-following momentum indicator that shows the relationship
between two moving averages. It is calculated by subtracting the
26-period Exponential Moving Average (EMA) from the 12- period
EMA. The result is the MACD line. A nine-day EMA of the MACD,
called the signal line, is then plotted on top of the MACD line,
which can function as a trigger for buy or sell signals. When the
MACD crosses above the signal line, it is typically considered a
bullish signal, and when it crosses below, it is considered bearish.

Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis: Ichimoku Cloud is a popular
technical analysis tool used to identify potential trends and
support/resistance levels in financial markets. It consists of
several components, including the Kumo (cloud), the Tenkan-sen
(conversion line), the Kijun-sen (base line), and Senkou Span
(leading span).
