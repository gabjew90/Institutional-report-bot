AI Debt Is Reshaping Corporate Bond ETFs 
Key Takeaways 
 
The AI-related exposure in the 
largest intermediate and long-term 
corporate bond ETFs in the U.S has 
grown in the last three years. 
 
Despite this, the sector exposure in these ETFs has not changed, since the debt issued by 
hyperscalers has replaced debt issued by traditional technology and communication services firms.  
 
Given this, their credit risk is now best viewed through a thematic risk lens, rather than just a GICS 
sectors lens, particularly since Utilities, traditionally large debt issuers, have become more AI linked.  
 
Hyperscalers now make up three of the top five issuers by exposure in some ETFs, although the risk 
is not evenly spread across them. Oracle (ORCL 128 ***) is most at risk if financing conditions 
tighten. 
 
While the rise of AI-related debt necessitates closer monitoring of these ETFs, it also creates an 
opportunity for investors to get higher yields while still staying in the investment grade category.  
Fundamental Context 
The rapid rise in AI-related debt issuance is resulting in an increased exposure to AI-driven businesses 
in corporate debt ETFs in the U.S. Table 1 lists the seven largest intermediate and long-term investment 
grade corporate bond ETFs listed in the U.S. The first three ETFs focus on holding long-dated corporate 
bonds with an average maturity of over 10 years. These ETFs tend to have higher exposure to AI-related 
debt, since hyperscalers tend to issue long-term bonds. These ETFs now have approximately 10% of their 
portfolio in AI-related debt, up from about 7% just three years ago. The bottom four rows list intermediate-
term ETFs with an average maturity typically between five and 10 years. While these ETFs have lower AI-
related exposure in absolute terms, they have also grown this exposure faster than their longer-term 
counterparts.

Classifying AI-Related Debt in Corporate Bond ETFs 
The largest category of AI-related debt in long-term investment grade U.S. corporate bond ETFs is 
issued by the hyperscalers. They account for about 7%-8% of total exposure in the long-term ETFs and 
4%-5% in the intermediate ETFs (see Table 2). To analyze the AI-related debt in these ETFs, we created a 
basic taxonomy where all the issuers were classified into three categories: 
 
Hyperscalers: These firms operate large-scale cloud and AI computing platforms, and their bonds 
are ordinary unsecured debt of the parent company. Examples of U.S. firms in this list include 
Amazon (AMZN 250 *****), Microsoft (MSFT 529 *****), Alphabet (GOOGL 341 ****), Meta Platforms 
(META 709 *****), and ORCL. Space X (SPCX 146 **) was also included in this category, due to its 
large-scale AI computing buildout. 
 
 
Neoclouds and data centers: These entities include specialist GPU-cloud companies and owners of 
data-center capacity, including bonds backed by a single AI campus and its long-term lease. This 
category is very small (less than 0.3% of exposure), since pure neoclouds like CoreWeave (CRWV 61 
***) borrow in high-yield or private credit markets, and can’t enter investment-grade indices. This 
category could grow over time if data-center project bonds earn investment-grade ratings, but most 
of this debt is currently rated high yield. CRWV has said it aims to win an investment-grade rating on 
more of its debt, which, if achieved, could bring neocloud debt into these ETFs. 
 
 
Semiconductors & hardware: This category includes suppliers of physical AI equipment including 
chip designers like Nvidia (NVDA 214 *****), memory providers like Micron (MU 879 ****), equipment 
providers like Lam Research (LRCX 264 ****), and power and cooling providers like Vertiv (VRT 206 
*****). Their exposure to AI is through hyperscaler spending, and currently this category accounts for 
2-3% of investment grade corporate bond ETFs, with the share being at the upper end of that range 
in intermediate ETFs, since these companies mostly borrow at 5-to-10-year maturities.

Changes in Nature of Risk Profile Due to Higher AI Exposure 
Interestingly, the growth of AI-related debt has not changed the GICS sector exposure profile of these 
corporate bond ETFs. This is because in GICS sectors like Communication Services and Information 
Technology, AI-related debt has replaced debt from traditional firms, with the total sector weights staying 
broadly unchanged. For example, in the Communication Services sector, exposure from META, GOOGL, and 
SPCX has increased by over 2%-pts, replacing debt from Comcast (CMCSA 22 ***), T-Mobile (TMUS 159 
*****), and Disney (DIS 111 ****). In the Information Technology sector, ORCL has now become the largest 
issuer with NVDA also increasing in exposure, while the value of debt issued by Apple (AAPL 343 ****) and 
MSFT have declined in these corporate bond ETFs. Similarly in the Consumer Discretionary sector, AMZN’s 
exposure has increased by 0.7%-pts, replacing debt issued by more traditional consumer firms like Home 
Depot (HD 310 ***) and Starbucks (SBUX 98 **). It is important to note that traditional issuers kept 
borrowing, but their maturing bonds were not fully replaced, while the hyperscalers’ new issuance was 
significantly larger per issuer. The increase in AI exposure within each sector reflects the size of hyperscaler 
borrowing rather than a pullback by traditional firms.  
Given this context, credit risk for these ETFs is now more appropriately viewed through a thematic risk 
lens, rather than a traditional GICS sector lens. While the weights of the Information Technology and 
Communication Services GICS sectors have not increased significantly in corporate bond ETFs, their 
exposure composition has clearly shifted towards AI-driven businesses. Additionally, Utilities tend to be a 
very large component of investment grade corporate bond ETFs due to their reliance on debt financing. 
Recent research by CFRA’s fundamental equity team indicates that the data center boom has changed how 
Utilities should be evaluated. Across CFRA’s regulated utility coverage, companies with credible hyperscaler 
agreements and expanding load pipelines have earned growth-stock re-ratings that have broadly offset rate-
driven headwinds. Meanwhile, those lacking the data center catalyst remain anchored to the sector's old 
rate-sensitive identity. According to the team’s findings, the sector’s transformation is real but uneven, and 
navigating it requires moving beyond the traditional frameworks that previously defined the Utilities sector. 
Hyperscalers now make up three of the top five issuers by exposure in VCLT (see Table 3), although the 
risk is not evenly spread across those issuers. CFRA’s equity research expects tighter financing conditions to 
favor AMZN, MSFT, GOOGL, and META, which hold more than $420 billion in cash and can fund much of their 
spending internally. ORCL, which is now essentially tied with AT&T as the top issuer by exposure, is much 
riskier since it is among the most leveraged large-cap tech issuers. S&P Global Ratings downgraded ORCL 
to BBB-, one notch above high yield, in July 2026. An ORCL downgrade, although it would not affect all of 
VCLT’s hyperscaler holdings equally, would directly affect one of its two largest positions. This 
concentration of hyperscalers among the largest issuers is more a feature of long-dated corporate bond 
ETFs. The top five issuers in the intermediate corporate bond ETFs tend to still be companies in the financial 
sector, which is generally the largest sector by exposure in most investment grade corporate bond ETFs.

Hyperscaler debt has not increased the average duration of corporate bond ETFs, but their high 
duration implies they will be negatively affected by potential rate hikes in the next few months. In 
VCLT, hyperscaler bonds now average 26.9 years to maturity, against 21.9 years for the rest of the 
portfolio. They are also longer than three years ago in October 2023 (25.1 years), as 30- to 40-year 
deals entered the index. At the fund level, however, the effect is small. Hyperscalers add only about 
0.08 years to VCLT’s duration (up from 0.04 three years ago in October 2023). As per CFRA's 
calculations based on VCLT's holdings, the fund’s average maturity dropped marginally from 22.8 to 
22.3 years because the average maturity of the remaining holdings shortened. IGLB and SPLB show the 
same pattern. Although hyperscaler debt has not increased overall fund duration, these ETFs are likely 
to be affected by the possibility of further interest rate hikes following the recent September 2026 hike, 
which seems likely given today’s above target inflation rate.  
Opportunities to Capture Higher Yields to Compensate for Risk 
While the rise of AI-related debt in investment-grade corporate bond ETFs warrants closer monitoring, it 
also creates an opportunity for getting higher yields. As an example, using IGLB’s holdings data as of 
October 13, 2026, hyperscaler bonds yield 7.12% on average against 6.51% for non-AI bonds. That is about 
60 basis points more than other bonds of the same duration. This extra yield is concentrated and driven by a 
few issuers.  Oracle yields about 8.2% (+171bp versus similar-duration bonds), SpaceX about 7.9% (+138bp), 
and Meta about 7.4% (+78bp). ORCL’s yield premium reflects real balance-sheet risk since CFRA’s equity 
team views ORCL negatively under tighter financing conditions. AA-rated Amazon and Alphabet yield only 
10-15bp more than the average investment-grade bond, which is still attractive for credits of that quality. For 
investors who want higher income without moving into the high yield segment, the hyperscaler bonds offer it. 
Investors should favor ETFs that focus on the higher rated hyperscalers and remember that the highest 
yields are on the weakest credits in the group. 
Investors willing to move into high yield can get more direct exposure to the riskier, higher-yielding end 
of the AI buildout. In the two largest high-yield corporate bond ETFs, the iShares iBoxx $ High Yield 
Corporate Bond ETF (HYG 77 *****) and the iShares Broad USD High Yield Corporate Bond ETF (USHY 36 
*****), AI-related debt made up 5.3% and 4.8% of net assets, respectively, as of October 13, 2026. That is 
lower than in the investment grade ETFs, but the mix is very different. Hyperscalers are almost absent, since 
they are all rated investment grade. Neoclouds and data-center financings instead account for 3.9% of HYG 
and 3.5% of USHY, compared with less than 0.3% or lower in the investment-grade ETFs. These bonds offer 
higher yields, but the extra yield comes with much greater risk. They face a large wall of debt coming due for 
refinancing over the next few years, and recent stress at CRWV shows how quickly sentiment can turn. For 
most investors, high-yield ETFs are therefore a way to add a small, deliberate position in the AI infrastructure 
buildout, not a substitute for the higher-quality hyperscaler exposure in investment-grade funds. 
Looking Ahead 
CFRA expects the share of AI-related debt in investment-grade corporate bond ETFs to keep rising. 
First, the indexes are still absorbing new supply. Bonds issued in 2026 year-to-date that would mature prior 
to October 2036 are already 11%-15% AI-related in the intermediate ETFs. That compares with about 8% for 
these funds overall. As older bonds roll out of the funds’ maturity windows and are replaced by new issues, 
AI-related weights should keep drifting upward. We estimate the increase at roughly 0.8-1.0%-pts per year 
for the intermediate ETFs, even if issuance does not pick up. Second, borrowing needs remain significant. 
CFRA expects capital spending by the Big Five, including ORCL, to reach $800-$835 billion in 2026 and rise 
about 50% in 2027, implying more than $1.2 trillion. A significant part of this will need to be debt financed, 
especially at ORCL, where capex now consumes most operating cash flow. Data center project debt will 
also rise, although that is likely to be included in high yield debt ETFs rather than investment grade ETFs.

Investors in these ETFs need to monitor AI exposure as a specific risk factor, particularly with riskier 
issuers like ORCL. We recommend monitoring hyperscaler capex guidance and AI revenue in the Q3 and 
Q4 earnings seasons. As one of the two largest issuers in the long-term ETFs and one notch above high 
yield at S&P, ORCL’s credit rating needs close monitoring. A downgrade below investment grade could force 
index ETFs to sell. Any spillover would most likely hit neoclouds and chipmakers that depend on continued 
capital market access, rather than the core hyperscalers, whose financial strength gives them an advantage 
in a tighter credit market. Finally, while hyperscaler bond yields are attractive, investors need to monitor the 
corresponding risk they are taking. Pairing long-term bond ETFs for yield with intermediate corporate bond 
ETFs may offer one way to manage risk, since they have lower AI exposure and lower interest rate 
sensitivity.
