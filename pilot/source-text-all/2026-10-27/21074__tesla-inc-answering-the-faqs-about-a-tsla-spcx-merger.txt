The possibility of a Tesla/SpaceX merger has increasingly become the consensus expectation, as
widely highlighted by media reports. Even Elon Musk has publicly alluded to a combination: On

With this report, we address some of the key FAQs around a potential combination with SpaceX
(covered by Anthony Valentini).

management comments indicate they see key benefits of a combination. Notably, while we
suspect there would be some financial synergies, the true value would be in vertical
integration.

•

What would be the structure of a deal?
While there are several potential tie-up scenarios, a SPCX acquisition of TSLA in an all-stock
transaction is widely viewed as a more likely form of the deal, with TSLA shareholders receiving
SPCX stock. At current market caps,the value of TSLA would equate to ~0.7x the value of SPCX;
however, it is typical for the acquired company to receive a premium over the current share
price, so we would expect this to be factored in. Due to the scale of such a transaction and
SpaceX's current balance sheet, we do not see debt acquisition as likely.
We expect SpaceX to be the acquiring company due to its dual-class share structure, which
would enable Musk to hold outsized voting share. In the 4Q23 Tesla earnings call, Musk noted
that a dual-class stock structure would be ideal to give him more voting power, yet changing
Tesla to a dual-class structure post-IPO would be exceedingly difficult. We believe control is
increasingly what may motivate Musk to pursue the combination, thus SpaceX would be much
better suited to be the acquirer given it would result in a much higher voting share of the
combined company for Musk.
Alternatively, another possibility is the formation of a new entity, with Tesla and SpaceX
shareholders both exchanging their stock for shares of a new entity – effectively similar to the
scenario above of Tesla investors exchanging their stock for shares of SpaceX, but instead
forming a new entity. But as noted above, we believe that Elon Musk maintaining voting control
of the final entity is more critical.
What are the timing considerations?
We see two potential announcement scenarios:
• Merger announcement ahead of Tesla's 2026 AGM: Corporate law requires Tesla to hold the
•
2026 AGM within 13 months of the 2025 AGM, implying the '26 AGM will be held on December
6 at the latest. In the past, Tesla has been inconsistent on whether it announces its AGM
through a preliminary proxy – last year, while Tesla announced its AGM in early July (~4mo
ahead of the meeting), the proxy was filed 50 days before the meeting.
While it's possible that the timing of a TSLA/SPCX merger announcement could slip later into
October or perhaps even until early November, we believe such timing may increasingly
challenge merger approval as part of the AGM.
Specifically, a merger would require a preliminary proxy filing, which would precede the
definitive proxy by at least 10 calendar days; note, there is no defined period by which a
definitive proxy must be filed. Texas law also requires a meeting notice at least 10 days before

What needs to be cleared for the deal?
We suspect the main hurdle would be the shareholder votes, as both Tesla and SpaceX
shareholders would likely have to approve the combination.
To our understanding, under Tesla and SpaceX certificates of formation, more than 50% of total
outstanding voting power must approve the combination. For the transaction vote, abstentions
would count as "No"/"Against" votes. As we have seen in recent Tesla voting results, both retail
and institutional holders tend to support proposals in favor of Musk.
• 77% of cast votes ("For"/"Against") approved Musk's 2025 comp package. 68% of total
•
outstanding votes ("For"/"Against"/"Abstained"/"Broker non-votes") approved the comp
package.
• 54% of cast votes ("For"/"Against") approved Tesla's investment in xAI. However, just 38% of
•
total outstanding votes ("For"/"Against"/"Abstained"/"Broker non-votes") approved the
investment, and as this fell below the applicable majority threshold (50%), the proposal did
not pass.
Given what we have seen in recent Tesla shareholder voting results, it will be crucial for Tesla
and SpaceX to receive increased shareholder support for a combination. Roughly 11% of total
outstanding votes in Tesla's 2025 AGM voting were "Broker non-votes", which are effectively
counted as "Against" votes, and we suspect Tesla will attempt to increase shareholder
participation in a potential SpaceX combination vote.
A key question is whether Musk would recuse himself from both merger votes given his financial
conflicts of interest; recall, in Tesla's 2016 acquisition of Solar City, Elon Musk did not vote his
shares, as Tesla's board conditioned a deal on a vote of the majority of Tesla's disinterested
shareholders7 . Under Texas incorporation laws, Musk would not technically have to recuse
himself from the vote, though doing so would signal greater independence in the vote and thus
would likely reduce governance concerns.
Both Tesla's and SpaceX's board of directors would also have to approve the combination,
though we do not expect this to present an issue.

What government support is needed for the transaction to pass?
While we lack visibility into the legal/regulatory process behind a potential transaction, we
highlight key considerations:
• FTC/DoJ HSR antitrust: Antitrust review would have to be passed, and as noted above, we
•
suspect TSLA/SPCX will likely try to complete the transaction under the Trump
administration's more supportive FTC.
• DoD/government-contract and national-security considerations: In July it was reported8
•
that Tesla was considering selling or spinning off its China business ahead of a potential
merger with SpaceX, as Tesla's large China presence could be a concern given SpaceX's status
as an important US government defense/space contractor; we believe the majority of Tesla's
current earnings can be attributed to China, with Giga Shanghai responsible for more than
half of Tesla's vehicle production, and with opex disproportionately skewed to AI investments
(which are focused on the US for now). The report, however, was refuted by Musk and by
Tesla China.
We assume this review process will likely be narrower than a CFIUS process, which we do not
believe would be required as both companies are US entities.
• Other government organizations – SEC, FCC: The SEC would oversee the process. We
•
suspect FCC approval could be needed, but it appears more likely than not in the current
regulatory environment.

Valuation Methodology: We value TSLA using a probability-weighted scenario analysis of 2030, reflecting the potential binary outcomes for the
stock. We forecast a model case, and then flex the results into bull/bear and uberbull/uberbear scenarios, each of which generating an independent
earnings estimate. From there we assign an EPS multiple and discount rate, deriving a share price for each scenario. Finally, we assess the probability
of each earnings / share price scenario under a base case, upside case, and downside case, generating a probability-weighted average target price
under each case. This process, using a 4% discount rate (over 4 years) and a 78x base case EPS multiple, generates our Equal Weight rating and $370
price target. We set our target multiples with reference to peer levels, adjusted for differing levels of profitability and growth.
