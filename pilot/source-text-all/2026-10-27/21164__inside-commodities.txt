US coaxing refiners to produce more diesel as prices surge, energy officials say 
Two top US Energy Department officials said on Tuesday they were working with refiners at home and abroad to boost 
fuel output as a diesel shortage has raised prices, driving dissatisfaction among voters with the White House ahead of the 
November midterm elections. 
 
Diesel prices have jumped to record highs because of disruptions from the Iran war and Ukrainian attacks on Russian 
refineries . Retail diesel prices averaged about $6.32 a gallon on Tuesday, according to motorist group AAA. 
 
The Trump administration has taken several steps to try to lower fuel prices that have surged since the start of the Iran 
war on February 28, including relaxing restrictions on Russian diesel exports, invoking the Defense Production Act to 
boost output and allowing commercial vehicles to run on red-dyed diesel normally limited to off-road equipment. 
 
Diesel is critical to the global economy, powering much of the transport, agriculture and heavy industry that underpin 
global trade and economic activity. The high cost of fuel has soured voters on US President Donald Trump's stewardship 
of the economy, with 36% approving of Trump on that issue, according to the most recent Reuters/Ipsos opinion poll. 
 
"We are talking about reopening every refinery we can," Energy Secretary Chris Wright said in Houston. "There are 
dialogues there, and that's a tough challenge. I'm not saying all these are going to happen." 
 
 
DIFFICULT TO RAISE US OUTPUT 
 
US refineries are running near full capacity, and with several facilities shutting in recent years, there is little leeway to 
quickly boost domestic output, analysts say. 
 
Refinery expansions are capital intensive and take months, if not years, to complete depending on the complexity of the 
additions. Wright said the US is also working with India to increase refining output there to bring products to global 
markets. 
 
American Petroleum Institute CEO Mike Sommers told Reuters on the sidelines of an energy forum in Titusville, 
Pennsylvania that US refiners are running at 95% to 100% of capacity. He said they are putting off planned maintenance 
because of high prices. 
 
“The American refiners are doing everything they can,” Sommers said. He said that using the Defense Production Act to 
restart idled plants, as the administration has floated, would require a lot of capital.

Last week, Vice President JD Vance said the administration would use the Defense Production Act to expand capacity of 
a Texas refinery owned by Delek US Holdings DK.N. 
 
Trump signed legislation last year that included $1 billion in Defense Production Act funding for energy-related 
projects deemed critical to national security. Wright said there was "more to do" on refining and the Defense Production

Sommers singled out China's spare refining capacity, saying their government is currently barring diesel exports. Beijing 
“is really going to be the key if we’re going to get long-term prices down,” he said. 
 
A spokesperson for theChinese embassy in Washington said the key to resolving the issue is to achieve a ceasefire in 
Iran. "China stands ready to maintain communication with all sides to jointly safeguard global energy security and keep 
industrial and supply chains stable," the spokesperson added. 
 
 
PUSH FOR EUROPEAN DIESEL 
 
Trump temporarily waived sanctions this month on Russian diesel in an attempt to lower prices. Russian diesel will likely 
move to New England, if it moves to the US at all, Wright said. Already, cargoes from Russia are returning to 
theirtraditional destinations after the US suspended sanctions, with two tankers signaling trips to Turkey and Brazil in 
recent days, Reuters reported on Tuesday. 
 
The Trump administration has also been pushing Europe to release more fuel to balance global markets. "We are working 
with our friends ... to find new and innovative ways to get more throughput" through refineries, said Energy Department 
Under Secretary Kyle Haustveit at the Drake Forum in Titusville. 
 
While the Trump administration has pushed Europe to release 50 million barrels of diesel, an agreement by industrialized 
nations fell short of that target. The Paris-based International Energy Agency said last week it would speed the release of 
crude and oil product volumes pledged in March, including more than 20 million barrels of diesel as soon as possible. 
 
Fatih Birol, chief of the IEA, which coordinates industrialized nations' releases of oil from emergency stocks, said on 
Tuesday the group is ready to coordinate more oil releases by member countries if needed. 
 
 
North Dakota oil companies speed up well completions as prices rise, state regulator says 
US oil and gas companies are accelerating well completions and boosting production in North Dakota, the nation's third-
largest oil-producing state, encouraged by high crude prices, state officials said on Tuesday. 
 
"Operators are acting more quickly to move wells to production to capitalize on elevated oil prices," Richard Suggs, a 
geologist with North Dakota's Oil and Gas Division, said during a call to discuss the state's August production data, the 
latest available.

US West Texas Intermediate futures topped$100 a barrel for the first time in nearly a month last week amid concerns that 
the Iran war could further tighten supplies. On Tuesday, WTI, the benchmark against which North Dakota crude is priced, 
was trading at around $102 a barrel. North Dakota crude traded at an average discount of about $4.21 a barrel to WTI 
through August, officials said. 
 
The time between drilling and preparing a well for production has roughly halved since the start of the year, Suggs said. 
Wells are also coming online more quickly than normal, he added. 
 
RECORD NUMBER OF PRODUCING WELLS 
 
The faster pace helped push the state's total producing wells to a record high of 20,177 in August, Suggs said. There are 
36 drilling rigs operating in North Dakota, up from 34 in September, with eight hydraulic fracturing crews active, according 
to state data. Oil production rose by 3,000 barrels per day to 1.161 million bpd in August. 
 
However, the price gap between refiners on the US West Coast and East Coast has widened, according to the latest data 
collected, said Justin Kringstad, director of the North Dakota Pipeline Authority. West Coast refiners paid about $93.51 
per barrel for North Dakota crude, compared with $88.27 per barrel on the East Coast in July. 
 
"This is the biggest spread I have seen in a little while," Kringstad said, adding the differential is typically 50 cents to $1 a 
barrel. Kringstad said East Coast prices are particularly sensitive to crude supplies arriving via waterborne imports, the 
US Gulf Coast and rail, and that disruptions to those flows may have contributed to the price gap. 
 
Kringstad said refinery demand remains strong. Refinery utilization rates have eased, particularly in the Midwest, as 
plants enter the September-through-November maintenance season, but remain above 80%, he said.
 
 
 
Global sugar market supply deficit to grow sharply, analyst says 
The global sugar market supply deficit, or the gap between production and demand, was projected on Tuesday to grow to 
6.28 million tons, 3 million tons more than a previous estimate, according to market analyst Green Pool Commodities. 
 
The analyst blamed poor production in major sugar-making regions like Brazil, India and the European Union for the 
revised outlook, as global agriculture struggles to cope with impacts from the El Niño weather anomaly. 
 
"Center-south Brazil harvest is now falling far short of early forecasts, with excess rainfall due to the ongoing El Niño 
event (hurting the crop)," the Australia-based sugar industry analyst said. 
 
A late September sugarcane crush in Brazil was again short of normal levels due to rain, it said, taking cumulative sugar 
production in the world's largest exporter to be 14% lower than the year-earlier period. 
 
Green Pool cut its estimate for center-south Brazil sugar production by 600,000 tons to 37.3 million tons.

Coffee stocks slide in Japan and Europe, with August readings at multi-year lows 
Green coffee stocks in Japan and Europe, two of the largest markets for the beverage, decreased in August compared to 
last year, according to figures from industry groups compiled by the Coffee Trading Academy. 
 
Japan's stocks stood at 1.99 million 60-kg bags in August, the lowest August reading since at least 2022 and 12.4% 
below a year earlier. 
 
The volume is about 27% under the five-year August average of 2.73 million bags. 
European green coffee stocks were at 7.14 million bags in August, down 16.8% from a year earlier and 1.4% below July. 
Coffee stocks in main consuming countries have been falling in recent years due to high prices and limited production in 
growing regions that were impacted by adverse climate conditions. 
 
Stocks of the type natural arabica were hit hardest, at 1.69 million bags, down 25.6% year-on-year. They have declined 
for three straight months. 
 
European robusta coffee stocks, at 3.03 million bags, were 16% lower than in August 2025, though up from 2.71 million in 
January. 
 
European stocks also trail their five-year seasonal average, the data showed.

Chile's Codelco sets 2027 Europe copper premium at record $350 a metric ton, sources say 
Chile's Codelco is offering its European customers copper at a record high premium of $350 a metric ton for next year, up 
more than 7% from $325 this year, two sources with knowledge of the matter said. 
 
Codelco, the world's biggest copper miner by production, declined to comment. 
 
Premiums set by Codelco for physical delivery of refined copper are paid on top of London Metal Exchange prices and 
are often referenced as a benchmark in global supply contracts for the metal used in electric vehicles, grid infrastructure 
and construction. 
 
The record premium — offered as the global metals industry gathers in London for LME Week — points to an emerging 
trend of higher copper premiums for next year due to expectations of shortages in Europe. 
 
Aurubis, Europe's biggest copper smelter, last week hiked its 2027 premium by around 10% to $347 a ton, also a record 
for the German company. 
 
Shortages in the world outside the United States are due to expectations of US tariffs on copper imports. 
 
Traders have been shipping copper to the US since President Donald Trump ordered a study on the potential for tariffs in 
February last year. 
 
The prospect pushed Comex prices to a record high of $6.83 a lb, or $15,057 a ton, on September 22. Comex-approved 
warehouses in the US hold around 70% of the world's visible copper stocks. 
 
Mine disruptions to copper supply, including Codelco's El Teniente deposit, which was hit by a deadly landslide this 
month, have also helped keep copper prices elevated. The metal hit a record high of $14,875 a ton on the LME last 
month. It was last around $14,380 a ton. 
 
Codelco offers separate premiums for the US and Asian markets, including top metals consumer China. 
 
 
Goldman Sachs raises copper forecast as US-China competition tightens supply 
Goldman Sachs on Monday raised its copper price forecasts, citing weaker-than-expected mine supply and anticipated 
deficits outside the United States as competition between the US and China for refined copper intensifies. 
 
Goldman raised its end-2026 copper price forecast to $14,700 per metric ton from $13,735 previously. The bank 
increased its 2027 average copper price forecast to $15,400 a ton from $13,800 a ton. 
 
Goldman said it now expects ex-US copper deficits of about 950,000 tons in 2026 and 220,000 tons in 2027, up from 
previous forecasts of 640,000 tons and 170,000 tons, respectively.

Europe's benchmark hits €90/MWh for first time since December 2022 
Europe's benchmark gas contract traded above €90 per megawatt hour (MWh) for the first time since December 2022 on 
Tuesday amid increased global competition for supplies tightened by the US-Iran war ahead of winter. 
 
The Dutch front-month contract at the TTF hub, Europe's benchmark, hit an intraday high of €90.16/MWh, or $29.62 per 
million British thermal units (mmBtu), its highest level since December 22, 2022, ICE data showed. 
 
It traded at €90.09/MWh, up €2.05, by 1144 GMT. 
 
The British front-month contract was up by 4.79 pence at 222.25 pence per therm, after hitting an intraday high of 220.60 
p/therm, also the highest since December 22, 2022. 
 
"With no solution in sight in the Middle East, the market is continuously pricing in increased risk premium ahead of a 
winter, which Europe enters with significantly depleted gas storages," analysts at Mind Energy said. 
 
Attacks by Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthis on a Yemeni military training camp and renewed attacks on Saudi energy 
infrastructure continued to fuel the geopolitical risk premium, analysts at Engie EnergyScan said. 
 
The recent uptick in attacks is also making Asian buyers more active in the spot market, snapping up any available 
liquefied natural gas (LNG) cargo on fears of further market tightening, Daniel Hynes, senior commodity strategist at ANZ, 
said. 
 
Asia's benchmark LNG price already lifted above $30/mmBtu on Monday for the first time since December 2022, 
increasing pressure on European buyers to compete on price. 
 
"If the price differential becomes too wide, US LNG suppliers have a greater incentive to redirect cargoes to Asia rather 
than Europe," analysts at Commerzbank research said. 
 
Europe's gas storage sites are 74.58% full, according to Gas Infrastructure Europe data, well below historical levels for 
this time of year.

EU still committed to keeping oil, coal and gas in ground in Arctic, new strategy says 
The European Union remains committed to "ensuring that oil, coal and gas stay in the ground, including in Arctic regions," 
according to a new Arctic strategy presented by the European Commission and EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas on 
Tuesday. 
 
"Due to its specific vulnerabilities, the Commission will work with partners to protect the Arctic environment and prevent all 
hydrocarbon reserve developments that pose a threat to the Arctic ecosystem," the document also says. 
 
However, it does not repeat a pledge from the previous strategy, issued in 2021, to work towards "a multilateral legal 
obligation not to allow any further hydrocarbon reserve development in the Arctic or contiguous regions" or buy any 
products from those developments. 
 
An EU official said that change reflected a view that "the world is changing there and that it is harder - five years into this - 
to get to that kind of multilateral, decision-making given the positions of various players". 
 
Norway has called on the EU to drop its opposition to new drilling in the Arctic. With many ageing fields, Norwegian 
production is set to decline in the 2030s unless companies such as Equinor make new discoveries outside mature areas. 
 
 
 
 
EU maize imports soar 40% after drought ravages crops, soft wheat exports stable 
European Union maize imports in the 2026/27 season jumped 40% year on year by October 18, led by a more than 
threefold rise in shipments from the United States, while soft wheat exports were flat, European Commission data showed 
on Tuesday. 
Analysts and industry lobbies had forecast that imports would surge this season as companies such as animal feed 
makers and starch companies seek to compensate for the drought-ravaged EU harvest set to be the lowest since at least 
2000. 
EU maize imports since the start of July had reached 6.64 million metric tons by Sunday, of which 2.9 million are from the 
US. By the same time the previous year, the EU had only imported about 900,000 tons of US maize (corn). 
Ukraine also appeared to have benefited from the rise in EU maize import demand, with shipments from the country more 
than doubling year on year to 1.62 million tons from 770,000.

French maize output to halve to 50-year low after drought, growers say 
French grain maize output is on track to halve this year to 7 million metric tons, after lower sowings and record hot, dry 
weather ravaged crops, maize farmers' group AGPM said on Tuesday. 
That would be the lowest level since 1976 and compares to 13.7 million tons harvested last year, it said. 
"The worst has happened," AGPM Chairman Franck Laborde told reporters, blaming the fall on climate change-driven 
heatwaves and France's limits on water storage, which kept farmers from using water that could have been collected 
during an exceptionally rainy winter. 
France has suffered one of its worst hot and dry spells on record, with record temperatures in most parts of the country 
between May and August damaging crops, pastures and water supplies. 
The crisis prompted the government to issue a €1.3 billion aid package, and pledge to expand agricultural water storage. 
But Laborde said further help was needed or many farmers would not sow grains ahead of next season. "Without 
immediate cash flow, there will be no next season," he said. 
The fall in maize output would cut the sector's sales by €2.5 billion ($2.8 billion), with a further impact on villages where 
agriculture is the main source of income, Laborde said.  
The extremely dry weather mainly affected non-irrigated crops, which usually account for about a third of grain maize 
output, and the damage pushed a large share of the crop into feed-grade grain, Aude Cerrera from crop institute Arvalis 
said. 
The collapse in maize production will also lead to France importing significant amounts of maize this season, for the first 
time ever, notably from Brazil and Ukraine. 
European feed makers group FEFAC last month estimated that drought across Europe would force the European Union 
to import an additional 7 million metric tons of maize in the 2026/27 season.
