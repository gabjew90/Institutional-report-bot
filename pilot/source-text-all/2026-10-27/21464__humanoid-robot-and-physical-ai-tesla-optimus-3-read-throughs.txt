Tesla (covered by Rajat Gupta, N rating) held its 3Q 2026 earnings briefing 
this morning. While the market was watching a range of topics including FSD, 
capex, and core auto margins, our focus was on the latest updates for the 
Optimus humanoid robot. Elon Musk provided several important messages for 
the sector. He confirmed that Optimus production has started at Fremont, and 
signaled that Tesla could be ready to start shipping Optimus robots to other 
companies by the end of next year. While this is a more confident timeline 
than some had expected, Musk was clear that the ramp will be gradual, with 
volume guidance of 10k–20k units before launching external sales—below the 
most bullish market hopes. He described the manufacturing process as an S-
curve, emphasizing that “10,000 things” need to work correctly and that the 
slowest component or process will set the pace for the entire ramp. Musk 
highlighted the robot’s hand as the main hardware bottleneck, and achieving 
human-like dexterity, reliability, and onboard intelligence remains a 
challenge. Tesla’s immediate focus is on deploying Optimus internally in its 
own factories for dangerous or repetitive tasks, with external commercial 
shipments targeted for late 2027. Consumer applications are further out. Musk 
also noted that a video demonstration of Optimus performing non-pre-scripted 
tasks is expected within the next one to two months, and that the integration of 
the next-generation AI5 chip in 2027 should provide a significant boost in 
compute power and enable more complex behaviors.
Our CIIF tour takeaways (see Humanoid Robot & Physical AI: Robotics tour 
takeaways: CIIF 2026—Ecosystem demand, workflow integration, and what’s 
next for the sector) also support this more selective stance. The most tangible 
demand we observed was not at the robot OEM level, but among suppliers of 
sensors, 3D vision, data-collection devices, reducers, and dexterous hands. 
Leader Drive’s management was notably concrete about Physical AI already 
contributing over 35% of its business, and it is planning multi-year capacity 
expansion. Orbbec highlighted a sharp ramp in robotics-related orders, 
especially for data-collection hardware and 3D vision modules. However, the 
demand is still “ecosystem demand”—R&D, pilots, and data collection—
rather than mass end-user deployment. The gap between humanoid robot 
demos and industrial-grade automation remains wide, and the real bottlenecks 
are now around perception consistency, manipulation reliability, and the 
ability to deliver repeatable ROI in real-world workflows.
Our stock views: Against this backdrop, we have become more selective on 
the broader humanoid robot supply chain as expectations realign. The recent 
rally in Chinese robotics OEMs—led by Unitree and UBTech—has been 
sentiment-driven, and we remain cautious on these names in the near term as 
the market digests Tesla’s more gradual production outlook. We see a risk of 
disappointment over the next 1–3 months as expectations reset and operational 
progress is reassessed, particularly for Unitree, Estun, and UBTech. At the 
same time, we continue to see Hengli Hydraulic and Sanhua Intelligent as the 
best-positioned Tesla bot supply chain plays, both confirmed suppliers that

Read-throughs from Tesla 3Q26 briefing and Optimus update
• Elon Musk’s latest commentary on Optimus signals a more measured 
ramp-up, with the initial volume ramp-up guidance of 10k–20k units 
coming in below market expectations. During Tesla’s 3Q 2026 earnings 
call, Musk stated that while production has begun at Fremont, the company 
is taking a cautious approach to scaling. He guided to 10k–20k units within a 
12-month timeframe, below prior market hopes, and described the 
manufacturing process as an S-curve, with “10,000 things” that need to work

• Tesla’s internal deployment strategy for Optimus is focused on self-play 
and training data generation, with external commercial shipments still 
at least a year away. Musk explained that Tesla’s immediate priority is to 
deploy Optimus robots for practical tasks, and Tesla is building an Optimus 
Academy where Optimus can do self-play and generate training data. As for 
the profile of initial customers, Tesla is considering industrial applications 
notably dangerous, repetitive, or ergonomically difficult tasks 24/7. This 
internal-first approach allows Tesla to collect proprietary real-world data, 
validate reliability, and iterate on both hardware and software before 
offering the robots to external customers. Musk stated that commercial 
shipments to outside companies are roughly a year away, and that broader 
consumer applications will follow only after reliability and cost targets are 
met.
• Tesla’s 3Q 2026 update highlighted the hand, onboard intelligence, and 
compute roadmap, while providing limited detail on reducers, vision, or 
battery. Musk was explicit that the hand remains the main hardware 
bottleneck for Optimus, citing the challenge of achieving human-like 
dexterity, reliability, and long-duration operation. On the “brain” and 
software side, Musk emphasized the need for further improvements in 
onboard intelligence and the ability to manage the robot’s many degrees of 
freedom, with the next major milestone being a video demonstration of 
Optimus performing non-pre-scripted tasks within the next one to two 
months. Tesla confirmed plans to integrate its next-generation AI5 chip into 
Optimus around April 2027, which management described as providing 
roughly an order of magnitude more compute than the current AI4 and 
enabling more complex, generalizable behaviors. However, there was no 
specific mention of vision systems, cameras, or battery technology, while 
Optimus Engineering Lead highlighted their relatively high confidence in 
body electrotechnical system. Musk did reiterate that the production ramp 
will be gradual, with the slowest component or process setting the pace, and 
that the company is deliberately reducing reliance on the Chinese supply 
chain, which adds to the complexity of scaling.
• Musk’s commentary reinforced that the path to mass adoption will be 
gradual, with initial customers likely to be industrial rather than 
residential. Musk reiterated that the first external use cases for Optimus will 
be in industrial settings, where the robot can take on tasks that are dangerous 
or highly repetitive. He was clear that consumer applications are still further 
out, as the company needs to prove reliability, safety, and cost-effectiveness 
in more controlled environments first. Tesla’s own regulatory filings also 
acknowledged that the company has not yet commercialized Bots and cannot 
predict demand from commercial or consumer applications, underscoring 
the early stage of the industry.
