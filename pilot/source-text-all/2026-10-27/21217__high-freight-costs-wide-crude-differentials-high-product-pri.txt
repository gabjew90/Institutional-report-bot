The Freight Rate Rally: Started in the Middle East, but Spread Globally 
 
 
The global average crude freight rate surged above $20/bbl from $3/bbl a year ago, with 
rates on Middle Eastern routes rising above $35/bbl (Exhibit 2). While traded crude 
benchmarks do not directly include transportation and insurance costs in their prices, 
i.e., are priced on a Free-on-Board basis at the producing country’s port of loading, a 
crude buyer—typically a refiner—has to cover crude delivery expenses, including 
transportation and insurance costs. High crude freight rates may therefore reduce 
refiners’ overall demand for crude as the effective price of delivered oil rises, weighing 
on demand for crude shipped via the affected routes. Refiners may also seek more 
closely located suppliers, changing established trading patterns and crude price 
differentials. 
In this Oil Analyst, we analyze the drivers of near-record-high crude freight rates and the 
implications for crude producers and refiners.  
 
The rally in crude freight rates initially began in the very large crude carrier (VLCC) 
segment and on Middle Eastern routes. A large share of cost of transporting oil through 
the Strait of Hormuz is fixed per tanker as it is related to the risk of tanker attacks (e.g., 
crew risk compensation). As a result, larger vessels have been preferred on Middle 
Eastern routes to minimize disruption risk-associated costs per barrel. The premium for 
a VLCC over a tanker with 70% less capacity on the route from the Persian Gulf to Far 
East Asia jumped to $11/bbl in mid-September vs. a $1/bbl discount in 2026H1. 
This cross-segment differential has narrowed substantially over the last two weeks. The 
crude freight rally has now spread across vessel segments and routes, as charterers 
outside the Middle East try to keep ships from diverting to more lucrative Persian Gulf 
routes. Freight rates on long-haul voyages to Asia have increased especially steeply, with 
the West Africa-to-Far East Asia freight rate rising by more than $40/bbl year-over-year 
(yoy) (Exhibit 3).

Explaining the Freight Price Rally 
 
 
Our estimate of the global average crude freight rate has edged down over the last 
couple of days. We suspect that a moderation in the freight risk premium helps to 
explain some of the recent decline in freight rates given limited changes in the tanker 
supply- and demand fundamentals. The weakening in global crude imports over the last 
week is also consistent with some reduction in refiners’ willingness to pay record-high 
delivery costs on top of elevated FOB crude prices. However, the global average crude 
freight rate remains seven times higher than a year ago and is unlikely to sharply drop 
further as long as the conflict in the Middle East persists. Higher freight rates for longer 
pose some upside risk to our Brent forecast along with the risks of more intense shipping 
attacks, damage to oil infrastructure, and a stickier geopolitical risk premium. 
Assuming that fundamentals fully explain the $20/bbl yoy surge in the average global 
crude freight rate (14DMA), we identify five key drivers of the rally (Exhibit 4). 
 
1. Hormuz Crossing War Risk Premium  
Following the recent escalation in attacks on tankers in Hormuz, the risk premium for 
crossing the Strait has risen significantly, reflecting higher insurance costs and crew 
payments. Independent regional shippers likely also have a lot of bargaining power, as 
producers are likely willing to discount their crude to accommodate high transportation 
premiums given their very low breakevens, a desire not to shut in production, and a 
decline in the perceived probability of a quick resolution to the Middle Eastern conflict.

To gauge the risk premium for Hormuz crossings, we compare prices of crude loaded at 
ports outside and inside of the Persian Gulf. The premium for Murban crude assessed at 
the UAE’s Fujairah port (east of Hormuz) vs. Basrah crude assessed at an Iraqi port (west 
of Hormuz) has increased by $35/bbl year-over-year (yoy) (Exhibit 5).1 Since nearly 1/6 
of global seaborne crude exports still go via Hormuz, our $35 war premium estimate 
explains $7/bbl or 1/3 of the yoy increase in the global average crude freight rate. 
 
2. Ship-to-ship Transfers 
A recovery in Persian Gulf crude exports can ease crude prices, but it lifts freight rates 
when ship-to-ship transfers are driving the export increase. About 1/6 of tankers 
operating in the Persian Gulf and Gulf of Oman engage in ship-to-ship transfers every 
day, tying up vessels for longer and reducing fleet availability (Exhibit 6). Kpler reported 
that the average round trip for a Gulf of Oman VLCC engaged in ship-to-ship transfers 
increased to 23 days vs. 10 days for a typical direct Hormuz round trip. We estimate that 
ship-to-ship transfers account for a $4 or 1/5 of the yoy increase in the global average 
crude freight rate.

3. Higher Maritime Fuel Cost 
Crude tanker operating costs have also risen substantially, reflecting higher crew salaries 
and, especially, elevated bunker fuel prices. We estimate that global maritime fuel oil 
prices have increased by nearly $50/bbl, or 70% yoy amid weak exports from the 
Persian Gulf and Russia and resilient bunker demand from tankers and container ships 
(Exhibit 7). Higher fuel costs explain an estimated $4/bbl, or 18%, of the yoy increase in 
the global average crude freight rate.

4. Inefficient Tanker Allocation and Redirections 
Reduced VLCC availability as more vessels are tied up in Persian Gulf transfers has led to 
suboptimal use of smaller tankers on long-haul routes typically dominated by VLCCs. But 
even among available VLCCs, half are currently in ballast—4 percentage points more 
than a year ago (Exhibit 6). The increase in tanker idling is concentrated outside the 
Persian Gulf, where tankers await the shuttle fleet, and near Russian ports amid an 
elevated risk of drone attacks at Black Sea ports. 
Meanwhile, more frequent redirections following attacks in the Red Sea and Black Sea 
lengthen voyages, keeping oil on tankers for longer. We estimate that inefficient tanker 
allocation and redirections explain $3/bbl or 17% of the yoy increase in the global 
average crude freight rate. 
 
5. Blockade of the Iranian Fleet 
The size of the Iranian fleet on the water has shrunk by nearly 40% yoy, with most loaded 
tankers used for storage, as the US blockade has sharply reduced Iranian crude exports 
(Exhibit 9). Although Iranian crude and the Iranian fleet have been sanctioned for years, 
Iranian oil and tankers previously met 1.7mb/d of Asian crude demand. Demand for 
these missing barrels has shifted to other non-sanctioned producers and shippers, 
putting further upward pressure on open-market crude prices and freight rates. We 
estimate that the blockade explains $2/bbl or 10% of the increase in the global average 
crude freight rate.

Overall, we expect the five drivers supporting crude freight rates to persist largely as 
long as the Middle Eastern conflict remains in its current state. Even under our baseline, 
in which Brent crude prices decline to $90/bbl by year-end and average $80/bbl in 
2027, recovering Persian Gulf production and stable exports would require continuing 
ship-to-ship transfers without reliable freedom of navigation through the Strait. The US 
blockade also appears unlikely to be lifted without a peace deal. Moreover, occasional 
attacks on tankers in the region are likely to keep the Hormuz war risk premium elevated. 
A lack of progress in restoring Middle Eastern refined product exports suggests that 
bunker fuel supplies may remain tight for longer. 
Implications from Higher Freight for Crude Producers and Refiners 
 
 
We see high freight rates widening crude price differentials by changing trading patterns, 
supporting refined product prices, and compressing refiners’ net margins. Because the 
recent increases have been particularly pronounced on long-haul routes, producers and 
refiners dependent on these routes for crude distribution and supply are most affected. 
How the burden is shared depends mainly on producers’ ability to find alternative buyers 
and refiners’ ability to find alternative supplies, pass higher transportation costs on to 
consumers, or absorb them. 
1. Crude Producers 
Refiners’ crude demand depends on 1) the effective delivered crude input costs, which 
includes the producer benchmark price, transportation, and insurance, and 2) refined 
product prices. Higher transportation costs therefore raise refiners’ effective crude 
prices and soften demand. Transportation accounts for a larger share of total crude 
costs on long-haul routes. Producers more dependent on distant buyers may 
consequently have to offer deeper discounts to retain demand when freight rates rise

Specifically, Middle Eastern producers sell nearly half their crude through long-haul 
exports, primarily to Far East Asia. Exceptionally high freight rates on Middle Eastern 
routes are pushing producers to discount crude sharply, especially grades shipped from 
within the Persian Gulf (Iraqi Basrah, UAE Upper Zakum, Qatari Al Shaheen). High freight 
costs from the US Gulf Coast to Northwest Europe also widened the Brent-WTI 
differential to $14/bbl last week, compared with the usual $5/bbl, as US producers 
lowered prices to maintain exports of nearly 2mb/d to Europe. 
Meanwhile, as refiners facing high freight rates seek alternative supplies, crude from 
more centrally located producers may benefit from demand reallocation. Specifically, 
Brent in Northwestern Europe strengthened in the first half of October on higher 
demand from European and Mediterranean refiners, while Russia’s ESPO crude 
benefited from some reallocation of Asian demand. 
 
2. Crude Consumers — Refiners 
Similarly, refiners more dependent on crude imported from distant suppliers would have 
to find alternative feedstock, pass higher freight costs on to final consumers, or accept 
lower net refining margins. Chinese and other Asian refiners—which import 50% and 
80% of their crude via long-haul routes, respectively—are particularly vulnerable to 
rising freight rates because freight accounts for a relatively high share of their crude 
costs (Exhibit 11). 
Refiners’ ability to substitute alternative crude is generally limited in the short run: they 
would need to outbid regular buyers, raising the cost of alternative supplies as well. 
Moreover, refineries are typically configured for a specific crude diet, and feedstock 
changes can reduce refined product yields.

demand is also limited by income and hence is limited in low-income economies.

Appendix: Our Global Crude Freight Rate Index 
 
 
Our new global crude freight rate index is a weighted average across 25 main crude 
freight rates. We use Platts freight rate assessments, measured in $/mt. We incorporate 
10 out of the 11 routes included in the Baltic Dirty Tanker Index (we exclude TD14 
because of low associated exports) and 15 additional routes emerged over the last 10 
years, largely to capture strong production growth in the Americas (e.g., US Gulf Coast to 
Europe, China and Canada, Brazil to China) and increased post-2022 exports from 
Russia to Asia (e.g., Russia to India and China). We average the 25 freight rate series with 
a time-varying weighted average, with the weights for each route proportional to a 
12-month moving average of seaborne crude exports with similar origin and destination. 
Our global dirty freight rate index covers 82% of total global exports with known 
destinations (2025 average).
