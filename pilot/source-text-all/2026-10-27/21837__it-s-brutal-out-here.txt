Weekly wrap-up 
Yesterday was a brutal day for markets with the S&P 500 down 1.2% to $7663, dragged lower by 
the Philadelphia Stock Exchange Semiconductor index, which marched its way to the lowest level 
since July of this year. Gold, meanwhile, sank 2.8% to $4023, near August-lows. But bad things for 
equities meant good things for US Treasuries, as the US 10-year yield moved back below 5% for 
the first time since September, despite higher energy prices, and the 2-year made its way back 
below 4.75%. Amid the Fed’s blackout period, the OIS curve implies investors are expecting a hold 
at the October 28 FOMC meeting. 
Across the pond, investors feel similarly about the ECB’s October 29 meeting—a sentiment 
echoed by Rabobank’s resident ECB-whisperer, Bas van Geffen, who says “The ECB has no reason 
to accelerate its hiking cycle” in yesterday’s published ECB-preview. The report also addresses the 
potential for the ECB to leverage its Transmission Protection Instrument should the stress in 
French markets metastasize to the rest of the EU, though such a deployment is likely premature at 
this juncture.  
But French yields continue to rise. Indeed, as the US 10-year breaks below 5%, the French 10-year 
marks a confirmed close above 5%, hitting the highest level since March 2002. To calm the chaos 
in the streets (the chaos in the markets still may be too much to handle), President Macron has 
banned any non-union-led protests on Saturday, hoping for this compensate for the failures of 
the pre-existing bans against looting and arson. 
The Associated Press reports that Iran says seven tankers have been damaged in the Strait of 
Hormuz which, if true, would surmount to the highest number of vessels attacked in the Strait 
since the war began. But President Trump is less convinced, saying on Truth Social that “Iran is 
doing everything they can to drive up the price of Oil prior to the U.S. Midterm Election. They are 
even making up fake hits, mines, and everything else, because more Oil is coming through the 
Hormuz Strait than it did even prior to the beginning of the Conflict.” 
Indeed, two days ago the Wall Street Journal reported that “more oil is now coming out of the 
Persian Gulf than before the Iran War,” citing the success of the “Hormuz Shuttle” and that 
shipment from the Middle East has averaged “more than 24 million barrels a day in the week 
through Oct. 18, surpassing prewar levels of 23.3 million.” 
However, as Rabobank’s Global Energy Strategist, Joe DeLaura, notes, the global energy complex 
is more complicated than just; more barrels = better. “The ‘Hormuz Shuttle’ is boosting crude 
flows through transfers near Oman, but rising insurance costs, tanker inefficiencies and attacks 
mean markets remain far from normal. Persian Gulf–China freight rates have reached $1.4m daily, 
adding roughly $13.5/bbl and particularly burdening Asian refiners. Brent and Shanghai physical 
oil consequently command $10–25 premiums over futures. Higher volumes therefore do not 
guarantee cheaper delivered energy: the cost of delivery is getting so expensive that it threatens 
the recovery.” 
But despite the headlines, markets are digesting Iran’s assertion that the Strait is still dangerous,

Prediction markets are high conviction on a Blue Wave sweeping the House and Senate, with

Canada has continued its efforts to diversify not only exports, but investment and economic 
alliances, away from the US, as Carney and Erdogan met in Ankara to discuss “increased 
cooperation, with a focus on energy and defense,” between Canada and Türkiye. The Globe and 
Mail notes that “Mr. Carney is the first prime minister to make a bilateral trip to Türkiye, purely to 
advance Türkiye-Canada relations.” At front and center was a “defense industry partnership deal” 
to “connect Canadian and Turkish companies to work together, creating good jobs at home, 
export opportunities abroad and a stronger NATO alliance.” 
Day ahead 
Today is PMI day in Europe, starting with preliminary estimates for October across the Eurozone. 
Aggregate Eurozone Manufacturing PMI is expected to tick lower from 52.9 to 52.8, while services 
are and composite measures are expected to fall from 52.5 and 52.7, respectively. The ECB will 
also be releasing its one- and three-year CPI inflation expectations. One-year inflation is expected 
to climb from 3.0% to 3.1%, and the three-year held flat at 2.9%. 
It’s also PMI day in the US, with preliminary estimates for October expected to register better than 
in the EU, but still show deterioration, with manufacturing falling from 55.9 to 55.5, services from 
58.8 to 58, and composite from 58.4 to 57.4. These data will be followed by final estimates for 
October UMich survey data, including sentiment and inflation expectations.
