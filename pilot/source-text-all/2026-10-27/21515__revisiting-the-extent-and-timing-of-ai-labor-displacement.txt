Revisiting the Extent and Timing of AI Labor Displacement 
 
 
Concerns of imminent AI-related labor displacement have cooled in recent months after 
hitting a fever pitch this spring.  As shown in Exhibit 1, the number of layoffs attributed 
to AI has pulled back sharply from its peak this spring.  On a similar note, the number of 
news reports on AI-related job losses surged through May but has since fallen back to 
levels seen at the start of 2026. 
 
As we noted in our most recent AI adoption tracker, the labor market impacts from AI 
remain visible but narrow.  Job growth in the most AI-exposed industries continues to 
underperform by around 10k relative to its long-run trend over the last 3 months 
(consistent with the underperformance over the last several years), although this has 
been roughly offset by increased hiring in AI-related construction jobs. Cross-sectional 
industry-level patterns are starting to show some signs of AI-related hiring headwinds 
from AI adoption, however.  As shown in Exhibit 2, the correlation between adoption and 
unemployment has turned positive (and statistically significant) in the service sector, 
while the correlation between AI adoption and job growth has turned slightly negative 
(albeit not statistically significant).  While these patterns suggest that a modest labor 
market headwind is emerging, the near-term headwinds remain smaller than feared 
earlier this year.

As a result, the AI-labor market discussion has started to shift from whether large job

investment.  As noted by our US portfolio strategy team, current capex projections imply 
that AI users will need to spend roughly $1 trillion annually on AI applications for 
hyperscalers and application providers to generate the profits which markets appear to

Against this backdrop, in this Global Economics Analyst, we use three different empirical 
approaches to revisit how technology has historically displaced labor.  Our results 
continue to suggest that AI automation will deliver significant time and labor cost 
savings, but that the timing and extent of automation and labor disruption will likely 
track the overall economic cycle. 
Historical Data Still Suggests Meaningful Headwinds to Job Growth 
Aggregate Evidence 
Our first approach builds upon our work from last year which considered how the impact 
of a technology-driven increase in productivity impacts the unemployment rate.  Using 
technology shocks identified via long-run sign restrictions (based on an assumption that 
technology shocks lead to permanent increases in labor productivity while demand 
shocks do not), we estimated that each 1pp boost in labor productivity due to 
technological innovation leads to a temporary 0.3-0.4pp peak boost to the 
unemployment rate that fades to zero after two years.

The rise in the unemployment rate due to a technology shock understates the total 
amount of labor displacement, however.  Some workers that are displaced may find jobs 
relatively quickly (thereby limiting the increase in the stock of unemployed workers), 
while others may decide to exit the labor force (thereby limiting the rise in the 
unemployment rate via a reduction in the denominator).  A better measure of 
technology-related labor displacement is therefore the impact on gross job losses (and 
gains). 
In Exhibit 4, we therefore extend our analysis to examine how a 1pp increase in 
productivity due to a positive technology shock (identified the same way as above) 
impacts gross job losses and gross job gains.  We again estimate that each 1pp increase 
in productivity raises the unemployment rate by 0.3-0.4pp, with the increase driven by a 
0.5-0.6pp cumulative increase in the gross job loss rate and a smaller 0.2pp drag on 
gross hiring.  The sum of these cumulative flow estimates are higher than the peak 
increase in the unemployment rate, which confirms that labor force exits or quick 
rehiring limits the rise in unemployment, even when automation is occurring.  
Under our baseline forecasts that AI-related efficiency gains will ultimately deliver a 15% 
gross boost to economy-wide productivity, these estimates imply that up to 9% of 
workers could be displaced during the AI transition, with an additional headwind from 
slower hiring weighing on overall employment by 3%.

Occupation-Level Evidence 
For our third approach, we consider labor market outcomes for 25 occupations that 
were exposed to significant technological disruption (e.g., photo processing, 
proofreaders, file clerks), which we identify (both in terms of occupations and timing) 
based on historical anecdotes and our internal large language models. Given that these 
occupations were selected as cases where technology had the largest impact on labor 
demand, the observed headcount reductions are likely upper bounds for 
technology-driven labor displacement from AI.  That said, the observed dynamics may 
be informative for how AI will impact labor market dynamics for workers in the most 
exposed occupations. 
Exhibit 7 shows headcount reductions in these occupations in the five years after 
employment started to decline in response to a technological innovation.  We find that 
among these occupations, employment fell by around 25% on average, suggesting that 
very large job losses are possible when technology makes labor redundant. 
 
More importantly, we again find that the gross hiring rates declined and gross job 
separations increased following technological disruptions, but that the labor adjustment 
was again more skewed toward an increase in separations. Using microdata from the 
Current Population Survey to track individual hiring and separations from these 
occupations, Exhibit 8 shows that in 13/25 cases the annual gross hiring rate declined, 
with a -0.7pp reduction on average, while in 19/25 cases the annual gross separation 
rate increased, with a 3.8pp increase on average.

Automation Is Cyclical 
Given that our estimates point to a significant amount of technology-related job losses 
to come, why has the impact been limited so far? 
The most obvious reason is that the US economy is still in the early stages of the AI 
transition, with only 23% of US establishments having incorporated AI into some regular 
workflows.  Furthermore, this extensive margin adoption rate overstates the amount of 
automation that is taking place today since most establishments have not yet 
incorporated AI into all work processes where it could ultimately be deployed.  So, while 
there is some preliminary evidence that productivity growth has improved in industries 
where adoption is further along, most of the AI-enabled productivity gains remain in the 
future. 
A second reason for the limited labor impact so far is that historically 
automation-related labor disruption is quite cyclical, with companies tending to 
automate work and shed labor costs when the economy starts to slow.  Indeed, each of 
our three empirical approaches imply more limited disruption when productivity gains 
arrive in a healthy economy but more disruption when productivity gains align with a 
cyclical slowdown. 
First (as we’ve noted previously), aggregate data imply that historically most of the labor 
headwinds from routine job automation are concentrated in economic recessions.  Using 
the definition of routine occupations in Jaimovich and Siu 2018, Exhibit 10 shows that 
the share of routine occupations (as percent of the population) has remained stable 
during expansions but declined sharply during downturns.

Third, our occupation-level analysis shows that occupation-level employment declines 
following the most significant technology disruptions are amplified by negative 
aggregate shocks.  The left chart of Exhibit 12 plots changes in occupation-level 
employment for technology-disrupted jobs against the overall change in the overall

significant negative correlation implies that occupation-level employment declines by an 
incremental 4% for each 1pp incremental increase in the overall unemployment rate. 
The right chart shows that occupation-level separation rates increase and that 
occupation-level job hiring rates decline when the labor market is weaker.   
 
These patterns can help explain why the labor market impacts have been smaller than 
feared so far: Given that the overall economy and labor market have remained resilient 
in 2026, companies may have had less incentive to focus on aggressively cutting labor 
costs. And under our baseline forecasts for near potential growth next year, our 
estimates suggest that AI labor disruption will likely only gradually increase rather than 
delivering an abrupt labor market shock. 
That said, a more concerning takeaway from our analysis is that a cyclical slowdown 
would likely accelerate AI-related automation and job losses, thereby amplifying the 
broader business cycle. The Fed and other central banks should therefore remain 
attuned to any emerging downside growth or labor market risks once the near-term 
inflation constraint is lifted, since a quick pivot to easing could help ensure that 
automation-related job losses do not accelerate at a time when the economy is 
ill-equipped to create the new jobs necessary to support a smooth AI transition.
