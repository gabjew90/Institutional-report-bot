WORLD GOLD COUNCIL
 4
The World Gold Council was founded to sustain and 
strengthen trust and integrity in the gold market, and 
that mandate is why we are stepping into the digital 
gold market. We intend to launch Gold as a Service, 
a shared infrastructure to enable digital gold product 
issuance, because we are confident of the scale of the 
opportunity. This report reinforces that confidence 
with data and proof points drawn from a global 
consumer survey and in-depth conversations with 
industry experts.  
We are also realistic about the scale of the task 
ahead, which includes challenges around trust, 
access, product utility and cost that have constrained 
market growth to date. There is much work to do, 
and no single institution can tackle everything alone.  
Indeed, building a liquid and trusted digital gold 
market will require the industry to embrace the same 
standards, and we are asking our peers and colleagues 
to work with us to bring that vision to life. Industry-
wide, open, and voluntary standards on transparency 
and investor protection could help build trust.
Regulatory clarity matters as much as any 
technical infrastructure. Where rules are unclear or 
inconsistent, digital products struggle to scale, even 
when demand is strong. Governments and regulators 
have a vital role to play, and we will continue to work 
with them to move the agenda forward. 
Digital gold presents both investors and providers 
with a significant opportunity. What must come next is 
collaboration to ensure the growing market meets the 
needs of all those who want to take part.
The appeal of gold has endured for thousands of years because 
what it represents has not changed: a scarce, physical, trusted and 
strategic asset, and a reliable store of value. However, as the financial 
world becomes increasingly digitalised, the gold market must adapt. 
Tokenisation and digital infrastructure will be the architectures 
through which a new global market will be built.

Why does it matter?
We are seeing signs of an inflection point. With more 
recently launched tokenised products triggering a 
surge in activity, the fledgling digital gold market has 
grown into a $45-$55 billion1 category. Tokenised 
gold generated $90 billion of cumulative spot trading 
volumes in the first quarter of 2026, more than in 
the whole of 20252. More importantly, wider financial 
markets are in the midst of a structural digital 
transformation. As a result, tokenised investments are 
increasingly mainstream. 
For the purposes of this report, and to understand 
digital gold’s potential, we surveyed more than 12,000 
retail investors globally and interviewed 20 leading 
experts, helping us unpack the drivers of adoption and 
factors shaping demand. Our key finding:
Appeal is strong but is not (yet) converting at 
scale. Nearly 9 in 10 retail investors signal a willingness 
to invest in digital gold under the right conditions.  
The physical investment gold market is worth about $13 trillion. 
But digital gold, including vaulted digital gold and tokenised gold, 
represents less than 1% of the global market.

Our modelling suggests that if stakeholders address 
the principal barriers to adoption, they can boost both 
participation and portfolio allocations, unlocking up 
to $700 billion3 in latent demand. Fully realised, 
this would create a market that broadly matches 
physically backed gold ETFs today.
Perhaps a more relevant question is: how much 
potential demand can be converted into investment 
over the next five years? Our base case is that the 
digital gold market could grow at approximately  
27% CAGR, reaching $160–$170 billion by 20314.
Fulfilling this potential will be contingent on market 
participants addressing four barriers:  
1 
Trust as the swing factor. Nearly four 
in five investors say their priority is to trust the 
provider before investing: when asked what would 
make them more open to digital gold, the most 
cited answer is evidence that the product is safe and 
legitimate. Another important trust lever is a clear and 
accessible path to redeem holdings for physical gold, 
which would give investors confidence that a real 
asset backs their position. Regulation and consumer 
protection, seen across investor segments as a key 
barrier to entry will play an important role, too.
2 
Access through familiar channels. 
Where digital gold is embedded in mainstream 
channels such as super-apps, payment platforms and 
banking apps, as seen in India, China and Indonesia, 
penetration is up to seven times higher than in 
markets where such distribution does not yet exist. 
3 
New utilities for gold. Unlocking latent 
demand partially hinges on what digital gold 
can be used for, not just how easy it is to buy. Yield 
generation, enabled by lending or use as collateral, 
is the top-cited unique selling point, ahead of being 
able to use it to make payments. This points to a 
potentially significant shift: product innovation could 
give investors new ways to use gold, alongside its 
long-standing role as a store of value.
4 
Competitive pricing is a prerequisite. 
Even where other barriers are addressed, 
digital products must compete on price with 
alternative formats, including physical gold and 
gold-backed ETFs. While a low price is not, on its 
own, a major reason to invest in digital gold, an 
uncompetitive price point is ranked as the second 
most common barrier to entry. 
New money for gold. 
Around 65% of latent demand comes 
from people who have not yet invested 
in gold. Among those who already hold 
gold in other forms, more than half would 
fund a digital gold investment with money 
not currently allocated to the asset. Rather 
than cannibalising demand for bars, coins or 
gold-backed ETFs, digital gold is thus likely to 
attract new investors, and new money.
Critical for near-term uptake is the 
activation of a younger audience. Awareness 
of digital gold reaches 50% among 25–34-year-
olds, compared with just 23% among those 
aged 55 and older. Younger cohorts are twice as 
likely as older cohorts to say they would invest if 
barriers to entry were addressed. 
In conclusion, the next five years will be crucial 
in determining whether digital gold will break 
out. Success in addressing the four barriers will 
spur participation, while a failure to address 
them at meaningful scale could leave digital 
gold as a relative niche in the wider market.

This report considers digital gold from the retail investor’s point of 
view. It sizes the market today and shows why so few investors who 
find it appealing have bought it, and what it would take to change 
that. The report focuses on two products, tokenised gold and vaulted 
digital gold (see page 9: This report’s scope). Our findings draw on a 
survey of more than 12,000 retail investors, interviews with 20 industry 
experts and a market model which points to three scenarios for the 
years up to 2031.
Digital gold sits in one of the largest, most liquid 
asset classes in the world, with about 220,000 
tonnes above ground. The gold market is worth 
more than $30 trillion at current prices, spanning 
jewellery, bars, coins, gold-backed ETFs, OTC5 and 
central bank reserves. The physical investment 
gold market is worth about $13 trillion6.

While physical gold is enduring, the ways in which 
people own it have changed over time: from bars 
and coins to paper certificates, ETFs, vaulted digital 
accounts and, most recently, tokens. Each innovation 
has lowered barriers to entry and expanded the 
market, often by splitting gold into smaller and more 
affordable units; from 400-ounce bars to one-ounce 
coins, to ETF shares worth just a few dollars.
Tokenisation extends that trend, offering the 
potential to divide ownership into even smaller 
fractions. It also connects gold to a broader shift 
in the financial industry, which is that digital assets 
are becoming increasingly mainstream, supported by 
younger, digitally native investors.

For now, digital gold remains a niche product.
Its total value outstanding is about $45-$55 billion, equivalent to around 370 
tonnes. This represents roughly 0.4% of physical investment gold and less than 
9% of the $600 billion gold-backed ETF market. One reason is that digital gold has 
faced structural challenges which have constrained adoption.
This report's scope
For the purposes of this report, digital gold 
encompasses two product categories (Exhibit 2):
Tokenised gold: A digital token that gives investors 
ownership of a specific quantity of physical gold 
held in secure custody. Each token corresponds  
to a fixed quantity of gold and the token price 
tracks the gold price. 
Vaulted digital gold (digital gold certificate): 
An app- or platform-based account enabling 
investors to own physical gold held in secure 
custody by a third party.
Physically-backed gold ETFs, leveraged products, 
and gold-backed stablecoins are excluded from  
the definition.

Examples are non-exhaustive
Of the two digital gold products considered here, 
vaulted digital gold is the more established, with the 
first products dating back to 2005 (e.g., BullionVault)7. 
Tokenised gold products such as Paxos Gold (PAXG) 
were first launched in 2019. That difference in market 
maturity is reflected in volumes. Vaulted digital 
gold accounts for about 89% of the digital gold 
market by value, while tokenised gold accounts 
for about 11%. Both product categories are highly 
concentrated, with a handful of providers associated 
with the majority of volumes. 
For vaulted digital gold, participants are drawn from 
across the gold value chain. They include bullion 
dealers and vault operators, sovereign mints, refiners 
and banks, alongside distributors such as super-apps 
and independent platforms. Meanwhile, tokenised 
gold issuance has generally been confined to a small 
group of crypto-native entities. But that is changing, 
with interest now expanding across the value chain 
and to more traditional financial institutions. HSBC’s 
Gold Token is one example8.

Vaulted digital gold's scale is also concentrated 
geographically, with more than half of it held in

That said, tokenised gold has been the primary source

Distribution through familiar 
channels drives mass adoption
Consumers tend to invest smaller amounts but 
at far higher penetration rates in markets where 
digital gold is available fractionally through familiar, 
mainstream channels. In China, for example, digital 
gold can be accessed through platforms such as 
WeChat, which has more than 1.4 billion monthly 
active users12. In India, it is available through 
Paytm, PhonePe and Google Pay, and in Indonesia 
through Pegadaian and Bank Syariah Indonesia. 
These channels provide access to large, established 
consumer bases and enable investment at much 
lower average ticket sizes than channels available in 
other markets. As a result, awareness and adoption 
are significantly higher: current penetration is up 
to seven times greater than in less mature markets, 
while awareness is more than double (Exhibit 4).
In markets where products are offered through 
specialist or less familiar channels (crypto exchanges 
or bullion-dealer platforms rather than banking 
apps), awareness is much lower and ownership 
remains concentrated among the wealthy. 
Penetration among higher-wealth investors is 
approximately two and a half times that of lower-
wealth investors. Access, in other words, determines 
not just how many people invest but who they are. 
Where access is constrained, demand patterns 
reflect those of specialist investment products.

Digital gold offers significant growth potential, but 
it lags when it comes to converting awareness into 
ownership (Exhibit 5). This stands in contrast to other 
investment products, which convert at higher rates.  
One reason is that they have addressed structural 
barriers, while digital gold has not.
What is holding investors back?
Four barriers stand in the way of scaling: 
These are trust, access, utility and cost. 
Trust is the swing factor. More evidence that the product 
is safe and legitimate, together with clearer information 
for beginners, are the factors most often cited as 
potential spurs to investment. Beyond those factors, 
33% say that better proof and transparency of 
physical gold backing would increase adoption, 
and 35% say that clearer regulation and consumer 
protections would have the same effect. 
Utility is another potential driver of uptake. If investors 
could pay with digital gold, earn a yield through 
lending, or borrow against it, they would have more 
reasons to buy. Access and cost complete the picture. 
While they are not key drivers of demand, restrictions 
on where digital gold can be bought and whether it 
can compete with alternative investment products 
are cited as top reasons that would prevent someone 
investing in the future.

Achieving the base case means making progress on 
all four barriers, meaning the digital gold market must 
focus on: 
    
Building trust. 
In many markets, product standards and 
regulatory regimes remain fragmented or absent, 
and often it is unclear who the relevant regulator 
should be. Equally, digital gold investors need greater 
assurance on physical backing and protection in the 
event of issuer insolvency or misconduct.   
More than 40% of survey respondents 
say that a major custody failure, fraud  
or hack would damage their confidence. 
While the products are ready, the ecosystem around 
them needs to catch up. Boosting trust in digital gold 
will require clear and consistent standards, effective 
regulation and greater transparency around product 
structures, as well as physical backing and investor 
safeguards. Still, there are signs that regulatory 
momentum is building. Indonesia has introduced 
a dedicated digital gold regulatory regime, while 
the UK Financial Conduct Authority has launched 
a Call for Input on tokenised gold. Hong Kong has 
committed to bringing gold-backed tokens into its 
tokenised investment product framework. These 
initiatives will not only reassure potential investors 
but provide the clarity that financial institutions need 
to bring products to market. 
 
 
 
Regulatory clarity addresses one 
source of doubt; physical backing is the 
other, and redemption is where that 
backing is proven. 
Redemption is possible today, but the terms are 
often restrictive, both in terms of where the metal 
can be redeemed (in many cases, one location 
worldwide) and in the minimum value required (for 
example, a 400-ounce bar). As a result, redemption 
currently functions mainly as a guarantee, and 
issuers that already offer it say it is a primary reason 
why customers trust the product. Widening that 
guarantee, through more redemption locations 
and lower minimum thresholds would fuel demand. 
Customers will trust digital gold products more if 
they can sell easily and exchange the product for 
cash or the physical metal. 
What would it take to achieve our 
base case?

Unlocking access.  
Digital gold must become easier to find and 
buy. This means offering products through reputable, 
familiar institutions and integrating them into 
established channels.
 
For the most part, digital gold is not yet accessible 
through everyday financial platforms. Indeed, particularly 
in the case of tokenised gold, access remains limited, 
often requiring investors to engage with unfamiliar 
routes to market, such as setting up a crypto wallet. As a 
result, adoption has been concentrated among crypto-
native and digitally confident investors. 
Where digital gold has been embedded into channels 
that investors already use, the results have been 
compelling. In India and Indonesia, digital gold 
products have been integrated into super-apps,

5.

The scenarios described in Section 3 hinge on how quickly the four barriers come down, but there will not be 
progress without deliberate effort. Change is predicated on progress across the market, from token issuers, 
vaulting providers, and regulators, as well as banks and platforms that ultimately connect with retail investors.
So how will we know if it is working? 
Four signals, each likely to manifest before 2031,  
will show whether digital gold is on track for the base 
case, the bull case or neither:
1 
Trust taking shape through 
regulation and redemption.  
The signal is whether consultations turn into 
regulation, from the UK FCA's Call for Input on 
tokenised gold to Hong Kong's move to bring 
gold-backed tokens into its tokenised investment 
framework, and whether redemption terms loosen, 
with physical gold available at more locations and 
lower minimums. Clarity on both fronts in leading 
markets is the base case, while a harmonised global 
framework paired with broadly available redemption 
would favour the bull case. With trust seen as the 
single biggest barrier for every group of investors, 
this is the one to watch first.
2 
Major banks putting digital gold 
in their apps.  
Asian markets have shown that making digital gold 
available through channels customers already use and 
trust, and especially banking apps, significantly drives 
uptake. Our base case is that banks in the remaining 
leading markets will follow suit. Banks and other 
scaled platforms offering access everywhere would 
point to the bull case. 
 
3 
New revenue models  
unlock competitive pricing.  
Digital gold providers are finding new ways to make 
money beyond simply charging customers for holding 
gold. By offering additional products and earning a 
share of trading fees, providers can generate new 
sources of income. Enabled by new technology and 
digital banking platforms, these revenue streams can 
help cover the costs of holding gold, allowing providers 
to offer customers more competitive prices.
4 
Utility breaking out  
of the crypto niche.  
Yield and collateral use cases are already live but 
mostly on crypto-native platforms. The signal is 
whether regulated institutions adopt them and 
whether payments follow. Partial progress in the 
leading markets is the base case. All three uses at 
scale would point to the bull case.
If the signals emerge faster and more powerfully 
than expected, digital gold can do what every earlier 
innovation in gold ownership has done: lower barriers 
to entry and grow the market as a whole. 
Capturing that opportunity will take an industry-wide 
effort. The World Gold Council is committed to act 
as a convenor, bringing together stakeholders who 
can make it happen. We encourage any organisation, 
regulator or individual who wants to shape this 
movement to reach out.

Methodology
Data inputs 
The findings of this report rest on a combination of primary and secondary research conducted between July and October 
2026. The primary research, conducted by Toluna, comprised a consumer survey of over 12,000 respondents in 12 markets 
and a programme of interviews with experts from across the gold and digital assets value chain. The secondary research 
comprised a product-by-product review of the existing market landscape, together with third-party datasets of country-level 
forecasts for population, banked and digitally enabled rates, and investable wealth. The survey covered Australia, Brazil, 
Canada, China, Germany, India, Indonesia, Japan, Mexico, South Africa, the UK and the US. Each market contributed around 
1,000 respondents, balanced on age, gender, income and region, so that results can be read both market by market and 
in aggregate. It explored what investors know about digital gold, what they own, what holds them back and what would 
persuade them to invest. Interviews were also conducted with 20 experts from organisations spanning issuers and platforms, 
refiners and mints, banks, asset managers, exchanges and market infrastructure, and industry bodies.  
 
 
Market sizing 
The market today  
Today's market has been sized from the bottom up: the sum of total assets under custody across all identified product that 
meets this report's definition of digital gold. The review identified over 70 products worldwide. For each product it recorded 
what is available and through which channels, the terms on which it is offered and, where publicly disclosed, total assets 
under custody, customer numbers and average holdings. Twenty-five of these products disclose their assets under custody 
and account for the large majority of the market. The remainder form a long tail of smaller issuers whose holdings have been 
estimated from customer counts or other proxies. All disclosures are restated on a common gold-price basis. 
Market growth  
From this base, future growth is modelled as new demand added on top of today's market. The model sizes each market as 
the product of four terms, summed across 96 countries, which together represent 98% of global investable wealth: 
addressable investors (#) × investable wealth per investor ($) × share holding digital gold (%) × share of portfolio allocated to 
digital gold (%) 
The first two terms come from external data sources. The last two carry the uplifts in share holding digital gold (%) and share 
of portfolio allocated (%), which are derived from the survey results.  
For each of the 96 countries, the population aged 15 and over (World Bank population forecasts) is narrowed first to those 
with a bank account and then to those able to transact digitally (World Bank Global Findex), producing the number of 
addressable investors. This figure is multiplied by the country's average investable wealth per investor (BCG Global Wealth 
Report). Average investable wealth includes financial assets but excludes the largest individual holdings, as these are not 
addressable through retail products. Both inputs are expressed in today's values and projected annually on a country-by-
country basis, so that both the number of addressable investors and their average investable wealth grow over time. These 
are what the report describes as the “underlying drivers of growth”. 
The larger driver is the removal of barriers, the effect of which was sized using a conjoint analysis. As part of the survey, 
each respondent was shown a series of cards, each describing a digital gold product with a different combination of 
barriers removed or left in place. For each card they indicated how much they would invest. Because the combinations vary 
systematically across cards and respondents, the effect of each barrier can be estimated independently of the others. This 
gives two values for each barrier removed, both set out in Chapter 2: the uplift in the share holding digital gold (%), and the 
uplift in the share of portfolio allocated to digital gold (%).

Because the uplifts are measured only in the surveyed markets, each remaining country is matched to the surveyed market 
that best represents how its investors are likely to behave. Proxy markets were selected using four criteria: GDP per capita, 
geographic proximity, precious metals holdings per capita, and crypto holdings per capita. Each country then inherits its 
proxy market's uplifts, applied to its own growth in addressable investors and investable wealth. 
Total latent demand and the growth scenarios answer two different questions, and the two figures are not directly comparable. 
Total latent demand: if every barrier fell at once, at today's population and wealth, the market would be approximately 
$700 billion larger than it is now. Nothing is phased and nothing grows, so the figure carries no date. It is the ceiling: 
how much demand the barriers hold back today, assuming investors respond in full and immediately. 
Growth scenarios: these ask how much of that demand is realistically converted by 2031. Here population and wealth grow 
year by year, barriers fall in different years in different markets, and each uplift phases in gradually rather than landing at 
once. Because those ramp periods run longer than the forecast window, only a portion of the latent demand is captured by 
2031. The gap between the scenario outcomes and the $700 billion is therefore a question of how quickly demand converts, 
not of whether it exists. 
Three things change between scenarios: which barriers are removed, when they are removed, and the ramp-up period, in 
years, from removal to the point at which the full uplift is realised. These assumptions are anchored in the product review, 
the expert interviews and public disclosures on regulation and forthcoming launches. The ramp-up period is benchmarked 
against the adoption curves of comparable product transitions. 
Three scenarios are modelled: a bear case in which no barriers are removed, so growth comes from the underlying drivers 
alone; a base case in which barriers are partially removed and the uplift materialises over a longer, 15-year period; and a bull 
case in which barriers fall faster and more widely. The detail of each scenario is set out in Chapter 3. 
 
 
Intentionally excluded from modelling 
A major hack or security breach could materially damage trust in digital gold. Among respondents, 44% said this would most 
undermine their confidence in the category.
A significant custody failure or fraud at a large issuer could trigger a broader collapse in retail trust, comparable to the major 
crypto-exchange failures of 2022. Some 41% of respondents said this would most damage their confidence.
Neither is reflected in the scenarios, which assume the market continues to function as it does today.
The modelling also excludes the effect of a material change in the gold price. A significant move either way would affect both 
the reported value of the market and investor demand, and could produce outcomes outside the scenarios presented.
