Trump invokes Cold War-era Defense Production Act for oil production, refining 
US President Donald Trump determined on Thursday that petroleum production, refining, and related activities are 
essential to national defense, a move that could open up US funding for fossil fuel projects. 
 
In a presidential memo, Trump also invoked part of the Cold War-era Defense Production Act, allowing him to provide 
financial incentives for oil-related activities, pipelines and storage of liquid fuels. 
 
Prices for crude oil and diesel have surged since the US and Israel attacked Iran in joint action at the end of February. 
Since then, the conflict has limited energy shipments globally, with Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries exacerbating 
the fuel shortages. 
 
The surge in costs poses a risk for Trump in the November 3 elections, as his fellow Republicans try to maintain control of 
both chambers of Congress. The Reuters/Ipsos opinion poll that closed Monday shows just 36% of Americans approve of 
Trump's handling of the economy. 
 
The memo said that without taking the actions, the US is not able to provide enough of those energy services in a "timely 
manner due to constrained financing, long lead times, permitting and infrastructure bottlenecks, and supply chain 
limitations." 
 
The memo did not mention specific projects that could be funded. The US averaged around 13.6 million barrels of daily 
crude production in 2025, making it the largest crude producer worldwide. Last week, Vice President JD Vance said the 
administration would use the Defense Production Act to expand capacity of a Texas refinery owned by Delek US 
Holdings. 
 
Trump signed legislation last year that included $1 billion in Defense Production Act funding for energy-related 
projects deemed critical to national security. 
 
US refiners have run harder in 2026 as tight diesel supplies boost margins, EIA says 
The US Energy Information Administration said on Thursday that US refiners have maintained higher-than-normal run 
rates this year, buoyed by strong margins stemming from tight global supplies of refined fuel products. 
 
Refinery utilization has averaged more than 95% of capacity between May and September, the highest for those months 
since 1998, the EIA said.

Trump's tax-exempt diesel draws skepticism from farmers, eludes others 
US President Donald Trump signed an executive order this month to expand access to tax-exempt diesel as soaring 
prices frustrate voters ahead of midterm elections. But farmers say it is too risky to use the fuel on public roads, while 
some other drivers do not have access to it. 
 
Trump has sought to lower prices and boost his sagging approval ratings through a series of actions focused on fuel, 
including an $8 billion financial support program he announced on Monday to offset diesel costs for farmers during the 
autumn harvest. 
 
His administration also relaxed sanctions on Russian fuel shipments, pushed allies to release emergency oil stocks and 
temporarily lifted restrictions on foreign-flagged vessels moving between US ports. Still, prices remain elevated because 
of the US-led war with Iran. 
 
Trump said his executive order would temporarily waive an off-road requirement for red-dyed diesel, which is generally 
sold for use in farm equipment. Red diesel is essentially the same as regular or clear diesel but exempt from highway fuel 
taxes. It is dyed red to indicate highway taxes have not been paid. 
 
Farmers said potential savings from using dyed diesel in semi-trucks on highways were too small to justify the risk of red 
residue staying in fuel tanks after the order expires. States have their own taxes and rules for red diesel use, and can fine 
drivers thousands of dollars if authorities inspect fuel tanks and find signs red diesel was used improperly on public roads. 
 
The Internal Revenue Service said it would not impose a penalty when dyed diesel is used on highways from October 1 
to December 31 but that Trump's order had no effect on state taxes and penalties.

"In my mind, it's not anywhere near worth the risk," said Glenn Brunkow, president of the Kansas Farm Bureau, who 
raises crops and cattle near Wamego, Kansas. "That residue might be in there for years." 
 
FINES UP TO $10,000 IN KANSAS 
 
Farmers use the most diesel during the autumn harvest season, burning dyed fuel in tractors, combines and other 
machinery. Under Trump's order, they could also use red diesel to haul crops to market in semi-trucks normally fueled by 
clear diesel, if allowed by states. 
 
Kansas, a major grain producer, prohibits using dyed diesel on public roadways unless it is purchased lawfully outside the 
state. Violators can be charged with a felony, have their driver's license suspended, and face penalties of $1,000 to 
$10,000, according to the Kansas Department of Revenue. 
 
Governor Laura Kelly, a Democrat, said the state could not legally implement Trump's order as drafted. The Kansas 
Highway Patrol was concerned it "would result in law enforcement difficulties once red dye diesel returns to its normal 
usage restrictions," she said in a statement. Democrats hope surging farm costs can help revive their support in the 
Republican heartland in November's midterm elections. 
 
Trump wants lower fuel prices, White House spokesperson Taylor Rogers said. "President Trump remains committed to 
unleashing American energy dominance, cutting costs, and putting more money back in the pockets of hardworking 
American families," Rogers said. 
 
For Jay Sorg, a fifth-generation farmer in New Richland, Minnesota, the order could save $1,500 to $2,000, but that is not 
enough to offset higher costs for fuel and other inputs. Like many farmers, he buys diesel in advance to store in tanks. "In 
the grand scheme of our business, it's kind of inconsequential, especially when we already have fuel bought and in the 
tank for highway use," Sorg said. "It's a move to try to win votes." 
 
Ryan Wagner, a farmer in Roslyn, South Dakota, worried that increasing usage of tax-exempt red diesel could drain 
government funds used to maintain roads. "It's a very short-sighted plan that could come back and haunt us in the future," 
he said.

Argentina exchange holds wheat forecast steady despite frost damage 
Late frosts have hit up to 15% of wheat in parts of Argentina, the Buenos Aires Grains Exchange said on Thursday, 
though it maintained its 2026/27 harvest forecast at 23.4 million tons. 
 
The exchange warned that recent low temperatures, rain and limited sunshine could further dent yields if conditions 
persist. "Crop condition does not reflect considerable changes," the exchange said in its weekly report, adding that 
continued adverse weather "could compromise yield potential." 
 
Argentina's wheat harvest begins in November. The Rosario grains exchange on Wednesday raised its wheat harvest 
estimate to 23.8 million tons. Cold, wet conditions in the west and south of the farming belt are delaying corn planting, 
with sowing 39.8% complete. 
 
The exchange maintained its 2026/27 corn forecast at 66 million tons. Argentina is the world's third-biggest corn exporter. 
 
 
 
Nickel Industries says Indonesia cuts coming as Chinese producers weigh steep reductions 
Australia-listed Nickel Industries says nickel producers in the Indonesia Morowali Industrial Park (IMIP), a key production 
base for the metal, will coordinate on output cuts as Chinese operators weigh steep reductions. 
 
"There will be coordinated cuts across all of the operations within the IMIP," Managing Director Justin Werner told 
analysts on an earnings call, without giving a percentage. "Certainly what we're seeing is the non-integrated producers ... 
are the ones that are going to suffer the hardest here." 
 
Nickel Industries owns a 10% stake alongside China's Huayou Cobalt in the Huayue nickel and cobalt project in the IMIP 
on the island of Sulawesi. It also counts nickel giant Tsingshan Group as a major shareholder. 
 
Chinese producers are considering coordinated production cuts following a meeting earlier this week, people familiar with 
the matter said, but they have not decided on the size or duration of any reductions. 
 
Companies are weighing halving production of mixed hydroxide precipitate (MHP), an intermediate product used to make 
chemicals for electric vehicle batteries, according to three sources.

Senior executives are also weighing cutting output of nickel pig iron (NPI), a stainless steel raw material, by 20%, said two 
of the sources and another two people familiar with the matter. 
 
Indonesia, the world's largest nickel producer, accounted for about two-thirds of global mined output in 2025, according to 
the US Geological Survey, so coordinated cuts by its Chinese operators could tighten supply of both battery materials and 
stainless steel feedstock as prices fall. 
 
The duration and volumes affected by any cuts remain unclear, the people said, as executives review output data for this 
year and consult shareholders of the projects. 
 
It also remains unclear whether proposed cuts would take into account reductions already underway across the industry 
for months, one trading source said. 
 
Companies involved include Tsingshan, Huayou, CNGR Advanced Material, Lygend Resources and GEM, according to 
the people, who spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorised to discuss the matter publicly. The 
companies did not immediately respond to requests for comment. 
 
Bloomberg first reported plans for Tuesday's meeting. Nickel on the London Metal Exchange jumped 3.7% on 
Wednesday following the report. 
 
Nickel Industries itself has reduced its Indonesian NPI production to 75% of capacity this month and delayed the full 
ramp-up of another nickel and cobalt project due to a lack of rainfall linked to El Niño, Werner said. 
 
Producers also face slumping nickel prices and rising production costs amid sulphur shortages and tighter Indonesian 
nickel ore mining quotas. 
 
Nickel fell nearly 25% from this year's $20,000-a-ton peak to $15,070 last week, its lowest since December 2025. NPI 
prices have also fallen, reaching 1,009 yuan ($151) per nickel unit on Wednesday from more than 1,100 yuan in early 
September. The sulphur squeeze already prompted an MHP production cut earlier this year.

Russian energy officials urge Europe to reconsider ban on Russian gas imports 
Senior Russian political and energy officials urged the European Union on Thursday to reconsider its plans to ban imports 
of Russian LNG, arguing the bloc's low storage levels had put Europe in a precarious position. 
 
The EU plans to halt imports of Russian gas completely by the start of next year in response to the war in Ukraine. 
However, those plans have been put in jeopardy due to unusually low gas stocks, while the European Court of Auditors 
said last month that the bloc was not investing enough to achieve its aims of fully replacing Russian energy with 
diversified fossil fuel supplies. 
 
Last year, the EU imported 14.94 million metric tons of liquefied natural gas from the Novatek-led Yamal LNG project in 
Russia's western Arctic. "I hope there is still time to reconsider these decisions," Leonid Mikhelson, the head of Novatek, 
Russia's largest liquefied natural gas producer, told an economic forum in Istanbul. 
 
"Under the 21st package (of EU sanctions against Russia), a decision was taken allowing European companies that had 
long-term contracts, and the European companies handling logistics under those contracts, to continue working with 
Russia to supply any other markets. It is worth considering whether some of this LNG could also remain on the European 
market. "Europe should support its industry, in my view. There is still time," he said. 
 
Russia, holder of world's largest natural gas reserves, has been able to divert its crude oil away from Europe to China and 
India, but its progress to re-route LNG to other markets has been slow. Supplies of Russian gas to Europe also suffered a 
serious blow after the September 2022 blasts at the twin Nord Stream pipelines on the bed of the Baltic Sea. 
 
Kirill Dmitriev, an envoy of Russian President Vladimir Putin told the conference that the Nord Stream gas pipelines will 
resume operations at some point in future. "It's economically inevitable," he said. "The resumption of energy supplies from 
Russia is absolutely inevitable," he added. 
 
The EU banned Russian pipeline gas under short-term contracts from June 17, 2026, and will apply it to long-term 
contracts from November 1, 2027. 
 
Russian energy giant Gazprom, the sole exporter of Russian pipeline gas, has in recent months published regular 
updates on low gas storage levels in Europe prior to the winter heating season.

US tariff war is fueling Vietnam's coal boom: Maguire 
A years-long trade war between the United States and China has helped shift manufacturing investment, factory output 
and export production into Vietnam, driving the Southeast Asian nation's coal-fired electricity generation to record highs. 
 
Vietnam's coal plants generated 132.9 terawatt hours (TWh) of electricity during the first nine months of 2026, which is a 
15% jump from the same months last year and the highest for that period on record, according to data from Ember. 
 
A roughly 12% decline in year-to-date hydro power production due to below-normal rainfall this summer has been a key 
driver of the greater coal-fired activity, forcing utilities to offset stunted reservoir output with higher coal production. But a 
larger factor has been the steady swell in Vietnam's export-oriented manufacturing sector, which has grown rapidly within 
the past decade and lifted the country's total electricity demand by nearly 50% since 2019. 
 
In many respects, that manufacturing expansion can be traced back to Washington. US tariffs on Chinese imports since 
2018 encouraged manufacturers to diversify production into lower-cost locations, with Vietnam emerging as one of the 
biggest winners. 
 
The shift has helped supercharge Vietnam's factory sector and power demand, but it has also tied an increasing volume 
of export production destined for the US market to a power system that still relies heavily on coal for electricity generation. 
 
AMERICA'S FACTORY FLOOR 
 
The switch in production origins shows up clearly in US trade data. In 2018, US imports from China were roughly 10 times 
larger than imports from Vietnam. This year, monthly imports from the two countries have been nearly level. 
 
When US imports from China hit their peak of roughly $539 billion, average monthly US trade flows to China were around 
$45 billion, data from LSEG shows. 
 
During that same year, average US monthly trade flows to Vietnam were around $4 billion. So far in 2026, average 
monthly US trade flows to China and Vietnam have been around $22.9 billion and $22 billion, respectively. 
 
That more than 400% rise in the value of Vietnam's monthly exports to the US helped fuel an economy-wide boom, with 
Vietnam's gross domestic product (GDP) posting an average annual expansion of 7.4% since 2022, according to the 
International Monetary Fund. 
 
That growth pace was one of the world's fastest during that period, and in turn triggered sustained growth in Vietnam's 
total electricity consumption, which expanded at an average rate of 6% a year between 2022 and 2025, data from Ember 
shows.

COAL-HEAVY POWER MIX 
 
Vietnam's electricity system is heavily reliant on coal, with coal-fired power stations accounting for an average of around 
49% of total utility supplies since 2019. 
 
By comparison, coal accounts for around a third of electricity generation globally, which means that Vietnam has a 
substantially more coal-dependent power system than most. However, coal's share of Vietnam's power mix can fluctuate 
significantly depending on the availability of alternative power sources in any given year. 
 
For instance, coal only accounted for 38.5% of the power mix in 2022 thanks to strong hydro production that year as well 
as a surge in international coal prices, which spurred nearly all cost-sensitive power systems to pare back coal use. 
 
So far in 2026, coal's share has averaged nearly 52% of the generation mix due to the lowest hydro production tally since 
2023. That coal share is the highest for that period since 2020, and has resulted in a 16% jump in power sector emissions 
to a record 129 million metric tons of carbon dioxide and equivalent gases so far this year, data from Ember shows. 
 
And with the peak hydro production period already passed, additional gains in both coal use and coal emissions are 
likely, which will help cement 2026 as a record for Vietnam's coal power reliance. 
 
Over the longer term, Vietnam does have significant non-coal power capacity under construction, including around 11 
gigawatts (GW) of gas-fired power, 1.4 GW of new hydro capacity, and around 1.3 GW of wind capacity, according to 
Global Energy Monitor. 
 
But there are also an additional 2.7 GW of new coal plants being built, with a further 2.6 GW planned after that. That 
means that, having won a growing share of the US import demand, Vietnam looks set to primarily power that production 
with coal. 
 
That in turn may serve to keep the cost of goods production relatively low, but comes with its own climate price tag that 
US importers may have to wrestle with as and when their supply chains come under scrutiny.

Romania to speed up development of Black Sea capabilities after cargo ship attacks 
Romania will accelerate its development of surveillance capabilities and equipment to counter mines and drones in the 
Black Sea after a series of drone attacks on commercial ships off its coast, its top defence council said on Thursday. 
At least seven cargo ships have been hit near the coasts of NATO members Romania and Bulgaria in the past two 
weeks, with some 30 sailors reported dead or missing. Romanian F-16 fighter jet pilots shot down two of the latest 
generation of Russian drones over Romanian territorial waters on Saturday. 
NATO said on Tuesday it will send more maritime patrol aircraft, radar systems and drones to the Black Sea. Romania's 
top defence council said latest ship attacks are direct consequences of Russia's aggression beyond Ukraine's borders. 
Threats to freedom of navigation and critical energy projects in the Black Sea pose a security crisis for Romania. 
The current risks require concrete measures to protect human lives, maintain free access to national ports, and ensure 
the security of energy facilities in the Exclusive Economic Zone. 
The top defence council said it will accelerate the development of capabilities for maritime surveillance, countering naval 
mines and intervening against drones illegally breaching national airspace and waters. Authorities will practice to improve 
the efficiency of their operational response. 
Romania will seek to improve cooperation with fellow NATO members Bulgaria and Turkey with whom it shares the Black 
Sea. 
"The Council members also analyzed ways to intensify coordination within NATO. This includes strengthening the 
deterrence and defense posture on the Eastern Flank and identifying new measures to respond to threats in the Black 
Sea region."
