CoreWeave (CRWV): Core-We've got bills to pay... lowering our PT
CoreWeave had a pretty bad week last week. In the last seven days, we’ve spoken with hedge
funds, LOs, bulls, bears, credit people, REIT investors, and everybody in between. These are
the top questions and themes that emerged:
How bad is the debt problem, and what do they need to fund the committed backlog?
Not great, not a surprise. We estimate CRWV needs to raise $85B to fund the existing
committed backlog, $9B of which is already covered by existing facilities. The unsecured
credit market are still open to them at rates in the 15%+ range, given where the bonds are
trading today, but we think there are a range of more reasonable options.
How does this problem evolve with their long-term shift towards shorter-term
contracts and more enterprise business? Herein lies the problem: CRWV needs to
pursue shorter-dated contracts, ideally with enterprise customers to be more competitive
and sustainable. Shorter-dated contracts allow them to capture higher $/MW. Enterprise
customers are likely to support longevity (hyperscalers will compete). Unfortunately those
contracts are riskier to creditors and do not help CRWV lower its cost of capital. DDTL 5.5
was at SOFR+5.5%—this is the best proxy; subsequent deals could price even higher.
What are the downside scenarios and how does this get resolved? Will NVDA step in?
We do not believe CRWV is a 0—they still have a large backlog, line of sight to powered shell
delivery, and while we don’t think the contracts are super attractive, they’re not underwater.
Whether NVDA (covered by Rasgon) will step in is meatier; we think they would intervene in a
true downside. Doesn’t necessarily re-energize the stock, but raises the floor.
Are we concerned about other neoclouds and/or the broader AI trade? For all our
bearishness, we think this could be a semi-contained risk. CRWV’s got high debt, very high
capex requirements, and questionable long-run demand. If it ceased to exist tomorrow, colo
contracts would be absorbed and the rest of the market would carry on.
We caught a lot of hate when we initiated on CRWV with a $56 price target seven months
ago. Today, it’s a lot harder to find bulls—certainly some of them just don’t call us anymore,
but there seems to be more broad acceptance of the challenges of this business model.
Could this turn around? Maybe: it’s a wildly volatile, momentum-driven stock, but barring
major moves, the fundamentals are probably stuck.

DETAILS
Over the past week, we have received a significant number of investor questions on CoreWeave amid recent market volatility
and heightened scrutiny of its ability to raise capital and at what cost. The discussions have centered on the company's financing
flexibility, the capital required to deliver its backlog, and the returns ultimately available to equityholders. Below, we consolidate
the main themes emerging from these conversations and provide our current thinking on CoreWeave’s financing strategy,
capital requirements, and overall economics.
Our overarching view remains, unsurprisingly, cautious: funding the backlog is only part of the challenge. The more
consequential questions are what that funding will cost, what returns CoreWeave can earn after financing and reinvestment
requirements, and how those economics evolve as its customer mix changes.
1. FINANCING STRATEGY, LEVERAGE, AND DEBT STRUCTURE
CoreWeave's use of equity ATMs and unsecured financing has prompted questions about its ability to continue funding growth
through secured debt. In our view, the broader funding strategy reflects substantial capital requirements alongside increasingly
constrained financing alternatives. Secured debt remains CoreWeave's most viable source of capital, but continued access
depends on signing new contracts, while a shift toward non-investment-grade enterprise customers would likely increase its
funding costs—as exemplified by DDTL 5.5. The ~15% unsecured bond yield as of last week provides an important signal of the
market's required compensation for risk. We would not apply that yield mechanically to all future financing, particularly secured
project-level facilities. Nevertheless, it raises the return hurdle for incremental investment to the extent unsecured funding
remains a necessary component of the capital stack. Given current unsecured yields, we therefore view equity financing as a
complementary source of capital and viable next step, as secured debt alone is unlikely to support the fulfillment of the existing
backlog and growth plan.
The allocation of debt and interest expense between the parent and project-level subsidiaries or SPVs is another important
focus. An increasing concern is how much of the maturing debt, and the approximately $3.2B of cited interest expense, sits at
the parent versus entities holding compute contracts. While most of CoreWeave’s DDTL maturities fall in 2030-31, and thus we
do not expect the company to see real distress anytime soon, principal repayments and covenant tests begin well before final
maturity, making near-term debt service and covenant headroom important considerations despite the longer-dated maturity
profile.

2. BACKLOG FUNDING AND CAPITAL EXPENDITURE REQUIREMENTS
The scale of CoreWeave's backlog has sharpened investor focus on the capital required to deliver it. Our working assumption
remains that the company can raise additional DDTLs as it signs eligible contracts, although availability, pricing, and advance
rates will depend on the underlying contracts and customer credit quality. A shift toward enterprise customers could increase
financing costs relative to facilities supported by stronger-credit counterparties, though we believe strategically it is the right
move for CoreWeave. Given the magnitude of CapEx, we also expect CoreWeave to require unsecured debt, equity, or other
financing structures beyond DDTLs. Our growth assumptions, particularly for 2028 and beyond, remain below consensus,
implying correspondingly lower capital requirements.
We distinguish between financing already committed, financing still required to deliver signed backlog, and financing needed
to support contracts not yet signed. Our current model assumes approximately $46B of growth CapEx next year to deliver
existing backlog, plus approximately $6B of maintenance CapEx. These estimates reflect our proprietary assumptions for
GPU purchases, cost per GPU, and GPU spending as a share of total growth CapEx. Investors have also questioned whether
CoreWeave could reduce spending to improve profitability or cash generation. We distinguish stopping new contract wins from
stopping investment: even without additional backlog, the company would still need to fund deployment obligations embedded
in existing contracts, as well as maintenance and replacement requirements. Even a no-additional-backlog scenario would
not produce immediate improvements in profitability given the funding requirements of existing contracts: we estimate that
delivering the current backlog will require ~$85B in total, including $9B of currently undrawn DDTL capacity, implying roughly
$76B of incremental financing still needs to be raised.

3. CUSTOMER MIX, CONTRACT DURATION, AND LONG-TERM STRATEGY
CoreWeave's pursuit of shorter contracts and non-investment-grade customers reflects a broader strategic challenge:
developing an enterprise business rather than relying indefinitely on hyperscalers that are also building their own capacity.
Shorter contracts may appeal to enterprise customers while giving CoreWeave opportunities to capture higher pricing,
particularly if GPU spot pricing continues to rise. Greater commercial flexibility, however, comes with a financing trade-off.
Shorter contractual duration and weaker customer credit could make funding less attractive or more expensive, offsetting some
of the benefit from higher pricing.
We view enterprise adoption as important to CoreWeave's long-term relevance, but assign a low, nonzero probability to the
company building a sufficiently differentiated enterprise business. We believe this creates a central tension in the company’s
go-forward strategy. Enterprise customers could improve pricing and diversification, but introduce different credit, contract-
duration, and financing considerations. The critical test, in addition to whether CoreWeave can win enterprise customers, will be
whether those contracts generate attractive returns after accounting for the associated funding and operating requirements.
4. CONTRACT RETURNS AND THE OUTLOOK FOR NEW BUSINESS
Investor discussions have highlighted a potential divergence between the economics of CoreWeave's historical portfolio and its
more recently signed contracts. We estimate a best-case unlevered IRR of 9-12% which we believe to reflect the Meta contract
(both original and expanded portions; Exhibit 9). Per management commentary, new contracts signed in 2Q and thereafter have
materially better economics, but we believe newer contracts may be too small to change overall blended returns meaningfully.
Specifically, the outlook for new contract returns reflects several competing factors. On the positive side, shorter-duration
contracts could allow CoreWeave to reprice capacity more frequently, while smaller enterprise deals and bundled offerings
could support higher margins. Still-tight supply could also support pricing, with upcoming colocation capacity providing
opportunities for additional deployments. Against these benefits, increasing competition from SPCX, hyperscalers, and
other neoclouds could pressure returns, while limited capacity available to sell before 2028 constrains CoreWeave's ability
to capitalize on attractive near-term pricing. Weaker customer credit terms and rising cost components could further offset
improvements in headline contract pricing.
Our view leans toward medium-term economics resembling the older contracts more closely, although there is a credible case
for improvement. The outcome will depend on the balance between stronger pricing and utilization on one side, and financing

5. DOWNSIDE SCENARIOS, CAPACITY TRANSFERABILITY, AND RESIDUAL VALUE
The potential transferability of CoreWeave's capacity is an important consideration in downside scenarios. Our view is that much
of the capacity could find another user in the current market, including capacity in more rural locations. However, we would
not rely on residual asset value to protect existing equityholders. Recoveries are highly uncertain and would depend on control
of the assets, contractual and financing restrictions, achievable pricing, and the allocation of proceeds through the capital
structure. Broad demand for compute also does not necessarily imply that a particular deployment would suit customers such
as Meta or OpenAI, especially if capacity optimized for training does not meet their inference requirements.
6. NEOCLOUD READ-THROUGH
Several of the concerns discussed above extend beyond CoreWeave, particularly capital intensity, financing dependence,
customer concentration, and GPU residual value. Exposure varies, however, with each company's balance sheet, contract
structure, customer mix, and commercial strategy. We would therefore distinguish sector-wide economic risks from company-
specific differences in funding and execution.
Although we do not cover any other neoclouds, our assessment is that others may be better positioned in several respects.
These include owned real estate, a healthier balance sheet, larger portion of uncommitted 2027 capacity, and in some cases, a
non-US cloud offering. These potential advantages do not insulate other neoclouds from the sector's underlying economic risks,
but suggest that balance-sheet capacity, deployment flexibility, and differentiation could produce materially different outcomes
across neocloud providers. Given our lack of coverage, we regard this comparison as directional only.
