 
Another Day, Another Data Center Faceplant: 
Korea's Biggest IPO Of The Year Craters 32% 
Within Seconds 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 20, 2026 - 03:33 PM 
Another day, another data center faceplant. 
This time the venue is Seoul, where data-center infrastructure company Elice (0158S0 KS) 
plunged in its trading debut on South Korea's tech-heavy Kosdaq after raising 201 billion won 
($151 million) in the bourse's biggest (and probably worst) initial public offering of the year. 
According to Bloomberg, the self-described "neocloud" priced at 90,500 won, the top of its 
marketed range, for a post-listing market cap of just over 1 trillion won (roughly $750 million). 
Shares then fell as much as 32% instantly to 61,400 won in early trading on Tuesday, after a 
brief opening pop to around 78,000 won which, for those keeping score, was itself still 14% 
below the offer price. 
At last check the stock was hovering around 62,900 won, or about 30% below where the IPO 
priced, wiping out roughly 300 billion won of paper value in under half an hour. 
 

 
 
Bloomberg politely frames the debut as "a test of investor appetite for the sector after a series of 
setbacks around the world." 
Well, consider it tested. Regular readers know we have been keeping a running tally of these 
now daily faceplants since Firmus slashed and then pulled its ASX float on Oct 8, and we have 
been warning since last October that the AI buildout was running on borrowed money. What 
Elice adds to the story is a new and rather uncomfortable data point: even the cheap, profitable 
one can't trade. 
400x Oversubscribed, 32% Underwater 
The demand was certainly there... on paper... according to the underwriters... who would never 
lie... right? Korean retail investors poured roughly 10.3 trillion won (about $7.7 billion) into 
subscription deposits for a shot at the shares, with the retail book oversubscribed more than 400 
times (or so they claim). Put differently, mom-and-pop Seoul parked about 51 times the size of 
the entire deal in escrow to get an allocation. Then they got one. 
Some context on just how fast it went wrong: under Korea's IPO rules, a stock can trade 
anywhere between 60% and 400% of the offer price on day one. Elice's floor was therefore 
54,300 won. At the low, it was about 13% above the floor and 83% below the ceiling, which is 
probably not how the subscription ads pitched it. 
 

 
None of this should shock anyone who has watched Korean flows this month. When the KOSPI 
tripped circuit breakers on Oct 15 for the first time since July (in its 7th straight down day), 
Goldman's Korea desk reported that foreigners dumped $2.7 billion of stock while Korean retail 
bought about $1 billion of it, as we noted in "Goldman: Demand For Compute Can Grow 
Exponentially, But The Capital To Finance It Cannot." Institutions selling, retail buying. Today the 
retail crowd just got to do it at the IPO price instead of the secondary market. Not that the 
volatility is anything new for what we have long called...

And it's not like the official bid has been there to catch anything: as we warned earlier this 
month, the KOSPI's only buyer had left the building (Oct 7). Overnight, Goldman's Seoul desk 
flagged in its Korea Open note (available to pro subs) that President Lee now says a correction 
"was inevitable after its rapid ascent," while insisting that domestic stocks "remain undervalued." 
Elice's new shareholders would probably agree with at least half of that sentence. 
The Cheap One Still Puked 
Here's what makes the Elice debut more interesting than just another frothy deal getting its 
comeuppance. Unlike pretty much all of its peers, Elice actually makes money. Per its 
prospectus, first-half revenue surged 200% to 34.4 billion won, the company swung to an 
operating profit of 10.3 billion won, and EBITDA jumped more than 11-fold to 23.2 billion won. 
Smartkarma's Douglas Kim expects an 83% sales jump in 2026 and an operating margin of 
about 33%. 
Do the math and the IPO valued the company at about 14.5x annualized revenue, falling to 
roughly 10x moments after actually opening for trading on Tuesday. Compare that to Nscale, 
which, as we detailed on Sunday, was asking public investors for $30 billion, or about 107x 
sales, while losing $1.02 billion in the first half. Even the "realistic" $16-19 billion range Nscale's 
would-be buyers are floating works out to some 57-68x. 
 

 
 
Which is why the local fund managers quoted by Bloomberg sound a bit bewildered. Zian 
Investment Management CEO Kim Dojoon argued that "investor sentiment may have weakened 
due to CoreWeave, but its financial structure is quite different from CoreWeave so I don't think 
investors need to view the company as particularly risky." Petra Capital's Albert Yong added that 
sentiment toward AI infrastructure has been "highly volatile and often disconnected from 
underlying fundamentals." 
Both are probably right about the fundamentals. The market, however, has moved on to a 
different question, and Goldman's Rich Privorotsky nailed it last week in his "GS Basics – Bonds 
Up/AI Down. Neoclouds. Computer Inflation" note (available to pro subs): 
"I think the market is discovering that demand for compute can grow exponentially, but the 
capital available to finance it cannot. I've learned to respect the credit market, and the widening 
across the AI vertical is a warning." 
In other words, the question now is how much capital a neocloud will need tomorrow and at 
what price - in other words, who is the greatest fool (that would be life insurers according to the 
CoreWeave founder) - and today's multiple says very little about that. And Elice's CEO Jae Won 
Kim was refreshingly candid on that front: the IPO proceeds go toward building more AI data 
centers in Korea and abroad. "We're going all-in on becoming a full-stack AI infrastructure 
leader globally," Kim told Bloomberg. "This IPO is about giving ourselves the financial firepower 
to execute." 
 

 
That is precisely the business model Privorotsky warned about: an equilibrium that "increasingly 
favors those with powered capacity and strong balance sheets over the leveraged middlemen." 
Or as he put it, "Perhaps this was always how crowding out would manifest itself… through the 
marginal borrower." 
Or in this case marginal momentum-chasing sucker, which South Korea has plenty of.  
And Korea is hardly the place where capital is getting cheaper. Goldman's economists expect 
the Bank of Korea to deliver a "hawkish hold" this week after back-to-back hikes in Q3, with 
another 25bp hike most likely in Q1 2027. Meanwhile power, the other input every data center 
needs, is about to get pricier: state utility KEPCO, sitting on 210 trillion won (c. $148 billion) of 
debt, wants to open talks on raising electricity tariffs, per the same Goldman Korea Open note. 
Higher rates plus higher power bills, for a company whose pitch is "high-density power systems" 
for AI chip clusters. (Yet another reason we keep saying behind-the-meter generation should be 
mandatory for these projects.) 
The Casualty List Grows Faster Than The Data Centers 
Elice is just the latest name on a list that is getting long enough to need its own table of 
contents. In roughly four weeks: 
●​ SB Energy: SoftBank's unit delayed the IPO meant to fund the world's largest data 
center (Sep 22). 
●​ Firmus: the Nvidia-backed Australian operator cut its IPO price by 25%, then pulled it 
(Oct 8), after asking for A$43.7 billion, nearly 3x its August private mark, on the back of a 
valuation metric best described as creative. 
●​ AirTrunk / Vitro: Blackstone's AirTrunk pushed its $1.5 billion Singapore REIT to 2027 
(Oct 14) even after sweetening the yield to 6%, while PLDT's Vitro deferred its own data 
center REIT. 
●​ Nscale: Microsoft walked from its UK deal and IPO investors told the company to slash 
its $30 billion ask roughly in half (Oct 18). 
●​ CoreWeave: shares have more than halved since their May peak, and its bonds traded 
into distressed territory last week, with CDS implying roughly 63% odds of default within 
five years.

Then there's Gartner, which last week predicted that by 2030 "cost pressure and a lack of a 
differentiating business model will force over 80% of neocloud providers to cease independent 
operations," with AI data center owners needing a cumulative $8.5 trillion in revenue through

Meanwhile, Over At Goldman... 
The timing of all this is, well, exquisite. On Monday morning, roughly 12 hours before Elice 
opened for trading, Goldman's Gabriela Borges upgraded CoreWeave to Buy, while cutting her 
 

 
price target to $106 from $139. Her reasoning, per the bank's "CoreWeave – Contract 
Economics Datapoints to Outperform" note (also available to pro subs): 
"We think investors are currently overestimating upward revisions to WACC and 
underestimating unlevered IRR; despite raising our blended debt cost assumption to 1,000bps, 
we expect the company to successfully finance its expansion through asset-backed delayed 
draw term loans." 
Read that again: the bull case now explicitly assumes the company borrows at around 10%, and 
Borges thinks new long-term contracts can "comfortably clear a 15% unlevered IRR" because 
she sees AI compute supply-demand constraints persisting "for at least the next 24 months." No 
wonder the market made an immediate mockery of her ridiculous analysis, with CRWV stock 
kneejerking higher only to close near the Friday lows.

To be fair, nobody really disputes the demand (well, some do). The question is the funding, and 
on that front Morgan Stanley offered a far less cheerful view in its latest AI capex work, which 
we highlighted on Sunday: combined hyperscaler and neocloud free cash flow hits its most 
negative point in 2027, with the hole just shy of $600 billion once lease payments are included. 
 
Translation: the year with the biggest capex bill is also the year with the biggest funding gap, 
and every dollar of it has to come from somewhere. The public equity market, judging by 
 

 
Firmus, AirTrunk, Nscale and now Elice, is increasingly unwilling to be that somewhere, at least 
at the prices sponsors want. That leaves debt, and that window is rapidly closing too.  
Firepower, Meet The Cost Of Capital 
"This IPO is about giving ourselves the financial firepower to execute," Elice's CEO said. And to 
be fair, he got the firepower: the 201 billion won is in the bank regardless of where the stock 
trades. The bill went to Korean retail, which handed over $7.7 billion in deposits for the privilege 
of buying a profitable, reasonably priced neocloud at the exact moment the market decided it 
doesn't want to own neoclouds at any price. 
Which brings us to what Elice really tells us. Firmus could be dismissed as a frothy outlier with a 
made-up multiple, and Nscale as a 107x-sales loss machine. Elice was neither, and it still lost a 
third of its value before most of Seoul had finished its first coffee. The problem, then, is the 
asset class and the cost of the capital needed to keep building it, and with global rates at their 
highest since 2007 (per Goldman's economists) and Korea's central bank still leaning hawkish, 
that cost is not coming down any time soon. Goldman can raise its debt cost assumption to 
1,000bps and still call it a Buy; the public market, apparently, would rather not be the one 
lending at those rates.
