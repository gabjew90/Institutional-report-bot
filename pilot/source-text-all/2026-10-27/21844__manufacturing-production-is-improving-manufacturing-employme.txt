Manufacturing production began to recover in early 2026 after nearly three years in recession. Yet
manufacturing employment has remained largely unchanged. In other words, a year and a half after tariffs
were imposed with the goal of boosting manufacturing jobs, that objective remains elusive. What is often
overlooked is that higher production does not necessarily require more workers. Improvements in
efficiency and productivity, particularly in the most advanced manufacturing industries, frequently allow
firms to increase output with the same number of workers or, in many cases, fewer workers.
Today, manufacturing production is more than 60% higher than it was in the 1970s, while employment
remains 6.8 million below its August 1979 peak. The implication is clear: manufacturing productivity has
increased dramatically, even though total manufacturing output has been relatively flat since the recovery
from the Great Recession.
The recent recovery in US manufacturing has been led by durable goods production. However, gains have
not been evenly distributed across industries. Only a handful of sectors are producing more today than
they did before the Great Recession, most notably computer and electronic products and transportation
equipment. Yet neither sector employs more workers today than before the recession.

The computer and electronic products sector deserves special attention given the ongoing expansion in 
artificial intelligence and data center investment. As the chart below illustrates, the sector's output has 
grown exponentially, making it one of the clearest examples of how productivity gains can transform an 
industry. Employment has fallen from roughly 2 million workers in 1990 to about 1 million today, yet output 
has increased by approximately 3,400% over the same period. Within the sector, production of computer 
and peripheral equipment has risen more than 6,000%, while semiconductor and other electronic 
component production has surged by more than 70,000%.
Transportation equipment manufacturing is the other major durable goods industry producing more today 
than before the Great Recession. While employment has declined from a peak of roughly 2.2 million workers 
in the early 1990s to about 1.8 million today, output has increased by approximately 85% since January 
1990. 
On the nondurable goods side, production growth has slowed considerably since the Great Recession but 
has remained relatively stable in recent years. Employment, however, has continued to weaken despite 
steady output. Among nondurable goods industries, food manufacturing stands out as the only sector 
employing more workers today than it did in the early 1990s. Although output remains slightly below its 
post-pandemic peak, employment remains one of the few bright spots in manufacturing. The industry 
employs approximately 1.8 million workers today, compared with about 1.5 million in January 1990.

Food manufacturing highlights an important distinction. Unlike many manufacturing industries, production 
in this sector remains labor-intensive and is less susceptible to substituting capital for labor. By contrast, 
many durable goods industries, including nonmetallic minerals, primary metals, fabricated metals, 
machinery, and electrical equipment, have increased production without meaningfully expanding payrolls.
Bottom Line: Manufacturing employment is significantly lower today than it was in the 1990s. However, 
productivity has surged across much of the sector. At the same time, some industries have become less 
competitive relative to foreign producers, particularly those in China. The United States continues to 
maintain a competitive advantage in technologically sophisticated industries and domestically oriented 
sectors such as food manufacturing, where labor remains a critical component of production. 
Competing against countries with substantially lower labor costs remains challenging. A manufacturing 
worker in China earns roughly $15,000 per year, compared with approximately $77,000 for a manufacturing 
worker in the United States before benefits. No realistic tariff can fully offset such a wage gap across all 
industries. A tariff high enough to do so would effectively become prohibitive, pushing the economy toward 
autarky, a system in which trade largely ceases and domestic consumption is limited to domestic 
production. Such an outcome would result in a significant decline in economic welfare.

NAHB Housing Market Index: The NAHB Housing Market Index was weaker than expected in October and the lowest since 
June of 2012. The three components of the index were lower in October than in September. Home builders’ average price 
cut was 6%, unchanged for the seventh consecutive month. Only one region of the country, the Northeast, bucked the 
weakening trend in October. The NAHB continues to show the impact of high mortgage interest rates, high input cost, and 
still-high home prices on the new home industry. We do not see any improvement in the new homes market during the rest 
of the year as well as into next year. The National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) Housing Market Index (HMI) was 
lower than expected in October, declining to 29 compared to a reading of 32 in September. This was the lowest reading for 
the NAHB HMI since June of 2012. The three components of the index were weaker in October compared to September. The 
Single Family Sales: Present index was 33 in October compared to 35 in September; the Single Family Sales: Next 6 Months 
was at 32 in October compared to 37 in September while the Traffic of Prospective Buyers index declined to 18 from 23. By 
region, three of the four regions in the country were lower in October and compared to September of this year. The only 
region that avoided a decline was the Northeast, which saw the index improve to 37 compared to 35 in September. In the 
Midwest, the index declined to 35 compared to 40 on September. In the South, it declined from 29 in September to 27 in 
October. Finally, the West showed the index at 20 compared to 30 in September. According to the release, 43% of builders 
cut prices in October. This was the highest share during the period 2020-2026. The average price cut was 6%, the same 
average cut reported during the last seven consecutive months.
Housing Starts/Building Permits: Housing starts were significantly weaker than expected in September, falling 5.2% 
month over month. While single-family starts declined, the sharpest drop came from projects with five units or more. 
Building permits were also soft but held up better, edging down just 0.2% from August. The decline in permits was driven by 
fewer single-family authorizations, while multifamily permits strengthened during the month. Although housing starts have 
weakened considerably, permits have stabilized and have not continued to deteriorate. We see limited upside for the new 
home market through this year and next unless mortgage rates and construction costs decline meaningfully. Housing starts 
fell to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 1.221 million in September, down 5.2% from August, according to the US Census 
Bureau and the US Department of Housing and Urban Development. Housing starts were also 7.4% below their September 
2025 level. Single-family starts declined 4.6% on the month but remained 2.6% higher than a year earlier. Starts of projects 
with five units or more fell 7.2% from August and were down 25.9% from a year ago. Regionally, starts were unchanged in 
the Northeast from August but were 30.9% lower than a year earlier. In the Midwest, starts fell 19.0% month over month and 
19.4% year over year. The South was the lone bright spot, posting a 6.1% monthly gain and an 11.0% increase from a year 
ago. In the West, starts declined 23.2% from August and were down 25.4% from September 2025. Building permits slipped 
0.2% from August and were down 3.0% from a year ago. The monthly decline was largely driven by single-family permits, 
which fell 4.0% on the month and 4.8% from a year earlier. Permits for structures with two to four units rose 8.3% from 
August but remained 10.3% below year-ago levels. Permits for projects with five units or more increased 5.9% on the month 
and were 1.0% higher than a year earlier. By region, permits rose 13.3% in the Northeast from August but were down 2.0% 
from a year earlier. In the Midwest, permits increased 0.5% on the month and 4.4% year over year. Southern permits rose 
1.5% from August but were still 1.8% below year-ago levels. In the West, permits fell 10.2% on the month and 11.3% from a 
year earlier. September marked the weakest level of housing starts since May. While permitting activity remains subdued, it 
has held up considerably better than housing starts and shows fewer signs of further deterioration.
Pending Home Sales: Pending home sales, as measured by the National Association of Realtors Pending Home Sales Index 
(PHSI), declined far more than expected in September. While housing activity does not always move in lockstep across 
regions, this month's report showed broad-based weakness nationwide. The decline was the largest monthly drop of the 
year and pushed the index to its lowest level on record since the series began in January 2001. As a leading indicator of 
existing home sales, the report suggests housing market conditions continue to deteriorate. However, unlike during the 
Great Recession, the softness in activity has yet to translate into falling home prices, largely because the market is not 
burdened by a significant inventory of distressed properties. Pending home sales fell 7.3% in September, according to the 
National Association of Realtors Pending Home Sales Index (PHSI). Compared with a year ago, the index was down 11.3%. 
The weakness was widespread, affecting every major region of the country. The Northeast PHSI declined 4.2% month-over-
month and 9.4% year-over-year. The Midwest fell 6.5% on the month and 8.7% from a year earlier. The South dropped 8.2% 
month-over-month and 12.0% year-over-year, while the West posted the largest decline, falling 9.2% on the month and 
15.0% from a year ago. According to Dr. Lawrence Yun, Chief Economist at the National Association of Realtors: "Market 
conditions weakened in September due to fast-rising mortgage rates." He also noted that mortgage rates may be 
approaching a peak, as they have already risen substantially ahead of the Federal Reserve's increases in short-term interest 
rates.
