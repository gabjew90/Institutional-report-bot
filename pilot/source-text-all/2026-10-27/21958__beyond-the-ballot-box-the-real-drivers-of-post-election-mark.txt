Beyond the ballot box: the real 
drivers of post-election markets
Michael Heldmann
CIO Equity
As the United States approaches its midterm 
elections, investors are asking what the outcome 
might mean for markets. Election seasons generate 
floods of predictions, scenario analyses, and political 
commentary. Yet investors should not overestimate the 
importance of electoral outcomes and underestimate 
the influence of broader market forces.
Of course, elections are not irrelevant. Changes in 
government can shape fiscal policy, regulation, and 
investor sentiment. However, for long-term investors, the 
key issue is often not which party wins, but whether the 
election increases policy volatility, changes the broader 
economic backdrop, influences interest rates, or alters 
corporate earnings prospects.
In our view, the coming midterms are unlikely to become 
a decisive turning point for markets. Instead, they may 
drive shifts in performance across sectors and styles, 
while the fundamental drivers of return will remain 
interest rates, earnings growth, and the continuing 
evolution of the AI investment cycle.
Policy clarity is more important than 
electoral outcomes
Political headlines can move markets in the short 
term, but uncertainty is often more important than any 
specific electoral outcome, explaining why we may see 
a rotation into defensive positions as more clarity on 
policy direction is awaited.

Indeed, markets frequently become more constructive 
after elections, as the reduction in uncertainty can lower 
the equity risk premium demanded by investors and 
support a recovery in appetite for riskier assets. 
Current polls and prediction markets suggest a realistic 
possibility of divided government in Washington, with 
control potentially split between the Democrats and 
Republicans. From a market perspective, divided 
government has often been viewed favourably as the 
likelihood of major legislative changes tends to fall 
and policy volatility is reduced. Fewer significant policy 
shifts can mean greater predictability and a more stable 
backdrop for businesses and investors.
While this does not imply stronger economic growth 
or better earnings per se, markets are certainly more 
comfortable operating in an environment where the rules 
of the game appear stable. And even if the Democrats 
were to sweep Congress, the outcome may still be 
constructive for markets, depending on the degree of 
policy clarity going forward. 
Rates still matter more than politics
While political developments attract attention, investors 
should not lose sight of a variable that has consistently 
exercised a great influence over equity prices in recent 
years: interest rates. The ultimate market impact of 
any election outcome is likely to depend on whether 
it changes expectations for inflation, and fiscal and 
monetary policies. Any shift that increases inflationary 
pressures could reinforce expectations for higher 
interest rates. Conversely, a more moderate fiscal 
backdrop could ease inflation concerns and support 
lower yields. 
These dynamics matter because they influence the 
relative attractiveness of different parts of the equity 
market. Higher inflation and interest rates have 
historically tended to favour shorter-duration equities,

outcome of the election affects the path of inflation 
and interest rates. Politics may be the trigger, but rates 
remain the transmission mechanism through which 
many market effects are ultimately felt.
The AI factor 
However, one reason we should be cautious about 
relying too heavily on historical election playbooks is 
the extraordinary importance of AI in this political cycle. 
Capital expenditure decisions, earnings expectations, 
and market performance across a wide range of sectors 
continue to rely on the AI growth narrative.

As a result, traditional relationships between politics, 
rates, and style performance may prove less reliable 
than in the past. For example, long-duration growth 
companies are often expected to struggle when interest 
rates remain elevated. However, businesses with direct 
exposure to AI infrastructure, semiconductor demand, 
or software innovation have continued to attract strong 
investor interest because the scale of the opportunity is 
perceived to be exceptional.
At the same time, the AI story itself is not immune from 
political developments. Regulatory scrutiny and policy 
oversight could become more prominent following the 
election, particularly if Democratic policymakers seek to 
establish clearer frameworks around AI deployment and 
governance. Greater uncertainty surrounding regulation, 
capital expenditure plans, or future returns on investment 
could challenge market assumptions, including the 
current aggressive pricing of some AI stocks.
Don’t forget earnings season
While politics dominates media coverage, investors 
should remember that companies ultimately create 
value through earnings, not election results. Indeed, the 
upcoming earnings season may prove more important 
than the election. Strong corporate results could help 
underpin market resilience regardless of the political 
outcome. Conversely, disappointing earnings could 
increase market sensitivity to political developments 
and amplify volatility.
This is particularly relevant given the high expectations 
embedded in parts of the market. Investors will 
be looking not only at earnings growth but also at 
management commentary on demand trends, capital 
expenditure plans, and the outlook for AI-related 
investments. History suggests that political events often 
create noise while earnings provide the foundation for 
long-term returns. That lesson may prove instructive in 
the months ahead.
A catalyst for rotation, not a market turning 
point
The temptation during election years is to focus on 
winners and losers. Yet investors may be better served 
by asking a different question: will there be substantive 
policy changes, and what will they mean for the market 
environment? Our view is that the overall impact on 
markets may be less dramatic than many expect. Rather 
than triggering a major shift in the market’s direction, 
the election is more likely to influence where market 
gains are concentrated.
Investors may rotate between defensive and cyclical 
exposures, between value and growth styles, and 
between regions as they reassess the outlook for 
inflation, interest rates and policy stability. Indeed, 
Europe and Asia may be beneficiaries; the former if 
investors seek greater diversification away from US 
equities or rotate towards regions with less direct 
exposure to election-related headlines, while several 
Asian economies have historically responded 
positively when investors perceive lower policy volatility 
in Washington. 
Ultimately, the most important determinants of market 
performance are likely to remain the same: the path of 
interest rates, the strength of corporate earnings, and 
the continued development of the AI investment cycle. 
Politics will undoubtedly dominate the headlines. But 
when the dust settles, investors may once again discover 
that policy volatility matters more than politics, and that 
fundamentals matter more than either.
