Tech continues to lead markets and remains the dominant driver of earnings growth, but leadership may be starting to broaden, and Energy, supported by higher oil prices, is expected to make meaningful contribution. Utilities, however, have
underperformed, but may now be showing early signs of emerging as a complementary trade, as rising power demand and investment increasingly reflect the build-out taking place across the tech ecosystem.
Following our discussion on the US Tech sector yesterday (again here), today, we take a closer look at Energy and Utilities through a range of technical charts and market indicators, before screening Energy names on both sides of the Atlantic for
strong quality and momentum characteristics.
Oil, yields and equities: a maturing dynamic?
The relationship between oil, Treasury yields and equities appears to have moved through three phases in 2026. The initial oil shock triggered a stagflationary response, lifting inflation concerns, weakening growth expectations and driving a sharp
correction in the S&P 500. In the second phase, oil and yields rose together as markets priced in stickier inflation and a higher-for-longer Fed, yet equities recovered on resilient activity and earnings.
The latest phase looks different. Oil and yields remain elevated, but the S&P 500 has held near its highs rather than repeating the earlier sell-off. This suggests markets may be becoming less sensitive to higher oil and yields, provided earnings remain
supportive and energy prices do not accelerate further.
If oil stabilises at a high level, base effects should help reduce its contribution to headline inflation. That may also support broader market participation, with Energy emerging alongside Technology as a more meaningful driver of earnings growth.
Still, any renewed acceleration in oil and yields could again weigh on margins and valuations.

Technology and energy: earnings leadership in a broadening market
Expected earnings growth has been broadening across the US market, with almost all sectors expected to make a positive contribution this reporting quarter. Even so, leadership remains concentrated. Technology is still the largest contributor to
expected S&P 500 EPS growth, adding 16.4 percentage points, followed by Energy at 5.7 points and Communication Services at 4.1 points, while contributions from the remaining sectors are much smaller.
The picture is therefore one of improving breadth, but still selective leadership. Technology remains the main driver of profit growth, while Energy has emerged as a more meaningful second pillar, supported by elevated oil and refined-product
prices. That mix reduces reliance on Technology alone and points to a somewhat broader earnings base.
The key condition is the path of oil prices. If oil stabilises at elevated levels, it could preserve Energy sector profitability, cash generation and shareholder distributions without adding materially to inflation pressure. A renewed rise in crude prices,
however, could lift inflation expectations and bond yields, tighten financial conditions and offset some of the sector’s earnings support. In that sense, Technology and Energy remain complementary, but the combination is most supportive when oil
stays high but stable rather than entering another accelerating phase.

For the broader market, however, what appears to be changing is headline sensitivity to higher oil. While rising fuel costs still weigh on parts of the economy, the latest increase in crude has not triggered the same deterioration in risk appetite as the
initial shock. Stronger earnings, broader sector participation and some repricing of higher yields into valuations may be reducing the marginal impact.
Again, the key distinction here is between the level of oil and the pace of the move. If crude stabilises at elevated levels, Energy could continue to benefit while base effects gradually reduce oil’s contribution to inflation. The main risk is a renewed oil

Utilities: where the tech and energy trades converge
If higher oil prices help explain renewed strength in Energy equities, the Utilities story is increasingly being driven by structurally stronger electricity demand. According to the IEA’s latest Electricity 2026 report, power consumption in advanced
economies is rising again after roughly 15 years of stagnation, supported by data centres, cooling demand, heat pumps, EVs and industrial electrification.
In the US, electricity demand rose 2.8% in 2024 and 2.1% in 2025, with more than 420 TWh of additional demand expected by 2030. Data centres are the main source of incremental demand, while transport demand is also rising. In the EU, demand
rose 1.6% in 2024 and an estimated 0.9% in 2025, with around 300 TWh of additional demand expected by 2030, driven primarily by transport electrification and, increasingly, data centres.
This is where the technology and Energy themes begin to overlap. Technology is driving electricity demand, while Utilities provide the generation, transmission and distribution capacity needed to meet it. In that sense, Utilities may offer a bridge
between the two sectors, linking digital growth with the infrastructure required to support it.
US utilities’ relative sector underperformance has moved to historically stretched levels, with recent stabilisation raising the possibility of a recovery
The chart below shows the one‑year relative performance z‑score of US utilities versus the broader S&P 500, using ETFs as liquid proxies. It illustrates how sector underperformance, which began with the 2025 trade-tariff shock, has been moving
towards historically stretched levels, with the latest reading around ‑1.6 standard deviations. Importantly, the deepest relative washouts in recent years — notably in 2021 and 2024 — occurred when the ratio approached ‑2 standard deviations, and both
episodes were followed by major rebounds in utilities’ relative performance.
Moreover, the move now appears to be stabilising, with the latest prints turning more range-bound after the sharp deterioration. If this bottoming process continues, it might suggest the start of a renewed recovery phase for utilities and reopen the
door to relative outperformance, particularly if attention shifts back to the structural electricity-demand story.

Switching on the search for power
The expansion of AI and data-centre capacity is driving a structural increase in electricity demand, bringing generation, grid investment and energy security more firmly into focus. Utilities sit at the intersection of these trends, as selected operators
stand to benefit from stronger power demand, regulated investment and long-term supply agreements, while also retaining part of their traditional defensive profile. Nuclear generation and uranium provide an additional route into the theme,
offering reliable baseload capacity with less direct exposure to oil-price volatility.
Higher sovereign yields have however been reducing the relative appeal of Utilities dividends, compressing valuations and increasing financing costs for capital-intensive generation and grid projects. Recent uncertainty around the AI-related power
theme has added further pressure, as stronger electricity demand will only translate into earnings if investment, regulatory approval and timely cost recovery align. Regulatory scrutiny has also increased, as authorities seek to ensure that data-centre
operators, rather than existing customers, bear the cost of the required infrastructure.
While debates around investment timing and returns remain live, they do not however undermine the structural increase in electricity demand. Moreover, although the sector has been caught up in the weak market breadth beneath the S&P 500’s
resilient headline performance in recent months, the chart below shows that the number of Utilities stocks trading above their 50-day moving average has recently seen a sharp rebound. Taken together, depressed breadth, improving technical

A constructive signal from Utilities microstructure

A classic short-term tactical breadth indicator for US Utilities (XLU) recently triggered a strong recovery signal: the share of constituents trading above their 10-day moving average rose from just 3% to 97%, with the threshold crossed on 9 October.
That marks a rare rebound from deeply washed-out levels to broad short-term participation in less than 21 days. Such moves typically point to a sharp improvement in sector internals rather than a simple short-covering bounce.
Historical evidence since 2000 suggests that, while performance has often been range-bound immediately after the signal, the medium-term profile has generally been more constructive. In past occurrences, 73% of outcomes were positive after 75
days, with a median return of 3.3%. This indicates that similarly extreme breadth reversals in Utilities have often been followed by a more sustained recovery phase.

Taken together, this makes the area technically significant, and the ETF has started to rebound from it. For now, the recovery remains underway and still requires confirmation through a break above the 50-day SMA at 42.10, followed by the key short-

A break above this zone would support the view that the low recorded in late September marked the end of a corrective wave 4 and that the current move is wave 5, implying scope towards 51–52.
The RSI has also rebounded from support in place since the Covid low, adding support to this interpretation.

European utilities are in a range since bypassing 2008 ATH
The European utilities sector accelerated after breaking out of the ascending channel in place since the 2022 low, lifting the ETF above its previous 2008 all-time high.
Since then, price action has turned more range-bound around this pivotal area. The ETF has pulled back towards 53.80, corresponding to the 23.6% Fibonacci retracement and an ascending support line in place since the 2025 low. At the same time,
the RSI has broken below the support that had held since 2022, pointing to softer near-term momentum.
The next break from this range is likely to be important. A move above the previous high would bring 66 and then 71 into view, while a break below 53.80 could open the way to a deeper Wave 4 pull-back towards 47, close to both the 50% Fibonacci
retracement and the 200-week SMA.

A complementary trio?
Technology remains the market’s performance juggernaut and the core source of structural earnings growth, supported by AI investment and resilient profitability (more here). Yet energy now adds a second source of support through stronger
earnings, cash generation and shareholder distributions, while Utilities may capture the next phase of physical infrastructure investment linked to rising electricity demand.
Together, the three sectors can reflect complementary parts of the same market story: Technology drives power demand, Energy benefits from firmer oil pricing, and Utilities provide the generation, grid and transmission capacity needed to support
both digital expansion and broader electrification. In that sense, the case is no longer just about two contrasting macro exposures, but about three distinct sources of potential earnings support.
The backdrop remains conditional, however. The performance mix is likely to be most supportive when oil stays high but broadly stable rather than accelerating sharply, and when bond yields do not rise enough to tighten financial conditions
materially. Subject to those conditions, however, the bigger AI investment story may support the view that market leadership can continue to broaden beyond Technology alone.

The Equity quantitative grades
Our equity quant process is one by which we rank and score the stocks within our equity universe according to various short-term (i.e.
EPS growth) long-term (i.e. quality) and positioning (i.e. technical) characteristics, and on relative and/or historical basis as
appropriate. The various scores make up a stock’s total or ‘global' grade’:Growth Grade: The Growth Grade is a proprietary formula
made up of earnings revision momentum, past earnings growth, earnings stability, and current and long-term earnings growth.  A
grade above 55 is considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45 and neutral between 55 and 45.
EPS Grade: The EPS Grade is a proprietary formula consisting of current and forward EPS growth, change and surprise data.  An EPS grade
above 60 or below 40 is considered predictive for future out/under performance.Sales Grade: The Sales Grade is a proprietary formula made
up of current and next year’s sales momentum, past sales growth, sales stability, and current and long-term sales growth. A grade above 55 is
considered bullish on a 3-month basis, bearish below 45, and neutral between 55 and 45.
Value Grade: The Value Grade is a proprietary formula made up of estimated P/E, P/B, P/S and P/CF ratios. 40% of the grade is based on
historical values and 60% on current market data.  A grade above 55 suggests a stock is cheap, below 45 expensive, and neutral between 55
and 45.
Quality Grade: The Quality Grade is a proprietary formula that focuses on the balance sheet (i.e. change in accruals, change in free cash flows
and profitability). A grade above 55 suggests a healthy balance sheet.Credit Grade: The Credit Grade focusses on the passive side of the
balance sheet.  It is divided into three sub-components to assess both short and long-term solvency.  A grade above 55 suggests a strong
capital structure, while a grade below 45 suggests a weak one.
Money Flow Grade: The Money Flow Grade is a proprietary formula that gives the accumulation/distribution based on the volume flows of a
stock. A grade above 55 indicates good money flow and a grade below 45 suggests weak money flow.Smart Sentiment Grade: The Smart
Sentiment grade is a contrarian indicator based on investor positioning measures such as the days-to-cover ratio, the put-call ratio, and the
short-interest ratio.  A weak grade suggests ‘too much’ optimism.
Relative Strength (RS) Grade: The RS grade measures the price momentum of a stock over its 1-year price performance.
Global Grade: The Global Grade is a weighted average of the Growth, EPS Sales, Value, Quality, Credit, Money Flow and Smart Sentiment
Grades.

The Exposure Optimisation Model
The PTS Exposure Optimisation Model (EOM) is a proprietary model designed to give tactical signals on the prevailing short-term
regional equity market regime. The model is applied to each market on a mutually-exclusive basis, with output expressed by way of
exposure scores (US; Europe; Japan and EM); in each case an average of underlying factors scores with dynamic weighting applied
according to the prevailing structural market volatility regime. Exposure score ratings are: very bullish (80-100%); bullish (60-80%);
neutral (40-60%); bearish (20-40%); and very bearish (20-0%). Factors are as follows:
Regime: A combination of technical indicators, moving averages and a Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance (CPPI) strategy, the regime
score is designed to reflect short-term market momentum in the region;
Valuation: Built around the forward price-to-sales ratio, the dynamic valuation score is based on a calculation that is re-set on bull/bear
market regime changes and calibrated to facilitate regional comparison;
Economics: The economics score draws on real economy activity data (i.e. heavy truck sales and shipping traffic), economic signals from
financial markets (i.e. yield-curves) and medium-term economic indicators (i.e. PMIs and unemployment data); 
Flows: The flows score draws upon both active and passive weekly EPFR fund flows data giving us an indication of risk appetite going into the
week ahead; 
Market Sentiment: The market sentiment score is built around classic regional market risk indices (i.e. VIX; ARMS) as well as soft survey data
(i.e. U.of Michigan Consumer Sentiment). The scoring system is applied in a similar fashion at both regional and market level, while at sector
level we refer only to the Regime, Flows and Valuation factors.

Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator that measures the speed and change of price movements. It ranges from 0 to 100 and
is typically used to identify overbought or oversold conditions in a market. An RSI above 70 suggests that a stock may be overbought, while an
RSI below 30 indicates that it may be oversold.
The Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD): A trend-following momentum indicator that shows the relationship between two
moving averages. It is calculated by subtracting the 26-period Exponential Moving Average (EMA) from the 12- period EMA. The result is the
MACD line. A nine-day EMA of the MACD, called the signal line, is then plotted on top of the MACD line, which can function as a trigger for
buy or sell signals. When the MACD crosses above the signal line, it is typically considered a bullish signal, and when it crosses below, it is
considered bearish.
Ichimoku Cloud Technical Analysis: Ichimoku Cloud is a popular technical analysis tool used to identify potential trends and
support/resistance levels in financial markets. It consists of several components, including the Kumo (cloud), the Tenkan-sen (conversion
line), the Kijun-sen (base line), and Senkou Span (leading span).
