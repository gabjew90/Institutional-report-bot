Core vs. Trimmed Mean Inflation: Does Trimming Still Make Sense? 
 
 
Following last week’s CPI and PPI reports, we estimate that core PCE inflation was 3.1% 
year-over-year in September, while the Dallas Fed’s trimmed mean PCE inflation 
measure was just 2.2% (Exhibit 1).1 The gap between the two measures has widened in 
recent months and now stands well above its 0.05pp long-run average. 
Whether trimming is sensible is ultimately a judgment call that depends on the prevailing 
economic environment. The case for trimming is strongest when many outlier 
category-level price changes arise from clear one-off causes but weaker when the 
source of the outliers is unclear or ongoing, because in those scenarios outliers might be 
early indicators of a shift in the inflation trend. In today’s Daily, we assess what is 
currently being trimmed and whether focusing on the trimmed mean still makes sense. 
 
Earlier this year, we argued that trimming was sensible because much of what was being 
trimmed reflected the one-off effects of tariffs, the energy shock, and AI-related price 
pressures and statistical mismeasurement. 
Since then, the trimmed mean has continued to run below the core most months, even 
as tariff effects have become less relevant. We find that the gap continues to largely 
reflect the trimming of AI-related increases in software & accessories and consumer 
electronics, airfare increases driven by the spike in jet fuel prices, and tariff-driven 
increases in other core goods. Based on our category-level translation of the CPI, PPI, 
and import price source data into PCE inflation, we estimate that in September, these 
three factors accounted for roughly 75% of the deviation of the core-trimmed mean gap 
1
The Dallas Fed trimmed mean PCE inflation measure excludes the categories accounting for the top 31% and 
bottom 24% of the index’s weight when ranked by monthly inflation rates. It trims more categories from the top 
than from the bottom to offset the fact that historically prices tend to have larger extreme drops than spikes, 
thus leading to an unbiased measure that tracks inflation better over the long run. Other measures of the 
underlying inflation trend also continue to run significantly softer than core PCE. We estimate that the Cleveland 
Fed median PCE inflation measure—which reflects the inflation rate of the category that falls at the 50th 
percentile when all categories are ranked by their inflation rates and weighted by their index weights—rose 2.6% 
year-over-year in September.

Our recent analyses of each of these three factors suggest that most of the impact from 
each is behind us. We expect the AI-related boost to ease next year as memory and 
computer price growth slows, we see limited scope for further tariff-related price 
increases, and we expect a smaller impact on airfares as energy prices gradually

considerations continue to support the case for trimming. 
But the case for looking through these outliers would weaken if the pressure from those 
forces instead started to look likely to be more prolonged or if price pressures 
broadened to categories beyond those directly tied to these three factors. In that 
scenario, trimming would risk discarding early signs of a genuine pickup in underlying 
inflation—that happened and made the trimmed mean a lagging indicator during the 
pandemic inflation surge. We find little evidence of such broadening so far. Among the
