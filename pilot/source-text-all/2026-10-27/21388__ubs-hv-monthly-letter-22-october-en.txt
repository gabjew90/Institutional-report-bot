Divided 
We estimate a 90% chance that the US midterms in November will result in a 
divided government, with Democrats controlling at least one chamber of 
Congress. But politics is not the only source of division. Investors are increasingly 
split over three questions: whether strong earnings growth can continue, where 
bond yields go next, and how geopolitical tensions will affect energy prices 
and markets.
On earnings, we remain constructive. We expect third-quarter S&P 500 earnings 
per share to grow 28% y/y. Yet with semiconductor profits contributing around 
half of that, management commentary on AI adoption and monetization will be 
crucial to determine whether the exceptional pace of AI investment can continue. 
We favor a diversified mix of structural and cyclical opportunities and reducing 
concentrations in single-stock positions.
On yields, investors are divided between concern about renewed inflation and 
hopes that tighter policy will ultimately contain price pressures. In our base case, 
we expect yields to decline over the next 12 months. Following the recent repric­
ing, we believe high-quality bonds offer sufficient income to cushion against a fur­
ther rise in yields. We favor medium- and longer-duration bonds but remain 
cautious on the longest maturities given fiscal concerns and elevated issuance.
On geopolitics, renewed disruption to energy flows has intensified concerns over 
oil prices and inflation. While a prolonged supply shock would present downside 
risks, we do not think the geopolitical developments alone justify a more defen­
sive investment stance. Broad commodity exposure can help diversify portfolios 
and provide protection against further disruption.

October 2026 – UBS House View Monthly Letter
The US political divide may attract attention in the coming weeks, but questions 
over earnings, AI, and interest rates are likely to matter more for portfolios. In this 
letter, we examine those key questions and set out how investors can navigate 
them. Rather than position for a single outcome, investors should remain diversi­
fied and focus on durable sources of growth and income.
Equities: Can earnings continue to underpin markets? 
With the third-quarter earnings season now underway, we forecast S&P 500 
earnings-per-share growth of 28% y/y, driven by a combination of AI demand, an 
improving industrial cycle, and resilient higher-income consumers. 
AI remains the main growth engine. We expect AI capital spending to reach USD 
900bn this year, with semiconductor profits contributing about half of third-
quarter earnings growth. We also expect average cloud revenue growth to accel­
erate from 48% in the June quarter to 58% y/y in the September quarter. As 
investors increasingly focus on monetization, management commentary around 
pricing, capacity constraints, and returns on investment will be key to shaping 
expectations.
Within the AI ecosystem, risks are rising, particularly in the more capital-intensive 
areas of the AI buildout, where funding pressures and execution risks could rein­
force each other. Recent share-price pressure on some specialized AI computing 
providers known as neoclouds—whose single-product business model focuses on 
renting compute capacity—illustrates these concerns. Nevertheless, broader 
demand trends remain robust, reflected in rising GPU rental prices and strong 
semiconductor earnings.

Aggregate consumption is also healthy: US retail sales grew 6% y/y in September,

Non-financial S&P 500 companies have a weighted average debt maturity of

Outside the US, we forecast 15% Eurozone earnings growth in both 2026 and 
2027, supported by a pick-up in manufacturing, continued cost discipline, and 
higher investment in AI, electrification, and defense. Political risks—particularly in 
France—may cause volatility, but corporate fundamentals remain solid, in 
our view.  
European equities have also historically performed well when rising interest rates 
accompany resilient economic activity. Across the last ten tightening periods from 
the Federal Reserve, following the initial policy move, European equities were on 
average 5% higher after six months and 14% higher after 12 months.
In Asia, with earnings growth running well ahead of price appreciation this year, 
equity valuations are now at multi-decade lows (the MSCI Asia ex-Japan trades at 
a 12-month forward price-to-earnings ratio of around 9x). Yet AI investment 
trends remain intact, supporting a robust earnings outlook for the region. We 
expect 20% profit growth next year and continue to see Asian equities as 
Attractive.
Overall, corporate earnings growth remains strong and should continue to sup­
port equities, although a slowdown from the exceptional pace seen earlier this 
year may moderate market gains. We expect the S&P 500 to reach 8,400 by end-

favoring AI, power and resources, and longevity. We like financials, industrials, 
utilities, health care, and consumer discretionary in the US. We also like Eurozone 
equities, and Japan, India, Taiwan, and Singapore in Asia.

Fixed income: Has the bond market repriced enough?
Bond markets have repriced dramatically in recent months. The 10-year US 
Treasury yield reached 5.36% in early October, its highest level since 2002, though 
it has since moderated to around 5.1%. German 10-year Bund yields rose above 
3.6% in late September. French 10-year yields were above 5% at the time 
of writing.
The ongoing wars in the Middle East and Ukraine have added to inflation. US 
monthly consumer price inflation accelerated to 0.6% in September from 0.4% in 
August, with core inflation at 0.3% for a second consecutive month. In the 
Eurozone, headline inflation accelerated to a three-year high of 3.8% y/y. Recent 
Fed communications indicate that policymakers are concerned that consumer 
inflation expectations could become de-anchored, and we now expect the Fed to 
raise interest rates again in both December and March. 
Meanwhile, fundraising for AI-related investment is adding to bond supply, and 
geopolitical developments are adding to uncertainty. In Europe, the bond market 
also appears to be demanding a premium for fiscal concerns: Over the past three 
months, the yield spread between 10-year French OATs and German Bunds has 
widened by around 75 basis points to 158bps. Contagion effects have also 
pushed spreads wider on other Eurozone sovereigns and weighed on the euro.
Against this backdrop, investors have understandably focused on how much fur­
ther yields might rise. Yet attempting to forecast the precise peak in yields is both 
difficult and, from a long-term perspective, potentially less important than inves­
tors assume. With bond yields at these levels, we think the more important ques­
tion is whether they provide sufficient income to compensate for the risks of 
further monetary tightening, persistent inflation, and fiscal uncertainty. We 
think they do. 
Unlike in equities, the bar for performance in fixed income has been dropping. At 
current yields, US Treasury yields in the two-, five-, and 10-year maturities would 
need to rise by around 254, 113, and 65 basis points, respectively, for capital 
losses to offset the income earned. We view such a move as unlikely: A spike in 
yields of that magnitude from here would likely prompt a swift response from pol­
icymakers, as it would have a negative impact on the real economy and could 
reveal financial stability risks.

We see fixed income as Attractive. In our base case, we expect a moderate decline

tightening is reflected in current yields. More importantly, we believe bonds offer

Oil and diesel: Should investors worry?
In September, crude oil and refined product exports from the Middle East stood at 
around 80% of pre-conflict levels. This reflected a partial restoration of flows 
through the Strait of Hormuz—including “dark” shipping, in which vessels 
obscure their movements or cargo origins—and rerouting through Saudi Arabian, 
UAE, and Turkish pipelines.
However, in October, geopolitical risks re-intensified. Iranian attacks on shipping in 
the Strait of Hormuz have increased: According to the British maritime security 
service UKMTO, 26 confirmed attacks took place from 1-18 October compared 
with 14 for September as a whole. Elsewhere, Russia continues to strike Ukraine, 
and Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries and oil infrastructure resumed after a 
brief halt. The conflict between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis also continues to 
pose risks to energy infrastructure. 
In response, Brent crude prices have risen from a low of USD 72/bbl in June to 
around USD 110/bbl currently. Diesel prices are also elevated and remain above 
2022 levels. In our base case, we see Brent crude prices staying high in the near 
term, given that tightness in the physical market leaves prices vulnerable to 
renewed attacks or further supply outages.

We believe prices could moderate into next year, assuming higher flows through 
new pipelines and additional production growth from other locations, though 
developments remain contingent on how the Middle East conflict evolves. Today's 
prices, for example, are influenced by high shipping costs (the cost of shipping 
crude from the Middle East to Asia is USD 30-40/bbl compared with below 
USD 5/bbl pre-conflict).
Our medium-term oil outlook assumes that the global energy system will continue 
adapting to the disruption. OPEC crude production rose by 0.92 million barrels per 
day month on month in September to 25.1 million (based on OPEC secondary 
sources), with both Saudi Arabia and Iraq increasing their exports. Trade routes are 
adapting as Middle Eastern exporters expand existing pipelines to bypass the Strait 
of Hormuz or plan to build new ones. Higher prices should also support produc­
tion growth in other regions such as the Americas. In our base case, we see Brent 
crude prices moderating to USD 90/bbl in the first half of 2027. That said, in the 
event of a diplomatic agreement resulting in a normalization of oil flows, prices 
would likely retreat faster. 
The impact on global GDP growth from higher oil prices has so far been limited. 
Oil prices have risen by around USD 40/bbl on average compared with last year. In 
isolation, we estimate such a price increase could reduce global GDP growth by 
0.3-0.5 percentage points, with the amount varying for each economy depending 
on how much energy they import. But the AI investment cycle and the strength of 
household balance sheets have enabled consumption to remain resilient. 
Moreover, following the 2022 oil shock, economies have become more adaptable 
to rising energy prices. Looking ahead, energy prices will likely continue to present 
a headwind for the global economy, but barring a significant and sustained rise 
from here, this should fade over the next 12 months.
A complication compared with the 2022 energy price shock is that this year, 
refined-product prices have become increasingly disconnected from crude oil. 
Damage and disruption to refineries in the Middle East and Russia have reduced 
the supply of finished products. While global refinery throughput has risen from 
its April lows, it stood at 81.8 million barrels per day in September, according to 
the International Energy Agency—about 2.8 million barrels per day below levels 
from a year earlier. The remaining refineries cannot quickly make up the shortfall.

Given this backdrop, we have modelled a risk scenario in which US gasoline prices 
rise to USD 170/bbl and US wholesale diesel prices to USD 250/bbl (from currently 
around USD 130/bbl and USD 200/bbl, respectively). Such high prices would likely 
result in some form of demand destruction, limiting further price increases. They 
could materialize if, for example, attacks on shipping in the Middle East and on 
energy infrastructure in Russia continue, leading to an acute supply squeeze. 
In such a scenario, we would expect US growth to slow toward the low 1% 
range, but we would still not expect a recession: A sharp rise in oil or diesel prices 
alone would not necessarily trigger the factors associated with larger economic 
impacts such as a collapse in AI investment, severe financial stress, disorderly 
credit conditions, or a major market breakdown.
So, while the outlook remains uncertain, we do not think geopolitical develop­
ments alone justify a more defensive investment stance. Broad commodity expo­
sure, including energy, can improve portfolio diversification and provide protection 
against a prolonged supply disruption. More broadly, tight physical markets 
remain consistent with our view that the fundamentals continue to support a 
commodity upcycle.

Investment ideas
Position for market upside: Markets are adjusting to higher bond yields, firmer 
energy prices, and persistent geopolitical risks, but earnings and economic activity 
remain resilient. We expect third-quarter S&P 500 earnings-per-share growth of 
25-30%, supported by AI investment, an improving industrial cycle, and healthy 
aggregate consumption. Investors should remain invested through diversified 
exposure to cyclical and structural growth opportunities and use market strength 
to reduce concentrated single-stock positions. Higher rates and energy costs war­
rant selectivity, but do not justify a broadly defensive stance.
Strong earnings growth should 
support the equity rally.
US midterms: A shift in the balance of power 
Ahead of the US midterm elections on 3 November, we 
assign a 50% probability to a Democratic sweep of 
Congress, a 40% probability to split congressional control, 
and a 10% probability that Republicans retain both cham­
bers. While we expect the balance of power to change, nar­
row majorities and the president’s veto should limit the 
chances of sweeping new legislation.
The president would retain considerable authority over tar­
iffs, foreign policy, and regulation, even if Congress and the 
Supreme Court could challenge some tariff measures. We 
also do not expect a change in congressional control to 
derail data center expansion, since most related decisions are 
made at the state and local levels. 
The most visible effects would likely be greater oversight and 
more difficult fiscal negotiations. Democratic control could 
soften or delay spending cuts, while debt ceiling negotia­
tions in 2027 may create volatility and result in less fiscal 
tightening than currently expected. Reversing enacted tax 
cuts also appears unlikely.
Nonetheless, we continue to expect Treasury yields to decline 
over the next year. Fiscal concerns are likely to persist, but 
we think that easing inflation is likely to prove a more pow­
erful force in lowering rates. 
Overall, we would not treat the midterms as a binary direc­
tional event. For markets, trends in AI investment, earnings, 
inflation, interest rates, and geopolitics are likely to matter 
more than the composition of Congress. Investors should 
monitor shifts in congressional control and the 2027 debt 
ceiling while keeping portfolios anchored to durable drivers 
such as economic growth and corporate earnings.

Consider capital preservation: We expect strong earnings to support equities, but 
higher interest rates, energy costs, geopolitical risks, and uncertainty around AI 
monetization could generate volatility. Capital preservation strategies can help 
investors remain invested while limiting the impact of a market drawdown. They 
may be particularly relevant for investors with concentrated positions, substantial 
gains, lower loss tolerance, or near-term liquidity needs. Investors should calibrate 
preservation and upside participation to their objectives rather than treating the 
outlook as an all-or-nothing decision.
Opportunities emerging in fixed income: The recent bond market repricing has 
restored meaningful return potential to high-quality fixed income. Current yields 
provide attractive income and a substantial cushion against further increases, 
while bonds could also benefit if inflation moderates or economic growth weak­
ens. We favor selective exposure to medium- and longer-duration high-quality 
bonds. We remain more cautious on the longest maturities given fiscal concerns 
and elevated issuance. Investors should focus less on identifying the precise peak 
in yields and more on using current starting yields to strengthen portfolio income 
and diversification. In Switzerland, where fixed income yields remain low, a more 
diversified approach to income generation remains important, in our view.
