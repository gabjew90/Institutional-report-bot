Investors appear concerned
about artificial intelligence
Investors are increasingly worried that the revenue from AI
companies will not be sufficient to service the debts of
companies that are building out AI infrastructure: This is
evidenced by the sharp rise in credit default swap prices for
AI-related bonds, and also by the sharp rise in yields for AI-
related bonds. The most aggressive builders of AI capacity
have been the so-called “neocloud providers”—specialized
providers of cloud infrastructure that rent out high-
performance hardware for AI and related services. Debts
issued by these providers have come under significant selling
pressure from investors.
A key concern among investors is that revenue growth may
not keep pace with the scale of recent investments. Tech
analyst Gartner predicts that 80% of neocloud providers will
“cease independent operations” by 2030, mainly because of
a massive gap between capital expenditures and the likely
return on those expenditures. Moreover, Gartner said that AI
data center owners will need to generate US$8.5 trillion in
revenue by 2030 to cover the cost of capex as well as
generate a good return on their investments. It predicts,
however, that such owners will generate revenue of “no
more than US$3.6 trillion” by 2030—in other words, there
is a nearly US$5 trillion gap. Gartner concluded that “a
continued AI infrastructure investment trajectory could leave
more than US$4 trillion in asset value exposed to write-
down risk through 2032.”
Investors appear anxious enough about holding AI-related
debt that the yield on US Treasury securities appears to have
fallen as investors dumped AI bonds. The yield on the 10-
year US government bond fell sharply to its lowest level in
three weeks, although it remains relatively elevated. The
same was true for bonds issued by the German government,
which were often seen as a very safe asset. It could be that
bond investors are concerned that a weakness of tech-
company bonds might be the first sign of broader economic
risks.
Investor caution is not limited to bonds. It is also affecting
the equity prices of companies that produce AI-related
products, especially chips. For example, one of the indices of
semiconductor equities fell 4.3% yesterday while the overall
S&P 500 index was down by a more modest 1.1%.
And the issue is not only insufficient demand for AI services.
The issue is also whether companies that are building AI
infrastructure will have sufficient electricity to supply
services. There are reports of infrastructure sitting idle due to
lack of electricity. Plus, Morgan Stanley estimated that,
between 2026 and 2028, more than half of new AI servers
might not have powered infrastructure into which they can
be placed. They estimated that new infrastructure will

2026 Nobel laureates in
economics show the importance
of communicating policy
Two American academics, Robert Hall of Stanford and
Michael Woodford of Columbia, won the Nobel Prize in
Economics for 2026. Normally, the highly mathematical
models developed by academic economists would be of little
interest to the readers of this update. Yet, this time is
different.
Their principal insight was that economic policy decisions
alone are not the only factor driving the behavior of millions
of households and businesses. Rather, households are
influenced by expectations about policy. For example, if
participants in the market economy expect a tightening of
monetary policy and, consequently, higher long-term
borrowing costs, it will influence their behavior. This means
that policy will be most effective when future shifts in policy
are publicly signaled.
Indeed, the insights they offered played a role in leading
major central banks to engage in forward guidance during
the past quarter-century. That is, central banks have become
accustomed to signaling their intentions and the likely path
of monetary policy.  Moreover, Professor Woodford’s 2003
book, Interest and Prices, became something of a bible for
central bankers.
The prize comes as the Federal Reserve, under new
leadership, has changed its approach to communicating
policy decisions: Indeed, when the Fed raised the benchmark
interest rate last month, it did not provide an explanation of
its thinking until the publication of the minutes of the
meeting three weeks later. This is unprecedented.  Moreover,
it left market participants uncertain about the Fed’s future
intentions.  According to the framework developed by Hall
and Woodford, reduced policy signaling could hamper the
effective transmission of monetary policy.
US inflation accelerates
When it comes to US inflation, the narrative mostly still
revolves around energy. And the situation is not getting
better. At the time of writing, the Brent crude price was
roughly US$105 per barrel—about 50% higher than just
prior to the start of the Middle East conflict. The price has
been elevated lately due to repeated attacks on Saudi sites by
Iran-aligned Houthi rebels from Yemen. Plus, even though
the United States has been successful in helping tankers to
pass through the Strait of Hormuz, Iran has been successful
in attacking ships in the Persian Gulf. Thus, from the
perspective of oil traders, there remains considerable
uncertainty about the ability to move oil out of the Gulf
region.
In any event, the US government reported that consumer-
price inflation accelerated in September. Consumer prices
were up 3.7% in September versus a year earlier—the
highest inflation rate since May. Prices were up 0.6% from
August to September—the biggest monthly gain since April.
Energy played a key role: In September, energy prices were
up 20.4% from a year earlier and up 5.2% from the
previous month. The price of gasoline was up 34.2% from a
year earlier and up 9.1% from the previous month. 
When volatile energy and food prices are excluded, core
prices were up 2.6% from a year earlier—the highest
increase since June. Core prices were up 0.3% from the
previous month. The monthly increase has not been higher
since April. As such, underlying inflation is starting to
accelerate but remains relatively tame. Still, some nonenergy
prices are certainly influenced by energy prices. For example,
airline fares were up 22.4% from a year earlier.
Meanwhile, although energy has played the primary role in
driving accelerated inflation, the massive investment in AI
has also contributed to inflation. The rollout of data centers
has tightened the availability of information technology
inputs. Consequently, the latest inflation report shows that
the prices of computers and peripherals were up 9.7% in
September versus a year earlier. Plus, the prices of software
and accessories were up 28.4% from a year earlier. On the
other hand, the prices of smartphones continue to recede,
down 9.9% in September from a year earlier.

Going forward, there are reasons to expect a further
acceleration in US inflation, regardless of whether oil prices
rise further. This is because the elevated price of diesel fuel,
now at a record high, may take time to work its way into the
prices of goods being delivered by trucks (lorries). This will
be especially true of food. In September, food prices were up
2.5% from a year earlier. This is now expected to likely
accelerate in the months to come.
What are the implications of rising inflation? First, we know
that, for the past several months, wage increases have not
kept pace with price increases. This means that households’
true purchasing power is declining, which, in turn, puts
pressure on financially stressed households. They reduce the
share of income they save, dip into savings, take on more
debt, shift to lower-priced products or services, and/or
postpone some purchases. This also has an impact on
consumer sentiment and may likely influence the upcoming
mid-term elections.
Second, accelerated inflation changes expectations for
monetary policy: The futures market shows that, although
investors still expect the Federal Reserve to not change the
benchmark interest rate this month, the probability has
shifted considerably. For example, a week before writing,
there was an implied probability of 82.3% that the Fed will
leave the rate unchanged later this month. Yesterday, the
probability was 77.9%, which was not a big change. But
after the release of the inflation report, the probability was
66.8%. Plus, the implied probability of two rate hikes before
the end of the year increased from 18.7% yesterday to
28.7% today. Meanwhile, following the inflation report,
bond yields rose while equity prices declined.
Chinese trade partly driven by AI
Investment in AI is widely viewed as an increasingly
important contributor to economic growth in many parts
around the world. It is also driving rapid increases in trade at
a time when, due to tariffs, one might expect an easing of
export growth. The growth of exports out of China can
partly be explained by AI, although not entirely.
In any event, China’s exports, denominated in US dollars,
were up 36.3% in September versus a year earlier. This was
the fastest growth rate since February and the second-fastest
rate of increase since February 2021 when the Chinese
economy was starting to emerge from the COVID-19
pandemic.
As indicated above, this growth was fueled, in part, by AI.
For example, exports of semiconductors and automatic data-
processing equipment accounted for 17.9% of total exports
in September. Yet, at the same time, these two categories
accounted for 36% of the growth of exports in September
versus a year earlier. In addition, it is reported that exports
of semiconductors were up 136% from a year earlier.
However, when adjusted for prices, such exports were up
only 1%. This means that almost all of the increase in export
revenue from semiconductors was due to rising prices.
Strong export growth was not entirely due to AI. There was
also a significant increase in exports of automobiles, which
were up 62% from a year earlier. Automotive trade has been
a point of political tension with other countries, especially
the European Union. As such, the European Union and
China just signed a deal to impose quantitative restrictions
on the movement of hybrid vehicles from China to the
European Union. Within Europe, but especially within
Germany, there have been concerns that China’s growing
strength in the automotive sector could pose a significant
challenge to Europe’s automotive industry—one that directly
and indirectly employs millions. Perhaps it is too early to
make such an assessment given Chinese branded vehicles still
only account for 13% of EU sales.
China’s exports to the United States were up 39.6% in
September versus a year earlier.  This news did not appear to
set off new trade tensions because the leaders of the two
countries met recently and agreed to roll back tariffs.
Exports to the European Union were up 25.3% in
September versus a year earlier. Also, exports to Southeast
Asia were up 52.3%.
Also, Chinese imports (denominated in US dollars) were up
31% in September versus a year earlier, hitting the highest
level on record. The strength of imports was due to strong
demand for industrial raw materials, minerals (including oil),
and electronic inputs. This demand was largely driven by the
needs of exporters rather than being related to domestic
demand, which remains comparatively subdued.
Finally, it should be noted that the sharp rise in both exports
and imports is not simply due to surging volume demand. It
is partly due to rising prices of commodities and electronic
inputs. This surge, in turn, is partly fueled by strong AI-
related demand. It is also fueled by the conflict in the Middle
East, which has led to shortages of key mineral commodities.

Contradictory US economic
news drives expectations of
monetary policy
Last week, in the United States, there was some good
economic news, and investors appeared to respond
accordingly. In the second quarter, growth in real gross
domestic product was upwardly revised, while inflation
eased slightly in August. These factors likely contributed to
investors’ expectations that the Federal Reserve is less likely
to tighten monetary policy at its next meeting. The result
was an initial rise in equity prices before they reverted. Yet,
bond yields continued to rise despite the good inflation news.
Let’s look at the details:
In the United States, August was a good month for consumer
spending, despite no growth in real income. Specifically, the
US government reported that, in August, real (inflation-
adjusted) disposable personal income (income after taxes)
was unchanged from the previous month.
Yet, real personal consumption expenditures were up 0.6%
from the previous month. This took place because the
personal saving rate dropped from 4.6% in July to 4.1% in
August; as recently as January, it was 5.6%. Households are
keen on maintaining their standard of living in the face of
declining real wages. Yet, reduced savings can only go so far.
If real incomes continue to stagnate, eventually it may have a
negative impact on consumer spending.
Meanwhile, the US government also reported on the
personal consumption expenditure deflator (PCE-deflator)—
the Fed’s principal measure of inflation—which was up
3.4% in August versus a year earlier. This was the same level
as July and lower than the three preceding months. Plus, the
PCE-deflator was up 0.3% from July to August—the biggest
monthly gain since May. When volatile food and energy
prices are excluded, the core PCE-deflator was up 3% in
August versus a year earlier—the same as in June and July
and lower than in the previous three months. Core prices
were up 0.3% from July to August, which was the biggest
gain since April.
This report suggests that inflation is not accelerating, despite
a recent jump in oil prices, new tariffs, and strong demand
from the technology sector. This news pleased investors, who
then revised their expectations for monetary policy. The
futures market’s implied probability that the Fed will raise its
benchmark interest rate next month fell. The probability of
two rate hikes before the end of this year fell as well.
Despite the favorable news on inflation and a reduced
expectation for monetary tightening, the yield on the US
Treasury’s 10-year bond continued to rise. The yield passed
5.3% today for the first time since 2002. It is likely that the
rise in the bond yield meant that investors were more
focused on the state of the real economy rather than inflation
or Fed policy. Indeed, there was positive news regarding the
strength of the economy. The US government released a
revised estimate of second-quarter GDP. Previously, the
government estimated that real GDP grew at an annualized
rate of 1.5% in the second quarter. Now, it says real GDP
grew at a rate of 2.2%.
Moreover, growth in consumer spending was revised from
3.4% to 3.8%. And if imports and exports, inventories, and
government spending are excluded, the economy grew at a
rate of 4.6%—revised upward from 4.2%. This is often
considered a good measure of underlying domestic private
sector demand. As such, investors saw an economy that
appears to be exceptionally strong in the second quarter.
Plus, based on recent purchasing managers’ indices, the
economy might have been strong in the third quarter. This
may help to explain rising bond yields. On the other hand, if
bond yields continue rising, it could have a negative impact
on credit-market activity, especially in the housing market,
which, in turn, could ultimately cause an economic
slowdown.
On the other hand, the US government reported that
employment growth was surprisingly weak in September.
This, in turn, led investors to further downgrade their
expectations for monetary tightening. Of course, one month
does not make a trend. Yet, investors saw the September
report as potentially signaling a weakening of the economic
picture and, consequently, weaker inflation. Let’s look at the
details:
The US government publishes a monthly employment report
that includes the results of two surveys: One is a survey of
households; the other is a survey of establishments. The

latter determined that, in September, only 29,000 new jobs
were created, down from 133,000 in August. Moreover,
employment fell in July and grew only modestly in June.
Thus, employment grew strongly in only one of the last four
months, which could signal weakness.
Moreover, in September, employment fell in financial
services, professional and business services, information,
mining, and all levels of government (federal, state, and
local). Employment grew by 23,000 in healthcare and social
assistance. Plus, employment grew by 10,000 in leisure and
hospitality. Thus, excluding these two categories,
employment declined modestly in September.
The establishment survey also includes data on average
hourly earnings. This indicator was up 3% in September
versus a year earlier—the smallest increase since May 2021.
Moreover, with inflation running well above 3%, real
(inflation-adjusted) earnings are declining. This could weigh
on consumer spending. In recent months, spending has
grown faster than income because households have been
cutting back on saving. This cannot go on indefinitely.
The separate survey of households, which includes self-
employment, found that, in September, the labor force (those
either working or seeking work) grew significantly faster
than the working-age population, which means labor-force
participation increased. On the other hand, employment
grew more slowly than the labor force, leading the
unemployment rate to rise from 4.1% in August to 4.2% in
September. Still, the unemployment rate remains very low.
This jobs report, which suggests the possibility of weakness,
led investors to revise their expectations for Fed policy.
Specifically, the futures market’s implied probability that the
Fed will keep the benchmark interest rate unchanged later
this month, increased. Indeed, this view was echoed in
comments by two Fed leaders: Philip Jefferson, member of
the Federal Reserve Board, and John Williams, president of
the New York Fed.
Jefferson said that “since our September meeting, yields
across the term structure have increased further, a sign that
investors are reassessing the evolving macroeconomic
landscape. My colleagues and I will need to come to our
own judgment, which may take more time.” Williams said
that there is “no need for urgency” about deciding on rate
increases. These comments appeared to influence futures
market probabilities.
Economic impact of rising US
bond yields
The yield on the US Treasury’s 10-year bond recently
exceeded 5.3%, having surged considerably in recent weeks.
Some observers say that there is no need for alarm given that
this level was typical prior to 2007. This is true, but there is
more to the story. Before 2007, the yield was around 5%
because inflation was often higher than today. Investors
expected higher inflation and required an inflation premium
to offset the risk of purchasing long-term bonds.
Lately, inflation expectations have been relatively tame and
stable, despite sharp rises in bond yields. The rise in yields
appears to be largely due to an increase in the yield after
inflation (the real yield). This is not simply my opinion.
Rather, we can estimate investor expectations of inflation
and, consequently, the real yield on bonds. And, as of now,
the real yield is at a record high.
Thus, contrary to what some observers say, this is not simply
a return to the normalcy seen before 2007. Rather, it likely
reflects at least a partial shift in supply and demand
conditions in the bond market. The massive issuance of
bonds by technology companies has likely increased demand
for credit, thereby contributing to the rise in real yields. It
may also reflect an increase in the risk premium that
investors require to hold bonds. That likely has to do with
increasing concerns about fiscal sustainability.
Should we be worried? Perhaps. A high real yield means that
the true cost (after inflation) of borrowing money is very
high. That, in turn, will likely have a negative impact on
credit-market activity. It could dampen activity in the
already weakened US housing market. After all, mortgage
interest rates follow Treasury bond yields. And it could stifle
transactions such as mergers and acquisitions. On the other
hand, high real yields could simply reflect expectations of a
strong economy and, consequently, strong demand for
credit.
What about tech investment in artificial intelligence?
Technology companies have issued a massive volume of debt
to fund the rollout of data centers. Plus, they plan to
continue issuing more debt in the near future. High real
yields on bonds will mean a higher cost of servicing those
debts. That, in turn, means that, to service the debt, these
companies will need to generate more cash than previously
anticipated. This could create additional pressure at a time
when US-based tech companies are facing increased
competition from Chinese suppliers of AI services.

And what about the government? Higher real yields mean
that the true cost of servicing the government’s massive debt
is now higher than previously. Plus, it likely means that any
decision to accelerate government borrowing (such as in
response to an economic downturn) could be met by a
further rise in yields. It means that the government may have
less flexibility to make new fiscal choices.
The United States is not alone: Real yields have risen sharply
in many other countries. The market for sovereign debt is a
global market that crosses borders. A rise in demand for
credit by US technology companies can influence the global
supply and demand conditions for bonds, thereby boosting
yields around the world. Plus, concerns about fiscal
sustainability are not unique to the United States. Such
concerns are also prevalent in Japan, the United Kingdom,
France, and Italy, to name a few countries.
If leaders want to reduce real bond yields, one approach
would be to announce credible plans to shift the trajectory of
fiscal policy, leading to less government borrowing in the
near future. This is not happening at the moment.
Southeast Asia and AI
There has been plenty written about the boom in data
centers in the United States and China. Interestingly,
however, Southeast Asia has become a major center of AI-
related investments. In Malaysia, for example, investment in
data centers now accounts for about 18% of GDP—a
stunning number that may partly explain Malaysia’s 6%
economic growth in the most recent quarter. Moreover, given
Malaysia’s long experience in fabricating semiconductors, it
is perhaps not surprising that exports are booming. On the
other hand, data centers now use about 7% of the country’s
electricity-generating capacity; this can potentially rise to
31% by 2035.
What about the rest of the region? In Indonesia, investment
in information technology is accelerating, having nearly
quadrupled from 2023 to 2025. For both Indonesia and
Malaysia, much of the demand for the services from data
centers appears to be coming from nearby Singapore, which
is a major center of finance and biotechnology.
Thailand is also getting in on the game: It is not surprising
that, in September, Thailand’s purchasing managers’ index
for manufacturing was the highest of the 32 countries
analyzed by S&P Global. There has been an acceleration in
investment in data centers. In 2025 alone, investors sought
permission to build digital projects valued at US$24 billion.
Finally, Vietnam is also accelerating investment in data
centers.
Burgeoning fiscal troubles in
Europe as inflation accelerates
Investors are selling French bonds and purchasing German
ones: The spread between the yields on French and German
bonds hit its highest level since 2012. While yields and
spreads on Italian government bonds have also increased, it
is France that is generating the most anxiety on the part of
investors who are concerned about the state of France’s
government finances, the inability of the legislature to find a
resolution to the fiscal imbalance, and concerns about what
might happen in next year’s presidential election.
France is by no means alone in dealing with these issues. Yet,
it has become the poster child for such concerns. Although
France is nowhere close to facing default risk, some analysts
are comparing this situation to the eurozone debt crisis in
2012, when the so-called PIIGS nations (Portugal, Ireland,
Italy, Greece, and Spain), all faced significant default risk
due to unbalanced fiscal policy and soaring debt-servicing
costs.
The crisis was only resolved when Mario Draghi, president
of the European Central Bank, said that the ECB will do
“whatever it takes” to save the euro. Then yields fell,
countries reformed their finances, and the crisis dissipated.
Today, Greece—which faced major fiscal challenges during
the crisis in 2012—has lower borrowing costs than the
United States.
The problem now is that crisis begets crisis. That is, the
perception that a crisis is brewing has led investors to sell
French bonds for the safety of German bonds. This, in turn,
can exacerbate the difficulty in servicing French debt. What
is needed is a credible plan by France to boost fiscal probity.
Yet, given the divided and fragmented legislature, it is hard
to see how this will happen anytime soon.
The European Commission released data on inflation in the
eurozone in September. It found that, in September,
consumer prices were up 3.8% from a year earlier—the
highest rate of inflation since September 2023. This largely
reflected the 18.8% increase in energy prices versus a year
earlier. Plus, energy prices were up 3.9% from the previous
month. When volatile food and energy prices are excluded,
core prices were up 2.5% in September from a year earlier,

which was roughly in line with core inflation in recent
months. Thus, it does not appear that energy-price inflation
has had a serious impact on non-energy prices—at least not

However, investors are concerned about rising inflation in
Europe: They likely view elevated energy prices as here to
stay. That suggests that energy prices may gradually seep
