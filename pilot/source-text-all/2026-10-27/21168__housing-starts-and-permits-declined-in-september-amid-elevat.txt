Housing Starts and Permits Declined in 
September Amid Elevated Mortgage Rates 
Headlines 
 
Total housing starts fell for a third straight month in September, down 5% to 
1.221 million. On a year-over-year basis starts were down 7%. 
 
 
Both multifamily and single-family housing starts fell on the month. 
Regionally, housing starts increased only in the South. 
 
Residential construction permits also fell on the month, down 0.2% to 1.400 
million units. 
 
Total housing completions rose 2% in September from August but were still 
14% down from a year earlier.  
 
PNC expects single-family homebuilding to remain weak through the end of 
2026. 
Details
Total privately-owned U.S. housing starts fell for a 
third straight month in September, down 5% from 
1.288 million units in August (upwardly revised from 
1.275 million) to 1.221 million units in September. The 
September reading came below PNC’s expectation 
of 1.280 million. The three-month moving average of 
housing starts, which smooths out some of the 
monthly volatility, dropped 5% in September to 1.293 
million. 
In the recovery following the pandemic, starts rose 
to a peak of 1.8 million units in March 2022 but have 
declined since then as the typical 30-year fixed 
mortgage rate has gone from below 3% to above 
7%. Starts have been volatile since early 2025 with 
uncertainty surrounding tariffs, the Iran conflict, and 
higher mortgage rates over the summer. On a year-
over-year basis, starts were down 7% in September 
2026. 
Both multifamily and single-family housing starts fell 
on the month, down 5% and 7%, respectively. 
Multifamily construction is much more volatile from 
month to month. On a year-over-year basis single-
family starts were up 2.6%, while multifamily starts 
dropped 25%.  
Regionally, housing starts increased only in the 
South, rising 6% in September after falling for two 
months. The increase in the South in September 
came entirely in multifamily starts, up 39%, with a 3% 
drop in single-family starts. Starts were flat from last 
month in the Northeast, while they fell 19% in the 
Midwest and 23% in the West.  
Total housing starts remained lowest in September 
in the slower-growing Northeast (114k) and Midwest 
(158k), well below levels in the South (714k) and West 
(235k).

Residential construction permits also fell on the 
month, down 0.2% to 1.400 million units in September 
(seasonally adjusted annualized rate). Multifamily 
permits rose in September (up 6.0%) while single-
family permits fell for a second straight month 
(down 4.1%). On a year-ago basis permits fell 5% for 
single-family and dropped less 
than 1% for 
multifamily. 
Total housing completions rose 2% in September 
from August but were still 14% down from a year 
earlier. Completions remain on a downward trend 
after peaking in August 2024. Completions rose 
steadily from 2011 through the first half of 2024 
before beginning to moderate. They will drift lower 
over the near term with the gradual decline in 
housing units currently under construction and 
housing starts since late 2025. 
Permit data suggest housing starts will remain 
broadly stable in the near term, although single-
family construction faces increasing downside risks. 
PNC expects homebuilding activity to remain 
subdued through 
year-end 2026 as elevated 
mortgage rates and poor affordability continue to 
restrain housing demand. The current average 
30‑year fixed mortgage rate is around 7.4%, roughly 
140 basis points higher than in late February and 110 
basis points higher than a year ago. The National 
Association of Home Builders’ Housing Market Index 
suggests weaker single-family construction in 
October, with the index down to a 14-year low. Both 
current sales of new single-family homes and 
measures of prospective buyer traffic remained 
subdued and are much weaker than at the start of 
2026. 
The labor market has shown signs of stabilization in 
2026, which should provide some support for 
housing demand. Nevertheless, downside risks 
remain, particularly from a prolonged conflict in the 
Middle East and elevated energy prices, as well as 
higher mortgage rates. Any renewed weakening in 
the labor market would also pose a near-term 
headwind to housing demand.
