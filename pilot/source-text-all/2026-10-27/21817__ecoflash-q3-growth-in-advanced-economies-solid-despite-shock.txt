Q3 Growth in advanced economies: solid despite shocks 
 
Growth looks to have held up in Q3 despite successive shocks (see our analysis), as it did in 2025 and H1 2026. A longer 
Middle East conflict has fed into more persistent inflation; drought has worsened in many countries, and interest rates 
have risen further – at the short end, with both the ECB and the Fed raising policy rates in September, and even more at 
the long end. However, our nowcasts show that growth is bending without breaking in most advanced economies. GDP 
figures (due on 29 October in the US and 30 October for the euro area and its member states) are expected to confirm 
that. In the Euro area, momentum looks stable (excluding Ireland, whose GDP is volatile). In Germany, growth has been 
uninterrupted since Q4 2025, while France is likely to have returned to growth after stagnating in H1. In the US, growth 
is likely to have gathered pace, according to the GDP Now, still driven by AI, but increasingly by other sectors as well.  
 
 
Euro area: resilience beneath the surface. Our nowcast points to growth slowing to 0.3% in q/q, though the pace is stable 
excluding Ireland (Irish GDP is volatile and had markedly boosted the Q2 figure). Excluding Ireland, growth should hold at its H2 
average, a sign of the bloc’s underlying resilience. Also, momentum has broadened to a growing number of sectors (see our 
analysis). While industrial production stalled over the summer, services remained buoyant. The services PMI rebounded sharply 
in Q3 and sits firmly in expansion territory (53.0 in September). Improving job-related survey data (PMI and European Commission 
surveys) and a stable unemployment rate (6.4% in August) hint at more hiring in Q3 – a support for household consumption that 
partly offsets purchasing-power losses from persistent inflation. Corporate investment is underpinned by buoyant new investment 
lending (8.7% y/y In August, 12-month cumulative). 
France: growing, nevertheless. The succession of shocks does not appear to have prevented France from returning to 
growth in Q3, at 0.2% q/q, according to our nowcast. One-off factors accounted for the stall in activity in H1. In Q3, the drought 
worsened and industrial production remained subdued. However, market services production rebounded in July and August, 
notably in business services, restaurants, digital and real estate. The latter points to residential investment recovering after a weak 
H1. Corporate investment likely followed, as reflected in buoyant credit flows and AI-related investment. Household consumption 
is likely to have improved slightly on higher electric vehicle sales.

Germany: growth still solid. The H1 rebound in activity is moderating but continues. Our nowcast points to a fourth straight 
quarter of growth in Q3, at 0.2% q/q (after 0.3% in Q4 2025, 0.4% in Q1 2026, and 0.3% in Q2). Manufacturing activity eased 
slightly, according to the September PMI survey (53.9, down 0.4 m/m). Government spending will remain growth-supportive, with 
defence and infrastructure outlays gaining traction. Infrastructure spending continues to underpin activity in public works, which 
stands at its highest since November 2021 (2.5% 3m/3m in August). Exports (4.4% y/y between January and August) remain the 
main growth engine, helped by a pivot towards Europe (7.2%). 
United States: an expansion undeterred by inflation. Growth is likely to have gathered pace in Q3. The Atlanta Fed’s GDP 
Now points to a 3.5% annualized growth rate (AR), close to our own 3.7% forecast (0.9% non-AR) and up from 2.2% in 
Q2. Domestic demand remains the engine. Consumption is weathering the pickup in inflation, while the AI-driven capex cycle 
remains robust – as reflected in retail sales and nondefence capital-goods orders (excl. aircraft). As a result, growth in final sales 

 
 
to private domestic purchasers1 should print above 4% AR again. By contrast, external trade should remain a drag on growth, 
reflecting AI-related import needs. Survey data point to momentum that is both solid and broad-based. The ISM indices have 
remained firmly in growth territory (54.9 quarterly average for manufacturing and services alike). At the same time, they highlight 
persistent price and supply-chain pressures.
