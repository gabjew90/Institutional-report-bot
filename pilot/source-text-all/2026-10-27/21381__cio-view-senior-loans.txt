Central scenario
Asset class performance maintained a steady upward trajectory until late 
September, with the Morningstar LSTA US Leveraged Loan Index reaching 
its highest level on 22 September. Since then, however, some gains have 
been given back as spreads widened. The recent rise in rates across the 
curve, amid higher energy-driven inflation, has begun to weigh on risk 
assets. Despite this, the asset class's floating-rate structure has supported 
year-to-date performance of 2.9%, making leveraged loans one of the top-
performing segments of credit markets this year, ahead of both investment-
grade and high-yield debt.
The Federal Reserve's 25bps rate hike in September raised the target 
range for the federal funds rate to 3.75-4%. Given the strong September 
consumer price index (CPI) reading, which showed building inflation 
pressures, we anticipate further Fed hikes in December 2026 and March 
2027. However, we do not expect a prolonged tightening cycle similar 
to that of 2022, as rates are already high and growth is much lower. 
Taken together, the rise in short-end base rates should continue to provide 
attractive carry for the asset class.
In September, software was the second-best-performing sector within the 
Morningstar LSTA US Leveraged Loan Index, after health care technology. 
The sub-indices gained 0.91% and 1.25%, respectively. Software came 
under significant pressure in 1Q as AI-related concerns raised questions 
about the sustainability of business models in the sector. Given software's 
large weight in the index, its recovery from the 1Q low has been 
an important contributor to performance. The software sub-index has 
narrowed its underperformance relative to the broader market, but remains 
down 1.82% year to date, compared with a 2.9% year-to-date return for 
the overall index.
The laggards in September were media and telecommunications, down 
1.54% and 1.84%, respectively. This weakness was primarily driven by 
concerns that SpaceX could become a key competitor to established 
telecommunications providers.
By rating, single-B and double-B loans, which together represent roughly 
83% of the index, delivered positive total returns in September. More 
stress was evident in the CCC segment, which had its worst month since 
February and fell 2.18%. Higher yields are increasing refinancing and debt-
servicing pressure on these riskier issuers. Given the floating-rate nature 
of leveraged loans, some issuers are struggling to service existing debt at 
current financing costs. Five of the 10 worst-performing loans were rated 
CCC.

For the overall index, PitchBook LCD reports that the US leveraged loan 
default rate rose slightly to 2.97% in September on a trailing 12-month, 
issuer-weighted basis. Excluding distressed debt exchanges, the issuer-
weighted default rate fell to 1.08%, its lowest level since February 2023. 
In Europe, default activity remained at a similar level to August, with the 
trailing 12-month default rate at 2%.
Over the next 12 months, we expect default rates to remain above average, 
at around 3-4%. This is also reflected in distress ratios, which have been 
above average over the past month. Distress ratios can be a leading 
indicator of default rates over the following year.
September has typically been a strong month for primary market activity. 
Given elevated yields, issuance was lower at USD 78.3bn this year, 
compared with more than USD 90bn in each of the previous two years. 
The weaker issuance reflected a decline in refinancing activity rather 
than softer underlying demand. Leveraged buyout (LBO) and merger and 
acquisition (M&A) financing continued to improve, reaching USD 30.6bn 
in September, the highest monthly volume in the past five years. Among 
the largest September deals by volume were the Paramount Skydance loan 
for the Warner Bros. Discovery acquisition and a loan provided to Volta for 
its data center project.
On the demand side, collateralized loan obligation (CLO) issuance has lost 
some momentum since the summer. Nevertheless, CLO managers priced 
nearly USD 14bn in September, making it the second-strongest September 
since 2000. Year-to-date CLO issuance totals USD 126.1bn, 17% below 
last year's pace. With three Fed hikes priced into the curve, prime funds 
are seeing increasing inflows as investors shift from fixed- to floating-rate 
products.
Floating-rate yields on US and European loan indices remain relatively 
elevated at around 9.1% and 8.4%, respectively. In our base case, coupon 
income should remain solid and be the main contributor to total returns.
Upside scenario
Goldilocks
A swift resolution to the conflict in the Middle East allows oil prices 
to stabilize below USD 90/bbl. Disinflation resumes in the US once the 
tariff and energy impacts have been passed through. Investment spending 
and AI adoption exceed expectations. Healthy consumption, buoyant 
investment, and strong productivity growth allow the US economy to grow 
by 2.5-3% in 2026 and 2027. Residual negative tariff effects are offset by 
business-friendly policies in the US, Europe, and China.
Downside scenarios
Yield pressure
Oil prices remain under upward pressure due to prolonged disruptions of 
flows through the Strait of Hormuz or widespread destruction of energy

concentrated in a few sectors. Concerns rise about longer-term inflation 
and the credibility of monetary and fiscal policy. Longer-term bond yields 
drift higher. Central banks face pressure to raise interest rates despite 
pockets of weakness in the economy. Labor market softness leads to 
further wage growth slowing.
AI bear market
AI stocks deflate due to growing concerns over returns on investment, 
elevated yields, and unsustainable commitments by large AI companies. 
Anticipated AI productivity gains do not materialize. Worsening investor 
sentiment leads to a broader market decline. The downward trend in 
equity markets feeds through to the real economy via the sentiment and 
wealth channels. Declining investment in AI infrastructure allows for a 
redistribution of resources into other segments of the economy, allowing 
US GDP to grow by 0.5-1% in 2026 and 2027. Inflation concerns give way 
to fears of a global economic decline, requiring central banks to cut rates 
aggressively in 2027.
