 
Billions In Nvidia Chips Collecting Dust In Amazon 
Warehouses As AI's "Paperweight" Problem Goes 
Mainstream 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 21, 2026 - 03:45 AM 
For much of the past year, we have been pounding the table on what is probably the most 
underappreciated problem in the AI capex boom. 
It goes like this: hyperscalers order hundreds of billions in Nvidia silicon, the chips get built, 
shipped and delivered on schedule... and then they sit in crates, because the data center they 
were meant for has no power, no transformers, or no roof. Until those chips are plugged in, they 
earn nothing, they depreciate anyway, and the scarcity they leave behind keeps the price of 
every GPU-hour that is online sky-high. In other words, a stockpile of unlit chips is the world's 
most expensive, and very inflationary, paperweight. 
Now we have confirmation from inside the biggest cloud of them all. 
According to a Business Insider report based on internal Amazon documents and a recording of 
an AWS staff meeting, Nvidia's newest GB300 racks, arriving at a clip of billions of dollars a 
month, have been staged at "intermediary warehouses" because the AWS sites they were 
meant for "had room for more racks but not enough available power to turn them on." Or, as we 
summarized it last night: 
The "Early Rack Receipt" 
The details are worth walking through, because they show exactly how a chip shortage turns 
into a chip glut without anyone ever cancelling an order. 
Per BI, internal emails from August show Amazon preparing a new "Early Rack Receipt" 
process for an expected wave of server racks carrying Nvidia's GB300 chips at US facilities. 
One email projected that Amazon would take delivery of about $5 billion of GB300 racks in 
August, followed by roughly $3 billion each month "for the foreseeable future." 
 

 
 
Normally, Amazon doesn't pay for racks until the gear is actually installed in a data center, which 
leaves the suppliers to carry the cost of equipment in transit (a very Amazonian way to conserve 
cash). Under the new process, however, Amazon takes ownership of the racks earlier in the 
supply chain, before they ever reach a data center. BI explains why: the scramble for Nvidia 
GPUs is so intense that Amazon has to compete harder for scarce components, so it grabs the 
hardware when it can, whether or not there is a socket waiting for it. 
Translation: Amazon is now paying for chips sooner, and plugging them in later. Any delay in 
getting racks installed and connected to reliable power "potentially pushes back the moment 
when revenue starts flowing from these assets," as BI puts it, while the cash is already gone. 
And it gets better: at least one AWS supply-chain team did not have "a clear forecasted path" for 
getting racks into data centers, according to one of the emails. Amazon's spokesperson pushed 
back, saying the company routinely stages finished hardware near deployment sites until power 
is available and calling the practice "best business practice." Which is probably true... and also 
exactly what you would say about $3 billion a month of very expensive inventory sitting in a 
warehouse. 
Bernstein, cited by BI, offered the sell-side's version of a shrug: power permits, cooling systems, 
electrical equipment, construction and hardware all move on separate schedules, and "timing is 
imperfect." 
 

 
"Congratulations" 
The most quotable moment, however, came from AWS CFO John Felton, who at an internal 
September staff meeting had a joke for Prasad Kalyanaraman, the executive running much of 
Amazon's AI infrastructure buildout: 
"You've taken us free cash flow negative as a company, so congratulations." 
Kalyanaraman did not argue. Amazon's machine-learning and AI business is "hugely free cash 
flow negative," he said, and he expects the company's FCF to stay negative for "the next couple 
of years or so." As a reminder, three months ago we reported that for the first time in years, 
Amazon's trailing free cash flow turned decidedly negative, -$7.6 billion, when it reported Q2 in 
July. 
 
Two years ago, Wall Street was debating what Amazon should do with its cash pile; this year 
the company is spending $220 billion in capex, and the cash pile is something people reminisce 
about at staff meetings. 
He also explained why the warehouses keep filling up. Amazon tries to commit capital at the 
"last responsible moment," when it has the clearest view of demand, but the forecast "is 
changing on a daily basis," including when data centers will be ready and how much equipment 
AWS needs to keep on hand. An eight-week supply buffer might be routine for a smaller 
company, he said, but at Amazon's scale it can mean holding "billions and billions of dollars" of 
extra equipment inventory. 
 

 
Some other gems from the BI piece: 
●​ Kalyanaraman told Andy Jassy in 2023 that AWS would "surely" be out of its capacity 
crunch by 2027. Amazon now expects demand to exceed available capacity in 2027 too. 
●​ An internal AWS dashboard is literally called "Secured Power at Risk," tracking 
transmission bottlenecks, curtailment requirements and "use it or lose it" clauses that 
can cost Amazon its grid allocation if a project misses milestones. 
●​ In West Texas there weren't enough pipefitters and electricians, so AWS is paying to 
build housing complexes for workers who will live on site for the two or three years it 
takes to finish the projects. 
●​ Even the crates the servers ship in have become a material line item. "We have new 
constraints that keep popping up," Kalyanaraman said. 
“Per Cleanview Research, Amazon has 44 gigawatts of data center capacity under development 
across 16 states, more than three times the 13.9 GW it operates today. And that 13.9 GW is 
already the biggest fleet in the business: 69% more than Microsoft and roughly double Google 
and Meta. That’s a lot of sockets that will need a lot of power before the racks in the 
warehouses can fill them.

Ten days later, after noting that it takes 2.5 years just to get the power transformers for a new

And last month, as Oracle's Project Jupiter campus in New Mexico (built to deliver $300 billion 
of compute to OpenAI) ground to a halt under a force majeure declaration (and looks 
increasingly like it may never be completed), we asked the obvious follow-up question: 
We weren't alone in suspecting it. Microsoft's Satya Nadella all but admitted it last November, 
when he said that "the biggest issue we are now having is not a compute glut, but it's power" 
and that Microsoft "may actually have a bunch of chips sitting in inventory that I can't plug in" 
(we covered it here). 
By April, half of the US data centers scheduled to start in 2026 were set to be canceled or 
delayed, with only about 5GW of the planned 16GW actually under construction. Michael Burry 
went further last December, calling for "pics and proof" of Nvidia GPUs being warehoused in 
mass quantities. Back then it sounded like a conspiracy theory. Now we have Amazon's own 
emails describing an entire process built around receiving racks before there is anywhere to put 
them (and that's before even considering double (or triple) ordering). 
And if Amazon, which by most accounts is the best (or among the best) data center builder on 
the planet, can't plug its chips in on time, maybe we should ask how everyone else is doing. 
Idle Chips, Record Rents 
Which brings us to the part of the story that matters for everyone who doesn't own Amazon 
stock: inflation. 
Logic would suggest that a glut of chips in warehouses should crush the price of compute. The 
opposite is happening, for the simple reason that a GPU in a crate produces exactly zero 
GPU-hours. What is scarce is energized compute, and its price keeps going up. 
Goldman's Rich Privorotsky, head of the bank's Delta-One trading desk, made precisely this 
point in his October 16 "GS Basics" note, (available to pro subs): 
 

 
"There is too much capital seeking a home relative to the available supply of investable, 
energized compute. Repricing is a process the market will continue to go through, sometimes 
gradually, sometimes acutely, with the potential to become systemic. The interesting 
contradiction is that spot compute pricing remains incredibly strong. B200s, H200s, H100s, even 
ageing A100s continue to command meaningful rental rates. Compute that should theoretically 
be depreciating rapidly remains valuable. There is an undeniable scarcity of available compute, 
which is structurally bullish for those who already own it." 
His chart shows the Silicon Data B200 on-demand rental index (non-hyperscale) at $6.01 per 
GPU-hour, within a hair of the March record of $6.11 and up 40% from January's low of $4.29: 
 
Goldman's equity research sees it the same way. In upgrading CoreWeave to Buy yesterday, 
Goldman's Gabriela Borges argued that compute pricing is "biased to the upside" over the next 
24 months (a call HSBC promptly took apart, as we discussed in "HSBC Slams Goldman's 
CoreWeave Bull Case" earlier today). 
Put the two together and you have the textbook definition of an inflationary bottleneck: 
enormous amounts of capital, labor, copper and electricity consumed upfront, with the output 
(compute) delayed for months or years, while the price of whatever capacity is available gets bid 
up. Every AI lab, enterprise and startup that rents GPUs pays that scarcity premium, and passes 
it on. 
The chips sitting in Amazon's staging warehouses were supposed to be the supply response 
that brings those rental rates down. Instead they're collecting dust! 
 

 
Revisiting Amazon's $8 Billion "Asset-Light" Chip Sale 
Which also casts a rather different light on a story from three weeks ago. 
On October 2, the FT reported that Amazon was seeking to offload about $8 billion of Nvidia 
Grace Blackwell chips to outside investors through a special-purpose vehicle. Amazon would 
then lease the chips back from the SPV, which would raise money through debt (expected to get 
an investment-grade rating off Amazon's AA), with up to 10% of the equity sold as well, so that 
Amazon "will not own any stakes in the entity." The stated goal: a more "asset-light" balance 
sheet. 
At the time we focused on the obvious angle: 
Later that day we followed up with "Amazon, Broadcom Pile Into Off-Balance Sheet SPVs As AI 
Circularity "Undeniably Increasing"", adding the Amazon deal to our running tally of hyperscaler, 
Nvidia and Broadcom off-balance sheet commitments, which by then had hit $3.1 trillion (up 
$1.3 trillion in three months). Sell the chips, rent them back, call it "asset-light." 
But with the BI report in hand, the sale looks like it may have been about a lot more than 
accounting optics. Consider the timeline:

Five plus three is, coincidentally, exactly eight: the projected August and September GB300 
deliveries add up to the precise size of the SPV. Now, to be fair, the FT's sources said the chips 
in the deal have been "deployed" in more than a dozen US data centers across five states, and 
the report doesn't say whether they are GB200s or the newer GB300s. So maybe it's just a 
coincidence. But it is a very convenient one: a company that started taking ownership of billions 
in racks it couldn't power, and whose cash flow just turned negative, went looking for someone 
else to own (and finance) $8 billion of Nvidia silicon within weeks. 
And whoever buys into that SPV is buying a depreciating asset. The FT notes Grace Blackwell 
"will soon be superseded" by Nvidia's Vera Rubin, while Amazon's filings assume each chip 
generation lasts at least five years. Every month a rack sits in a warehouse is a month of that 
five-year economic life burned without a single dollar of revenue (even if the accountants don't 
 

 
start the depreciation clock until it's plugged in). Then again, when your July $25 billion bond 
deal already ran into weaker demand for long-dated paper, as the FT also notes, selling chips to 
insurers and pension funds via an IG-rated SPV is probably the path of least resistance. 
Past The "Last Responsible Moment" 
Kalyanaraman's own philosophy, as recorded by BI, is that "if we can wait to spend the capital 
as late as possible, it's better for us." The AI arms race has flipped that on its head: Amazon is 
now spending as early as possible, on chips it can't use, just to make sure a competitor doesn't 
get them first (where they are likely to also sit unused). Multiply that by every hyperscaler and 
neocloud fighting for the same Nvidia allocation, and you get a warehouse problem measured in 
the tens of billions, most likely parked in "construction in progress," where, as we explained in 
"The Teaser Period", it doesn't even start depreciating until it is placed in service. Out of sight, 
and out of the income statement... for now. 
Our take: the glut gets bigger before it gets smaller. Much bigger. Nvidia keeps shipping, the "for 
the foreseeable future" $3 billion a month keeps arriving, and the bottleneck is still power, which 
no SPV can finance into existence on a 2.5-year transformer backlog. That is why we keep 
saying "behind the meter" generation has to become mandatory (think modular reactors, gas 
turbines, and batteries). And since all of this is increasingly financed with debt (as we have been 
warning since last October), every month a chip sits unplugged is a month of interest paid on an 
asset that earns nothing. In the meantime, the paperweights keep compute scarce, rental rates 
keep climbing, and the bill shows up in everyone's cost base. As for the capital markets, they 
have already started voting on the companies building the warehouses' worth of capacity with 
borrowed money: 
 

 
 
Amazon is (for now) a very long way from that list. But if the best data center builder in the world 
needs warehouses, early rack receipts and an $8 billion SPV to keep the conveyor belt moving,
