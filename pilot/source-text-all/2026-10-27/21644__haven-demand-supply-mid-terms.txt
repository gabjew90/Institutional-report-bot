Haven demand 
AI jitters and volatility in French government bonds have created safe haven 
demand for Treasuries, taking the 10Y yield back towards 5.00%, 30bp from the 
peak traded in early October and right on top of its 50-day moving average 
(Figure 1). Markets will focus on the October FOMC meeting, PCE data, month-
end auctions and extension trade next week. Our house view is for the FOMC to 
hold rates next week, followed by a hike in December, in line with market pricing 
(Figure 2). 
Despite an expected FOMC hold, the stronger-than-expected inflation data in 
September makes the October meeting more interesting. After CPI and PPI, our 
economists forecast core PCE tracking at a relatively firm pace in September, with 
the MoM near the cusp of rounding between 0.3-0.4% MoM with the YoY rate up 
to 3.1% from 3.0%. This is not ideal for the Fed, though core FOMC members have 
suggested a bias towards holding in October. 
November refunding preview 
We expect the Treasury to maintain stable coupon auction sizes at the November 
Treasury Refunding Announcement (QRA) on Wednesday 4 November, with total 
nominal coupon supply of USD125bn comprising USD58bn of 3Y notes, USD42bn 
of 10Y notes and USD25bn of 30Y bonds. We also expect TIPS auction sizes to 
remain unchanged, consistent with recent refunding announcements. 
The Treasury has maintained guidance since February 2024 that nominal coupon 
and FRN auction sizes would remain unchanged for at least the next several 
quarters (Figure 3). While rising budget deficits ultimately point to higher coupon 
issuance over time, we do not expect any immediate changes at the upcoming 
refunding announcement.

Instead, bill issuance has been doing most of the adjustment. The Treasury has 
gradually increased bill auction sizes across the curve since early October, and we 
expect bill supply to remain elevated through the remainder of 2026. Bills currently 
account for about 22% of outstanding marketable debt. 
Nevertheless, investors will closely scrutinise issuance guidance for any indication 
of future changes to the maturity composition of Treasury borrowing. If officials 
decide that additional coupon issuance is required, they may prefer to increase 
issuance at the front end of the curve. Such an approach would reduce the 
weighted average maturity (WAM) of Treasury debt and would be consistent with 
the administration's stated objective of lowering long-term borrowing costs. 
The Treasury will also release its quarterly financing estimates on Monday 
2 November, including updated guidance on the Treasury General Account (TGA). 
At USD970bn, the TGA remains above the Treasury's year-end target of 
USD850bn. Markets may also receive an update on plans to invest excess TGA 
cash balances in overnight repo facilities to generate additional returns.

Another key area of focus will be Treasury buybacks. Investors will pay particular 
attention to buyback operations in the long end of the curve. Despite an increase 
in long-end buybacks, long-term Treasury yields have remained elevated. This 
raises the possibility that Treasury officials may eventually consider reducing 
issuance in the 20Y and 30Y sectors, although the timing of such a move remains 
uncertain. 
As a result, issuance guidance will likely be the most closely watched aspect of the 
refunding announcement. If the Treasury ultimately reduces long-end coupon 
supply, we expect the adjustment to be offset through increased issuance of 
Treasury bills and/or shorter-dated coupon securities. 
Deficit and mid-terms 
Mid-term elections are approaching and could have important implications for the 
fiscal outlook and Treasury market. Our base case is that Democrats regain control 
of the House. Under this scenario, legislative gridlock is likely regardless of which 
party controls the Senate. 
A divided government would reduce the likelihood of major spending programmes 
and tax cuts, easing upward pressure on Treasury term premium and long-term 
interest rates. All else equal, this would be supportive of lower long-end yields and 
a flatter yield curve. Front-end rates, however, are primarily driven by monetary 
policy expectations. With energy prices remaining elevated and the Fed likely to 
deliver additional rate hikes, front-end yields such as the 2Y Treasury yield are 
likely to remain high. 
The impact of the mid-term elections is therefore likely to be greater on lawmaking 
than on monetary policy or broader macroeconomic trends. If Democrats gain 
control of the House, passing major fiscal legislation would become more difficult 
regardless of Senate control. Senate procedures and presidential veto authority 
generally require a degree of bipartisan support to enact significant policy changes. 
There would nonetheless be meaningful implications beyond legislation. Senate 
control would remain important for judicial appointments and the confirmation of 
senior government officials. Changes in congressional control could also lead to 
increased oversight through hearings and investigations, potentially affecting the 
administration’s policy agenda and political priorities. 
Government funding negotiations are likely to remain a recurring source of tension, 
meaning the risk of future shutdown episodes cannot be ruled out. In contrast, 
trade and foreign policy are likely to be less sensitive to election outcomes because 
these areas remain largely directed by the executive branch. 
Regardless of the election outcome, the fiscal trajectory remains challenging. 
Federal budget deficits are likely to continue expanding over the coming years, 
requiring higher Treasury issuance and increasing the overall supply of 
government debt.

The federal budget deficit reached USD1.99trn in fiscal year 2026, which ended 
30 September, reflecting USD7.4trn of spending (up 6% from the prior year) and 
USD5.4trn of revenue (up 3%). This compares with deficits of USD1.775trn in fiscal 
year 2025 and USD1.817trn in fiscal year 2024. At approximately 6% of GDP, the 
deficit remains unusually large for a period that is not characterised by recession 
or a major economic shock such as the Global Financial Crisis or the Covid-19 
pandemic. 
The CBO forecasts the debt-to-GDP ratio to rise from 100% this year to 120% in 
ten years (Figure 4). While stronger economic growth can improve the fiscal 
outlook at the margin, it is unlikely to fundamentally alter the trajectory of federal 
debt accumulation. According to Congressional Budget Office analysis, real GDP 
growth would need to reach approximately 6% to stabilise federal debt levels 
assuming interest rates around 5%.  
Taken together, the outcome of the mid-term elections may influence the pace of 
future fiscal expansion, but it is unlikely to materially change the long-term debt 
trajectory. The more important implication for Treasury markets remains the 
continued rise in borrowing needs and the resulting increase in Treasury issuance 
over time.
