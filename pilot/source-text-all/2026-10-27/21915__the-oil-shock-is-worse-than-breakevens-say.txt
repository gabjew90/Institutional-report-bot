 
The Oil Shock Is Worse Than Breakevens Say 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
THURSDAY, OCT 22, 2026 - 03:15 PM 
Authored by Simon White, Bloomberg macro strategist, 
Breakevens are underestimating the inflationary impact of the oil shock. 
Oil prices have experienced one of their largest ever positive shocks, yet you wouldn’t have 
thought so from looking at inflation breakevens. They are lower in several maturities since the 
Iran war started, despite a rise of more than one and a half times in crude. 
 
There is no contradiction, however, if we cease to think of TIPS as unimpeachable forecasters 
of inflation, and instead acknowledge them for what they actually are: risk assets with some 
inflation (specifically, CPI) protection built in. 
The oil shock has increased inflation risks, but it has also raised macro and geopolitical risks. 
The two pull TIPS yields in opposite ways: the first depresses them, widening breakevens; the 
second boosts them, causing breakevens to fall. 
 

 
The nature of the oil shock has led to the latter having an outsized influence, causing 
breakevens to, perversely, decline (at the five-year maturity). But breakevens are just that: the 
inflation rate required to break even from a long TIPS and a short Treasury position. They are 
no more a forecast of future price growth than the implied exchange rate from FX forwards is a 
prediction of where spot will be. 
Inflation expectations would be appreciably higher if it weren’t for macro and geopolitical risks. 
By how much we can gauge using the Federal Reserve’s DKW model, which estimates a 
liquidity premium for TIPS. 
 
A greater TIPS liquidity premium means a higher TIPS yield and a correspondingly lower 
implied breakeven. As the chart above shows, five-year breakevens would be ~50 bps higher 
since the Iran war, taking them to close 2.90%, their highest since 2022, if the liquidity premium 
had not changed. 
It is key that we are not experiencing a more typical demand-driven oil shock but a supply one 
that is geopolitical in origin. This type of shock is impacting TIPS in two ways: 
1.​ Worsening the relative liquidity of TIPS to Treasuries; and 
2.​ Diminishing the interchangeability of oil and TIPS as inflation hedges 
The relative liquidity of inflation-linked bonds tends to suffer when macro risks increase as there 
is a greater shift to benchmark collateral, ie Treasuries, while TIPS suffer from having a 
narrower buyer base. 
 

 
We are seeing evidence of this. Dealers’ inventory of TIPS has been rising, most notably in the 
shortest maturity bucket, followed by the next shortest one. 
 
This likely reflects weak end-user demand. Shorter-maturity TIPS take the brunt of reduced 
investor appetite as they are the most volatile, having a higher correlation with commodity 
prices, spot CPI and other economic data. 
The Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee noted in its August presentation that two-year 
TIPS trade cheap on the IOTA curve, ie they are cheaper to nominal Treasuries than the 
inflation-swap market would infer. 
 

 
 
This lack of demand and resulting cheapness is consistent with the TIPS liquidity premium rising 
and consequently the implied break-even rate falling. 
We can tie the reduced demand for TIPS directly to the oil market. Go back to the first chart 
above. There is an uncannily close relationship between changes in breakevens and oil — 
closer than one might expect given that inflation is impacted by many things in addition to crude 
prices. 
This is explained by the interchangeability of oil and TIPS as inflation hedges. Inflation traders 
can use oil as a more liquid, although imperfect, near-term inflation hedge. 
But the most recent divergence in the chart — with breakevens remaining largely static even as 
the oil price has jumped — probably reflects that crude in the current supply-driven shock is no 
longer seen as a clean inflation proxy, and has become more of a macro-economic and 
geopolitical hedge. 
That’s also reflected in oil becoming more negatively correlated to risk than it has been in more 
than 30 years. 
 

 
 
Put simply, oil rising is now risk-off, and that same backdrop is having a material impact on 
demand for TIPS, causing their liquidity premium to rise and breakevens to fall. 
The risk element to TIPS can be seen by looking at the close relationship between their liquidity 
premium and other measures of risk such as the VIX and credit spreads, with the latter shown in 
the chart below. 
 
 

 
But lately the liquidity premium has diverged from credit spreads, as well as from the VIX. What 
gives? 
Credit and stock vols are benefiting directly from the AI boom. The dominance of just a handful 
of firms in equity indices is keeping index correlation low, which in turn ensures the full impact of 
higher volatility from single stocks is not feeding through to vol at the index level, ie the VIX. 
And as volatility is typically an input into credit pricing models, as per the Merton model, credit 
spreads have remained remarkably tight, especially given the surge in AI-related issuance. 
But TIPS have no AI offset. They feel the risk of the geopolitical oil shock, but unlike the credit or 
stock market, there is no opposing force compressing it. As discussed, this has the effect of 
squeezing breakevens lower, giving too dovish an interpretation on future inflation. 
The situation was similar in 2013 when there was also a divergence between the liquidity 
premium and credit spreads, as per the chart above. The taper tantrum was detrimental to TIPS 
liquidity via rising real-yield volatility, but credit spreads remained flat as they benefited from the 
portfolio rebalancing effect of QE, more so than TIPS. 
Breakevens are to this day used as the go-to for many market participants as to what long-run 
inflation will be, and whether it is likely to remain contained and will eventually return to target. 
But that heuristic is flawed. Investors would be wise to assume that the increase in 10-year 
yields - over 110 bps since the Iran war - has more than a paltry 10 bps rise in inflation 
expectations behind it.
