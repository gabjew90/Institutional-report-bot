The race to pace AI
Frontier labs – including OpenAI and Anthropic – are 
the architects of the world’s most advanced artificial 
intelligence (AI) models, but after several high-risk 
incidents this year, the industry’s leaders are taking 
stock of whether AI is outpacing its makers.
The term AI pacing refers to the speed and direction 
of the technology’s development. It has recently come 
into focus following a run of incidents at key frontier 
artificial intelligence labs moving safety from a 
background concern to an immediate priority. In several 
cases the trigger was the same: models operating 
inside “restricted” cybersecurity testing environments 
concluded that the most effective route to completing an 
assigned task was to break out of the environment and 
pursue the objective by any means available, including 
compromising live systems. In each case this happened 
autonomously, with no human in the loop.
Key takeaways
•	 Major agentic AI models have revealed 
breaches and malicious activity 
•	 Industry players have acknowledged the 
challenges of the goal-oriented capability of 
their models
•	 Global coordination to mitigate potential risks 
will be critical to limiting further incidents 
•	 Active investor engagement can contribute to 
the pacing of AI 
•	 AI pacing is unlikely to prove a significant 
headwind to AI’s trajectory

Four particular incidents stand out this year:
Mythos moment – April 2026: Arguably the first clear 
signal of these safety breaches was when Anthropic’s 
Mythos model demonstrated it could autonomously 
discover software vulnerabilities and execute 
sophisticated, multi-step cyberattacks with minimal 
human guidance. At the time this was read as an early 
wake-up call and a spur to improve cybersecurity tools.
OpenAI compromise of Hugging Face – July 2026: This 
went further – internal agents escaping their intended 
testing constraints gained internet access by exploiting 
vulnerabilities and compromised parts of Hugging Face’s 
systems. They also breached OpenAI’s own internal 
grading systems to maximise their score on the task and, 
more strikingly, communicated and collaborated with one 
another to cover up any malicious activity. The Hugging 
Face hack developed from one agent working on a 
workflow in a separate sandboxed environment1 to over 
700 agents communicating together and sharing general 
purpose ways to ‘cheat’ their respective tasks. 
Prompted by that breach, Anthropic ran a sanity check 
on its own evaluation suite and found comparable 
behaviour with its Claude models compromising real-
world systems. 
The OpenAI intrusion into an Australian health service 
– August 2026: This saw agents access Australia’s 
Medicare Statistics Reporting Service portal, reaching 
both public and non-public files. The intrusion occurred 
in June but was only identified in August, during 
OpenAI’s internal review following the Hugging Face 
incident. Since then, reports by Transluce, an AI research 
non-profit, have identified this intrusion as similar to 
other incidents. The report found that some OpenAI 
agents conducting mundane data-retrieval tasks would 
hack databases if they came across security barriers, 
treating them as ‘obstacles’ meant to be evaded rather 
than strict boundaries.  
Google Gemini incident – September 2026: This was a 
restricted test to find information on a fictional company. 
The agents located internet access and targeted a 
real company sharing the same name as the fictional 
company. Notably, the model reportedly stopped once it 
realised it had reached real-world systems.
Taken together, these episodes point to an uncomfortable 
conclusion: goal-oriented agents are difficult to predict 
and to contain. Each system pursued whatever course 
of action was most likely to achieve its objective, and in 
doing so reached for unintended methods. The tangible 
impact in every case was minor, but collectively the 
incidents have been read across the industry as a stark 
warning about the potential of agentic AI.
Shift in tone from AI leaders
In recent weeks, senior figures from frontier labs have 
publicly backed tighter control over model development. 
Dario Amodei, Anthropic co-founder, called on the industry 
to “slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of 
AI models.”2 His concern is that recursive self-improvement 
may be advancing faster than researchers’ ability to 
understand and control the resulting systems – and that, 
on the current path, autonomous incidents of the kind 
above could precede more serious events of bioterrorism 
and economic disruption within 6–12 months.3 His 
proposed remedies include independent evaluators, 
shared safety standards among democratic countries, and 
further international coordination.
Sam Altman (OpenAI), Demis Hassabis (DeepMind) and 
Elon Musk (x.AI) echoed these comments, agreeing on 
the case for common safety standards across the frontier 
labs against a backdrop of several safety researchers 
departing those same labs over precisely these concerns. 
Not everyone agrees. Mark Zuckerberg (Meta) and 
Jensen Huang (Nvidia) have argued for safety measures 
only where clearly needed, rather than as a blanket 
requirement for all models. They point variously to the 
merits of free-market discipline, the risk that regulation 
entrenches progress among incumbents, and a sense that 
the risks are being overstated.

When challenges become risks
The recent incidents have sharpened concerns around 
recursive self-improvement (RSI) – the process by which AI 
increasingly contributes to AI research and development 
in place of human researchers, producing compounding 
progress. RSI is widely viewed as the primary driver of the 
industry-wide acceleration in AI development, and the fear 
is straightforward: progress is beginning to outrun human 
understanding of what these systems are doing, eroding our 
ability to control them.
The scale of the risk is illustrated by Anthropic’s own 
workflow. By May 2026, Claude was writing more than 
80% of the code for its models, up from low single digits in 
February 2025. However, many at Anthropic believed Claude-
written code was initially of lower quality than human-written 
code and only reached rough parity recently. This suggests an 
initial quantity-over-quality dynamic that has since corrected 
but could be a point of scrutiny elsewhere across the industry.
We are not yet at full RSI: a human still decides which goals 
to pursue. But the direction of travel – fully agentic AI – is 
towards minimising the human contribution, which in turn 
demands far greater reliability on how agents act. Amodei’s 
message highlights two areas where that reliability is still 
lacking. The first is alignment, where considerable work 
remains to ensure models are trained to be safe, ethical and 
compliant with guidelines. The second is interpretability: in his 
words, “we still only understand a tiny fraction of what goes 
on inside these models.”3 Interpretability is improving as a 
field, but the pace of capability development far outstrips it. 
This helps explain why, when a model is handed a goal, 
its route to that goal is hard to anticipate – the central 
concern raised by the incidents above. The two problems 
are also mutually reinforcing: weaker interpretability makes 
misalignment harder to detect, while stronger interpretability 
would make it possible to locate misaligned behaviours and 
remove or modify them. Amodei has argued that getting a 
handle on interpretability is central to safe and controlled 
progress – making it an increasingly investable field in its own 
right, particularly if regulation tightens.
Pacing, not stopping
Crucially, “pacing” has been framed explicitly as controlled 
progress rather than a complete stop. The industry has 
reached a point of very advanced – and still advancing – 
capability, largely on the back of RSI and following recent 
incidents, safety should be reconsidered. As a result, OpenAI 
decided to pause the release of its newest AI model, GPT-
6.1, after failing to meet internal safety standards. The Head 
of Safety Systems at OpenAI, stated a refocus on the ‘trade 
off’ between alignment and safety after the model showed 
higher levels of deception and autonomy and occasional 
pursuit of tasks without user permission.
For all the focus on pacing the frontier, we do not read this 
as a headwind for AI infrastructure or capex which underpin 
current technology valuations and, increasingly, the wider 
US economy. Increased AI adoption and in particular new AI 
use cases can result in growing demand for AI Infrastructure. 
Just recently Meta launched its first consumer personal AI 
Agent, Muse. Notably Muse is not being run using a frontier 
model, instead it uses Meta’s internal Muse Spark models, 
which are not currently at the frontier level. Initial adoption 
has been viral with millions of users downloading the Muse 
application within the first week post-launch, making it the 
most downloaded free app in the US and Canadian Apple 
app stores. Further consumer AI agent products are expected 
to be launched later in 2026, potentially creating a new large 
AI use case. This kind of demand is supportive of the capex 
trajectory, driven by rising demand for compute.

Our take on proposed steps
Through our stewardship team, Allianz Global Investors 
has been engaging on AI governance since 2025 to better 
understand how companies are balancing innovation 
with the safeguards needed to mitigate emerging 
risks. We have previously written about the theme of 
responsible AI, discussing the importance of alignment 
of human values with AI development, and we reiterate 
this now, even more so in the face of technical advances 
outpacing governance and policy. Against that backdrop, 
we regard the steps proposed by the frontier labs as a 
directionally welcome development towards responsible 
AI, though some are more credible than others:
•	 Independent third-party evaluation of model 
development – to verify that models, and the processes 
behind them, are properly aligned – is widely supported 
across the industry and, in our view, a necessary first 
step towards safer models.  
•	 Shared safety standards are a reasonable mechanism 
for managing the pace of development. Representatives 
of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta and Nvidia recently 
attended the White House to discuss establishing 
voluntary industry standards through which frontier 
developers would, in effect, regulate themselves without 
government oversight. While the absence of US federal 
support severely limits the prospect of any industry-
wide, federally applied standard today, it does provide 
the foundation for future regulation. It is important 
to note that leading AI companies made similar 
voluntary safety commitments in 2023 under the Biden 
administration. While these helped establish frontier-
safety norms, recent AI incidents have raised questions 
about whether voluntary industry commitments alone 
are sufficient to govern increasingly capable AI systems.
•	 Global coordination among major governments is the 
most ambitious of these three proposals. We think broad 
alignment on regulation is unlikely in the near term but 
key in the long term. Realistic preliminary steps such as 
knowledge-sharing around interpretability could still lift 
the baseline of safety in AI development globally while 
having limited implications for competitive positioning. 
The underlying difficulty is that the technology is 
advancing far faster than any globally coordinated 
response, leaving a patchwork of regional approaches 
instead. The US administration has taken the stance 
of framing AI primarily through geopolitical rivalry 
and national security rather than safety, which has 
pushed regulatory activity away from the federal level 
and down to a growing raft of state initiatives. The EU 
moved early with the AI Act, but parts of its entry into 
force have been delayed and further changes may 
be underway to ensure innovation and productivity 
improvements are not unnecessarily compromised. China 
has seen strong progress in AI governance, evolving 
from a narrow focus on content control to a broader 
framework covering frontier risk and agentic AI. However, 
industry transparency remains a notable weakness with 
inconsistent disclosures on safety reporting. 
While existing safety measures are widely 
agreed to be inadequate, a careful approach 
is needed going forward, in our view. 
Widespread and nondescript regulation could 
create limitations to future use cases while 
unchecked progress could impact trust in the 
technology, also impeding uptake. We do not 
see the proposed ‘pacing’ as a headwind to 
the long-term trajectory of AI or the capex 
underpinning it, and our engagement on 
the topic of responsible AI with our investee 
companies continues to confirm that the risks are 
understood and mitigated where possible.
