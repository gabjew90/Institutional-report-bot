 
Brazil's Diesel Cliff Has An Expiry Date: Goldman 
Says Petrobras Needs A ~50% Hike... Right After 
The Election 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
FRIDAY, OCT 23, 2026 - 02:30 AM 
Governments can cap the price of diesel. They can't cap the price of the tanker that carries it. 
That, in a nutshell, is the problem Brazil is about to run into according to Goldman's Brazil oil & 
gas team led by Bruno Amorim (with LatAm chief economist Alberto Ramos), whose note out 
late Wednesday is titled "Petrobras (PBR): Imminent diesel price hike ahead? Implications for 
PBR, fuels distributors, Brazil inflation" (available to pro subs). The question mark in the title is 
doing a lot of work, because the answer in the body is basically yes: 
We estimate Petrobras' realized diesel price (including subsidies from the government) stands 
21% below international diesel parity, but under a subsidy asymmetry currently favoring 
imported diesel over domestically refined volumes. If this asymmetry were to end after the 
elections (BRL 1.40/liter additional subsidies to diesel importers should be valid until 
mid-November), we estimate PBR would have to raise diesel prices to distributors by ~50% 
(~BRL 1.5/liter) to match international parity, with upside risk if management also seeks to 
compensate for the time PBR's diesel spent below parity so far this year. 
In other words, Brazilians have been buying diesel at a discount the rest of the world stopped 
offering months ago, and the discount has an expiry date: mid-November, a few weeks after 
Sunday's presidential runoff. 
 

 
 
Regular readers know where this is going. We have spent the better part of this year on the 
diesel squeeze, from Goldman saying "Diesel Is At Epicenter Of Supply Squeeze" back in July 
to US distillate stocks hitting seasonal record lows just yesterday. Brazil, it turns out, has been 
hiding the same squeeze inside its state-run oil company's price list. 
"21% Below International Diesel Parity" 
The key exhibit, which Goldman's LatAm sales desk recirculated Thursday morning in its daily 
election countdown (available to pro subs), breaks the gap down. Petrobras charges fuel 
distributors BRL 3.31/liter; Brasília adds a BRL 2.12/litersubsidy to local producers, for a 
realized price of BRL 5.43. The international benchmark is BRL 6.89. Hence the 21%. 
 

 
 
Note the third bar. Importers currently get a BRL 3.52/liter subsidy, which by our math is the 
same BRL 2.12 everybody gets plus the extra BRL 1.40 importers get until mid-November. That 
drags the "adjusted" import price down to BRL 3.57, which is roughly what Petrobras charges. 
As the Goldman desk put it, while PBR's realized price is still below parity, "prices charged to 
fuel distributors are now mostly in line w/ the subsidized diesel import price." 
Translation: the whole system is priced off a subsidy. Pull the importer subsidy, and imported 
diesel suddenly costs a lot more than Petrobras diesel, which leaves Petrobras with two 
choices: ration its cheap barrels or raise prices. And the gap to parity (BRL 6.89 minus BRL 
5.43) is BRL 1.46/liter, which is where Goldman's "~BRL 1.5" comes from. 
The Freight Bill Nobody Can Subsidize Away 
Why is "international parity" so far above what Brazilians pay? Because the oil war has made 
the boring part of the oil trade the expensive part. As we discussed yesterday in "Seven Times 
Higher": Goldman Dissects The $20 Tanker Premium, Goldman's commodity team put it bluntly: 
The global average crude freight rate surged above $20/bbl from $3/bbl a year ago. The rally in 
crude freight rates initially began in the very large crude carrier (VLCC) segment and on Middle 
Eastern routes, but it has spread to all major crude trading routes, particularly affecting 
long-haul voyages. 
 

 
 
Read that again if you happen to be a country that imports a big chunk of its diesel from across 
an ocean. Which Brazil does. 
The bill is already showing up in other people's CPI prints. Per Goldman's overnight Asia macro 
recap, UK motor fuels "soared by 30% in the year to September," pushing headline CPI to an 
11-month high of 3.5% and leaving the BoE on track for its first hike in more than three years. 
And the refining side isn't getting easier: last week the IEA warned that the diesel squeeze will 
persist into 2027, writing that "the gasoil market balance will remain tight well into 2027 and is 
dependent on a recovery of product supply from the Middle East." That echoes Goldman's own 
September warning that a "nightmare" refining crisis could prolong diesel and gasoline pain 
through 2027, and explains why diesel prices are so much higher than gasoline. Meanwhile the 
diesel crack, which is what refiners actually earn on a barrel, went parabolic weeks ago:

Two Subsidies And A Calendar 
So how has Brazil dodged it so far? The same way South Korea and Taiwan did back in March, 
and Italy's Eni did last month: by deciding the pump price is a political number. 
Specifically, two subsidies are doing the heavy lifting: 
 

 
●​ The BRL 2.12/liter diesel subsidy, in place since late May. Bloomberg reported on Sep 
28 that the Finance Ministry extended it for "at least 30 more days" ahead of the first 
round, with 5 billion reais set aside to cover the fuel tax relief. Napkin math (ours): 30 
days from the late-September expiry lands right around Sunday's runoff. 
●​ The extra BRL 1.40/liter for diesel importers, which Goldman says should be valid until 
mid-November. 
Election year, state-run oil company, underpriced fuel, subsidies timed to expire just after the 
votes are counted: frankly, we are shocked. Even Petrobras CEO Magda Chambriard told 
reporters last month (per the same Bloomberg piece) that the company can't react to every daily 
oil price swing under its pricing policy because it would lose market share. A remarkably polite 
way of saying "not before the election." 
And somebody is paying for all this. Ramos' own runoff preview notes that Brazil's budget 
deficits have exceeded 8% of GDP over the past four years while public debt has climbed above 
82% of GDP: 
 
Not exactly a sovereign with room to keep buying everyone's diesel indefinitely. 
Here's Where It Gets Good: "~50%" 
If the importer subsidy goes, Petrobras has to close the gap itself. Goldman's estimate is a 
~50% hike to distributors (~BRL 1.5/liter), and the bank flags upside risk "if management also 
 

 
seeks to compensate for the time PBR's diesel spent below parity so far this year." In plain 
English, Petrobras could try to claw back what it already lost on top of closing the gap. So the 
50% is the floor. 
At the pump, Goldman says that works out to roughly a 20% increase, all else constant. Then 
the inflation hit: 
Total impact on IPCA of a 20% diesel price increase at the pump is estimated at 21-24bp. 
Twenty-odd basis points on headline inflation from one fuel may not sound like much. But diesel 
moves Brazil's trucks and its harvest, so the first-round number is the easy part to measure. 
(Anyone who remembers the 2018 truckers' strike that paralyzed the country over diesel prices 
knows the second round isn't purely an economic question.) 
Local investors are clearly doing the same math. Per Goldman's LatAm desk this morning: 
A recurring theme in convos w/ local clients is that PBR could hike prices right after the 
elections.

Winners, Losers And White Flags 
For investors, Goldman's read-across is straightforward: 
While a domestic diesel price hike would be a positive for Petrobras, it could be negative for 
large fuel distributors which source a higher share of their diesel from local refineries vis a vis 
white flag distributors. 
Put differently, when the cheap refinery diesel gets repriced, the distributors that leaned on it 
most lose their edge over the unbranded "white flag" crowd that has been buying (subsidized) 
imports. The desk names Vibra (VBBR3) and Ultrapar (UGPA3) as the read-through risk, and 
frames the hike as "an upside risk to 4Q refining margins" for Petrobras (PBR). Separately, 
Goldman downgraded Ultrapar to Neutral this morning. Petrobras' own ADR, for what it's worth, 
closed Tuesday at $25.70, already above Goldman's $24.30 price target. 
The Bill Comes Due In November 
Goldman, diplomatically, sees a diesel hike as imminent once the importer subsidy runs out, 
Petrobras as the winner, and the inflation hit as a manageable 21-24bp. 
Our take is less diplomatic. Brazil has spent an oil war pretending diesel costs 21% less than it 
does, and the pretense is scheduled to end just after the votes are counted. Freight on crude is 
up more than sixfold, UK motor fuels are up 30%, the IEA says the gasoil squeeze lasts well 
into 2027, and US distillate stocks are at seasonal record lows. The question is only who pays, 
and here we'll pick a side: whoever wins Sunday will blink first and extend some version of the 
subsidy, rather than hand truckers a 20% pump shock in the first month of a new term. Which 
means the bill doesn't disappear. It just moves from the pump to a budget already running 
deficits above 8% of GDP, and from there to the real and the DI curve, the same place every 
"temporary" fuel subsidy in history eventually ends up. 
Then again, Petrobras has managed to sell diesel 21% below the world price through the worst 
freight shock in years, so perhaps it can defy gravity for one more month.
