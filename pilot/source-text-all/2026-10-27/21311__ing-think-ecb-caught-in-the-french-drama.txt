ECB caught in the French drama
The European Central Bank meeting on 29 October is not expected to
result in an interest rate hike. Instead, the French crisis is increasingly
eroding the need for additional rate hikes
With the surge in bond yields on the back of the crisis in France, we expect the ECB to remain on hold at
next week's meeting
Back in September, the ECB surprised markets with a more hawkish message, at least between
the lines. With headline inflation higher for longer and core inflation expected to increase in
2027, the stage seemed set for at least one more rate hike at the December meeting. Some
even speculated about a back-to-back increase in October.
One month later, and after a severe fiscal and political crisis in France, the ECB is unlikely to
repeat the hawkish tone at next week’s meeting. At face value, macro developments since the
last meeting would still justify another rate hike. Headline inflation increased again in
September and most leading indicators point to continued resilience in the eurozone
economy. However, the surge in bond yields on the back of the French crisis is already
imposing a new restrictive monetary policy stance. In fact, the surge in German bond yields
since the September meeting has already had roughly the same impact on growth and
inflation as an additional 25bp rate hike. For France, it's closer to a 50bp hike. Or in other
words, the bond market has taken over the ECB’s job.
All of this means that the ECB is very likely to stay on hold next week and will delay any next

rate hike decision to December. This is when new staff projections will be available and when
there will also be more insights into whether higher energy prices will eventually bring knock-
on effects on the rest of the economy or not. In fact, there are three main parameters that, in
our view, will determine the ECB’s path going forward: i) the length and intensity of the war in
the Middle East. The longer oil prices remain elevated, the higher the probability of more rate
hikes; ii) the knock-on effects of higher energy prices. So far, there has hardly been any pass-
through of higher energy prices in the rest of the economy. If this remains the case, the need
for further rate hikes drops. If it doesn’t, the likelihood of new rate hikes goes up. iii) France.
Any solution to the current French fiscal woes could bring some relief to bond markets and
allow the ECB to continue hiking rates. Without any solution, bond markets have taken over
the ECB’s role, making rate hikes less likely (and less needed).
Because it's France
Even if France does not play a role in the ECB’s policy statement next week, it will be an
important element at the press conference. Political and fiscal concerns in France, and the
resulting widening in French spreads, have raised questions about whether the ECB could
intervene through the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI), which allows it to purchase
securities issued by member states whose financing conditions have deteriorated for reasons
not justified by country-specific fundamentals. However, before activating it, the Governing
Council considers a cumulative set of criteria to determine whether the countries concerned
are pursuing sound and sustainable fiscal and macroeconomic policies. In practice, this
includes compliance with the EU fiscal framework, which would, at a minimum, require the
proposed French budget to be fully adopted.
The ECB has nevertheless left itself some discretion, stating that the intervention criteria “will
be dynamically adjusted to the unfolding risks and conditions to be addressed”. This brings
back memories of the famous “because it’s France” by former European Commission President
Jean-Claude Juncker. Generally speaking, the ECB will be in a difficult position: intervening too
quickly in support of France may be viewed unfavourably by other member states and
interpreted as monetary financing of the debt of a country unwilling to follow the rules. That
argues strongly against early intervention. Also, don’t forget that the leadership changes at
the ECB over the next months (Schnabel, Lane, Lagarde) could also affect how the ECB views
TPI.
Given the challenges of justifying the activation of TPI at present, we expect the ECB to adopt a
three-pronged approach to address the French crisis.
The first line of defence is a more dovish approach to policy rates. As bond markets are
