 AI export boom: who are the spillover 
winners and how should policy respond? 
The AI export boom has become a major growth pillar for Asian economies like Taiwan, 
Korea, and China. The ongoing AI-led export cycle has been an important source of 
growth for China, Korea and Taiwan this year. Year to date, exports have risen 20% yoy 
in China, 57% in Korea and 44% in Taiwan (Exhibit 1).  
The key macro question is why the same external shock is producing different domestic 
effects, and how fiscal and monetary policy will respond. We find that tech sector size, 
capacity constraints and the broader business cycle favor stronger spillovers in Taiwan, 
followed by Korea. China also benefits, though domestic headwinds temper these gains. 
Fiscal revenue gains support spending and rate hikes in Taiwan and Korea, but increase 
their exposure to a reversal in the AI boom. China remains more insulated, with weaker 
exports likely to trigger additional policy easing. 
Exhibit 2: Higher electronic prices support nominal export growth in Korea and Taiwan, but less so 
in China 
Export growth by economy (% yoy ytd) by volume and price 
Source: Haver, BofA Global Research estimates 
BofA GLOBAL RESEARCH 
The capex multiplier favors Taiwan and Korea 
Taiwan and Korea stand out on the conditions that favor stronger capex spillovers: a 
sizeable AI-exposed sector relative to the economy, tight tech production capacity, and a 
positive aggregate output gap. AI-exposed exports are equivalent to around 67% of 
Taiwan’s nominal GDP and 20% of Korea’s, and semiconductor shortages are forcing 
producers to expand domestic capacity. In China, the comparable export exposure is 
roughly 5% of GDP and stronger tech demand is primarily lifting manufacturing 
utilization from declining levels rather than driving capacity expansion. Broader 
investment remains dominated by cyclical and structural headwinds: weak domestic 
demand, local-government funding pressures, and the prolonged property downturn. 
Tech income and wealth determine consumer spillover 
Household spillovers are generally smaller than the capex boost, but their strength also 
varies across economies, reflecting differences in tech employment, equity exposure and 
broader wealth trends. Electronics-related manufacturing accounts for around 11% of 
employment in Taiwan, versus 3% in Korea and 1.8% in China, while bonus rather than 
broad-based wage growth has been the main income channel. Taiwan sees the strongest 
wealth effect due to its larger equity rally and greater household exposure to stocks. In 
China, falling property values, lower savings returns and weak income expectations 
offset direct tech income gains, leaving the consumption recovery more fragile.

Diverging domestic effects drive policy divergence 
The export boom is lifting fiscal revenues in all three economies. That said, tax windfalls 
are funding transfers and investments in TW and KR, while China has not seen a 
comparable spending boost partly due to local-government fiscal constraints. Domestic 
spillovers and positive output gaps add to inflation pressures in KR and TW, supporting 
further rate hikes, while softer demand in China still warrant more policy easing.

Risks: Tech concentration is a double-edged sword  
That said, the same channels amplifying today’s technology upcycle could deepen a 
future downturn. In Taiwan and Korea, a correction in AI demand could reverse capex, 
bonuses, equity wealth effects and fiscal windfalls, leaving both economies more 
exposed to the technology cycle. China’s more diversified economy limits these direct 
reversal risks. If Chinese export growth weakens materially, we would expect 
policymakers to respond more proactively with fiscal and monetary easing.
