CIO View: Bonds
Frederick Mellors, Strategist, UBS Switzerland AG
• We maintain an Attractive recommendation on the bond asset 
class, with corresponding Attractive recommendations on high grade, 
investment grade, emerging market, and high yield sub-asset classes.
• The past month has seen both long-end rates tick up, and credit 
spreads widen, particularly in the more credit-sensitive segments. 
These moves have translated into slightly negative returns across 
various segments. The ongoing conflicts in both the Middle East and 
Russia-Ukraine continue to disrupt energy markets and put upward 
pressure on prices. This is now feeding through to official inflation 
prints and there is evidence that second round effects are apparent in 
goods and services where energy is a key component. Central banks 
pivoted to a more hawkish posture a number of months ago and are 
now delivering policy rate hikes. During the month of September, we 
witnessed the Fed, the ECB and the BoJ all raising rates in the same 
month, a historical first.
• The two new developments for markets in recent weeks have been 
the underperformance of French government bonds and questions 
about whether artificial intelligence (AI) capital expenditures (capex) 
and returns on investment can continue to meet lofty expectations 
given that financing costs for a number of AI players have moved 
sharply higher.
• On the French situation, French government bond yields are now 
back at pre-global-financial-crisis levels. More importantly, as a spread 
to other Eurozone sovereign bond markets, they are at historical 
highs. The catalyst for the repricing is the worsening fiscal backdrop 
for Europe's second-largest economy. Although France has not had 
a budget surplus since 1974, the direction of travel has progressively 
worsened in recent years, with the deficit now above 5%. Politics 
is polarized, making it increasingly difficult for the government to 
implement austerity or cut social spending. Taxes are already high, 
giving little headroom to raise additional revenue. Adding to the 
uncertainty is the presidential election next April, where, based on 
current polls, the next president looks like they may come from the far 
right or far left of the political spectrum. The ECB has developed an 
array of crisis-fighting tools over the years which could be activated 
in the event France's issues become more systemic to the Eurozone 
given France's unquestionable importance to the single currency 
union. The dilemma, though, is that activating of those instruments 
would create moral hazard and remove the market discipline currently 
being imposed on French policymakers to credibly rectify the fiscal 
situation. Added to that is the fact that inflation is elevated, so 
any liquidity operations may add upside risks to inflation. We see 
the current situation as a stress test rather than a crisis and expect 
pressure to remain heading into next year on French bonds.

• In terms of AI, there has been a sharp repricing of the credit risk on a 
number of neocloud players who have actively used debt to finance 
the purchase of chips and data centers in order to lease computer 
power to big tech customers. This has mostly impacted the high-yield, 
loan, and private markets where most debt financing was placed. 
The demand for AI compute continues to be robust. Therefore, at 
this stage it is a cost of capital and return on investment debate. 
For the large technology companies, which over the last year have 
sought to finance more of their capex with debt rather than operating 
cash flow, spreads have cheapened and now trade with implied multi-
notch downgrades. As a number of their projects are being funded 
using off-balance-sheet structures and given those projects are not 
yet online, the companies are not required to account for the future 
operating-lease commitments. Credit spreads are to a certain extent 
already pricing in this future obligation today. However, given the lack 
of transparency and the fact that a number of these projects will take 
years to complete, the market may be ascribing excessive premia.
• On our views, our base case is that the Fed hikes two more times, 
once in December and a follow-up hike in March. This remains 
below market expectations, which have been fluctuating between 
another three and four hikes. We have similar expectations in other 
markets such as the Eurozone and the UK. This view, if correct, would 
generate some capital gains for the overall fixed income asset class; 
however, we continue to see the bulk of total returns being generated 
from the high level of outright yields. It is important to note that 
the high level of yields provides a sizable cushion against further 
unexpected increases in rates. In order to generate negative total 
returns over the next year, we need to envisage extreme scenarios 
where growth accelerates meaningfully and inflation continues to 
run hot. We do not believe the monetary policy transmission is 
broken, as evidenced by softness in the more cyclical, rate-sensitive 
parts of the global economy. Financial conditions have tightened 
in recent weeks. Banks reported that they have started tightening 
lending standards. High levels of government debt and deficits have 
become political issues limiting the ability for further meaningful 
fiscal stimulus. Ongoing strong levels of AI investment are now being 
challenged by higher costs of capital. Lastly, high energy prices are 
eating into margins and savings. These factors all make the current 
risk-return for fixed income appealing.
• On the credit side, we recommend that exposure to higher-risk credit 
remain relatively short-dated, whereas stronger investment-grade 
issuers offer attractive carry across medium tenors. Overall portfolio 
credit risk and duration should ultimately reflect investors’ objectives 
and investment time horizons.
• Absolute view: Attractive, Relative preference (in comparison to other 
asset classes): Neutral.
• October has provided a timely reminder of the role that fixed income 
can play within diversified portfolios. While the asset class has generally 
been challenged by higher energy prices and shifting central bank 
expectations since February, high-quality bonds have performed well 
during recent periods of market concern around the financing of 
massive AI-related capital expenditure programs and whether this will 
continue to be the dominant driver of economic growth. The re-

emergence of negative correlations between high-quality bonds and 
risk assets is a reminder of their stabilizing impact on overall portfolio 
resilience. This is in line with our broader thesis that current yield levels 
offer an attractive combination of income, diversification, and medium-
term return potential.
• The income appeal of the asset class remains the most compelling 
attribute supporting healthy forward-looking returns, with benchmark 
yields in many major markets near 20-year highs, underpinning our 
Attractive asset class preference. The income cushion this provides 
investors should not be underappreciated. For instance, it would now 
take a 113bps move higher in 5-year US Treasury yields from current 
levels for capital losses to offset the annual income earned. We view 
such a move as unlikely: A spike in yields of that magnitude from here 
would likely prompt a swift response from policymakers, as it would 
have a negative impact on the real economy and could expose financial 
stability risks. This leaves us with a bias toward near-term yield stability 
and a gradual move to lower bond yields over time. Monetary policy 
expectations still appear somewhat more hawkish than our central 
case across major markets as growth moderates and inflation concerns 
subside. While we continue to favor short- to medium-term maturities 
from a strategic perspective, the recent rise in term premiums means 
more tactically minded investors or those looking for greater portfolio 
diversification should look for opportunities to extend duration to the 
10-year area in high-quality issuers.
• In the US, 10-year Treasury yields hit a new cycle high of 5.36% in early 
October before retracing sharply lower amid a risk-off move in equities 
and credit centered on the financing of AI-related capital expenditures. 
For investors in bonds, this is encouraging on two levels. First, it seems 
that yield levels and rate volatility likely reached a point where there were 
broader implications for financial conditions. This point of reflexivity is 
something the market had been looking for as a signal that the sell-
off in bonds had gone too far; we continue to expect 10-year Treasury 
yields to find an equilibrium level in a 4.5-5% range. Second, recent 
market moves illustrate that bonds can still provide meaningful portfolio 
protection when growth expectations come under pressure. Countering 
some of this bond optimism are near-term uncertainties about the path 
of inflation as well as November's midterm elections. On the former, 
while core CPI inflation surprised to the upside in September at 0.3% m/
m, this seemed mostly driven by statistical volatility in shelter inflation, 
which is likely to revert. Nevertheless, pass-through from higher energy 
prices remains a valid concern and could further delay the return to the 
Federal Reserve's 2% inflation target. Our view is that this is already 
well reflected in market pricing for an additional 75bps of tightening, 
which would take the Fed beyond our base case of "recalibration hikes." 
Our core view remains that the 2- to 5-year segment of the Treasury 
curve offers the best risk-reward. Yet, the recent rise in term premiums 
has created a more compelling case for selectively extending duration 
toward the 10-year maturity area, particularly for investors seeking 
greater portfolio diversification.
• In Europe, high-quality bonds outperformed peers due to flight-to-
quality demand. In particular, German Bunds performed strongly as 
investors sought relative safety during periods of market volatility 
and amid widening spreads on French OATs. There are also potential 
European Central Bank (ECB) policy implications. While energy prices 
and inflation developments remain important considerations for 
policymakers, it is difficult to imagine the central bank becoming more

hawkish at a time when intra-Eurozone sovereign spreads are under 
pressure. In fact, in the near term, policymakers are more likely to 
need to address questions about whether there will be a shift in the 
ECB's balance sheet policy to help alleviate some of the recent spread 
moves and improve monetary policy transmission across the bloc. All 
told, we continue to think investors are well compensated for taking 
duration risk, with market expectations of an additional 75bps of 
tightening unlikely to be realized. Recent divergence in performance 
within European sovereign markets does, however, reinforce the benefit 
of extending duration selectively in the highest-quality issuers.
• Yields remain low in Switzerland. However, the case for holding bonds in 
CHF lies largely in their defensive properties rather than valuations, given 
the lack of yield on offer relative to peers. Markets have become more 
focused on the prospect of eventual tightening from the Swiss National 
Bank (SNB), yet policymakers continue to signal that any normalization 
is likely to be gradual. Supporting this assertion, inflation remains low 
at 1% y/y relative to most developed markets, with the pickup driven 
by energy prices rather than evidence of domestic price pressures. 
The 50bps of rate hikes priced in over the next year does not seem 
unreasonable given this backdrop.
• In the UK, the bond market has had to contend with competing 
domestic forces. On the one hand, stronger-than-expected economic 
data has reinforced the idea that the Bank of England (BoE) will need 
to follow its peers and begin tightening policy as soon as November. 
While the cyclical data warrant scrutiny, investors are already well 
compensated for a meaningful policy tightening cycle, with nearly 
125bps of hikes priced into the front end of the rates curve. At the 
same time, a confluence of factors is aligning to put longer-dated UK 
government bonds on a firmer footing. Most notably, we have been 
highlighting that the UK's fiscal trajectory compares favorably with 
many developed-market peers. The IMF Managing Director recently 
singled out the UK for its fiscal restraint, while the 28 October Budget 
seems likely to be aimed at policy continuity on this front, which 
should be supportive for gilts. In addition, the Bank of England's recent 
adjustments to its quantitative tightening program should help reduce 
upward pressure on term premiums over time. Taken together, we 
also see scope for tactical extension toward the 10-year segment in 
high-quality GBP bonds given current valuations, even if our strategic 
preference remains focused on short- to medium-term maturities.
• In credit markets, higher funding costs and capital expenditure concerns 
weighed on AI-related credit, contributing to wider spreads across 
the broader credit universe. Investment grade credit has proven more 
resilient, with index spreads widening by only 10bps over the past 
month. On the demand side, investor appetite for high-quality corporate 
bonds has remained generally robust this year amid appealing yields, 
resilient growth, and solid earnings, as evidenced by continued fund 
inflows. Strong demand has helped absorb elevated bond supply. A 
large portion of the increased supply came from US hyperscalers funding 
AI-related capital expenditures, a trend that is likely to continue in the 
year ahead given ongoing elevated capital spending needs. Our view has 
been that a sustained elevated pace of bond supply from hyperscalers 
could lead to some supply indigestion and moderately wider spreads, 
which we have seen since the start of the year. Hyperscaler bonds 
currently represent about 4% of the US investment grade Intermediate 
index. However, from a fundamental perspective, we note that most 
hyperscalers are rated AA or higher and have considerable debt capacity

• In high yield, the market started to feel the squeeze from higher rates, 
with spreads widening by around 50bps and now close to 330bps in 
both the US and EUR markets. As a result, the US high yield index 
returned -2.49%, while the EUR high yield index returned -1.79%, 
marking the weakest monthly performance since September 2022. 
Lower-rated credits came under the greatest pressure, with CCC spreads 
widening sharply to more than 1,000bps as higher financing costs 
weighed on issuers with limited debt-servicing capacity. However, the 
weakness in the high yield market has not been confined to CCCs. 
BB and B spreads widened by 43bps and 55bps, respectively, over 
the past month, indicating broader market pressure. Nevertheless, 
aggregate credit fundamentals remain broadly intact, which should help 
limit spread widening at the index level, especially among higher-rated 
issuers. While uncertainty around AI-related investment and financing 
needs may continue to weigh on select segments of the credit market, 
AI-linked data center issuers now represent only around 3% of the US 
high yield market. Looking ahead, we see the asset class's high current 
yield and low duration as its key value proposition and a solid anchor 
for total returns, with possible upside if government yields decline.
• Emerging market bonds also came under pressure over the last 
month. The main headwind was the shift higher in benchmark yields, 
although some spread widening also took place. Going forward, 
we expect EM credit spreads to remain range-bound in the coming 
months, and our constructive view on EM credit reflects stronger 
fundamentals compared with previous cycles. For instance, foreign 
exchange reserves are higher, providing better shock-absorption 
capacity; current account positions have improved; fiscal deficits are 
manageable; and a frontier-market distress cycle is coming to an end. 
Overall, this provides support for the asset class, and we maintain an 
Attractive preference on EM debt and continue to view EM credit as a 
compelling source of risk-adjusted returns within global fixed income 
portfolios.
• In summary, we do not expect tight credit spreads to be a key source 
of returns for the overall asset class, but a stable macroeconomic 
backdrop, solid corporate fundamentals, upward-sloping yield curves, 
and ongoing demand for yield should continue to provide support. 
We recommend that exposure to higher-risk credit remain relatively 
short-dated, whereas stronger investment grade issuers offer attractive 
carry across medium tenors. Both overall portfolio credit risk and 
duration should ultimately reflect investors' objectives and investment 
time horizons.
High grade bonds
We have an Attractive view on high grade bonds. Bond performance has 
been modest of late as concerns about elevated inflation and bond supply 
have pushed up longer-dated yields. In the near term, we see potential for 
upside as monetary policy rate paths look overly hawkish. Longer term, 
the high frequency of supply shocks remains a challenge for bonds. Yet, 
we continue to find the elevated yields in high-quality bonds appealing 
on both an outright basis and relative to cash, in a world of heightened 
geopolitical risks and lofty risky asset class valuations.

We hold an Attractive view on investment grade bonds. We find the 
outright level of yields appealing (5.7% in USD and 4.2% in EUR) and 
believe investors with excess cash holdings should look to add short- to 
medium-duration investment grade bonds for appealing income prospects. 
Investor demand for high-quality credit should remain strong given 
elevated yield levels and solid investment grade corporate fundamentals.
High yield bonds
We maintain an Attractive view on high yield bonds. US and EUR high yield 
yields have increased to close to 8.3% and 6.7%, respectively, driven by a 
repricing higher of central bank policy rate expectations following the start 
of the US-Iran war. Spreads have also pushed wider over the last month as 
downside growth risks due to higher rates and energy prices have started 
to bite. Balance sheets remain healthy in public credit markets and default 
risks remain low. Given our view that the market is likely overestimating 
the path for policy rates, we see price upside going forward.
Emerging market bonds
Emerging market US dollar debt performance has been stable over the last 
year despite ongoing geopolitical and market volatility, reinforcing its role 
as a key diversifier from developed markets. We believe elevated yields and 
supportive fundamentals underpin a positive outlook for emerging market 
debt, which we view as Attractive, and expect it to outperform cash.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the 
collective judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total 
returns on liquid and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
