Thought of the day
Brent crude oil hit USD 114/bbl for the first time since May and is on track 
for a third consecutive week of price gains as fresh attacks in the Middle East 
have heightened concerns about further disruptions to energy supplies. Since 
the start of this month, Brent crude has risen over 11%.
Saudi Arabia reported attacks on an Aramco oil field and an airport in its 
Eastern Province, while the US ordered diplomats to leave the country amid 
Houthi strikes. Separately, the New York Times reported that the US and Israeli 
militaries had developed specific plans for renewed attacks on Iran after the 
US midterm elections, which take place on 3 November.
With the Russia-Ukraine conflict also continuing, geopolitical risks are unlikely 
to subside in the near term, keeping inflation concerns elevated and adding 
to macroeconomic uncertainty. The S&P 500 fell 1.2% on Thursday.
But geopolitics are not the only factor driving market performance. Resilient 
economic growth, robust corporate earnings, and strong AI demand should 
continue to underpin equity gains. Our view remains that investors should 
focus less on the possibility of short-term drawdowns associated with 
geopolitical events and more on ensuring that their portfolios can withstand 
periods of volatility.
History suggests that geopolitical shocks are rarely a sufficient reason 
to stop investing. Since 1941, the S&P 500 has been higher 12 months 
after the start of a geopolitical crisis roughly three-quarters of the time. 
For diversified portfolios, the long-term cost of remaining uninvested has 
often proved greater than the temporary cost of living through a geopolitical 
drawdown.

The global energy system should continue to adapt to the disruption.
While still below its pre-war levels, OPEC crude production rose by 0.92 
million barrels per day in September to 25.1 million, or around 85% of 
February’s output, according to the group’s latest Monthly Market Report. 
Trade routes are also shifting, while the G7 decision to release additional 
emergency oil stocks should provide some temporary relief. Together, these 
developments demonstrate the global oil market’s ability to respond to 
disrupted supplies. We acknowledge that the physical oil market remains 
tight and is vulnerable to further supply shocks, but our base case is that a 
gradual recovery of energy flows and production should allow oil prices to 
moderate into next year. This means that the impact of higher energy prices 
on the global economy should remain insufficient to derail growth.
Exposure to commodities can help manage volatility. Instead of 
retreating from markets, investors can use energy exposure to insulate 
portfolios against such supply-driven volatility and boost diversification. 
In fact, we think investors should consider broad commodities as a 
differentiated source of return, especially as rising power demand, 
AI infrastructure investment, electrification, and constrained supply are 
supportive of an upcycle. Historically, commodities have exhibited low 
correlations with traditional asset classes, and we think an actively managed 
approach can help investors navigate across the commodity complex.
So, we retain a constructive market outlook and believe investors should stay 
invested for further equity gains via broadly diversified exposure across sectors 
and regions. We also believe investors can strengthen their portfolio resilience 
by incorporating fixed income, broad commodities, and select alternatives 
according to their risk tolerance, liquidity needs, and financial objectives.
Caught our attention
French bond stress intensifies. The premium on 10-year French 
government bonds over German Bunds briefly widened to 178 basis points 
on Thursday, its highest level in 15 years, as investors weighed the ongoing 
political gridlock, fiscal pressures, and the prospect of further protests in 
France. France’s 10-year bond yield rose to 5.14%, its highest level since 
2002, before easing back to 5.08%, and the 2-year bond yield rose to 3.88% 
before retreating to 3.78%. French equities also weakened, with the CAC 
40 falling 1.4%. The moves came amid a broader retreat from risk linked 
to weaker technology stocks and a rise in Brent crude oil. Markets remain 
concerned that France’s parliament will struggle to establish a credible fiscal 
consolidation path ahead of next year’s presidential election, while public 
opposition limits the government’s room to address the debt burden.
Our view: France’s fiscal and political challenges could keep European 
sovereign markets volatile, and we cannot rule out a further rise in yields. 
But our base case is not for the current pressure to develop into a broader 
European funding crisis. Well-capitalized banks, relatively limited domestic 
sovereign exposure, and Europe’s institutional safeguards reduce the risk 
of stress spreading through the financial system. France’s latest debt sale 
also attracted solid demand, supporting our view that fiscal pressure is 
more likely to build gradually than become an imminent funding problem. 
We expect European borrowing costs to fall into next year as inflation 
pressures moderate and market pricing moves closer to our expectation of 
limited further ECB tightening. We rate fixed income overall as Attractive. 
Current yields provide attractive income and a substantial cushion against 
further increases, while offering scope for capital gains if policy expectations 
moderate.

Perceived safe currencies rally, but not the US dollar. The combination of 
AI-driven weakness in equities, elevated oil prices, and Middle East concerns 
led to sharp moves in perceived safe-haven currencies on Thursday, excluding 
the US dollar. USDJPY fell 0.9% to 156.76, its biggest daily decline in a month 
or so. The JPY also strengthened against the euro, sterling, and the Australian 
dollar after trading tightly for much of October.
Our view: We think the US dollar’s yield advantage can still provide some 
near-term support, particularly against the CHF, but expect USDCHF to decline 
over time as Federal Reserve support fades and the Swiss National Bank 
likely tightens monetary policy. We forecast USDCHF at 0.81 in December 
and 0.79 in March 2027. For the Japanese yen, the Bank of Japan’s 
normalization should limit renewed weakness, but we would not chase this 
rally. We forecast USDJPY at 160 in December and 158 thereafter, with 
sizable volatility around these levels entirely plausible. Higher FX volatility may 
provide opportunities for investors to use structured strategies to harvest 
yield, depending on their willingness and ability to bear the unique risks of 
investing in options.
Chinese equities decline. Chinese stocks extended their declines on Friday 
after entering a technical bear market on Thursday, with the onshore CSI 
300 index now 20.6% below its June level. The weakness was attributed 
to concerns that China's export VAT rebate cuts could extend to other 
industries, renewed sensitivity to internet regulation, speculation around 
potential selling linked to the offshore tax declaration deadline, and reduced 
expectations for near-term stimulus measures following better-than-expected 
3Q GDP growth.
Our view: We view this week's stock market performance as being driven 
by a combination of policy concerns, positioning, and risk reduction 
rather than a broad deterioration in fundamentals. While any further VAT 
rebate changes could weigh on corporate profitability in certain export-
oriented industries, the correction appears largely driven by sentiment 
and positioning. Meanwhile, the fundamental picture in the tech sector 
remains constructive—selected semiconductor equipment, hardware, and 
localization beneficiaries continue to show healthy order and backlog 
visibility, while earnings revisions remain on an upward trend. We retain 
a selective approach to Chinese equities, favoring A-shares over H-shares. 
We believe the recent pullback has improved entry points for A-shares, 
and we continue to favor exposure to areas with the strongest structural 
earnings visibility. These include semiconductor equipment, some hardware 
and advanced manufacturing, innovative pharmaceuticals, and contract 
manufacturers, as well as quality dividend payers such as large Chinese banks.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
