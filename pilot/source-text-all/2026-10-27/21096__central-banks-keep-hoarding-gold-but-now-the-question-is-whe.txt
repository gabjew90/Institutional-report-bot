 
Central Banks Keep Hoarding Gold, But Now The 
Question Is "Where To Store It": Goldman 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
TUESDAY, OCT 20, 2026 - 01:40 AM 
Gold may be stuck in a rate-hike funk, but the world's central banks clearly didn't get the memo. 
According to the latest monthly nowcast from Goldman's Lina Thomas (available to pro subs), 
central banks bought an estimated 79 tonnes of gold in August, nearly five times the pre-2022 
average of 17 tonnes, and up from 44 tonnes in July. And once again, the biggest identifiable 
buyer was China. On a three-month seasonally adjusted basis, purchases are running at 85 
tonnes a month. 
Translation: annualized, that's roughly 1,000 tonnes a year of official-sector buying (napkin 
math, 85 x 12), versus about 200 tonnes a year before Russia's reserves were frozen in 2022. 
Before you ask: no, that's not a typo. 
 
 

 
But the more interesting part of the note isn't how much gold central banks are buying. It's 
where they want to keep it. 
The Buying Binge Rolls On 
Here is the summary from Goldman (emphasis ours): 
Central bank gold demand remains strong. Our GS nowcast estimates central bank purchases 
of 79 tonnes in August, well above the pre-2022 average of 17 tonnes, with China accounting 
for a large share. On a three-month seasonally adjusted basis, purchases are running at 85 
tonnes per month. 
 
We continue to see central bank demand as a structural support for gold. EM central banks, 
particularly China, remain underweight gold relative to plausible long-term allocations, while the 
motivations for diversifying away from G7 reserve currencies, including geopolitical and financial 
risk, likely remain in place. 
To be fair, the smoothed trend has come off the boil a bit: 85 tonnes a month now versus 91 in 
the July nowcast, and roughly 100 in the June update we covered (Sep 3). But "slowing" from 
more than five times the historical pace to merely five times is the kind of slowdown gold bears 
probably shouldn't celebrate. Goldman's nowcast shows it: 
 
 

 
It's also well ahead of what Goldman needs for its forecast. Last month the bank raised its 
central bank demand assumption to 60 tonnes a month on average through 2026-27, and said 
official buying contributes nearly all of its expected 23% gold price gain through end-2027, to a 
$5,400/toz target (from Thomas' "Fed Hikes to Slow, Rather than Derail, the Gold Rally" note, 
Sep 18, also available to pro subs). 
China: Still Buying More Than It Admits 
Regular readers know where this is going. Goldman says China was the largest identifiable 
buyer in August at 25 tonnes, based on its London OTC estimates, while the PBoC's own 
reported purchases were roughly half that (as best we can read off the chart). It's the same 
pattern we flagged last month, when Goldman pegged July Chinese buying at 35 tonnes versus 
the official 19.9 tonnes, and the same story we have been telling since 2024, which the FT later 
confirmed(Nov 17, 2025).

In other words, the official PBoC number remains a politically managed figure, released at a 
pace Beijing finds convenient, while the real buying runs through back channels. Diversification 
at its finest. 
From "Whether To Own Gold" To "Where To Store It" 
The more notable shift is in the developed world. DM central banks have mostly sat out the 
buying wave since 2022, partly because they already hold huge legacy reserves. But according 
to Thomas, the debate is increasingly shifting from whether to own gold to where to store it, 
which points to broader concerns about reserve security and cross-country trust. That links back 
to her earlier "Gold and Central Banks: Storage Dilemma" note (available to pro subs). 
The data back it up. Foreign central bank gold held at the New York Fed fell by 40 tonnes in 
August, while custodial holdings at the Bank of England rose by 46 tonnes. That follows a 
63-tonne jump at the BoE in July, and the Netherlands' disclosure in early September that it had 
moved nearly 90 tonnes out of New York and Ottawa to London in the name of "crisis 
preparedness" (Sep 3). 
The chart below, based on Bank of England, IMF and New York Fed data compiled by Goldman, 
shows the trend: by our rough reading, NY Fed custody holdings are down around 200 tonnes 
since early 2025, while BoE custody holdings are up roughly 500 tonnes. 
 
We noticed this a month ago:

One caveat that cuts in the bulls' favor: last month Goldman noted that its London-based 
nowcast doesn't capture changes in foreign central bank gold held at the BoE. When BoE 
custody rises by more than NY Fed holdings fall, the official buying is probably even bigger than 
the nowcast suggests. In August, the BoE gain (46 tonnes) again exceeded the NY Fed drop 
(40 tonnes), though only narrowly this time. 
The Price Hasn't Gotten The Message (Yet) 
All of this is happening while the gold price is going nowhere fast. Spot was around $4,186/oz 
this morning per Goldman's materials desk. That is below the ~$4,350 level when Goldman cut 
its year-end fair value to $4,650 last month, and getting uncomfortably close to the $4,070 
"near-term floor" the bank laid out in its hawkish-Fed downside scenario. The Fed has hiked, 
Goldman's economists (as of last month) expect another hike in October, and higher rates are 
weighing on ETF demand. 
Not everyone is buying the structural story. BofA's Lawson Winder argued last week that higher 
rates should keep a lid on gold (Oct 12), preferring copper and uranium and downgrading 
Kinross to Underperform. As we noted last month (Sep 15), SocGen has also flagged signs of 
demand destruction at these price levels, with H1 2026 global demand running below its 
10-year average. 
Goldman's answer: the Fed is the near-term drag, but central banks set the floor. As Thomas 
put it last month, continued central bank buying should progressively raise the floor under prices 
even in a sharply hawkish scenario. 
Bottom Line 
Goldman's conclusion is short and to the point: central bank demand remains "a structural 
support for gold," and the reasons behind it (sanctions risk, geopolitics, distrust of G7 reserve 
currencies) aren't going away. 
We agree, and we'd go a step further. The tonnage is the headline, but the custody data is the 
real story. When the Dutch, and apparently others, start moving bars out of Manhattan and into 
London "for crisis preparedness," that says less about gold than about how much trust reserve 
managers place in the custodian. A 79-tonne month is a buying signal. Forty tonnes leaving the 
 

 
New York Fed in a single month is a trust signal. ETF tourists can sell all they want into a hiking 
Fed; the buyers who matter are price-insensitive, they buy the dip, and they increasingly don't 
want their gold sitting in New York. 
We'll check back when Goldman's September nowcast lands next month, and when the PBoC 
tells us how little it says it bought.
