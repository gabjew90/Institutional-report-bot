In an election cycle, the investment question is not who wins, but what a win may change. Over the next 
15 months, voters across the US, Europe, and several major emerging markets head to the polls amid 
elevated public debt, entrenched geopolitical competition, and an accelerating AI capex cycle. Our 
Public Investing teams examine how we believe these outcomes might reach asset prices, seeking to 
separate the policies an election can genuinely move from those already set in motion by structural 
forces beyond the ballot.

Why the 2026–27 Election Cycle Matters 
National elections are a routine feature of the investment backdrop; some cycles demand more attention than others. In 2024, the largest 
election year on record, more than 60 countries representing roughly half the world's population voted.1 We believe the next 15 months 
bring another concentration of elections. Market relevance, in our view, is uneven: 
• 
US midterms fall on November 3, 2026. Market attention is on fiscal policy, as trade, foreign policy and immigration are now primarily 
executive-driven. In our view, a divided government implies limited fiscal change. In contrast, maintained unified Republican control is the 
less anticipated scenario, potentially introducing a wider range of fiscal and interest rate outcomes. 
• 
Europe faces a crowded electoral timeline, including Spain's November 2026 snap vote and 2027 general elections in France, Italy, 
and Greece. We believe the key market risk is not the election of a specific party, but the potential for fractured parliaments unable to 
execute critical fiscal consolidation. As governments navigate elevated debt servicing burdens, any deviation from credible 
consolidation paths may lead to volatility across the euro, sovereign and corporate bonds, including bank debt, and domestic equities. 
• 
Emerging markets, including Nigeria, Argentina and Poland are among the countries to go to the polls over the coming year. The 
central investment focus is on whether incoming administrations adopt or sustain the orthodox economic frameworks currently taking 
hold across EM economies. 
High-Impact Policy Channels 
Elections reach markets through two effects on different timelines. 
The short-term effect is event risk; uncertainty rises into the vote, market volatility can increase, and some capex, hiring and M&A 
activity may be deferred. This drag typically fades once the result is known, allowing risk premia across assets to compress and paused 
activity to resume. 
The lasting effect is the post-election policy regime, which shapes growth, inflation, corporate profits, fiscal credibility and sector 
leadership. Recent examples span both developed and emerging markets: 
• 
US: Trump's second term: Policymaking has concentrated in the Executive branch and made tariffs a central, fluid policy tool—
shifting the trade regime and raising market volatility. 
• 
Japan: “Sanaenomics”: The reflationary policy framework of Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, following her October 2025 election 
win, has reset expectations for growth and inflation. This has contributed to higher Japanese government bond yields, a pressured BoJ 
policy rate normalization path, a weaker yen and stronger Japanese equities. 
• 
Turkey: Return to orthodoxy: The pivot back to monetary orthodoxy after the 2023 election restored investor confidence and 
contributed to a sovereign credit rating upgrade. 
• 
Poland: Pro-EU pivot: Donald Tusk's 2023 election victory unlocked the release of EU recovery and cohesion funds, boosting the 
zloty and Polish equities. 
• 
South Africa: Reform coalition: The formation of a business-friendly government in 2024 moved policy toward reform and fiscal 
discipline, benefiting South African assets.

Below are four key channels that can drive change. 
 
1. Fiscal policy: The channel bond markets price 
Fiscal policy determines government borrowing needs, debt sustainability and sovereign bond pricing. Germany's 2025 constitutional 
reform of the debt brake shows how an election can unlock a genuine fiscal regime shift. The UK's September 2022 mini-budget provides 
a cautionary example: political change eroded fiscal credibility and triggered rapid repricing outside the electoral cycle. 
Today's combination of wide budget deficits, high government debt-to-GDP, higher debt servicing costs and sticky inflation is already 
pushing long-end government bond yields higher and generating volatility. We expect this to persist independently of the electoral 
calendar, given that no major economy is on a near-term fiscal consolidation path. The election focus is therefore on which outcomes 
make a credible fiscal consolidation path more or less likely. 
Finally, fiscal improvements may not necessarily be channeled into consolidation. With an ever-expanding list of spending needs, from 
defense to energy security, governments may instead use any fiscal space created to spend more. Japan's consumption tax cuts, enacted 
as inflation erodes its debt burden, are a case in point. 
2. Regulation and industrial policy: A stock-selection and sector-rotation channel 
Regulation and industrial policy tend to be felt at the industry level, creating winners and losers. In Europe, German fiscal expansion and 
higher defense and infrastructure spending are supporting companies in defense, construction and grid-infrastructure supply chains. In 
the US, a lower after-tax cost of heavy capex may serve as a tailwind for automation, utilities and domestic semiconductor equipment, 
including in small caps. We view clean energy as the most election-sensitive subsector, making it important to separate companies 
dependent on tax credits and subsidies from those whose earnings hold if policy support is delayed, narrowed or reversed.a 
3. Immigration: A supply-side variable 
For investors, immigration policy is a labor force input affecting potential growth, wages, services inflation, housing and consumption. 
Enforcement of immigration controls can shift quickly, particularly in the US, but the economic effect is ambiguous: smaller labor supply 
also reduces domestic demand, so the net inflation impact may be modest. The clearest effects are sector-specific, concentrated where 
labor constraints already bind—construction (relevant for data centers), healthcare, hospitality, agriculture and logistics. 
4. Trade, technology, energy and geopolitics largely sit above the election cycle 
Trade, energy and frontier technology are increasingly governed by national security and strategic competition rather than electoral 
cycles. In exposed sectors, this means that the traditional pre-election capex pause may be less pronounced in this cycle. 
The AI cycle sits at the center of this trend, bridging national security with export controls, antitrust, data regulation, fiscal incentives and 
energy infrastructure. It is worth distinguishing the layers: AI export controls are a federal, executive matter, while data center permitting 
and oversight sit largely with state, local and agency authorities. 
Electoral outcomes may shift the scope, intensity or legal mechanisms of tariffs and trade actions, but the overarching direction remains 
structurally defensive. This is a global phenomenon, not unique to the US. Europe is similarly hardening its posture where foreign 
competition threatens its own strategic sectors. 
The critical implication for investors is not a traditional left-versus-right policy analysis. Instead, we believe the focus should be on 
distinguishing structural trends from election-sensitive noise, as the most durable opportunities reside where policy support is bipartisan 
or strategically entrenched.

Beyond the above key policy levers, it is also worth monitoring new channels through which elections matter. This includes central bank

to hiking, and the BoJ has adopted a swifter pace of tightening despite political pressure for easing. Market-based inflation expectations

Regional Perspectives 
US midterms: A fiscal-optionality event 
Republicans currently hold unified control of the presidency, House and Senate. Under a divided government, practical legislative change 
would be limited and major fiscal shifts unlikely. However, a division between Congress and the Presidency reintroduces shutdown and 
debt-ceiling risk, historically disruptive but short-lived, with limited sustained market impact. 
A less anticipated outcome is maintained Republican control of both chambers, which could reopen the door to fiscal expansion at a time 
when bond market sensitivity to debt metrics is already elevated.

Potential investment implications: For US risk assets, the AI cycle, oil and the interest rate path matter more in the near term than the 
midterm result. The base case of divided government is the most benign for bonds, because it removes near-term fiscal expansion risk. 
Maintained Republican control of both chambers could invite market volatility since it may open the door to additional fiscal measures. 
European elections: A divergence story with France in focus 
A dense European political calendar, led by Spain's snap election on November 29, 2026, followed by major 2027 votes in France, Italy, 
and Greece, is raising the sovereign risk premium across the Eurozone. Rather than focusing on partisan victories, market participants are 
pricing the risk of political gridlock that could challenge the path to fiscal discipline.3 
France is in focus due to both its fiscal and political setup. It has run a budget deficit for more than 50 years, one of the longest 
uninterrupted runs of fiscal deficits among developed markets, with the debt-to-GDP ratio nearing 120%.4 Political fragmentation and 
instability have been in play since President Macron’s June 2024 snap election produced a hung National Assembly. This has led to 
legislative gridlock and recurring no-confidence votes, particularly around the budget. A key risk heading into the 2027 election is that a 
hung parliament could persist regardless of who wins the presidency, further delaying the pivot to fiscal consolidation at a time when 
higher rates and weak growth are already straining fiscal sustainability. Beyond the risk of a divided executive and legislature, limited 
clarity on candidates' fiscal plans and on the implications for European cohesion—at a moment when European institutions may be 
needed as a backstop—is likely to keep risk premia in French government bonds and risk assets elevated into the vote. Reflecting these 
pressures, France has been downgraded by major rating agencies across 2024–2025 and now has ratings in the A− to low-AA range, 
though it remains 4 to 5 notches above the high-yield boundary. In the equity market, the CAC 40 has lagged major European peers since 
President Macron’s June 2024 snap election triggered a hung parliament. Investors continue to price a broad political risk premium into 
French assets ahead of the 2027 vote.5 Consequently, nearly every French sector in the equity market trades at a pronounced valuation 
discount relative to its European counterparts. While this systemic drawdown in asset prices has weighed heavily on index performance, it 
may also create selective potential opportunities to invest in high-quality businesses exposed to global, secular growth themes that are 
currently trading at multiples largely decoupled from their underlying fundamentals. 
French political risk is concentrated in two key market signals. The euro has depreciated versus the US dollar in 2026, and the spread 
between French and German government bonds—the OAT–Bund spread—has widened year-to-date. Both reflect investor concern over 
French political instability rather than broad euro-area stress. The ECB's Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI) is a potential backstop 
for sovereign bond volatility, but an untested one. Introduced in July 2022, the TPI permits the central bank to purchase bonds to counter 
disorderly market dynamics unwarranted by country-specific fundamentals. Two caveats matter: the TPI has never been activated, and 
eligibility is conditional on sound fiscal policy—precisely what a hung parliament calls into question. Critically, the TPI is designed to cap 
euro-area systemic risk, not France-specific spread risk. Markets can therefore reprice France materially before any backstop is 
considered—and eligibility itself could become contested.

Potential investment implications: Sovereign bond volatility can spill over to European equities and credit, as markets price companies 
by domicile rather than by where earnings are generated—creating dislocations between price and fundamentals, and an opportunity for 
security selection. But the catalyst for fiscal consolidation is often market pressure itself, as the UK found in 2022, so dislocations can 
widen before they correct. That argues for a disciplined and diversified approach around electoral events, and for relative value over 
outright duration in European government bonds. The euro is also exposed. If French political stress raises broader questions about 
European cohesion or fiscal credibility, the currency is a fast and liquid channel through which that risk is priced. 
Emerging markets: Discipline and dispersion 
The classic election-year EM dynamics—fiscal easing, rate cuts, and slower growth amid uncertainty—are unlikely to hold this cycle, and 
for constructive reasons.7 Many EM economies have moved toward policy orthodoxy: firmer commitment to fiscal rules and wider 
adoption of inflation targeting. During the inflation shocks of 2022 and 2025, EM central banks were more proactive than their 
developed-market peers—muting the traditional election-year risks of fiscal slippage and premature easing. 
Several forces reinforce this policy discipline. Sticky inflation across a number of EM countries is keeping central banks conservative, 
holding real rates high to anchor expectations. And with markets squarely focused on fiscal and debt sustainability in both DM and EM, 
policymakers have a clear incentive to protect their credibility rather than spend it. 
Crucially, elections can now act as a positive catalyst rather than a risk. Since 2023, Argentina has turned a reform agenda into a credible 
mandate; annual inflation has fallen from a peak above 280% to about 34%, the government has run a primary surplus since 2024, and 
the sovereign spread has compressed from over 2,700 bps to roughly 600 bps. Hungary’s election result in April 2026 has improved 
prospects for repairing EU relations and unlocking funding access. The election outcome in Colombia in June 2026 has improved the 
outlook for fiscal transparency and credibility. 
The common thread: in today's EM, the durable question is not who wins, but whether the winner sustains market-friendly policy 
credibility. In every case, continued improvement hinges on execution. 
Potential investment implications: In our view, investor allocations to EM have not kept pace with the improvement in institutions and 
macroeconomic buffers, some of it catalyzed by election outcomes. That gap between perception and reality presents an opportunity, 
but capturing it, in our view, requires investors to consider an active, selective approach rather than a passive index allocation. Dispersion 
remains wide, and local policy discipline is likely to remain the primary driver of returns.

Investment Playbook: Build Portfolios for Resilience, Not Election Results 
Most secular investment themes—AI infrastructure, defense, energy security and supply-chain resilience—are relatively agnostic of 
election outcomes. Navigating elections therefore means leaning into what is strategically entrenched while hedging what is genuinely 
election-dependent. The latter includes sovereign bonds, currencies and sectors sensitive to political developments and influenced by 
regulation or fiscal support. Our asset-class views below are guided by two principles: building resilience before a vote and retaining 
flexibility to act after the result. 
 
 
Fixed Income 
Position for divergence: We expect wide fiscal deficits and heavy government borrowing to keep pushing long-end 
yields higher, independent of the election calendar. We favor expressing diverging fiscal paths through relative-value 
and curve rate exposures rather than directional duration bets tied to a result. Staying dynamic matters: Greece and 
Portugal demonstrate that fiscal credibility can be rebuilt, while countries facing persistent political fragmentation or 
renewed fiscal expansion may continue to experience higher risk premia. We would also watch for potential spillovers 
from sovereign-bond markets to credit markets. Select EM debt, in our view, can add income and diversification where 
election results reinforce credible policy frameworks. 
 
Currencies 
Fast to respond, often overpriced: Currencies are often the first markets to respond when political developments alter 
expectations for growth, inflation or fiscal sustainability. Recent examples include the yen's response to Japan's 
reflationary shift, the zloty's appreciation following Poland's pro-European pivot and the euro's sensitivity to concerns 
around French fiscal credibility. As bond markets focus on debt sustainability, currencies may become the fastest and 
most liquid mechanism through which political credibility is expressed. In our view, this favors diversified currency 
exposures and disciplined currency options positions rather than concentrated directional bets on individual election 
outcomes. 
 
Equities 
Own structural beneficiaries; be selective within sectors: Election effects typically influence sectors more than broad 
equity indices, making security selection increasingly important. We stay mindful of country-specific risk and favor 
businesses benefiting from structural investment programs supported by geopolitical priorities, national security 
objectives, and economic competitiveness initiatives. For example, across many developed economies, defense spending 
commitments have become increasingly bipartisan and strategically embedded, making the sector less election-sensitive 
than investors often assume. We separate companies that depend on subsidies or tax credits from those that stand on 
their own earnings. We remain more cautious on business models whose earnings depend heavily on subsidies, tax 
credits, or policy support that may be delayed, narrowed, or reversed following an election. Overall, we believe policy 
uncertainty emphasizes the case for diversification across regions, sectors, and styles.

Portfolio construction: Test exposures against scenarios 
Scenario analysis is more useful than point forecasting, particularly as polls have become a less reliable guide to outcomes. In our view, 
investors should map exposures to the key policy levers—fiscal policy, regulation, competitiveness and geopolitical risk—to surface 
unintended concentrations. Investors should understand which holdings are owned because of secular growth drivers and which are 
effectively tied to a political outcome. 
Bottom Line 
The 2026–27 cycle matters, but in our view the question is not who wins. It is which policy channels are genuinely election-dependent, 
which are structurally embedded, and where outcomes could move fiscal credibility, sector leadership or geopolitical risk premia. We see 
this as pointing to a two-part conclusion. First, build resilience before the vote: own what is strategically entrenched, hedge what is 
genuinely election-dependent, and avoid concentrated bets on any single result. Second, stay nimble to act after it once uncertainty falls 
and the policy picture becomes clear. 
In a world of rising political fragmentation and structurally higher policy uncertainty, the objective should not be to build portfolios for a 
particular electoral outcome. It should be to build portfolios that can withstand a range of outcomes while retaining the flexibility to 
exploit opportunities once uncertainty has been resolved.

RISK CONSIDERATIONS 
Equity investments are subject to market risk, which means that the value of the securities in which it invests may go up or down in response to the 
prospects of individual companies, particular sectors and/or general economic conditions. Different investment styles (e.g., “growth” and “value”) tend to 
shift in and out of favor, and, at times, the strategy may underperform other strategies that invest in similar asset classes. The market capitalization of a 
company may also involve greater risks (e.g. "small" or "mid" cap companies) than those associated with larger, more established companies and may be 
subject to more abrupt or erratic price movements, in addition to lower liquidity. 
Investments in fixed income securities are subject to the risks associated with debt securities generally, including credit, liquidity, interest rate, prepayment 
and extension risk. Bond prices fluctuate inversely to changes in interest rates. Therefore, a general rise in interest rates can result in the decline in the 
bond’s price.  The value of securities with variable and floating interest rates are generally less sensitive to interest rate changes than securities with fixed 
interest rates. Variable and floating rate securities may decline in value if interest rates do not move as expected. Conversely, variable and floating rate 
securities will not generally rise in value if market interest rates decline. Credit risk is the risk that an issuer will default on payments of interest and 
principal. Credit risk is higher when investing in high yield bonds, also known as junk bonds. Prepayment risk is the risk that the issuer of a security may pay 
off principal more quickly than originally anticipated. Extension risk is the risk that the issuer of a security may pay off principal more slowly than originally 
anticipated. All fixed income investments may be worth less than their original cost upon redemption or maturity. 
International securities may be more volatile and less liquid and are subject to the risks of adverse economic or political developments. International 
securities are subject to greater risk of loss as a result of, but not limited to, the following: inadequate regulations, volatile securities markets, adverse 
exchange rates, and social, political, military, regulatory, economic or environmental developments, or natural disasters. 
Emerging markets investments may be less liquid and are subject to greater risk than developed market investments as a result of, but not limited to, the 
following: inadequate regulations, volatile securities markets, adverse exchange rates, and social, political, military, regulatory, economic or environmental 
developments, or natural disasters. 
The risk of foreign currency exchange rate fluctuations may cause the value of securities denominated in such foreign currency to decline in value. 
Currency exchange rates may fluctuate significantly over short periods of time. These risks may be more pronounced for investments in securities of issuers 
located in, or otherwise economically tied to, emerging countries. If applicable, investment techniques used to attempt to reduce the risk of currency 
movements (hedging), may not be effective. Hedging also involves additional risks associated with derivatives.
