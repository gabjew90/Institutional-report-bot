Rising goods prices, but not war related drivers
Goods prices in the euro area have gradually risen in 2026, and the inflation rate is now clearly above
its historical average. However, while the ECB has focused on the indirect effects of higher energy
prices and the possibility that these may spill over into a broader rise in inflation, the increase so far
appears to reflect a shift in the rate of change in ICT goods prices. The annual rate of change in ICT
goods prices is normally negative, but in recent months it has even turned positive. This means that
inflation developments this year and expectations for the period ahead are not fully comparable with
the historical pattern.
Key points
Core goods prices are rising higher
ICT-product a key driver
Signs of energy-related indirect effect still absent
 
The inflation rate for core HICP in the euro area, has broadly moved sideways since the beginning of 2025.
However, trends in the various subcomponents have diverged in 2026. Services inflation has gradually
declined, albeit in small steps, and remains too high. At the same time, the trend in core goods inflation has
been upward, and the annual rate is now well above its historical average (here we are referring to
consumer goods excluding energy, food, alcohol and tobacco). Looking at shorter-term price momentum in
seasonally adjusted data, the trend core goods inflation has clearly diverged from that of services.

One of the ECB’s most important questions this year has been whether, to what extent and when any
indirect effects from high energy prices will spill over into rising prices across broader product groups (i.e.
indirect inflation effects). In addition to being highly uncertain, indirect effects also materialise with a time
lag. Both we and the ECB have assessed that the largest indirect effects will emerge only in the first half of
2027. Even so, it is important to understand the current situation, not least to assess whether the effects
are arriving earlier than expected.
Still few signs; other drivers dominate for now.
We agree with the ECB that there have so far been few signs of spillover effects, although there are some.
Food price developments, for example, have been weak in 2026, even though this is an area where pass-
through might otherwise be expected to occur relatively quickly. But when we look at core goods and their
subgroups, the latest inflation report (for September) reinforced the pattern for which we had previously
seen indications.
The rise in goods prices is largely being driven by higher medicine and health product prices, but above all
by the fact that the trend in ICT goods this year has been completely different and stronger than we are
historically used to (by ICT we mean mobile phones, computers, audio systems, etc.). Pharmaceutical
prices are often affected by various regulations, which appears to be the case this time, and the effect
also seems fairly isolated to Germany. Although the rise in medicine and health product prices is
approaching historically high levels, the shift seen specifically in ICT goods is of greater interest.

  In fact, when we calculate the contribution from these two subgroups, they explain virtually the entire
rise in goods inflation. The increase in ICT-related products is also visible in German and US producer
prices (and is even more pronounced in the US). We know that pressure from AI investment has pushed up
the prices of processors, memory chips and computers. But it now also appears that these increases have
reached final consumer prices. Whether this effect is temporary or more persistent is, of course, uncertain.
However, a potential change in trend means that, for a time, we should expect goods prices overall to rise
somewhat faster than in the past.
The fact that we have even seen outright price increases in ICT products in recent months marks a clear
reversal and is something we have essentially never seen before. In one sense, the threshold for higher
technology prices to feed through to the CPI is higher. Because the CPI is adjusted for quality changes,
improvements in the performance of technology products have historically often meant falling prices in
CPI terms. The fact that prices are now rising means that the underlying price increases are more than
offsetting the effect of quality improvements.

Higher goods prices, but not because of energy
As we approach the end of 2026, we can conclude that central banks are having to deal with several price
trends. Headline inflation is rising as a result of higher energy prices, but the risk of indirect effects will be
determined more by the inflation data in spring 2027. Goods prices, by contrast, are already rising faster
than we expected.
Central banks normally try to look through supply shocks such as rising energy prices and are more
inclined to respond to demand-driven inflation. What we have seen so far can instead be linked to stronger
demand, which under normal circumstances should make the central bank more inclined to raise interest
rates.
Much suggests, however, that the price increase is related to higher demand for AI goods and services
across the wider economy, rather than being driven by consumer demand itself. The driver can therefore
be found at an early stage of the production process and could accordingly be viewed as a supply shock.
This would amount to a change in relative prices across the economy: technology should cost more, while
something else becomes relatively cheaper. Exactly how this will play out remains unclear. But if it is
merely a relative-price change, there are arguments for central banks to allow the price increase to pass
through without trying to dampen demand for what is driving the change in that particular part of the
economy. A parallel can be drawn with periods when commodities become more expensive, something
central banks cannot influence (unless it drives up prices for final consumers).

Brief comparison with Sweden: Similar, yet different
It is natural to ask whether we see the same pattern in the Swedish CPI. As regards the change in the
contribution from ICT products, the answer is yes. In recent months, the contribution from ICT products
has been considerably less negative than before. Developments in overall goods prices are, however,
much more subdued in Sweden than in the euro area, because the contribution from other major goods
categories, such as clothing, footwear and furniture, is lower or even negative. The appreciation of the
krona in recent years has also contributed to this year’s decline.
