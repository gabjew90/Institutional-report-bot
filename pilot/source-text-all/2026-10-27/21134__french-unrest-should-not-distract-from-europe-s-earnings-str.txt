Thought of the day
French authorities imposed curfews in parts of several major cities this week, 
including Paris, Lille, and Nice, following a weekend of riots and looting 
linked to nationwide anti-government protests. Around 2,400 people were 
arrested, nearly 1,000 police officers were injured, and almost 3,000 shops 
were affected, according to French authorities and a retail federation.
The unrest comes as the government seeks to rein in France’s budget deficit 
and stabilize its rising debt burden. The protests underline the political 
difficulty of restoring fiscal discipline at a time of pressure on household 
purchasing power and public services.
But neither the unrest itself nor France’s fiscal strains should obscure an 
improving Eurozone economy and an earnings cycle that continues to 
strengthen.
Previous episodes of social unrest suggest that the direct economic 
impact should remain limited and concentrated in the most exposed 
sectors. France’s national statistics agency estimated that the 2018 gilets 
jaunes, or Yellow Vest, protests reduced French fourth-quarter GDP growth 
by only around 0.1 percentage point. While retail, hospitality, transport, 
and tourism were disrupted, there was little evidence of a broader supply-
side shock. The main transmission channel was temporary weakness in

Economic activity across the Eurozone is improving, providing a 
supportive foundation for corporate revenues. The Eurozone composite 
purchasing managers’ index rose to 53.1 in September, its highest level in 
41 months, as activity accelerated across both manufacturing and services. 
Manufacturing new orders increased at the fastest pace since March 2022, 
while foreign orders recorded their first sustained expansion in more than 
four and a half years. Germany is also providing an important counterweight 
to strains elsewhere in the region: The government recently raised its 2026 
GDP growth forecast to 1.3% from 0.5% and its 2027 forecast to 1.1% from 
0.9%, supported by greater investment in infrastructure and defense. Flash 
PMIs due later this week should provide further evidence that the recovery 
continued into the fourth quarter.
European earnings look set to outpace even this improving regional 
economic backdrop. More than half of the Stoxx 600’s market capitalization 
is due to report over the next two weeks, with third-quarter earnings 
expected to have risen 20.7% from a year earlier, according to LSEG I/B/E/S. 
While that would be slightly slower than the second quarter’s 24% pace, it 
would still rank among the fastest rates of earnings growth in recent years. 
Analysts have raised aggregate estimates ahead of the season rather than 
cutting them as they typically do, with technology earnings forecast to rise 
by almost 33% and industrial profits by 11%. Many of Europe’s leading 
companies operate in global markets and are positioned to benefit from 
investment in AI and data center infrastructure, automation, electrification, 
reshoring, and defense, rather than depending primarily on domestic growth. 
We continue to forecast Eurozone earnings growth of 15% in both 2026 and 
2027, with this year’s growth supported by secular drivers and cost controls 
and next year’s growth expected to broaden as revenues rise across more 
sectors. Notably, the energy sector should benefit from higher prices as the 
conflict in the Middle East continues.
So, we keep our Attractive rating on Eurozone equities. We like information 
technology, industrials, banks, consumer discretionary, health care, and 
Germany, alongside our "European leaders" theme. Improving regional 
activity is supportive, but the combination of global investment demand, 
broadening profit growth, and exposure to powerful structural trends 
provides an even stronger foundation for many of Europe’s leading 
companies.
Caught our attention
Tech earnings in focus. US stock futures rose during Asia morning trading 
on Wednesday amid positive tech headlines. SpaceX posted September-
quarter net income that was well above market projection and highlighted 
ambitious plans to expand its data center capacity over the coming year. 
Super Micro Computer’s shares rose over 15% in post-market trading after 
it said preliminary quarterly revenue may be some 25% above analysts' 
estimate. A leading AI lab, meanwhile, signaled it could spend as much as 
USD 750bn in the coming years to secure more compute capacity. All this 
came ahead of Meta’s results later today and those from other hyperscalers 
next week.
Our view: Without commenting on individual companies, we think investors 
will be looking for evidence that AI can drive meaningful monetization 
as mega-cap tech companies report their earnings. Beyond adoption and

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
