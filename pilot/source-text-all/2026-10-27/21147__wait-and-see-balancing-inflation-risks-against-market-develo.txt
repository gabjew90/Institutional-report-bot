ECB Preview
21 October 2026
Wait-and-see, balancing inflation risks against market developments
We expect the ECB to leave its key policy rates unchanged at the 29 October meeting. Although
energy prices appear to lie between the levels assumed in September’s adverse and severe
scenarios, the recent surge in bond yields has attracted increasing attention from Governing Council
members. Their communication supports our view—and current market expectations—that the
next rate hike will come in December.
Key points
Interim checkpoint: The October meeting offers an opportunity to assess incoming data,
with major policy decisions likely to remain on hold until the updated staff projections are
published in December  
Energy prices versus market tightening: High oil and gas prices point towards the
adverse scenarios, but the sharp rise in global long-term bond yields is already doing much
of the ECB’s tightening  
Rates on hold: We maintain our forecast of 25bp rate hikes in December and March.
 
The ECB’s meeting on 29 October will serve as an interim checkpoint between the September and
December rounds of macroeconomic projections. In recent years, including the two most recent rate hikes
in June and September 2026, the ECB has preferred to make major policy decisions alongside more in-
depth analysis, including updated staff projections and scenarios. The intervening meetings have served
as checkpoints for assessing incoming data, evaluating how risks have evolved and identifying emerging
risks.
Against this background, we highlight and comment on the latest developments below:
 1.    Inflation outcomes have been broadly in line with projections. Goods inflation continues to edge
higher, while services inflation is gradually easing or perhaps levelling off. Energy prices remain the main
driver of inflation, with an increase spreading from fuel to household energy. There are few, if any, signs of
indirect or second-round effects from high energy prices.
2.    Real economic activity continues to show clear signs of improvement. This is most evident in survey
data, with confidence rising in both manufacturing and services despite the prospect of energy prices
remaining higher for longer. Consumer confidence has improved since the beginning of 2026 but remains
weak by historic metrics.
3.    Energy prices—and the conflict in the Middle East itself—remain key. Crude oil and, in particular,
natural gas prices are significantly above the levels assumed in the ECB’s September baseline scenario,
which had a cut-off date of 19 August. Oil and natural gas prices are currently just above the levels
assumed in the adverse scenario. However, futures prices suggest that natural gas will track the severe

4.    Financial conditions have tightened substantially, marking the most significant change since the
September monetary policy meeting. Expectations for higher policy rates and, above all, the sharp rise in
global long-term bond yields should weigh on economic activity with a lag. To some extent, this is a
circular argument, as part of the rise in yields reflects expectations of higher central bank policy rates.
Nevertheless, markets have already done some of the ECB’s heavy lifting, making the final rate hike less
certain. This creates a new downside risk to the policy-rate path, a point highlighted by a growing number
of Governing Council members in recent weeks.
 
ECB rhetoric and assessment
Recent ECB speeches emphasise that elevated energy prices have so far had no major spillover effects on
other prices or, more importantly, on the expectations that shape wage-setting. There also appears to be
broad agreement that further rate hikes are likely to be needed to address inflation risks and range of
possible outcomes outlined in the latest staff projections. The overall impression is that there is no urgent
need to act, allowing most policymakers to wait for the more comprehensive analysis in December. At the
same time, they stress that the ECB stands ready to deliver additional hikes if necessary. The tightening of
financial conditions in recent weeks has also attracted increasing attention.
Future leadership line-up
Alongside monetary policy, the ECB’s future leadership has attracted growing attention, as the terms of
three key members—Lagarde, Lane and Schnabel—are due to expire in 2027. Isabel Schnabel is set to
leave the ECB on 3 January. Rumours persist that President Lagarde could leave early either to lead the
World Economic Forum or to enter the French presidential race. The time for resolving these uncertainties
is drawing closer. Given the ECB’s close institutional ties with other EU bodies, the appointment of a new
president would probably form part of a broader political package involving other European leadership
roles. Economic developments and the inflation outlook will remain decisive, but the composition of the
Governing Council can influence how the ECB interprets and responds to developments, with implications
that extend beyond communication alone.
