Health Care Has Rarely Mattered More to the Economy—or Less to the 
Market
Early evidence is emerging that both the health care sector and the patients it serves will benefit, 
perhaps substantially, from advances in AI. Yet, we believe today’s investors are assigning little value to 
that long-term potential. 
By the end of 2026, health care is expected to account for over 18% of the US economy, yet it currently 
represents only 9.1% of the S&P 500.1 That gap highlights a striking disconnect between the sector’s 
economic importance and its market value, just as AI appears poised to begin reshaping its long-term 
growth potential. 
Health care is one of the largest sectors in most developed economies—yet it remains 
one of the least recognized beneficiaries of artificial intelligence (AI). We think the 
market may be underestimating AI’s potential to reshape the sector. To understand why, 
it helps to revisit the "Iron Triangle", a decades-old framework for health care systems 
that focuses on the constraints between quality, access and cost.

A Decades-old Constraint: Health Care’s Iron Triangle 
In Medicine’s Dilemmas, published in 1994, William Kissick described health care as a give-and-take 
process between three critical variables: quality, access and cost. Kissick used a triangle to symbolize 
these trade-offs with each side representing one variable and each variable constraining the others. 
The idea reflects what we have all experienced: 
Broader access raises spending and / or reduces quality. 
Increased quality involves accessing a finite number of specialists with above-average fees 
and a penchant for expensive technology and medicines. 
Aggressive attempts to control costs invariably restrict access to these same higher-quality 
specialists, drugs and technologies. 
The triangle is “iron” because Kissick saw few ways to change the restrictive compromises between 
quality, access, and cost. 
The trade-offs look different by system and country but are present everywhere: single-payer 
systems tend to maintain cost and quality by rationing access through waiting lists; the US 
maintains access and quality for the insured at exceptional cost; and many emerging markets 
lack the specialist supply to deliver quality at any price.

AI May Improve Access and Cost Before It Revolutionizes Drug 
Development
Innovations in drugs, devices and diagnostics have traditionally improved the quality side of the 
triangle through greater efficacy and more personalized care. Recent advances in AI could accelerate 
breakthroughs already underway across cancer, cardiovascular disease, autoimmune disorders and 
other areas. But meaningfully improving success rates in drug development may still be years away 
given the extraordinary complexity of human biology, limited high-quality training data, and the need to 
validate results in years-long human trials. In the interim, the more transformational opportunity for AI 
may lie on the other two sides of the Iron Triangle: access and cost.
The triangle was iron for a reason: medical intelligence was scarce. It lived inside a finite number of 
experts, buildings, and 15-minute appointment slots, so expanding access to it meant spending more or 
diluting quality. AI changes the underlying constraint. When specialist-grade intelligence can be 
delivered through a coach, a nurse, or an app at low marginal cost, expanding access no longer 
requires expanding spending—and continuous, low-cost access unlocks the cheapest form of care 
there is: prevention.
The opportunity to improve access is significant given more than 100 million Americans live in 
communities without adequate primary care.2 Demand far exceeds supply—many physicians are 
retiring, burnout remains high, and fewer than half of graduating medical doctors express an interest in 
primary care.3 
AI-infused hybrid care models are beginning to close this gap
By combining AI with nurse practitioners, health coaches, and smartphone apps, these models put 
specialist-grade medical intelligence in patients’ hands around the clock—in ways that build patient 
trust. Many patients now prefer these low-cost, continuous relationships to waiting months for a primary 
care physician or specialist appointment.

The Stakes Are High in Health Care, but Systemwide Trust Is Low 
Most health systems, and the US fee-for-service model in particular, often pay to fix us after we break, 
not to keep us from breaking in the first place. Medical intelligence has historically been locked inside 
appointment slots—at a specialist’s office, a hospital, or a laboratory—and reaching it means navigating 
an expensive, disjointed maze of health care providers.  
Some companies are now pursuing a different approach with AI-infused hybrid care that bypasses 
dysfunctional legacy infrastructure and separates intelligence from place of service. The result 
improves quality, increases access and lowers cost—simultaneously.

These companies are using AI systems to build trust with patients. The adage that “no one cares how 
much you know, until they know how much you care” carries extra weight in health care, where the 
stakes are high, but systemwide trust is low. Patients are unlikely to embrace new innovations if they 
don’t trust the providers recommending them—and trust is hard to build when patients see their 
doctor for just 15 minutes a few times a year.5 When AI absorbs the paperwork and helps guide 
interactions, coaches and nurses gain the time to know a patient’s life and connect the care plan to 
what that patient truly wants—whether that is staying independent or dancing at a granddaughter’s 
wedding. And when trust runs high, patients often grant providers more freedom to innovate.

AI Is a Force Multiplier for Innovation Through Recombination
Most innovations involve recombining existing ideas with new ones to arrive at novel solutions. To 
illustrate the power of recombination, consider the four established approaches to treating cancer: 
surgery, radiation, chemotherapy and immunotherapy. The combination of these approaches results in 
24 total treatment options for a given cancer (4x3x2x1).  
In August, Moderna and Merck announced that the Phase 3 trial of their first-in-class mRNA cancer 
vaccine, known as V940, succeeded in the treatment of skin cancer (melanoma). Used in combination 
with Merck’s immunotherapy, Keytruda, V940 demonstrated statistically significant and clinically 
meaningful improvements in recurrence-free survival and distant metastasis-free survival compared 
with Keytruda alone.   
If the cancer vaccine developed by Moderna and Merck ends up representing a new approach for 
treating cancer, then society’s treatment options didn’t simply increase by one, they increased by a 
factor of five, to 120 (5x4x3x2x1).8 Adding a new treatment modality expands the number of ways 
existing approaches can potentially be combined to create exponentially more options. This is the 
power of recombination as a central driver of innovation across not only therapeutics, but also specialty 
diagnostics, medical devices, and countless industries outside of the healthcare sector.

The Market Is Giving Little Credit for the Benefits of AI in Health Care
We see clear early signals that AI-infused health care solutions can break the historical 
constraints between health care quality, access and cost. 
Regulations and misaligned incentives may slow the pace of adoption, but the direction of travel is 
clear and the magnitude of impact looks significant.  
All investments can be thought of as some combination of price paid (what you give) and future 
earnings power (what you get). At current prices, we believe investors are giving little credit for 
AI-driven innovations that can increase the long-term earnings potential of many health care 
companies in the US and around the world.
