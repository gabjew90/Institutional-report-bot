Digging deeper
Deep Fission’s business update this morning served primarily to retell its narrative,
anchored by a major milestone: a binding 25-year master energy purchase agreement
with the Great Plains Development Authority. This foundational deal covers an initial 150
MW phase targeted for September 2028, scaling up to 2 GW by 2031.
Please dive into our note for the full breakdown of this agreement and other key
updates (including deep borehole drilling advancements). As this was strictly a business
update, we will update our model after full 3Q26 financials are disclosed.
First, let us also take the opportunity to retell Deep Fission’s story from our perspective.
The fundamental breakthrough the Deep Fission team unpacked was realizing that
the natural geology of the Earth could replace the most expensive, heavily engineered
components of a traditional surface nuclear plant. In a standard pressurized water
reactor, massive, multi-billion-dollar steel and concrete containment domes are required
to handle immense pressures and protect against surface hazards. By placing a small
modular reactor down a narrow borehole a mile beneath the surface, the weight of the
water column and the surrounding rock naturally provide that pressure and containment
for free.
Liz Muller, Co-Founder and CEO, recognized that this design could help answer the
modern energy crisis, particularly the seemingly insatiable, 24/7 power demands of
AI data centers. Because the reactor operates underground with a minimal surface
footprint, it could theoretically be co-located right next to digital infrastructure without
the massive land requirements or multi-decade timelines of traditional gigawatt-scale
plants.
Cheaper. Faster. Safer.
The concept quickly caught the attention of Silicon Valley and Washington alike.
Backed by venture capital firms like 8VC, Deep Fission fast-tracked its engineering.
The momentum culminated in a major milestone when the U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE) selected the company for its Nuclear Reactor Pilot Program, providing crucial
institutional backing to accelerate their path toward achieving criticality. And, today, the
company has transitioned from theoretical physics to physical operation, actively drilling
data acquisition wells at the Great Plains Industrial Park in Kansas, working to turn their
underground vision into commercial reality.
Now, look: there are certainly risks. For one, a mile underground, geology is a double-
edged sword. While deep strata provide natural containment, drilling a wide borehole
for nuclear use demands decades of absolute precision. Minor seismic shifts or casing
failures can warp the narrow shaft, trapping the reactor canister and permanently
transforming an operational asset into an inaccessible, subterranean liability. Surface
plants offer hands-on maintenance, but a mile deep in a narrow pipe, operators are
effectively blind. It's doable but not easy.
Then there’s the sheer physical brutalism of drilling a mile-deep borehole - a high-
stakes engineering feat that includes maintaining absolute verticality, clearing heavy
cuttings, and wrestling massive casings into place.

Deep Fission also relies on a mile-high column of water to generate the 160
atmospheres of hydrostatic pressure required for operation. However, water under
extreme pressure and heat is intensely corrosive. Managing water chemistry in a
sealed well is a materials-science challenge; any structural failure risks leaking high-
temperature, irradiated fluids into deep rock formations.
As nuclear waste management challenges mount, the company’s strategy of
repurposing existing boreholes into deep geological repositories offers an elegant, cost-
effective solution. The catch? If emerging fuel-recycling technologies take off, which
appears increasingly likely, burying these valuable materials today might soon look like
a waste of tomorrow’s fuel.
Technology is only half the battle; bureaucracy could prove just as daunting. While
Deep Fission uses proven pressurized water technology, the Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) has never licensed a deep borehole reactor. Navigating this
unpaved regulatory frontier could prove a multi-year marathon. Fortunately for Deep
Fission, however, the company's participation in the DOE's Nuclear Reactor Pilot
Program combined with NRC's evolving Part 57 regulatory framework open a potentially
expedited, risk-informed path to commercial deployment relatively in-line with firm
targets.
Let us add another: capital. On the call, management indicated that they ended 3Q26
with ~$72M in cash and expect to end the year with ~$40M. We expect the company
to need additional capital to complete the commercial pilot program.
As we close, let us add this: yes, again, Deep Fission is not immune to risk. We have
charted those - here and in our initiation. But let us also be clear about this. In this
nuclear arena, perfection is a ghost. Every contender bears its own vulnerability. Even
the grand, weathered monoliths of traditional nuclear power, deployed across decades,
have stumbled beneath the crushing weight of material overruns.
In this, Deep Fission stands in familiar company. Indeed, their answers to the
industry’s most stubborn bottlenecks carry a distinct elegance, in our opinion. And,
they are moving their platform forward by leveraging existing technology channels.
We believe that the West stands at the threshold of a new nuclear dawn - a high-stakes
race where history will name its winners and its losers, navigated through a gauntlet
of regulation, cost, and fuel. Amid the fog of uncertainty, pioneers will rise. Measured
against a crowded field, Deep Fission remains a formidable contender.

Deep Fission's NRC licensing approach
Dual-pathway licensing strategy: DOE pilot authorization first, NRC
commercial licensing second. Deep Fission benefits from a government-mandated
time-to-market advantage. As previously mentioned, Deep Fission was selected as 1 of
10 companies designated to the Reactor Pilot US DOE Program, an initiative established
under Executive Order 14301 implemented to fast-track the licensing, testing, and
deployment of advanced, lower-consequence reactors.
• Streamlining regulation. Importantly, this designation facilitates FOAK
demonstration, and subsequent commercial deployment, under the DOE
Authorization licensing pathway (as is the case for all elected program participants).
The DOE Authorization pathway substantially streamlines regulatory complexity
relative to conventional NRC licensing frameworks and allows participants to bypass
the NRC logjam.
• Commercial licensing via Part 57. Rather than filing a traditional, cumbersome
Part 50 or Part 52 application for each borehole, Deep Fission targets commercial
licensing via recently proposed Part 57—designed as a risk-informed, microreactor
licensing framework that supports series deployments. In addition to the benefits
from Part 57, Deep Fission plans to aggregate operational data from the FOAK
demonstration, overseen by the DOE, to establish a “presumption of adequacy” for
the reactor’s technical baseline and future deployments.
• DOE approved Deep Fission's Nuclear Safety Design Agreement (NSDA).
FISN got an NSDA for its Gravity pilot reactor under the Reactor Pilot Program. The
NSDA establishes the project's safety framework, design requirements, planned
safety analysis methodology, and regulatory engagement process. Management
described DOE approval as an initial milestone in the safety review process, with
additional documentation and reviews required before potential DOE authorization
for reactor operations.

Proven drilling techniques
Deep Fission's borehole drilling has been proven commercially. FISN's reactor
is expected to be deployed approximately 1 mile underground and leverage drilling
techniques and equipment originally developed by the oil and gas industry. The
company began drilling for data acquisition in March 2026, to a planned depth of
approximately 6,000 ft at an 8-inch diameter. A second well, to 2,500 ft, is planned to
demonstrate deployment of major components, including lowering a prototype reactor.
The speed and success of drilling a large-diameter well depend entirely on 3 critical
factors: keeping the hole vertical, effectively removing cuttings, and properly setting
the casing.
• Historical validation. Large diameter drilling is not new. Between 1959 and 1970,
American contractors drilled holes up to 120 inches in diameter to depths beyond
5,000 feet. They used oilfield rigs, oilfield crews, and methods still in commercial
service today. FISN's planned boreholes are expected to range from 30 to 50 inches
in diameter.
• Techniques and equipment still exist today. Deep Fission cites that a Florida
contractor, the Youngquist Brothers, has drilled 163 wells in Florida with a top
diameter of between 54–74 inches and a bottom diameter of 20–36 inches at depths
to 10,000 ft.
• Large-diameter casing strings have been proven at comparable depths and
diameters. Deep Fission's reactor will sit inside a steel casing that extends deep
into the borehole. FISN cites that a 54-inch casing string weighing approximately
5 million pounds was installed to 5,600 ft in the 1960s. Management argues that
this demonstrates large-diameter casing installation has already been achieved at

Deep Fission's business model
Build, own, operate. Deep Fission will sell vertically integrated, power-as-a-service.
Deep Fission seeks to cut through the Gordian knot of nuclear ecosystem complexity
that has resulted in cost overruns, utility bankruptcies and the broad deployment
malaise. In addition to designing the reactor and plant, Deep Fission’s vertically
integrated model involves building, owning, and operating the reactor – providing an
accessible, direct pathway to what most customers truly want: reliable nuclear power.
• Upfront & recurring revenue streams. In addition to revenue generated from
electricity sales, Deep Fission’s revenue framework breaks down into upfront and
recurring revenue streams. Upfront revenues are tied to engineering, deployment,
and initial commissioning of individual reactor modules, while recurring revenue
streams are tied to operational maintenance and IP licensing (as of June 2026, Deep
Fission’s IP portfolio included 2 patents issued and 38 patents pending).

• Safety: replacing mechanical machinery with the basic laws of physics and geology.
Deep Fission’s novel borehole reactor deployment model provides two primary
safety advantages. First, natural hydrostatic pressure of ~160 atmospheres at the
bottom of the borehole ensures coolant accessibility without mechanical pipes or a
thick, steel-walled pressure vessel, eliminating radioactive exposure risk by laws of
physics and gravity. Second, millions of tons of surrounding bedrock provide natural
geological containment and acts as a highly effective heat sink.
• LEU fuel and a differentiated waste management strategy. Gravity reactors
use LEU (<5% fissile uranium-235 concentration) in uranium-oxide ceramic fuel
form, the same as conventional, above-ground PWRs, ensuring accessible fuel supply
while limiting proliferation concerns. Once LEU fuel is depleted to a level that no
longer sustains the chain reaction (Gravity reactors are expected to have a core-
life of ~6-years), a new reactor core and steam generator can be placed on top
of the spent core to begin another operating cycle, or the borehole can be sealed,
storing the reactor (and byproduct radioactive waste) in deep geological repositories
(DGRs).
• Leveraging mature technologies and mature supply chains. Deep Fission’s
approach to nuclear power generation blends mature oil and gas, geothermal and
nuclear technologies. By acting as a system integrator rather than a foundational
components manufacturer, their supply-chain strategy breaks down into three core
pillars: sourcing off-the-shelf nuclear components/fuel, leveraging established oil &
gas subsurface infrastructure supply chains, and leveraging established geothermal
heat transfer and conventional power generation infrastructure supply chains.
• Reducing capex by up to ~80%. Given design-driven reactor part reduction, off-
the-shelf equipment supply chain accessibility, LEU fuel, and anticipated expeditious
deployment lead times, management expects to reduce reactor capital costs by
70-80% relative to conventional, above-ground PWRs. Management estimates a
FOAK overnight construction cost of ~$152M (~18,944/ kW) and a NOAK overnight
construction cost of ~$84M (~5,5667/ kW).
• Reduction in proliferation risk. Gravity reactors make LEU fuel less accessible
than conventional PWRs. Deep Fission’s reactor core sits at the bottom of the
borehole, ~1-mile (~5,280 feet) underground. Retrieving nuclear material from the
borehole, whether for nefarious or operational purposes, is challenging, necessitating
highly specialized, heavy industrial drilling rigs, high-tensile lifting cables, and hours
of continuous, loud, and easily detectable mechanical hoisting operations.
A reactor designed to leverage existing supply chains. The Gravity reactor is
designed to leverage existing, cross-industry industrial supply chains intended to drive
capital cost reduction and shorten deployment lead-times.
• According to the company, “major reactor components are expected to utilize
established materials and manufacturing processes drawn from the nuclear and
broader industrial sectors, while the reactor’s compact and simplified design is
intended to enable greater use of standard industrial fabrication methods in place of
conventional large-scale nuclear construction practices.” More specifically, "Surface
power conversion and balance-of-plant equipment associated with the Gravity
Reactor are expected to utilize commercially available industrial components.”
• Supply chain partners. Supplied by R-V Industries, reactor canisters will
be manufactured in qualified facilities and delivered to project sites prior to
emplacement in the borehole. Supplied by Urenco, LEU fuel will be installed into
Gravity canister cores either at the manufacturing facility or at the borehole site
depending on the local environment and transport regulation. For the physical
execution, engineering, and deployment of the first-of-a-kind borehole reactors,
Deep Fission has partnered with Day & Zimmermann, a major nuclear-grade
construction firm.

Risks to achieving Target Price / Valuation:
Deep Fission - FISN
• Technology. Deep Fission’s technology leverages proven technologies across oil/gas, geothermal and nuclear power. Despite the
technology’s independent operating history, we see operational risks associated with the unprecedented integration of nuclear
operations with deep-borehole engineering. More specifically, operating a reactor at the bottom of a 1-mile-deep borehole
introduces remote inspection/maintenance complexity, geological dynamics and potential regulatory concerns.
• Regulatory/sentiment risk. Regulatory changes/application denials could materially impact deployments and subsequent power
sales. Deep Fission currently does not have NRC approval and therefore is not permitted to operate a commercial Gravity reactor.
Navigating the NRC approval process introduces deployment timing uncertainty especially considering Deep Fission’s novel design.
Moreover, we also see some regulatory risk to Deep Fission’s approach to waste management. If this is not approved, the company
may not be able to operate commercial reactors.
• Costs. Given limited operating experience for reactors of this configuration and scale, FOAK Gravity reactors could face cost
overruns and NOAK Gravity reactor cost savings may not materialize to the degree expected.
• Market risk. While we acknowledge microreactor market applicability, the market for microreactors using advanced nuclear
technology has not yet been established. Considering low power output per Gravity reactor, we see some risk to market
materialization as customers may opt for higher output small modular reactors to satiate application-specific power needs.
• AI driver may not materialize. Nuclear’s rapid resurgence has been driven, at least in part, by onerous AI power demands
amidst an already vulnerable US power grid. If AI power demand wanes, or commits to an alternative power source, Deep Fission’s
sales could be materially limited.
