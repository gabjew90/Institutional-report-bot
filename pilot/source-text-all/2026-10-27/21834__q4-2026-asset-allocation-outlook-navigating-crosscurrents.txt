the rising tide of corporate earnings has 
spread across sectors and geographies. 
The market is taking the prospect of a

income seriously as AI augments and 
displaces labour.
Positioning for competing forces
Our response to those offsetting forces 
has been to dial up our equity exposure 
in the aftermath of the conflict flare-

particularly in investment grade-rated 
markets. Having lagged the rest of the

Could robust spending trends

To sustain spending growth, households

It is possible that statisticians continue to

The equity wealth effect
The other major contributing factor 
to consumer spending has been the 
rise in equity wealth. A recent Federal 
Reserve paper argues the sensitivity of 
aggregate consumption to changes in 
the stock market has increased by three 
times since 1990. This is a function of 
the soaring share of equity wealth in 
household balance sheets. This is despite 
the increasing concentration of equity 
wealth in the top end of the wealth

CAPE fear?
The elevated CAPE ratio is often cited as evidence that US 
equities have become too expensive. We examine why the 
relationship between valuation and future returns may be more 
nuanced than commonly assumed.
Robert Griffiths, Global Equity Strategist 
The cyclically adjusted price/earnings 
ratio (CAPE) is one of the most widely 
used measures of equity market 
valuation. Rather than comparing 
current prices with recent or forecast 
earnings, it uses the average of the 
previous 10 years of inflation-adjusted 
earnings, helping to smooth out 
the effects of economic cycles and 
temporary fluctuations in profitability.
At 40.5x, the US CAPE currently sits in 
the 98.9th percentile of its near 150-
year history, offering a useful soundbite 
to those concerned that US equities have 
moved decisively into bubble territory. 
What CAPE can and can’t tell us about 
future returns
How worried should we be about 
the elevated level of this measure? 
We believe there are a number of 
factors which temper the bearish 
message some might draw from 
such an elevated reading, and we 
review a few of them here. 
First, the common scatter charts often 
circulated as evidence of the apparent 
relationship between starting-point 
CAPE and subsequent long-term 
returns (i.e. the higher the starting point 
multiple, the lower the subsequent 
return) are built upon shaky statistical 
foundations. Second, we are currently 
in a regime of historically rapid earnings 
per share (EPS) growth. Of course 
that might not last, but even some 
tentative extrapolations forward will 
bring down the CAPE rapidly as the 
10-year earnings window shifts. And 
third, outside of the US, valuations 
on most measures, CAPE included, 
are simply not that extended. 
Producing a scatter chart of CAPE 
versus subsequent 10-year real returns 
creates an apparently compelling 
inverse relationship: the higher the 
starting point price-to-earnings (PE) 
ratio, the lower the subsequent 10-
year returns, and vice versa. Such 
analysis is at the core of the concern 
around the currently elevated level of 
the CAPE. However, as my colleague 
John Southall has discussed at length 
across a range of blogs, the optics of 
this chart do not stand up to scrutiny.
First, the apparent relationship rests 
on significant overlapping of data: 
the actual number of independent 
datapoints is relatively small. Second, 
what counts as cheap or expensive 
changes through time: a 5% earnings 
yield would have been expensive in 
1995, and thus implied low returns, 
but now looks average. And third, 
such analysis rests on a finite pool of 
data. One hundred years may sound 
like a reassuringly large dataset, 
but John argues persuasively that 
it’s a pool of datapoints which can 
inflate our confidence in the ability 
to predict equity returns, which are 
an inherently volatile asset class. 
Beyond those theoretical pushbacks, 
it is also important to recognise 
that recent earnings growth may 
reflect a structural shift rather 
than a typical point in the cycle.
Across the 150-year Shiller data set, 
the real earnings compound annual 
growth rate (CAGR) has been 2.2%. 
Over the last 10 years, US equities have 
delivered an earnings CAGR of 9.4%, 
and over the last three years that rises 
to 14%. That puts the last three years 
near the 90th percentile for three-year 
earnings growth, which is all the more 
remarkable given that it is not a period 
of recovery from recession or crisis.

Could earnings growth bring valuations 
back down?
Looking ahead, this powerful earnings 
uplift will inevitably start to bring down 
the elevated CAPE level. By way of 
example, if the US equity index level 
tracks inflation higher over the next three 
years (i.e. is flat in real terms), and US 
equities deliver the same EPS growth 
as seen over the past three years, the 
US CAPE would fall to 29x, putting the 
valuation in the 20th percentile seen 
over the last decade. Those are optimistic 
assumptions on earnings of course, but 
the simple maths does illustrate the 
power of earnings to de-rate the US 
market, even on a CAPE basis. 
Finally, given the high weighting in fast-
growing tech companies today in the 
US, it feels less appropriate to use an 
earnings base from a notably different 
index 10 years ago when computing 
valuation. Non-US indices have seen less 
transformation (though the weighting 
of tech, direct and indirect, has risen in 
all cases), and here valuation multiples 
on a CAPE basis are just unremarkable, 
though moving toward the upper end of 
historic ranges in some cases. 
In Europe, the CAPE is around 20, in 
China 18 and in Hong Kong less than 
10x.⁵ Yes, US equities are about 63% 
of global market cap,6 but as we often 
highlight, our own approach to investing 
continues to highlight diversification, 
and the opportunities offered by unloved 
assets. Elevated US valuations are 
therefore best viewed as a regional 
phenomenon rather than a definitive 
verdict on global equity markets. A high 
US CAPE today is not a stick to beat 
global equities with.

The role of commodities 
in strategic asset 
allocation
Commodities can provide diversification and inflation 
protection, but their long-term return potential remains 
debated. We examine the evidence for a commodity risk 
premium and what it might mean for strategic asset allocation. 
John Southall, Head of Strategic Research
Commodities occupy an unusual 
place in strategic asset allocation. 
Determining their strategic role requires 
balancing their diversification and 
inflation-hedging benefits against 
how much return investors should 
expect to be rewarded for holding 
them relative to other assets. 
A helpful question is whether 
commodity returns have historically 
been higher than we’d expect from their 
exposure to traditional market risk. In 
academic language, that excess return 
is often described as Jensen’s Alpha. 
I draw on two studies: Levine et 
al. (1877–2015 sample period), 
who used an equal-weighted 
index of 29 commodity futures, 
and Doeswijk et al. (1960–2017), 
who constructed returns for the 
global investable market portfolio⁷ 
(“GMP”) including commodities. 
The return puzzle 
The realised arithmetic risk premium in 
the Levine study was 4.8% per annum, 
versus an estimated 1.7% I calculated 
we’d expect from beta alone.8 That 
leaves a Jensen’s Alpha of around 
3.1% per annum. I calculated this has 
a “p-value” of 0.03, meaning if the true 
alpha were zero, you’d only observe this 
alpha or higher with a 3% chance. 
My equivalent calculation using the 
Doeswijk data9 produces a slightly 
larger alpha, around 3.5% per annum, 
but with a much higher p-value of 0.17 
due to the shorter sample period. 
The Levine study provides statistically 
significant evidence of positive alpha. 
However, evidence against a zero alpha 
is not the same as evidence that the 
true long-run alpha is 3.1%. The key 
question is how much weight to place 
on these historical estimates when 
forming a forward-looking assumption? 
Why might a premium exist? 
There are several theories for why 
commodities earn a positive risk 
premium. One is hedging pressure: 
producers and consumers often 
have commercial reasons to transfer 
commodity price risk, and investors 
willing to absorb that risk may earn 
compensation in return. This is 
not captured by a simple CAPM10 
framework, where expected returns are 
explained solely by exposure to broad 
market risk. Another explanation relates 
to a “convenience yield” which is the 
benefit of owning a commodity that is 
in short supply or urgently needed.11
Why be cautious? 
The obvious objection is: if expected 
excess returns are really that attractive, 
why haven’t they been competed 
away? Markets aren’t perfectly efficient 
in our view, and there are limits to 
arbitrage, but it remains healthy to be 
sceptical of large alpha opportunities. 
There’s also the problem of relevance. 
Long histories provide more 
observations. But by the time we have 
enough data to make strong statistical 
claims, the market we’re studying may 
have become more efficient. That’s a 
recurring problem in financial research. 
The fact commodities don’t generate 
cashflows also reduces our confidence 
in any estimate of their expected return. 
The theoretical case is also not 
one-sided. Storage costs and other 
factors can create a drag on returns 
for long futures investors.12 
Finally, investors may like commodities 
for their inflation-hedging properties 
but that cuts both ways because 
they should, all else equal, command 
a lower expected return. 
What does this mean for strategic 
allocations? 
A natural starting point is the GMP, 
as this is the average allocation 
held by investors. Because futures 
(the instruments financial investors 
typically use to gain exposure) have 
zero net supply, the investable weight 
of commodities is difficult to estimate. 
I assume a 2% allocation, broadly 
in line with the Doeswijk study. 
If I also assume no Jensen’s Alpha, 
that points to a very modest allocation 
(2%) if the investor has average risk 
appetite and average desire for inflation 
hedging. If I allow for a forward-
looking alpha of 1% per annum, my 
calculations13 indicate the allocation 
could rise to around 7%; with 2% 
alpha, around 13%. Those figures 
illustrate how sensitive the answer 
is to the assumed excess return.

A measured conclusion 
The strongest case for commodities 
isn’t exceptional returns. It’s that the 
empirical evidence is difficult to entirely 
dismiss, there are credible theoretical 
reasons for a risk premium, and their 
diversification and inflation properties 
can be valuable at the portfolio level. 
For a diversified multi-asset investor, 
commodities may deserve a small 
strategic role, and there is a reasonable 
case for increasing that role when 
inflation sensitivity is especially valued. 
A low-single-digit allocation may be 
a neutral starting point; something 
closer to 5% or 10% might be justified 
where investors place more weight 
on the historical and theoretical case 
for a commodity premium. Beyond 
that, the burden of proof should rise. 
Commodities are useful, but they aren’t 
exempt from the usual discipline of 
strategic asset allocation: be clear what 
risk is being taken, why it’s rewarded, 
and how it improves the portfolio as a 
whole.

Our capital market assumptions

expected returns from two 
complementary perspectives – an 
equilibrium-based model driven by 
risk considerations, and a valuation-
based model driven by fundamentals 
and market pricing. This framework 
recognises that while expected 
returns are likely to converge towards 
equilibrium over the long run, shorter-
term return expectations may deviate 
from those levels as valuations 
become stretched or depressed.

based estimates have declined across 
most asset classes, with government 
bonds being the notable exception. 
This has led to modest reductions 
in expected return assumptions.
