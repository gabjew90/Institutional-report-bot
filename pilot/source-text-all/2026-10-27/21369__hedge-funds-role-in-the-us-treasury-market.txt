Hedge funds’ role in the US Treasury market
Alternative investments
 
 Authors: Tony Petrov, Alternative Investments Strategist, UBS Switzerland AG; Karim Cherif, Head Alternative Investments, UBS Switzerland AG
• In recent years, hedge funds have become more active 
players in the Treasury market. As a result, the speed 
of market moves matters more than the level of 
yields. Their trades are largely hedged, so leverage is a 
funding risk, not a duration risk. A volatility spike can 
force them to unwind together.
• Arbitrage is driving their growing role. Heavy 
issuance, constrained dealer capacity, and demand 
for futures create price gaps that hedge funds seek 
to exploit. Their market share has almost doubled 
since early 2023. The market's shock absorbers have 
strengthened, but reliance on bank financing remains 
a vulnerability.
• Higher rates remain supportive for hedge funds. 
We favor macro, equity market neutral, and event-
driven strategies, which stand to benefit from greater 
dispersion. The same price gaps create opportunities 
for relative value and multi-strategy managers with 
strong risk management and diversified financing.

Hedge funds' Treasury footprint has grown
Hedge funds have become a major participant in the 
US Treasury market. In the cash market, Federal Reserve 
estimates show they owned roughly 8.5% of Treasuries 
outside the Fed and US government accounts in late 2025, 
up from less than 5% in early 2023 (Fig. 1). More recent 
regulatory filings, which also count futures, show that large 
hedge funds held around USD 2.4tr of long and USD 1.5tr 
of short Treasury exposure in 2Q26. That gross exposure of 
USD 3.9tr is more than twice its early 2023 level, though 
below its late 2025 peak.
Each figure measures a different aspect of hedge fund 
activity. The 8.5% share reflects estimated cash holdings 
only, whereas gross exposure adds long and short positions, 
including futures. Repo borrowing, discussed below, reflects 
the financing behind these positions rather than additional 
exposure.

These numbers sound large, but they need context. Long 
and short positions should not be added together as if they 
were independent positions. Most are two legs of the same 
hedged trades, most of the short exposure is in futures, and 
the positions are largely financed in the repo market rather 
than with investors’ capital (Fig. 2). In regulatory data, funds 
focused mainly on relative value run balance sheet leverage 
of around 9-10 times, meaning they hold USD 9-10 of gross 
assets for every dollar of investor capital and finance the rest 
largely through borrowing. This is how a Treasury footprint 
of this size can rest on a much smaller capital base.
Ten times leverage sounds high, but in relative value 
strategies, it is largely applied to offsetting positions. 
Research from the Office of Financial Research (OFR) also 
finds that changes in the positions of more highly levered 
hedge funds had a weaker relationship with Treasury yield 
movements compared to lower levered peers (OFR Working 
Paper 22-05). The risk that leverage creates is one of 
funding, rather than direction, as we set out below.

Three structural shifts have created arbitrage 
opportunities:
• Treasury supply has outgrown dealer balance 
sheets. Since 2007, Treasury debt relative to primary 
dealers’ balance sheets has increased about four times. 
Large fiscal deficits have lifted supply, while capital 
and leverage rules in the wake of the 2008 global 
financial crisis have made it more expensive for banks 
to warehouse bonds. As a result, cash bonds can trade 
cheaply against futures and swaps, a gap that hedge 
funds seek to exploit.
• Traditional buyers have become less dominant.
Foreign investors held about half of all Treasuries in 2009 
but only about a third by 2025, while the Fed reduced its 
holdings through quantitative tightening. Hedge funds 
have become increasingly important intermediary holders 
of Treasury securities.
• Asset managers increasingly use futures. Real money 
investors can obtain duration efficiently through synthetic 
exposures such as Treasury futures and interest rate 
swaps. Hedge funds often take the other side by selling 
futures, buying the cheaper cash bond, and financing it in 
repo, which is also known as the cash-futures basis trade 
(see Fig. 3 below). Fed research describes hedge funds 
in this role as effectively holding Treasuries on behalf of 
asset managers. The resulting supply-demand imbalance 
has generated arbitrage opportunities, which hedge 
fund managers actively pursue. Although the underlying 
price gap is small, leverage can make the economics 
meaningful. Rough estimates suggest that historically, 
the trade generated around 2-15% annualized gross 
return on equity (ROE), depending on leverage and trade 
economics (BofA estimates).
How the basis trade works
1. An asset manager wants duration exposure and buys 
Treasury futures, which require little capital.
2. A hedge fund sells those futures and buys a Treasury 
bond that can be delivered into the contract, locking 
in the small price difference between the two as prices 
converge.
3. The bond is financed in the repo market and posted as 
collateral. Because the price gap is small, positions are 
typically run with high leverage.

The trade earns a premium for converting cash bonds into 
the futures exposure that asset managers want. Its profit 
depends on the gap between cash and futures prices—
driven by rate volatility, the shape of the yield curve, Treasury 
supply, futures demand, and repo conditions—rather than 
simply on the direction of yields.
The curve shape matters in two ways. It sets the carry—the 
bond’s coupon income less its repo financing cost—which is 
positive when the curve slopes upward and negative when

it is inverted, and it helps determine which bond is cheapest 
to deliver into the contract.
The basis trade reached about USD 830bn by September 
2025 on Fed estimates, roughly twice its early 2020 peak. 
Managers have also expanded into other relative value 
trades, notably swap spreads. The Fed's May 2026 financial 
stability report noted that the decline in basis exposure had 
been largely offset by growth elsewhere in relative value, 
leaving overall hedge fund leverage elevated.
More recent CFTC data also show that, after years of steady 
growth, asset managers’ net long and leveraged funds’ 
net short positions in Treasury futures have peaked and 
started to ease (Fig. 4). Leveraged funds’ net short position, 
a broader but more timely gauge of the trade than the Fed 
estimate, has fallen from around USD 1.16tr at the start of 
2026 to about USD 0.84tr by mid-October, more consistent 
with some moderation in basis trade positioning than with 
a disorderly forced unwind.
Recent research from the Dallas Fed (October 2026) 
suggests that changing trade economics help explain this 
trend. As longer-term yields have risen toward the 6% 
reference used in Treasury futures conversion factors, the 
bonds eligible for delivery have become more similar in 
value, reducing the value of the embedded delivery option. 
This has reduced the premium asset managers receive for 
obtaining duration through futures rather than cash bonds. 
Meanwhile, swap spread trades now offer hedge funds 
comparable risk-adjusted opportunities, encouraging some 
capital to move away from the basis trade.

However, falling futures positions do not necessarily imply 
a comparable reduction in risk. A significant share of the 
decline has been in two-year futures contracts, which carry 
less interest rate risk per dollar of notional. Swap spread 
positions, meanwhile, can carry more risk for the same 
notional exposure, meaning even a modest reallocation 
can sharply reduce reported futures positions. In our view, 
capital is thus rotating toward the best relative value 
opportunities, but the overall Treasury footprint, and the 
leverage supporting it, remains large enough to matter for 
market functioning. The question is whether that creates 
fragility as yields rise.
Treasury exposure varies by hedge fund strategy
Treasury market dynamics affect all strategies indirectly, but 
direct exposure varies significantly:
• Most affected (largest direct exposure): Fixed income 
relative value (FIRV) funds and multi-strategy platforms. 
They typically run the basis, swap spread, and maturity-
matched trades (offsetting positions of similar duration, 
such as on-the-run versus off-the-run Treasuries) that 
make up almost two-thirds of the exposure (Fig. 7), as 
well as curve trades, financed in repo. Multi-strategy 
and relative value funds have historically accounted for 
close to 60% of hedge funds’ long Treasury exposure 
(32% and 26% on average over 2013-2020, according to 
Alquist and Yamarthy, 2022). This is where funding and 
liquidity risk is concentrated.
• Less affected (direct, but smaller exposure): Global 
macro and managed futures/commodity trading advisors 
(CTAs). Macro funds accounted for around 18% of long 
Treasury exposure and managed futures at around 4%. 
Macro funds hold curve and directional rate positions, 
so a disorderly move affects them through market 
pricing, although rate volatility can also be a source of 
opportunity.
• Least affected (mostly indirect): Equity long/short, 
event-driven, and credit strategies. Equity funds 
accounted for only around 2% of long Treasury exposure. 
For these strategies, the main effect comes through 
broader market channels, including discount rates, 
financing costs, credit spreads, collateral values, and 
forced deleveraging during periods of stress.
A related concern is that rising yields erode the value of 
Treasury collateral. For most hedge funds, the effect is small. 
Margin is typically posted in cash or short-dated bills, whose 
short maturity means that their market value changes little 
when yields move. Higher yields also lift the interest earned 
on cash collateral and short sale proceeds, partly offset by 
higher financing costs. Where longer-dated Treasuries are 
financed in repo, they are usually one leg of a hedged trade, 
so falling bond prices are offset by gains on the futures 
or swap leg. The risk is that margin calls and gains on 
offsetting positions can settle at different times, creating 
liquidity strains even in a hedged trade.

Why the level of yields is not the main risk
Higher yields alone are not the main risk, for the reasons 
outlined below:
• The strategy is levered but leverage is a funding 
risk, not a duration risk. Relative value trades capture 
small price gaps, so they are typically run with substantial 
leverage. Balance sheet leverage—gross assets relative to 
investor capital—stood at around 10 times for relative 
value funds and eight times for macro funds at end-2025 
(net asset-weighted), and considerably higher when 
weighted by fund size (Fig. 5). Hedge funds’ repo 
borrowing has grown to around USD 3.4tr in 2Q26 (Fig. 
6).
With capital of only around a tenth of gross assets, even 
a small rise in margins or repo haircuts can force funds 
to cut positions quickly. Around 90% of Treasury exposure 
is concentrated in the 50 largest funds, a pattern already 
visible before 2020. That concentration matters. But gross 
leverage can overstate directional risk, as the same ratio 
can represent very different risks depending on whether 
exposure comes from repo-financed bonds, futures or 
swaps, and on how far longs and shorts offset. The leverage 
mainly magnifies moves in the spread between the cash 
bond and its hedge, rather than the outright direction of 
yields.

• Positions largely offset each other and help the 
market function. For instance, in a basis trade, the 
long cash bond is paired with a short futures position 
of similar duration. The Fed's decomposition of hedge 
funds' USD 2.4tr of long Treasury exposure in September 
2025, the latest available, makes the point clearly (Fig. 
7): around 35% is in basis trades, 13% in swap spreads, 
17% in maturity-matched trades, and a further 23% in 
yield curve positions. Around 10% is held as a liquidity 
buffer and only about 3% is classified as long-only 
exposure. These strategies also provide an important 
market function by arbitraging price gaps and absorbing 
supply that dealers are less willing to warehouse. Rising 
gross positions are partly a byproduct of heavier issuance. 
Absorbing more supply efficiently requires more positions 
linking cash, futures, and swap markets—a sign of 
liquidity provision rather than one directional leverage.

• Directional risk is low—a liquidity squeeze is the 
real issue. There is no single yield level at which the 
system breaks. Past stress episodes have come with spikes 
in rate volatility at very different yield levels (Fig. 8), and 
with the yield curve both inverted and upward-sloping. 
When 10-year yields reached their highest level since 
2002 in October, volatility stayed well below those peaks. 
For a hedged book, the real damage comes when the 
spread being arbitraged widens sharply at the same time 
as funding becomes more expensive or less available. 
Higher futures margins, a spike in repo rates, or forced 
selling that overwhelms dealer balance sheets can turn 
a mark-to-market loss into a liquidity problem. If many 
funds reduce risk together, those flows can amplify the 
move—as in March 2020 and for swap spread positions, 
April 2025. The basis trade has a natural anchor: futures 
and the deliverable bond converge at expiry, usually 
within three months, so a fund that can hold its position 
is less exposed to interim price swings, though not to 
funding costs or losses. Swap spread trades lack that 
anchor, which helps explain why it was swap spread 
positions that unwound in April 2025. The key question 
is therefore whether the adjustment becomes disorderly, 
rather than whether yields rise.

Can a liquidity crisis occur?
Our short answer is yes. A liquidity squeeze cannot be 
ruled out, particularly if a sharp market move combines 
wider relative value spreads, rising margins and tighter 
funding. Events in March 2020, which led the Fed to buy 
around USD 1.6tr of Treasuries by June, showed how quickly 
these pressures can reinforce one another, although we 
caution that this is not a direct precedent for today, given 
hedge funds’ larger role and subsequent changes in market 
structure. The UK gilt market offered a similar lesson in 
September 2022. A 160bps jump in 30-year yields within 
days triggered margin calls on pension funds’ leveraged 
liability-driven investment (LDI) strategies, and forced selling 
fed the move until the Bank of England intervened. It was 
the speed of the move, not the level of yields, that did the 
damage. More recently, the April 2025 sell-off saw some 
crowded relative value positions unwind without developing 
into a broader funding crisis. A rate shock thus does not 
automatically result in a liquidity event.
Market structure is also becoming more resilient. Mandatory 
central clearing should allow multilateral netting, where 
offsetting Treasury trades across counterparties are netted 
against each other, reducing gross settlement flows and 
dealer balance sheet usage while improving post-trade 
transparency. It does not eliminate liquidity risk; margin 
calls can themselves become pro-cyclical. However, because 
netted trades take up less space in dealer balance sheets, 
dealers have more room to intermediate when clients need 
to sell, which should increase the market’s capacity to 
absorb stress.
Policymakers also have more tools available than in 2020. 
The Fed now operates a standing repo facility, bank 
leverage rules have been recalibrated to support Treasury 
intermediation, and Treasury buybacks provide an additional 
mechanism to support market liquidity. Hedge funds cannot 
use the Fed’s facility, however. It serves primary dealers 
and banks, so whether that liquidity reaches hedge funds 
in a stress depends on their lenders’ willingness to keep 
repo lines open. These safeguards cannot prevent every 
dislocation, but they should make a temporary shock easier 
to contain.

What we monitor
Greater resilience should not be mistaken for an absence of 
risk. Treasury funding remains orderly, but the next liquidity 
shock could originate elsewhere—for example, in crowded, 
leveraged positions in other markets—and spill over into 
broader funding markets.
Policy is another variable to watch. Regulators have 
repeatedly flagged leverage outside the banking system, 
and Fed staff have examined minimum haircuts on repo 
financing. Blunt limits on leverage would reduce tail risk, 
but at a cost: a less liquid Treasury market, larger price 
swings around auctions, and potentially higher Treasury 
yields relative to swap rates. So far, policymakers have 
relied mainly on central clearing, stronger backstops, and 
bank capital relief rather than direct limits on hedge fund 
leverage.
Within Treasuries, we focus less on any particular yield level 
and more on how the market absorbs market moves. A 
rapid rise in yields alongside wider basis or swap spreads, 
higher margins, tighter repo, or weaker market depth would 
signal that an orderly repricing could become a liquidity 
event. Because data on hedge fund positions arrive with 
a lag, we pay close attention to real-time dollar funding 
indicators: bank reserves, short-term repo rates relative to 
the Fed’s policy rates, use of the Fed’s standing repo facility, 
and the cross-currency basis. Banks are more likely to cut 
repo lines to hedge funds when reserves become scarce. 
We also monitor where leverage is migrating, particularly 
whether declining basis trade positions are being offset by 
growing exposure to swap spreads and other relative value 
trades.
Implications for investors
Higher yield environments have historically been supportive 
of a broad range of hedge fund strategies (see report: 
“Higher rates are a tailwind for hedge funds,” published 
on 23 September 2026). Elevated rates tend to increase 
dispersion both across and within asset classes, creating 
a richer opportunity set for active managers to generate 
alpha. In our view, current Treasury market dynamics 
should remain supportive of this backdrop over the coming 
quarters.
At the same time, the analysis presented above suggests 
that the principal risk is not the level of yields itself, 
but the speed of the market move and the potential for 
a disorderly adjustment to trigger funding and liquidity 
stresses. This distinction is particularly relevant for strategies 
with a large Treasury market footprint, notably fixed 
income relative value (FIRV) and multi-strategy funds. 
Elevated Treasury issuance, greater policy uncertainty, and 
more volatile auction dynamics are creating attractive 
relative value opportunities for well-capitalized managers. 
However, tighter funding conditions, regulatory changes 
affecting leverage, or episodes of market microstructure 
dysfunction could temporarily impair performance and 
therefore warrant continued monitoring.
Importantly, market structure has become more resilient 
since 2020. Expanding central clearing, the Fed’s standing 
repo facility, Treasury buybacks, and other safeguards should 
reduce the likelihood of a broad-based deleveraging episode 
similar to those experienced in previous periods of stress. 
While liquidity squeezes remain possible during disorderly 
Treasury market adjustments, the system today appears 
better equipped to absorb them.
Against this backdrop, we remain constructive on the 
broader hedge fund universe, with a particular preference 
for macro, equity market neutral, and event-driven 
strategies, which stand to benefit from higher dispersion 
while carrying more limited direct exposure to Treasury 
funding dynamics. We also maintain a positive view on 
multi-strategy and relative value managers, provided they 
demonstrate strong risk management, diversified financing 
sources, and the ability to withstand periods of market 
stress.
More broadly, we believe the most robust portfolio 
construction approach remains a diversified allocation 
across complementary strategies and alpha sources. Such 
diversification helps reduce reliance on any single market 
environment, strategy, or manager. As always, manager 
selection remains critical. For relative value managers in 
particular, funding resilience may prove just as important as 
investment skill in determining long-term outcomes.
Finally, investors should recognize that alternative strategies 
are often less liquid, may involve extended lock-up periods, 
and can carry additional risks related to transparency, 
leverage, and volatility. As such, they are best considered as 
part of a well-diversified portfolio and within the context of 
an investor's broader risk objectives.
