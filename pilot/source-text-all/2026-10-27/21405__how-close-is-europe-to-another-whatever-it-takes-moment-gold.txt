 
How Close Is Europe To Another "Whatever It 
Takes" Moment? Goldman Builds A Fragmentation 
Alarm To Find Out 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 21, 2026 - 03:50 PM 
It's starting to feel like 2011 again in European bond markets, only this time the epicenter isn't 
Athens or Rome, it's Paris. 
On Tuesday the French-German 10-year spread broke back above 150bps, with Bloomberg 
flagging the Oct. 2 high of 159.2bps as the level to watch. By the close, the OAT-Bund spread 
was 5bps wider at 153bps after touching 157bps intraday, while French 10Y yields sat at 4.92%, 
a few bps from the 2002 highs hit earlier this month. This came a day after French 10Y yields 
surged 16bps, the biggest one-day jump since March 2025, as the country put riot-hit cities 
under curfew after the interior minister admitted police had "lost control" (Oct 19). Goldman's 
rates desk summed up Monday's damage tersely: OATs "continued to underperform (10y 
+13bp) following weekend protests."

Which brings us to the only question that matters for anyone short OATs (or long the euro): at 
what point does the ECB step in? Because as everyone who lived through 2012 knows, the end 
of a euro debt crisis isn't decided by spreads, it's decided by Frankfurt. 
Conveniently, Goldman economists Sven Jari Stehn and Giovanni Pierdomenico have just 
published a note titled "Measuring Euro Area Fragmentation Risk" (available to pro subs), in 
which they build a brand new index designed to do exactly one thing: predict when the ECB will 
be forced to intervene in bond markets. Their conclusion: 
The index suggests that fragmentation risk remains limited despite the recent widening in 
spreads. (emphasis ours) 
 

 
 
Translation: the euro isn't breaking up yet, and the ECB isn't coming to save France yet either. 
But the details of whyGoldman reaches that conclusion, and what would change it, are far more 
interesting than the headline. 
Below we walk through why spreads alone are a lousy guide, what actually moved before every 
past ECB rescue, how Goldman's new index is built, and why it is so calm right now. 
Spreads Lie (Just Ask Italy In 2018) 
The obvious place to start is sovereign spreads, and Goldman notes that spreads above roughly 
250bp have "often been associated with ECB bond market intervention, including in 2010-2012, 
2020 and 2022." The problem is that the same levels have also cried wolf. 
Spread levels alone can also give "false positives" for expected ECB interventions. Italian 
spreads in 2018, for example, notably exceeded these levels without prompting ECB 
intervention. (emphasis ours) 
 

 
 
Put differently, a spread level by itself tells you nothing about whether Lagarde picks up the 
phone. Goldman's look at the run-up to seven major interventions, from the first SMP in May 
2010 through "whatever it takes," OMT, PEPP and the TPI launch in July 2022, found that the 
GDP-weighted 10Y spread averaged about 250bp at the time of intervention, about 300bp 
debt-weighted and 350bp+ in the worst-hit countries, but "individual episodes show significant 
variation." That's economist for "it depends." 
It's worth noting where we are on that chart: the GDP-weighted spread (blue line) is only now 
jerking higher off multi-year lows, and the 90th percentile spread (red, which is mostly France at 
this point) is around 150bp, still well below the roughly 250bp seen when the TPI was launched 
in 2022, let alone the 2011-2012 hellscape. Then again, France at 150bp over Bunds is a very 
different beast than Portugal at 150bp: one is a rounding error, the other is the euro area's 
second-largest economy and its biggest sovereign debt market. 
What Actually Moves Before The ECB Blinks: Volatility And 
Dispersion 
So what does work? Going back to ECB communications, from Draghi's 2012 speeches to 
Schnabel's June 2022 description of fragmentation as "a sudden break in the relationship 
between sovereign yields and fundamentals," Goldman lists a broader set of signals: the speed, 
 

 
dispersion and volatility of spread moves, plus liquidity, bank stocks and the euro. When they 
line these up around past interventions, two stand out: 
We find the clearest pattern for spread dispersion and volatility around ECB interventions. 
Dispersion and volatility usually spike notably, capturing disorderly market movements. The 
current levels of volatility and dispersion, however, are low compared to past ECB intervention 
points. (emphasis ours) 
 
Look at the blue diamonds: current 10Y yield dispersion sits at roughly 0.02 standard deviations 
versus a mean of about 0.35-0.4 at the moment of past interventions, and volatility is around 
0.03 versus about 0.16. In other words, French yields are going up steadily and in an orderly 
fashion, which is exactly the kind of selloff the ECB is designed to ignore. 
Goldman then formalized this with logit models scored on precision-recall (which punishes false 
alarms, sensible when actual interventions are rare). Volatility won hands down, led by the 
rolling standard deviation of the 10Y spread; spread levels came second (with 10Y spreads over 
Bunds beating spreads over swaps), and dispersion came third. Bank spreads and the euro, 
despite all the hand-wringing about EURUSD every time OATs sell off, ranked near the bottom. 
 

 
 
Introducing The Goldman Fragmentation Index 
The economists then combined seven of the best performers: (1) 10Y spread volatility, (2) the 
90th percentile 10Y spread, (3) the change in the 10Y spread versus its trailing one-year 
median, (4) and (5) cross-country dispersion of 10Y and 2Y yields, (6) bid-ask spreads and (7) 
senior bank spreads over Bunds, each weighted by its predictive accuracy. Spread volatility gets 
the biggest weight (roughly a quarter of the index), followed by spread levels and dispersion.  
The result: 
Our index captures the main episodes of sovereign stress well. In particular, the ECB has 
usually intervened when our index moved 1.5–2 standard deviations above its average. Our 
measure also avoids the false positive of the Italian spread widening of 2018, as other indicators 
(such as volatility and dispersion) moved only little. (emphasis ours) 
 

 
 
And here's the punchline: with French spreads at levels last seen during the euro crisis, 
Goldman's fragmentation index is hovering right around zero, i.e., its long-run average, and well 
below the 1.5-2 standard deviation shaded "intervention zone." 
Goldman's decomposition shows just how far we are from the danger zone: in prior 
interventions the index averaged about 2.7 standard deviations (and about 2.6 just before the 
TPI was announced), while as of Oct. 2 it was a hair above zero. The index has risen roughly 
half a standard deviation since end-2025, "driven mainly by increased volatility and spread 
widening," with the spread contribution (dark blue) climbing sharply since September. 
 

 
 
For those wondering whether this is just Goldman reinventing the ECB's own stress gauges, the 
bank compared its index to the ECB's CISS and Sovereign CISS. The SovCISS looks broadly 
similar, but Goldman's version had the best hit rate for predicting interventions, ahead of plain 
spreads and the SovCISS, although the economists concede "the difference in forecasting 
power is quantitatively limited." Some honesty from the sell side: always refreshing. 
 
 

 
The Catch: Disorderly vs. "Deserved" 
There is, however, a giant asterisk, and Goldman deserves credit for putting it front and center: 
Our analysis also shows that identifying the degree of fragmentation risk that would prompt ECB 
bond-market intervention is difficult. It is much easier to capture disorderly market dynamics 
than it is to assess whether those reflect fundamental factors. Doing so requires broader 
judgements about the macroeconomic, fiscal and political backdrop. (emphasis ours) 
That is the entire French problem in two sentences. The TPI can only be used against 
"unwarranted, disorderly market dynamics," and eligibility hinges on, among other things, 
compliance with the EU fiscal framework and sustainable public debt. A country with a 5%+ 
deficit, a government that can barely pass a budget, a fresh downgrade (Sep 18), and riots in 
the streets is going to have a hard time arguing its spread blowout is "unwarranted." As regular 
readers will recall, we flagged France as the likeliest trigger for the next euro debt scare back in 
August ("France's €107 Billion Deficit Shock: The Next Euro Debt Crisis?", Aug 5), and by Oct 1 
European bond markets were crashing and French CDS was exploding, so we'll allow ourselves 
a modest victory lap.

Others are reaching the same place. Bloomberg's Markets Live noted on Tuesday that French 
BBB corporate spreads, which traded in line with German BBBs just a month ago, have since 
gapped wider, and that "it's debatable how far French government bonds can underperform 
other sovereigns before the ECB steps in." Meanwhile, the market is not exactly pricing a dovish 
rescue: money markets still see 23bps of ECB tightening by year-end and 71bps by end-2027. 
Hiking rates into a sovereign spread blowout: what could possibly go wrong? 
And for what it's worth, the contagion so far is contained: Italy's BTP-Bund spread was only 1bp 
wider at 112bp on Tuesday after touching 119bp, and Goldman's FX desk noted that EUR 
option volumes, a key gauge of portfolio hedging, are more than 30% off their highs. This is a 
French crisis, not (yet) a euro crisis, which is precisely why Goldman's dispersion and volatility 
gauges are so quiet. 
 

 
Bottom Line 
Goldman's conclusion is measured: 
The new fragmentation-risk index helps capture bond-market tensions and clarify the relative 
importance of different indicators. It currently points to limited fragmentation risk, although we 
will continue to monitor it for signs of rising tensions. (emphasis ours) 
Our take: the index is a clever piece of work, but read it for what it is, a measure of panic, not of 
solvency. By construction it only fires once volatility and dispersion explode, i.e., once the slow 
grind turns into a stampede, and the ECB has historically shown up only after that happens. A 
steady, orderly march wider in French spreads is in some ways the worst-case scenario: it never 
trips the alarm, never qualifies as "unwarranted," and just keeps ratcheting up France's interest 
bill until the arithmetic breaks. The index won't flash red until it's too late, and by then, as in 
2012, the only thing that will matter is whether Frankfurt is willing to do "whatever it takes" for a 
country that refuses to do anything at all. Low fragmentation risk is not the same as low risk. 
The next test comes quickly: Lagarde speaks in Wiesbaden on Wednesday, and if the 
OAT-Bund spread takes out the 159bp Oct. 2 high, we expect the "ECB monetization" chatter to 
get very loud, very fast.
