Investment Research
The invisible correction
Weekly - Regional View Italian
Matteo Ramenghi, Chief Investment Officer, UBS WM Italy, UBS Europe SE, Succursale Italia
In the coming weeks, attention will remain focused on politics and 
geopolitical tensions.
The US midterm elections will be held on 3 November. The most likely 
outcome is a divided government, with the Republican administration 
potentially losing control of at least one chamber of Congress.
The president would still retain broad powers over tariffs, foreign policy, and 
regulation. But a Democratic-led Congress could scale back spending cuts, 
pushing the already elevated deficit—currently above 6%—even higher.
On the energy front, oil flows returned to pre-conflict levels in September. 
In October, however, Iranian attacks in the Strait of Hormuz and the conflict 
between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis created renewed tensions, sending 
Brent crude back to USD 110 per barrel.
So far, higher oil prices have had a limited impact on the global economy. 
Compared with 2022, major economies are better able to absorb energy 
shocks. This resilience also reflects the stimulus generated by investment 
in artificial intelligence, defense, and infrastructure programs such as 
Germany’s.
However, these investments require substantial financing, and increased 
bond issuance has pushed yields higher. Tensions in the Middle East are also 
affecting monetary policy: Inflation driven by higher oil prices has prompted 
central banks to raise interest rates.
Indeed, the bond market has undergone a sharp correction in recent months. 
In early October, the yield on the 10-year US Treasury reached 5.4%, its 
highest level since 2002, before easing to around 5.1%. The yield on the 10-
year German Bund, meanwhile, touched 3.5%.
In Europe, the situation is further complicated by France’s fiscal and political 
tensions, which are spilling over into other countries, including Italy.
Despite the volatility that may continue to characterize the bond market in the 
coming months, we find bonds Attractive at current yields. We also expect 
yields to decline moderately over the next year.
Pinpointing the peak in yields is nearly impossible, but we remain positive on 
investment grade bonds and favor short- and intermediate-term maturities.

Yet rising energy prices and bond yields leave one question unanswered: Why 
haven’t stocks corrected?
In theory, the mechanism is straightforward: When yields rise, the rates used 
to discount companies’ future cash flows also increase, all else being equal, 
reducing their present value. At the same time, bonds become relatively more 
attractive.
At times, this mechanism has led to painful corrections: 22% in 1966, 36% in 
1968-70, 48% in 1973–74, 34% in 1987, and, more recently, 25% in 2022 
for the S&P 500.
By contrast, since the start of the year, the S&P 500 and global equities have 
risen 11%, seemingly unaffected by higher discount rates.
The answer lies in earnings: Earnings estimates have risen faster than stock 
prices. For example, the S&P 500 is trading at roughly 19 times forward 
earnings, compared with about 23 times at the end of last year. In other 
words, the correction has already taken place in valuation multiples without 
translating into lower share prices.
Artificial intelligence remains the main driver of this growth. We estimate 
that AI-related capital expenditures will reach USD 900 billion this year. The 
semiconductor sector should account for roughly half of the increase in third-
quarter earnings. Outside the technology sector, we also expect solid results 
from companies in cyclical industries.
As the third-quarter earnings season gets underway, we forecast S&P 500 
earnings per share growth of 28% from a year earlier. In the euro area, 
we expect earnings to rise 15% this year, supported by increased defense 
spending and Germany’s infrastructure plan.
However, this invisible correction could eventually show up in prices if 
earnings are no longer strong enough to support them. Each earnings season 
is therefore a crucial test.
In conclusion, we maintain a constructive view on equities, but investors 
should avoid excessive concentration in the market’s most crowded areas, 
especially now that roughly 10 technology stocks account for more than 40% 
of the US equity market.
Beyond artificial intelligence, we favor the themes of electrification and 
longevity, along with the financials, industrials, utilities, health care, and 
consumer discretionary sectors. Geographic diversification also remains 
important, including exposure to the euro area and Asia.

Global asset class preferences definitions
The asset class preferences provide high-level guidance to make investment decisions. The preferences reflect the collective 
judgement of the members of the House View meeting, primarily based on assessments of expected total returns on liquid 
and commonly known indices, House View scenarios, and analyst convictions over the next 12 months.
Absolute view
Attractive: We consider this asset class to be attractive. Consider opportunities in this asset class.
Neutral: We do not expect outsized returns or losses. Hold longer-term exposure.
Unattractive: We consider this asset class to be unattractive. Consider alternative opportunities.
Note: For equities, we have a five-tier rating system with two additional preferences. When equities are included with the 
other asset classes in the three-tier rating system, we collapse “Most Attractive” with “Attractive” and “Least Attractive” 
with “Unattractive.”
Most Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the most attractive. Investors should seek opportunities to add 
exposure.
Least Attractive: We consider this asset class to be among the least attractive. Seek more favorable alternatives 
opportunities.
Relative preferences
For the multi asset view, we also include relative preferences to help investors compare between items.
Preferred: We view this item more favorably than others within its asset class.*
Neutral: We see this item similarly to others within its asset class.*
Least preferred: We view this item less favorably than others within its asset class.*
*In the case of cross-asset, compared to other asset classes.
Note: The tactical asset allocation (TAA) positioning of our different investment strategies may differ from these views due 
to factors including portfolio construction, concentration, and borrowing constraints.
