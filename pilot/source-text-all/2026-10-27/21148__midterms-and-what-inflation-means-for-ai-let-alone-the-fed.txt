Midterms and what Inflation means for AI, let alone the Fed 
• 
It doesn’t look like the Fed will hike in October despite the strong inflation data but 
how Warsh addresses the underlying outlook in the press conference is made more 
significant and problematic. The risk is the curve bear steepens on a less than 
credible delivery. 
• 
Underlying inflation shows ever so slight signs of improvement but it is so high and 
the improvement likely so slow, the Fed will have little choice but to maintain a decent 
hiking profile, in our view. This isn’t so much because hiking quickly delivers lower 
inflation – that depends more on inflation expectations- but hiking will help stabilize 
longer dated rates and avoid a disorderly tightening in financial conditions. According 
to our Granger models, inflation will come to target in 2028, and progress picks up 
early 2027 but mainly conditioned on a normalization of 1y1y expected inflation, 
followed by Fed tightening. 
• 
We show that supply inflation itself is closely correlated with profit margins. This is 
also expected to dissipate, as part of the move lower in overall inflation. It suggests 
that tech investors should hedge weaker earnings growth and buy expected inflation 
adjusted for oil. 
• 
We analyze the upcoming midterms in the context of the last 5 midterms. There have 
been a couple of instances where the Republican President has lost overall control of 
Congress. Polls are suggesting the same this time with the House almost certainly 
lost and the Senate leaning the same way. 
• 
We don’t think there is too much to take lessons from as circumstances are different 
but the risk is for equities to remain on a back foot and rates to move higher on 
concerns for fiscal stimulus especially if there is a Democrat clean sweep and Trump 
moves to “make a deal”. This makes it even more important for the Fed to retain a 
strong tightening bias, in our view, to contain the back end. 
• 
Although the Treasury questionnaire wasn’t explicit around Treasury issuance 
changes, we suspect this is coming in the next one! 
• 
Trades: we remain bearish on 5y5y rate, expecting the Fed to hike at least as much 
as the forwards with the belly pressured on slow to decline inflation. The long end 
MIGHT be trying to stabilize but it’s too soon to be too constructive until we see more 
evidence that expected inflation is subsiding. That will involve not just any oil fix but 
also better clarity on the new political make-up.

Inflation and the Fed 
October Funds are pricing less than a 20 percent chance of a second Fed hike. Late 
September it was pricing around an 80 percent chance. If it wasn’t for the dovish Fed 
speak (Williams, Jefferson) plus Warsh’s self-declared guidance abstinence, given the 
disappointing September inflation data plus Warsh’s war on “underlying” inflation, there 
really shouldn’t be any reason not to hike other than a Trump pre-election payback. 
More importantly, if Warsh sticks to his other self-declared learn-from-the-markets, then if 
the Fed passes on an October hike, the press conference will be doubly important in 
terms of driving subsequent market reaction. Strictly, Warsh could stay “mum” i.e. no 
guidance but the consequence we think would be very negative for rates and the curve, 
interpreted as a (renewed) reluctance to hike despite poor inflation data. 
Perhaps more credible would be a hawkish press conference that dances around the 
impelling need to increase the policy rate and bring down underlying inflation despite 
somehow finding a reason to skip this meeting. Perhaps the logic here might be 
embedded in some sense that: the effects of the last tightening are working through; 
there are reasons to be optimistic; and don’t want to risk a slower economy etc.. 
There was a Bloomberg article today that picked up on the idea that underlying inflation 
on Warsh’s metric is improving. As our readers will appreciate we have been monitoring 
Warsh’s “framework” for a while, and the Bloomberg article isn’t exactly wrong but misses 
the main points. First in Warsh’s strict world of the majority of PCE sectors running above 
3 percent there has been barely any improvement in the recent data although since the 
March peak of 119 sectors, there are now “only” 113 sectors running above 3 percent, 
the same in September as August (we use 219 PCE subsectors, slightly more than 
Warsh). As for sectors running below 2 percent, these have increased from a low of 71 in 
April to 78 in September. This is decidedly better than Covid peaks of around 163 and 42 
respectively but nothing like typical more normal averages of plus or minus 100, 
consistent with “price stability”. 
Trimmed mean measures of inflation really though are simplistic approaches to more 
substantive measures of trend inflation that extract shocks from the demand and supply 
side. Using the San Francisco Fed’s approach, we calculate wholistic measures of trend 
inflation and the associated demand and supply shocks. Here, underlying trend is around 
3.2 percent and 0.25 percent month on month (3m average). This uses a cumulative 
regression that overweights more recent observations through the sample rather than a 
simple log linear regression that equal weights observations. It is therefore more 
consistent with Warsh’s view of inflation than say Powell’s or Waller’s as it necessarily 
downplays the size of current demand and supply shocks – we are after all fitting the 
models to the same data. 
On our informed-Warsh methodology underlying inflation has improved ever so slightly 
but is still elevated. The demand shock is weak having spiked in q2, but the supply shock 
is still somewhat elevated. We look across three measures of inflation, the headline, the 
core ex energy and core ex energy ex tech. Note for the headline the oil shock will show 
up in the shocks of the headline not the trend.

improving fine, but it hardly represents a return to target.

Inflation and AI 
Supply shock inflation dissipates over time without Fed tightening, which is intuitive. What 
may be less intuitive is that supply shock inflation is well correlated with profit margins. To 
some extent we know this in that about half of SPX earnings are related to the AI build 
out and that tech itself is a big part of inflation, adding about 30+ bps on the core ex 
energy measure vs core ex energy ex tech on the supply side only. Ie the demand shock 
and trend measures are similar with or without tech. 
To the extent that supply inflation anyway dissipates with or without Fed tightening, there 
is a strong case that profit margin growth will slow sharply as inflation overall slows. To 
the extent that investors are confident that Warsh will succeed in bringing down inflation, 
there should be a caution around ongoing earnings growth via a view that supply inflation 
is likely to dissipate. 
The charts below show the Powell and Warsh measures for supply and demand inflation 
on the core ex energy ex tech measures versus the yoy growth in profit margin from the 
unit non-financial sector GDP. Note during Covid demand inflation seemed to be the 
initial and more significant driver of margin but then supply inflation “took over” and 
sustained those margins. More recently, supply inflation is clearly the driver of margin. It 
would make sense for tech investors to hedge the earnings outlook by being long inflation 
expectations i.e expecting 1y1y inflation to fall faster than forwards but adjusting for 
energy!

Midterm Risks 
The upcoming midterms look set to deliver a pretty comprehensive defeat for the 
Republican party in the House and, also quite likely albeit much closer, a defeat in the 
Senate. The Republicans would thus lose both chambers. 
Based on Real Clear Politics analysis of the polls, the House is already well lost with the 
Democrats comfortably ahead in key districts so that even ignoring the e 22 toss-up seats 
(of which 5 lean Democrat) they already likely have a majority. The Senate is neck and 
neck on the no toss up seats up for election. However, there are 8 toss ups and all but 
one lean Democrat with 3 decidedly so. The other seats are within the polling margin of 
error but even with one seat that polls Republican (Iowa) it is barely so. 
We think investors should presume a Democrat clean sweep. The question is to what 
extent this matters for markets. 
Broadly speaking there are two sets of issues. The first is whether the outlook for fiscal 
policy materially changes. The status quo, of the Big Bill, might be the default i.e. 
diminishing stimulus turns into (delayed forward) fiscal tightening, starting slowly from FY 
2028 (presumably the timing is by design with the next election). The risk is that since the 
Big Bill, there is a push to increase defense spending so that whilst we might not see 
more tax cuts, we might well see more spending if the Republicans were to retain both 
chambers. Ironically though, even if they were to lose both chambers, Trump’s penchant 
for “making deals” risks an even worse outcome from the fiscal rectitude perspective 
whereby as a compromise for increasing defense spending, the President might be 
willing to accede to a dilution in Medicaid cuts, which is high up on the Democrat list of

The tables below show the history of midterms and market moves around the elections. 
Clearly one has to take the historic empiricism with a pinch of salt, but it is still worth 
noting a couple of common traits. 
We consider percentage changes in the SPX and the dollar index 4 weeks before and 
after the midterms going back to 2006. We consider changes in bps for 2s, 5s and 30s. 
There are five midterms of which two saw a Republican President lose control of 
Congress. Bush 2 in 2006 (both Senate and House flipped) and Trump 1 where the 
House flipped. 
In the case of SPX under Bush 2, SPX performed well in the run into the election and 
subsequently. In the case of Trump 1 it was though the reverse, with sharp drawdowns 
before and furthermore modest weakness afterwards. Of course, Bush 2 was the tail end 
of the housing boom before the GFC manifested itself in 2007/8. Trump 1 reflected 
concerns around the mature Fed tightening cycle and a slowing economy, pre-Covid.

Although less relevant today, the Republican gains under Obama 1 and 2 were both 
positive for SPX and very positive under Biden. 
In the case of the dollar: Bush 2 was quite negative but Trump 1 positive, the latter in line 
with the more immediate concern for growth. Republican flips were mixed for Obama 1 in 
terms of the election run in and out; very positive for Obama 2 both run in and out; but 
very negative on run in and out under Biden. 
Note that as of now for this cycle, the SPX is weak in line Trump 1 but the DXY is flat. 
 
As regards the bond market: 
For 2s, there were sharp rallies after the election under Bush 2 reflecting the increasing 
concerns for the housing market. Similarly, under Trump 1, reflecting the mature Fed 
tightening cycle. 5s rallied the same under Bush 2 but even more under Trump 1. 30s 
also slumped in yield under both Bush 2 and Trump 1. Bush 2 was a slight steepening 
bias, more of a parallel shift. Trump 2 was a stronger belly led rally.  
As for the Democrat loss of control, it was generally bearish under Obama 1 and 2 but 
very bullish under Biden but only reversing the pre-election sell off. This was especially 
true for Obama 1 but really reflects the aftermath of the GFC and the political consensus 
around bail out and stimulus. 
In terms of this cycle, we’ve seen sharp relief rallies going into the election. Yields are 
elevated so to a large extent we think the lack of optimism around this election is being 
discounted. 
In sum we don’t think there is too much to glean from historic empiricism around the 
midterms more than what is already obvious: the market is focused on inflation, AI and 
fiscal rectitude. The midterms are unlikely to deliver anything soothing on any of these 
fronts.
