Economics Week Ahead
 
Next week, we expect the FOMC to hold rates steady. A pause runs counter to Chair 
Warsh's inflation fighting bravado, but we remain of the view that hiking rates can do 
little to slow today's supply-side inflation problem. There will be no updated SEP or dot 
plot, and we suspect the Chair will be reluctant to offer much forward guidance, though 
a December hike is still the base case. Consumer spending should remain resilient and 
support Q3 GDP growth, with firmer income fundamentals likely sustaining household 
demand. Housing remains a weak spot, however.
In Canada, we expect the BoC to hold rates steady, though risks are tilted toward a hike. 
Like the Fed, we expect the ECB and BoJ to remain on hold next week before hiking in 
December. Stronger-than-expected momentum in Mexico points to firmer Q3 GDP 
growth, reinforcing our base case for Banxico to remain on hold through 2027.

U.S. Week Ahead
New Home Sales • Tuesday
We would be surprised if new home sales rose in September. The 
average 30-year fixed mortgage rate broke 6.7% at the start of 
the month and surpassed 7.0% by month-end, reflecting a lift 
from longer-term treasury yields. This has intensified the housing 
affordability issue for buyers who already feel crunched by rising 
fuel and food prices. Although builders have found success in the 
past using price cuts and rate buy-downs to improve affordability, 
those incentives no longer appear to be moving the needle. Builder 
sentiment sank three points in September and another three points 
in October, dipping to its lowest level since June 2012. We estimate 
that new home sales fell 7.2% in September to a 635K annual rate.

FOMC Meeting • Wednesday
The FOMC looks poised to leave the fed funds rate unchanged 
next week, although the decision feels inconsistent with Chair 
Warsh's inflation fighting rhetoric. At Jackson Hole, Chair Warsh 
laid out a hawkish message, and the FOMC delivered on that 
bravado with a 25 bps rate hike a few weeks later. Since then, we 
got a hotter-than-expected inflation reading paired with a run of 
Fed speak that signaled the October meeting likely would be a hold. 
If the Fed hiked in September and inflation has picked up since then, 
why not hike this time? Our own view is that gradual rate hikes 
make more sense when done as a catch-up to a higher neutral rate 
rather than as a tool to combat today's supply-side inflation.
We think the meeting itself probably will be on the quieter side, 
assuming the FOMC does in fact keep rates unchanged. There 
will be no updated SEP or dot plot, and the short post-meeting 
statement doesn't seem likely to change much. We suspect the 
Chair will channel his inner Greenspan and strive to mumble with 
great incoherence in the press conference. We see no reason to 
think Warsh will offer much in the way of forward guidance about 
what is coming next. If he has a lean, we suspect it will be hawkish 
given the aforementioned hot inflation data and the high likelihood 
that the Fed hikes rates in December.
One agenda item we will be listening closely for in the press 
conference is an update on the task forces. Our sense is there 
are no imminent changes coming on this front, but Chair Warsh 
originally laid out a timeline for the task forces to start reporting 
their findings around the turn of the year, so we won't be surprised 
if attention starts turning to them in the coming months.

Personal Income & Spending • Thursday
Next week's personal income & spending report should show a 
consumer that remains willing and able to spend. We forecast 
personal spending rose 0.6% in September, or about 0.1% after 
accounting for higher prices. Spending was likely relatively broad-
based during the month, with some weakness concentrated in auto 
sales. Control-group retail sales point to continued growth in goods 
consumption, while restaurant sales suggest services spending 
remained on solid footing.
More broadly, the report comes on the heels of annual revisions 
that showed household incomes grew faster than previously 
believed. Along with continued support from rising household 
wealth, the revised income backdrop helps explain why consumer 
spending has remained resilient despite elevated borrowing costs 
and persistent inflation pressures. We look for personal income to 
rise 0.4% during the month, though firmer inflation likely weighted 
on real purchasing power.
Even as this data follows the Fed's policy decision the day prior, 
the market will remain focused on the inflation data. We forecast 
the headline PCE deflator increased 0.5% in September, with the 
core measure up 0.4%, lifting the annual rates to 3.7% and 3.1% 
respectively. This report will also arrive alongside the first estimate 
of third-quarter GDP on Thursday morning. We expect real GDP 
expanded at a 3.2% annualized clip, driven primarily by consumer 
spending and a continued expansion in business investment, 
namely high-tech related capex. Indeed, we estimate real consumer 
spending increased at a 3.4% annualized pace in Q3, reinforcing the 
view that households remain a key source of support for economic 
growth. While spending should moderate from the robust pace 
seen in the third quarter, firmer income fundamentals suggest the 
consumer should remain a source of economic support through 
year-end.

Advanced Economies Week Ahead
Bank of Canada Monetary Policy Meeting • Wednesday
The odds of a Bank of Canada (BoC) hike next week have increased 
sharply, but we continue to think the risk of a hold may be 
underappreciated. Market pricing has moved from ~30% a 
week ago to more than 80% following Governor Tiff Macklem's 
comments last weekend, which highlighted higher oil prices could 
boost domestic growth but also potentially increase the need for 
the bank to hike if higher costs spread beyond energy.
Markets took those comments as distinctly hawkish, but Monday's 
September CPI report did little to reinforce a sense of urgency. 
Headline inflation rose less than expected to 3.1% amid higher 
gasoline prices, while the BoC's preferred core measures were 
little changed and, in some cases, eased further toward target. 
Meanwhile, labor market conditions have softened since the 
BoC's last meeting, and there remains downside-risk to growth 
heading into year-end from the recent escalation in tariffs. Our 
base case has been that policymakers leave rates unchanged next 
week, though the risks have shifted toward a hike with the BoC 
somewhat boxing itself into that outcome.

Beyond next week, market pricing looks even harder to justify. 
Investors currently expect roughly 100 bps of cumulative 
tightening by year-end 2027, placing the BoC among the most 
hawkish major central banks. While that outcome cannot be 
ruled out entirely given there is little sign of an end to the Iran-

European Central Bank Monetary Policy Meeting • 
Thursday
Bond markets are complicating the European Central Bank’s (ECB) 
next move. We expect the Deposit Rate to remain unchanged at 
2.50% next week as France’s fiscal and political turmoil pushes 
borrowing costs higher. Communication will matter most: tighter 
financing conditions could temper the need for further hikes, even 
as energy prices keep inflation risks elevated.
In our view, the economic backdrop still supports further tightening. 
September headline inflation rose to 3.8% year-over-year and core 
inflation to 2.5%. We expect next week’s Q3 GDP release to show 
growth of 0.4% quarter-over-quarter, with the drag from France 
likely limited by its late-quarter onset. Slightly softer October PMIs 
still point to expansion (outside France).
For now, December remains our base case for the next hike. A 
resolution to France’s fiscal troubles could bring relief to bond 
markets, while a disorderly sell-off that spreads to other highly 
indebted Eurozone sovereigns could derail that call. The key will be 
whether the ECB signals that tighter financing conditions warrant 
more patience without closing the door on further hikes.

Bank of Japan Monetary Policy Meeting • Friday
We expect the Bank of Japan (BoJ) to hold its policy rate at 
1.25% next week after hiking at its last meeting. The focus 
will be on updated forecasts and whether policymakers signal 
greater confidence in the durability of domestic inflation pressures. 
September headline inflation rose to 2.1% year-over-year, above 
the BoJ’s 2% target, while its preferred “core-core” measure, which 
excludes fresh food and energy, rose to 2.4%. In our view, the 
backdrop continues to support further normalization. Policymakers 
describe financial conditions as accommodative despite the latest 
hike, with low real borrowing costs and strong bank lending growth. 
Meanwhile, geopolitical uncertainty has supported safe-haven 
demand for Japanese government bonds, with rising bond prices 
pushing yields lower.
We continue to expect another hike in December and a further 
increase in 2027 to bring the policy rate to 1.75%. Risks remain 
tilted toward more tightening as financial conditions remain 
supportive and domestic inflation pressures persist.

EM Week Ahead
Mexico GDP • Friday
Mexico’s economy looks set for another solid quarter, with stronger 
year-over-year growth despite a modest slowdown from Q2’s 
quarterly pace. We expect next week’s GDP release to show growth 
of 1.1% quarter-over-quarter on a seasonally adjusted basis and 
3.2% year-over-year on a non-seasonally adjusted basis. Mexico’s 
August Economic Activity Index rose 0.56% month-over-month and 
3.33% year-over-year, supported by industry and services. The solid 
print points to continued strength in Q3 and should support full-
year 2026 growth.
Stronger activity also gives Banxico less reason to ease, even as 
policymakers sound dovish. Headline inflation accelerated to 3.62% 
year-over-year in the first half of October, with upward pressure 
from both core and non-core components. El Niño adds to upside 
inflation risks and reinforces the case for patience.
We continue to expect Banxico to remain on hold at 6.50% through 
2026 and 2027. A firmer growth backdrop and persistent inflation 
risks should keep policymakers cautious despite their dovish tone.
