MARKET INSIGHTS
Within the current AI ecosystem, electricity may be the most limiting constraint today. A large AI data center campus can 
require a gigawatt or more of continuous power, roughly equivalent to the aggregate consumption of a mid-sized American 
city. While that need may seem massive, the challenge is less about generating that power than connecting to it. An AI data 
center can typically be built in 18 to 24 months, but connecting a facility of that size to the public grid can take five to seven 
years, due to required engineering studies, regulatory approvals and physical upgrades.1  In Texas alone, ERCOT, the state 
grid operator, is reviewing requests from large new customers, roughly 90% of them data centers, for about 474 gigawatts, 
which is more than five times the most power the state has ever used at once.2 
Why Is This Happening?
The cause of today’s bottleneck lies in the two decades leading up to the introduction of ChatGPT in 2022. As Figure 1 
shows, U.S. electricity use grew steadily from 1975 to 2005, then stopped. More efficient lighting, appliances and factories, 
plus the movement of heavy industry overseas, kept electricity consumption essentially flat for nearly twenty years. 
With electricity demand flat, utilities and independent power producers commissioned new plants only sparingly, and the 
manufacturers of turbines, transformers and other grid equipment calibrated their production to replacing aging assets 
rather than enlarging the fleet. The launch of ChatGPT changed the demand picture abruptly. Data centers consumed 
roughly 4% of U.S. electricity in 2023, and we estimate that share could reach approximately 30% by 2030, depending on 
the pace of AI adoption.  We project total U.S. electricity demand growth could exceed 6% a year through 2040, a pace not 
seen since the postwar era. We recognize this is a bold projection, well above most official forecasts and a sharp break from 
the recent past. In our view, however, it reflects the scale of data center capacity already planned or under construction, and 
Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as one of the most powerful secular growth themes of our time, 
transforming how businesses operate and how people live and work. Yet AI is not a single technology. It is a 
complex ecosystem supported by a long and interconnected value chain, spanning semiconductors, 
networking equipment, data centers, power infrastructure, and software. As AI adoption accelerates, strain 
can emerge at key points along this supply chain, creating bottlenecks where demand outpaces available 
resources. While these constraints can create uncertainty, we believe they can also reveal compelling 
investment opportunities. In this series, we explore the most significant AI bottlenecks, the forces driving 
them, and the investment opportunities they may create.

The Solution: Bring Your Own Generation
As we wrote back in April in The AI Power Problem 
commentary, data center operators have responded 
by taking power into their own hands. Bring Your Own 
Generation (BYOG), the practice of installing gas turbines, 
engines or fuel cells on site to power the data center 
directly, has moved from a niche solution to a central 
strategy. Binding orders for on-site power generation 
equipment now total about 75 gigawatts, and OpenAI’s 
flagship Stargate campus in Texas alone will run on more 
than 500 gas engines.4
A one-gigawatt campus can produce tens of billions of 
dollars in revenue each year. Multiply that by a five-to-
seven-year wait for a grid connection, and the opportunity 
cost of delay can exceed $100 billion.5  Paying a premium 
for on-site power that can be running in under two years is 
a rational decision. BYOG also sidesteps a growing political 
problem. Concerns about higher household electricity bills, 
driven in part by the cost of grid upgrades to serve new 
data centers, have made data centers unpopular neighbors; 
in August, Texas paused approvals of new grid-connected 
data centers pending a statewide audit. A campus that 
brings its own power is not asking ratepayers to fund new 
transmission lines on its behalf. 
Looking Ahead
For data center operators, we believe on-site power is a 
necessity rather than a choice, and the companies that 
supply it are booked years in advance, which gives them 
strong pricing power. In our view, select gas turbine and 
engine manufacturers, makers of the switchgear and 
transformers that connect generators to servers, and the 
specialized contractors that install them remain primary 
beneficiaries of this bottleneck. The grid will catch up 
eventually. Until it does, the companies that help data 
center operators bypass the wait for a grid connection will 
continue to command a premium.
