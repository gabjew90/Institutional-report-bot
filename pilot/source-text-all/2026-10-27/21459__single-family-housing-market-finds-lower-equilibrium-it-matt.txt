◼ Single-family construction holding up given the real cost of funding and loss of 
secular underpinnings – but a cyclical downturn in coming in the next few months 
◼ Housing demand has settled into a new lower equilibrium, like auto unit sales, but 
neither industry’s value-add matters to GDP as it once did – further complicating the 
transmission of monetary policy 
◼ Equity pricing of homebuilders is a bit rich, but so too is the overall market 
Time was the US economy ran on housing and autos, two easy industries for the Fed to 
accelerate or slow, but that time is long gone. Unit sales of autos (& light trucks) topped out at 
around 18 million in 2000 and that has been the effective ceiling ever since, even lower post-
Covid. To rekindle overall growth through housing construction, we read a lot about housing and 
interest rates stifling demand and all sorts of schemes to boost demand through affordability – 
including Congress passing the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act over Trump’s objections. 
Trying to get housing demand back to pre-08 levels has proven a dead end since the GFC ended. 
Even negative real mortgage rates from 2014 to 2018 failed to revive demand to any significant 
degree. There was a surge around COVID, mostly due to the disease, but that bump proved short-
lived. It isn’t that demand has disappeared, new home sales are tracking their long-run median, or 
that demand would fail to respond to mortgage rates to some degree, it is just that overall 
demand for single-family homes has downshifted – and new home construction is about to 
downshift further in the months ahead. Households have discovered equities as a better store of 
wealth, especially in a world where personal confidence in future income growth is low (no longer 
is the mantra that being house rich, cash poor is always a winning strategy). The rush to invest in 
equities is, however, probably going to end as badly as the mortgage-mania that was built on the 
certainty that house prices never fall. In real dollar terms, home prices in Las Vegas and Phoenix, 
epicentres of the mortgage boom, are below their 2006 highs (Chart 1).

In the very near term, construction of single-family homes will drop further, and this a drag 
real GDP growth lower in the first half of 2027. The NAHB survey of new homebuilders, notably 
the question about expectations for sales in the coming six months, foretells future construction 
with about a three-month lead (Chart 2). 
High real mortgage rates are an obvious reason to expect lower sales going forward, but rates 
are not the whole story. My calculation of the real mortgage rate is to take the nominal 30-year 
fixed mortgage rate and subtract the inflation rate for median new home prices for the past five 
years. Negative real rates helped fuel housing demand in 2004-07 and they also helped boost 
sales in 2014-18 but not by nearly as much as past relationships would suggest (the return of 
mortgage underwriting standards mattered, but also a lack of confidence in a home as an 
investment against uncertain expectations for future wage growth).(Chart 3) Nevertheless, 
perhaps as a sign of resilient base demand for new homes, even though real rates have trended 
more positive since 2018, sales (and starts) have moved sideways –save for the COVID moment.

While much has been made about the relative boost in multifamily construction (people need 
to live somewhere), but multi-family has also essentially trended sideways since 2015. There 
was a bit of run in the past two years, but the peak fell short of the immediate post-Covid jump. 
(Chart 4) The problem is that absorption rates slowed with the slowdown in payroll growth and 
rise in unemployment back over 4%. The recent pickup in hiring and time have fixed the 
overbuilding in several metro areas. It is reasonable to expect better levels of multifamily 
construction in the coming year. A boost to GDP growth, but not as much as the drag from slower 
single-family construction.  
Single-family construction was trending higher heading into COVID, owing to millennials 
coming into their prime home-buying years – but, again, the level of construction falls well short 
of expectations. There is a record 36.4 million employees aged 25- to 34-year-olds. The level of 
high real mortgage rates is not the story. Nor is affordability the whole – this measure, as a

The difference is confidence in wage growth and an unshakeable belief that equities only move 
higher over time. This is evident in Chart 6, showing how post-GFC, households became net 
buyers of equities and their pace of taking on mortgages runs below the 1965-2000 average. 
TINA is about equities versus other financial assets, but it has increasingly mean homes as well. 
As for the equity value of homebuilders, the S&P500 homebuilder index (others have the same 
price performance), there was a post-COVID moment when homebuilder shares outperformed 
the S&P 500. But since 2025, with the expectation of higher costs and higher yields (higher funds 
rate directly, translates into more expensive construction loans, then there is the issue of the price 
of materials from higher tariffs and the loss of immigrant labor), homebuilder equity prices fell 
relative to the S&P – driven as it is by tech.

Odds are, homebuilder equity values have more downside as higher mortgage rates downshift 
earnings expectations – analysts always seem to be the last to know. Looking at the forward 12-
month P/E, the ratio for homebuilder has softened but it has dropped a lot more for consumer 
discretionary. The two are very much tied together in terms of real spending. 
How much does it all matter to the economy? Clearly the economy responds to residential 
construction – about $1 of money spent on residential construction generates about $3 in total 
economic activity. But, looking at the contribution to GDP from autos and housing since the mid-
1960s, these are much less important industries (Chart 9) – and there is no reason to believe this 
trend will reverse. AI is the new kid in town that drives the economy, every cycle brings in a new 
industry to lead, some more transformative than others.

In sum, expect a short cyclical dip in residential construction in the coming months but it will 
be short lived – but that’s only part of the story. There is strong demographic demand for 
housing, and it will be fuelled in the coming year by accelerating employment and incomes. 
Stronger does not mean housing returns to pre-08 levels of activity – there has been a 
fundamental downshift in demand and, even, if possible, why go back to turning renters into 
owners. Part of this downshift owes to reduced forward confidence in income growth and 
improved confidence in the forever upward trajectory of the equity market. This is not going to 
end well, it never does when everyone is making the same play, but for now we go with the flow. 
As for homebuilder equity values, they seem a bit expensive on a forward 12M basis, but nothing 
dramatic. In the end, housing matters less to the economy and this makes Fed policy that much 
less potent than it used to be. Perhaps people rediscover the value of a house as an inflation 
hedge.
