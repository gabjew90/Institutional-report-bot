Views in summary
This is how we see global listed real 
estate markets evolving and how we 
derive our market preferences.
Our preferences
Global listed real estate started 2026 on a stronger footing, temporarily reversing its prolonged underperformance relative to global equities. 
More recently, increased volatility in longer-term yields and a persistent investor preference for equities have created renewed headwinds. 
Sector fundamentals remain compelling, with strong rental growth and limited new supply continuing to support performance. We believe 
concerns about the cost of capital are becoming less pressing given growing rental income and generally conservative balance sheet leverage, 
especially in the US. Market liquidity remains an important factor and is showing signs of resilience despite higher bond yields.
We favor companies that pursue growth, fund acquisitions through accretive issuance, and demonstrate strong pricing power, profitable 
pipelines, attractive yield gaps, and robust cash flows. We remain selective when considering companies still trading at a discount to net asset 
value (NAV), as they may be value traps unless they can successfully grow their businesses.
Our findings
Earnings growth has been robust despite higher financing costs this year. We do not expect a significant acceleration ahead but expect 
attractive growth to continue. Higher government bond yields increase the opportunity cost of investing in the sector and slow any repricing 
momentum. Global listed real estate trades at a discount to NAV of about 12% (as of 19 October 2026). Among subsectors, health care, 
lodging, retail, and self storage are outperforming, while residential and office are lagging. We expect listed real estate earnings per share to 
grow by 11% in 2026 and 7.7% in 2027 (excluding emerging markets), driven mainly by rental indexation, reversion, and select portfolio 
transactions or acquisitions. Some private funds still have capital to deploy, while others are returning capital to limited partners. Open-ended 
funds facing redemptions are likely to sell properties, while direct market valuations may become more attractive in the short term in a higher-
yield environment. Most companies should be able to keep financing costs reasonably stable or offset modest increases with rising rental 
income, while a minority will likely continue to dispose of assets. Average debt maturities exceed five years in most markets, allowing 
companies to reduce duration risk marginally through shorter-term refinancing.
The sector’s current global average dividend yield is 3.5%, slightly below its seven-year average of 3.6% (excluding emerging markets), and 
REITs trade at a 3% discount to NAV based on our estimates (as of 19 October 2026). We remain optimistic at the index level and expect a 
rerating over the medium term, although heightened bond-yield volatility has again delayed this prospect. The sector's current 12-month 
forward price-to-earnings ratio is in line with its long-term average of 14-15x

2021 and 2022 stand in stark contrast
•
In 2021, particularly attractive refinancing conditions 
amid balance sheet expansions by central banks 
triggered a valuation boom for commercial real estate 
(CRE).
•
The US CRE market was supported by ample fiscal 
stimulus, while the UK reversed its 2020 losses in 2021.  
•
The abrupt tightening of monetary conditions by 
central banks in 2022, retreating from their 2021 
expansive policies, brought overvalued and over-levered 
residential landlords (especially German ones) under 
pressure, as their refinancing costs suddenly surpassed 
their implied portfolio yields.
•
This situation and the expectation that other sectors 
would fall into negative leverage created a quasi-
standstill in market transactions.

the Bank of Japan’s unchanged monetary policy.  
•
Also, Continental Europe rebounded sharply in the 
expectation of diminishing interest rates.

Out- and underperformers converge only slightly
•
Different real estate sectors have experienced 
significant divergences in total returns in recent years. 
•
The rapid increase in opportunity costs and higher 
financing costs in 2022 at first reduced this divergence 
in performance between industrial, self-storage, and 
residential on one side, and office, retail, and even 
health care on the other side. 
•
We do not expect this divergence in performance to 
close rapidly, as companies and investors are not 
currently changing their investment preferences. 
•
For performance gaps to close, important new and 
earnings-accretive equity issuances by office and retail 
landlords are needed. This may happen only if the 
undersupply of new office space or retail space were to 
become more supportive, which is gradually beginning 
to happen.

•

again delayed as we have no signs of a strong reversal 
despite visible gradual normalization.

Residential clearly came under selling pressure in 2022
•
In 2022, lodging and retail performed comparatively 
better, as they went through an earlier value correction 
compared to other real estate subsectors. 
•
Residential came under significant selling pressure. The 
comparatively low-yielding subsector principally 
suffered from the rapid surge in yields and entered 
negative leverage territory: The implied portfolio yield 
was below the marginal cost of debt.
•
In the case of industrial space, the poor performance 
can be explained by market technical reasons—i.e., 
relative valuation, as the subsector was one of the best 
performers in 2021.
