Drivers of synchronized global rate hikes and a critical 
turning point for Japan’s economy 
September brought what could be described as synchronized global rate hikes, with 
the European Central Bank raising rates on 10 September, followed by the Federal 
Reserve on 16 September and the Bank of Japan on 18 September. 
These actions reflect global efforts to address inflation, and in particular the higher 
energy prices stemming from the war in Iran. However, the underlying factors include 
both shared and country-specific issues. 
Expansionary fiscal policy and inflation concerns are driving long-term rates 
higher 
One factor common to all countries and regions is the direction of long-term interest 
rates. Japan, the US, and Europe have all seen their 10-year government bond yields 
move higher. A proximate cause of this increase appears to be concerns that inflation 
will remain elevated given that there is no end in sight to the Iran conflict. 
However, these countries also share another problem—namely, fiscal policy has 
become highly expansionary even though their economies are performing reasonably 
well. 
In Japan, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has made proactive fiscal policy central to her 
economic agenda. As a result, total budget requests for FY2027 from government 
ministries and agencies reached a record high for a fourth consecutive year, leaving a 
highly expansionary impression. 
The US fiscal deficit has also continued to grow under the Trump administration 
despite a relatively strong economy supported by the AI boom. In a monthly report 
released on 8 October, the Congressional Budget Office forecast that the federal deficit 
would increase from $1,775bn last fiscal year to $1,993bn in FY2026, which runs from 
October 2025 through September 2026. This would represent 6.2% of nominal GDP, a 
worrying level given the absence of any major crises. 
Moreover, the Republican Party looks to be facing a tough battle in the midterm 
elections next month. In response, US President Donald Trump has announced a 
series of policies that can only be described as handouts to the voting public. I think 
these campaign pledges, which reveal a lack of concern for fiscal discipline, are also 
part of the reason why US Treasury yields are rising.

European commitment to balanced budgets fades amid changing security 
environment 
Additionally, the Trump administration has issued strong demands for Japan and Europe 
to increase their defense spending, and the resulting increase in fiscal expenditures 
presents an entirely new challenge for both. 
In Europe, Germany was for many years the leading advocate of balanced budgets. 
However, changes in the security environment prompted Chancellor Friedrich Merz to 
adopt a stance that no longer rules out expansionary fiscal policy. 
Other EU countries have also become less constrained by fiscal scruples since boosting 
expenditures in response to the pandemic. France, one of the region’s two largest 
economies, has seen its budget deficits widen sharply. Many observers are concerned that 
this could damage perceptions of both the euro and the EU in international financial markets. 
Recent increases in long-term bond yields therefore reflect two common factors: stronger 
global inflation concerns arising from the war in Iran and increasingly expansionary fiscal 
policy. This state of affairs has exacerbated the problems facing central banks. 
Quantitative tightening by central banks also driving long-term rates higher 
Quantitative tightening (QT) by central banks in Japan and Europe has also helped push 
long-term yields higher. Under QT, a central bank allows bonds purchased under 
quantitative easing (QE) to mature without reinvesting the proceeds in other bonds, 
essentially withdrawing the funds from the market. 
More than 13 years have passed since former BOJ Governor Haruhiko Kuroda launched 
quantitative and qualitative easing (“QQE”) in April 2013. A substantial portion of the 
bonds purchased under that program have already matured. Under Mr. Kuroda, 
however, the Bank immediately reinvested the proceeds in other JGBs, thus preventing 
any deterioration in bond market supply-demand. 
The statement released after the April 2013 Monetary Policy Meeting, when QQE began, 
explicitly said the BOJ would purchase JGBs so that “their amount outstanding will 
increase at an annual pace of about ¥50trn.” Following the additional easing of policy in 
October 2014, the Bank stated that purchases would be conducted so that “their amount 
outstanding will increase at an annual pace of about ¥80trn.” Both statements explicitly 
targeted large increases in the central bank’s outstanding stock of bond holdings. 
BOJ moving steadily ahead with QT 
Under Governor Ueda, however, the Bank has stopped immediately reinvesting the 
proceeds from JGB redemptions in other bonds. Like the Fed under Janet Yellen and the 
ECB more recently, it is implementing QT by withdrawing those funds from the market.  
Market liquidity naturally declines as part of this process. As a result, the seasonally 
adjusted monetary base in September 2026 was 21.9% below its April 2024 peak 
(Figure 1). 
The QQE program launched by Mr. Kuroda in 2013 involved JGB purchases on such a 
large scale that it was described as “unprecedented.” More than a decade later, the 
volume of those bonds coming up for redemption is also enormous. If the BOJ were to 
withdraw all the proceeds, the JGB market could become severely strained. 
To soften this shock, the Bank continues to buy JGBs in a deliberately visible manner. But 
in reality, these purchases merely camouflage the QT taking place in the background, and 
the amounts purchased are far smaller than the redemption proceeds being withdrawn. 
This is why the monetary base continues its steady decline, as shown in Figure 1.

QT alone cannot explain why 10-year JGB yield hit 3% 
QT naturally places upward pressure on government bond yields. However, it seems 
difficult to attribute the 10-year JGB yield’s rise above 3% exclusively to this factor. 
The 10-year JGB yield stood at 0.7% before Mr. Kuroda introduced QQE. It 
subsequently fell to around 0% under that policy and yield curve control. A complete 
policy reversal and implementation of QT could reasonably explain an increase to 
around 2%. However, I think additional factors are necessary to explain the 10-year 
yield’s rise to 3%. 
The Fed, for example, launched QT in 2017, but this did not lead to a substantial rise in 
US Treasury yields. The ECB introduced QT in 2023, yet German Bund yields exhibited 
little movement from then through the end of last year. 
The 10-year German Bund yield did see a sharp rise from around −0.2% at end-2021 to 
2.5% at end-2022. However, this occurred while the ECB was responding to inflation by 
raising its policy rate—the deposit facility rate—from −0.5% to 4.0% over a period of 14 
months starting in July 2022. It had little to do with the QT that began subsequently. 
By comparison, the BOJ has raised its policy rate by only 135bp in the two and a half 
years since it ended negative interest rates in March 2024. Yet the 10-year JGB yield 
has risen by 300bp.

Yen weakness due to wider interest rate spread has also pushed 10-year JGB yield 
higher 
In that case, why has Japan’s 10-year government bond yield risen so much? In addition 
to the factors discussed above, I think imported inflation due to the weak yen has played 
a major role. 
The current decline in the yen began when central banks in the US and Europe raised 
policy rates in response to the inflation that followed the end of the pandemic. At the 
time, the BOJ was alone among the major central banks in refraining from hiking. 
Before the pandemic, all of the major economies were experiencing balance sheet 
recessions, and policy rates were extremely low. But from 2022 onward, the ECB raised 
rates by a cumulative 450bp, and the Fed carried out 525bp of tightening. The BOJ took 
no action until 2024, and its subsequent rate hikes were far smaller than those in the US 
and Europe. The large and persistent interest rate differential between Japan and 
overseas economies caused the yen to weaken against a broad range of other 
currencies. 
BOJ constrained by belief that deflation is “root cause of all economic ills” 
The BOJ stayed on the sidelines because both the Japanese government and the central 
bank were effectively held hostage by the doctrine that deflation was responsible for all 
economic problems, as discussed in my last report. Recent developments have shown that 
this view was mistaken: inflation has returned, but economic growth remains weak. 
At the core of this ideology was the claim that aggressive monetary accommodation 
could defeat deflation. However, quantitative easing in Japan, the US and Europe failed 
to boost bank lending or generate inflation during the balance sheet recessions in those 
economies. Nor did it end deflation until the pandemic subsided and the Ukraine conflict 
began. 
The weakness in the yen caused by Japan’s sustained QE benefited some export 
industries and businesses serving inbound tourists. But at the same time, imported 
inflation depressed real incomes in Japan and triggered a slump in domestic demand. 
Food inflation, which households confront on a daily basis, has been particularly high 
(Figure 2). I suspect many people may also have concluded that the BOJ was 
responding far too slowly to inflation and decided to move funds overseas, thus creating 
a vicious cycle of further weakness in the yen, an acceleration of domestic inflation, and 
higher long-term interest rates.

Trump’s complaints about weak yen create urgent need for normalization of 
Japanese monetary policy 
One goal of Governor Kuroda’s monetary accommodation was to correct an overly 
strong yen, but this approach reached a dead end after President Trump complained 
about Japan’s undervalued currency at the recent bilateral summit. The nation can no 
longer expect to depend on a weak yen to stimulate its economy.  
President Trump probably worried that, if Japanese manufacturers were to launch a full-
scale export offensive with the USD/JPY exchange rate close to 160, US industry—and 
the automobile sector in particular—would likely suffer severe economic harm. 
US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent understood that the single most important 
component of Mr. Trump’s support base is the group of people harmed by the trade 
deficits that were allowed to run unchecked by previous Democratic (and Republican) 
administrations. Last year he began pressing Japan to normalize monetary policy as 
soon as possible, as it is the main cause of the decline in the yen. The situation has now 
reached the point where Mr. Trump himself has begun complaining about the weak yen. 
These concerns led to the recent coordinated Japan-US intervention to stem the fall in 
the Japanese currency. However, intervention alone is clearly insufficient, and the 
normalization of BOJ monetary policy is therefore an urgent priority. 
BOJ is pursuing QT while trying to mitigate its adverse effects 
The obstacle is the belief that deflation is the root cause of all economic ills, derived from 
the first “arrow” of Abenomics and discussed in my last report.  
Under pressure from this ideology, the BOJ implemented a QE program several times 
larger than those undertaken by the US and the UK. At one point, liquidity supplied via 
QE in the form of bank reserves amounted to 76% of Japan’s GDP. 
This was far greater than the corresponding ratios of 16.5% for the US, 29.1% for the 
eurozone, and 20.0% for the UK. Now that inflation has actually emerged, this has made 
it much more difficult for the BOJ to wind down QE by implementing QT.  
Since QE was so large, proceeding directly to QT could create a shock too severe for the 
bond market to bear.

Governor Ueda is therefore implementing the QT required to urgently normalize 
monetary policy while avoiding making any explicit references to it. By emphasizing that 
the BOJ is still buying JGBs, he is trying to cushion the resulting shock to the market. 
Fed also introduced transitional measures when it launched QT in 2017 
Similarly, when the Fed in 2017 became the first central bank to embark on QT, then-
Chair Janet Yellen was deeply concerned about the potential impact on the bond market. 
The Fed therefore released an official schedule consisting of extremely small reductions 
that would be gradually increased over time.  
At the time, the lingering effects of the balance sheet recession that began in 2008 
meant US inflation was still running below the 2% target and private-sector borrowing 
demand remained weak. 
The Fed decided to unwind the QE program introduced to generate inflation before 
inflation actually emerged. I suspect this was because policymakers at that time 
understood how difficult it would be to implement QT once private-sector loan demand 
recovered and inflation took hold.  
Bank lending growth now recovering in Japan and US 
This time, however, every major economy is implementing QT at a time of inflation, and 
private-sector demand for funds is also picking up. During the balance sheet recessions 
of the 2010s, global bank lending growth remained extremely weak despite zero interest 
rates. Over the past several years, however, lending growth has been normalizing. 
Japan is no exception in this regard. 
From the collapse of the asset-price bubble in 1990 until just before the pandemic hit in 
2020, bank lending in Japan grew at an annual rate of only 0.9% despite zero interest 
rates and QE. This contrasts sharply with annualized lending growth of 11.8% over the 
five years starting in March 1983.  
More recently, Japanese lending has been rising at a rate of 5.3% y-y. Excluding the 
pandemic-driven surge in lending in 2020, this is the strongest growth seen since 1991, 
some 35 years ago. 
A similar pattern emerged in the US and Europe around 2008. US bank lending grew at 
an annual rate of 8.4% during the 10 years before the housing bubble collapsed in 2008. 
Following the Lehman shock, annual growth slowed sharply to 3.3% through 2019, just 
before the pandemic struck.  
US bank lending is now rising at a rate of 7.6% y-y, indicating that borrowing demand 
has largely returned to normal. 
Japan is withdrawing liquidity despite significant adverse effects from QE 
QT carried out after private-sector demand for funds has normalized naturally puts 
upward pressure on interest rates. The private sector wants to borrow, and with the 
central bank no longer buying, the government must sell refunding bonds to private 
investors as existing bonds mature. 
QT at a time of resurgent private-sector loan demand may therefore be pushing up 
global long-term interest rates. Combined with expansionary fiscal policy and imported 
inflation due to the war in Iran, I think it is placing considerable pressure on government 
bond markets.  
Of these three factors, QT poses the greatest challenge for Japan because its QE was 
several times larger than those of the US and Europe. This is also why the BOJ must 
continue buying JGBs to help cushion the blow to the financial system.  
Even so, Governor Ueda is moving steadily ahead with QT, and Japan’s monetary base 
has declined by nearly 22% over the past two and a half years as a result. Any notion

Japan’s 22% is smaller than the ECB’s 37.9% reduction in base money from its peak

New Fed chair repeatedly emphasizes need for QT 
In the US, Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh has repeatedly emphasized the need for 
quantitative tightening. He has said the specific process will be guided by the 
conclusions of the Fed’s task force on QT, which are due in one month. 
The form the Fed’s QT will take is therefore likely to become clear by year-end. But given 
how frequently Mr. Warsh has emphasized the need for this policy, it may already be 
largely priced into today’s higher US Treasury yields. 
The Bank of England has also steadily implemented QT in recent years, leading to a 
30.3% decline in the monetary base from its end-2021 peak. 
ECB may have accelerated QT because of negative carry at eurozone central 
banks 
At least one reason why the eurozone pushed ahead with QT may be that rate hikes by 
the ECB following the post-pandemic inflation surge generated substantial negative carry 
at the national central banks responsible for implementing QE, including Germany’s 
Bundesbank and the Dutch central bank.

The ECB launched QE when German Bund yields were already at or below zero. As a 
result, the interest income earned by the Bundesbank and the Dutch central bank on 
their domestic government bond holdings was also close to zero.  
However, inflation subsequently emerged due to supply-chain disruptions as the 
pandemic subsided and also to the conflict in Ukraine. In response, the ECB raised its 
policy rate—the deposit facility rate—from −0.5% to 4.0%. 
The Bundesbank was then required to pay that high interest on the reserves held in the 
deposit facility, which were supplied under QE, while the government bonds purchased 
under QE continued to yield close to nothing. This left it facing sharply negative carry.  
The increase in the Bundesbank’s interest expense reduced its year-end remittances to 
the German Finance Ministry by a corresponding amount, thus causing the fiscal deficit 
to expand. 
This risked becoming a major political issue because it meant that the unelected 
governor of the central bank could have a direct impact on the national budget without 
the approval of an elected government. 
The issue reportedly did not attract a great deal of attention in Germany—due partly to 
consideration shown by the governing parties at the time—thus sparing the Bundesbank 
from a bigger controversy. Nevertheless, it would not be surprising if this experience 
encouraged eurozone central banks to accelerate QT to avoid such problems in the 
future. 
Scale of QE greatest obstacle to BOJ policy normalization 
By contrast, the Fed purchased US Treasury securities offering meaningful yields. The 
income generated by those securities resulted in substantial remittances to the federal 
government. Even after rate hikes caused carry to turn negative, the Fed could still argue 
that taxpayers had not suffered on a cumulative basis. 
Like the Bundesbank, the BOJ purchased low-yielding government bonds under QE. 
However, it did not raise its policy rate until 2024 despite the emergence of global 
inflation in 2022. It therefore avoided a sharp increase in interest expense at the time. 
As a result, however, the bank reserves created under QE remained in place for more 
than two years longer than in the US and Europe. These reserves now represent a major 
obstacle to the normalization of monetary policy being pursued by Governor Ueda. 
Japan faces a particular problem because the yields on JGBs purchased under QE were 
low, while the program itself was exceptionally large. If the BOJ raises its policy rate, it 
must pay that higher rate on a total of ¥390trn in bank reserves. A 100bp increase in the 
policy rate therefore raises the Bank’s annual expenditures by ¥3.9trn which increases 
the government’s fiscal deficit by 12%. 
Viewed from this perspective, the central banks of Japan, the US and Europe may have 
carried out what appeared to be coordinated rate hikes last month, but their underlying 
circumstances differ considerably. 
The BOJ in particular faces two problems not shared by other central banks: a very weak 
local currency and the enormous scale of its QE program. Both of these factors 
complicate its policy response. 
Flow-of-funds data suggest Japan should not discontinue fiscal stimulus 
Many commentators argue that long-term yields are rising because fiscal policy is 
expansionary and that the government should therefore end such policies and pursue a 
balanced budget. However, I do not think Japan’s economy satisfies the conditions 
required for such a shift.

As noted in my last report, Japan’s flow-of-funds data show that both the household and 
corporate sectors continue to run a financial surplus, indicating excess savings. This 
situation has persisted for the past 30 years. 
In a normal economy, businesses borrow and spend the money saved by households. In 
Japan, however, the balance sheet problems triggered by the collapse of the asset 
bubble discouraged companies from borrowing, and the private sector as a whole 
became a net saver. 
This means that some entity outside the private sector must borrow and spend those 
excess savings to keep the economy going. Only the government or the overseas sector 
can perform this role. 
On the latter, the US has turned toward protectionism and expressed its displeasure with 
the weak yen. China is experiencing a severe balance sheet recession and is not in a 
position to boost imports. Unless the government continues to borrow and spend the 
excess savings of the private sector, Japan’s economy therefore risks stalling again. 
In that sense, it is important that Japan not abandon proactive fiscal policy prematurely. 
It first needs to confirm that the corporate sector has resumed borrowing. The latest flow-
of-funds data suggest that this has not yet happened. 
Does recovery in bank lending mean it is time for Japan to pursue balanced 
budget? 
At the same time, it is important to note that Japanese bank lending is beginning to 
normalize after more than 30 years of extreme weakness. If bank lending data reflect the 
behavior of Japanese companies more accurately than the flow-of-funds accounts, 
private-sector borrowers are returning. If so, the government should quickly abandon its 
expansionary fiscal policy and shift course toward a balanced budget. 
If the recent rise in JGB yields has been driven by a recovery in corporate borrowing 
demand, further government spending could not only crowd out the private capital 
expenditures that have finally begun to emerge but also drive interest rates even higher. 
In recent remarks, Mr. Ueda said that bank lending was among the indicators he was 
monitoring. This is an entirely appropriate perspective given Japan’s current 
circumstances. 
If the recent growth in bank lending genuinely indicates that Japanese corporate 
behavior is finally returning to normal, it would mean both that monetary policy must be 
normalized and that fiscal stimulus is no longer required.  
Which to believe: flow-of-funds accounts or bank lending data? 
If companies are shifting their focus from minimizing debt to maximizing profits, as 
standard economic theory assumes, it means that Japan has finally put behind it the 
balance sheet recession that began with the collapse of the asset bubble 35 years ago 
and its lingering effects. 
The government will no longer need to act as borrower of last resort if companies begin 
actively borrowing and investing household savings. 
That would represent the welcome normalization of Japan’s economy that we have been 
awaiting for the last 35 years. During that period, however, the BOJ injected an 
astronomical amount of liquidity into the market, and politicians and industry became 
accustomed to enormous levels of government spending. 
At present, it is difficult to determine whether the recent rise in the 10-year JGB yield to 
3% reflects: 1) concerns about the weak yen and inflation caused by the BOJ’s delayed 
response to inflation; or 2) a resumption of borrowing by Japanese companies that have 
finally put the balance sheet recession and its aftereffects behind them. The flow-of-

funds data suggest Scenario 1 is responsible, while bank lending data point to Scenario 
2. 
Is growth in bank lending mainly financing purchases of existing assets? 
The composition of bank lending further complicates the issue. If loans are funding 
expenditures that add to GDP, such as corporate investment and business expansion, 
they would be supportive of Scenario 2. However, that would not be the case if the loans 
are being taken out to finance purchases of existing assets, such as real estate 
transactions and M&A. 
I may be incorrect, but my impression from recent discussions with a small number of 
banking-sector contacts is that a sizeable portion of the lending growth has been driven 
by real estate. If so, that would suggest that Japan’s economy has not yet entered 
Scenario 2. 
At the same time, companies now have more reasons to expand capex and the 
borrowing needed to finance it. Labor shortages are becoming more severe, while AI-
related investment is growing explosively, particularly in the US. This has been very 
beneficial for semiconductor-related companies in Japan. 
Whether the recent rise in long-term yields is attributable to Scenario 1 or Scenario 2 will 
therefore be crucial to the outlook for Japan’s economy. 
BOJ policy normalization essential under either scenario 
The BOJ needs to normalize policy in either scenario because the Japanese economy is 
moving toward higher inflation regardless. However, the reason why normalization is 
required differs in the two cases. 
Under Scenario 1, the BOJ must rectify its delays in responding to inflation. If it succeeds 
in establishing its credibility as an inflation fighter, that should help curb currency 
weakness and increases in long-term interest rates. That, in turn, would allow the 
Takaichi administration to pursue proactive fiscal policy while putting an end to the 
imported inflation that is directly lowering standards of living for ordinary Japanese 
households. 
In other words, Scenario 1 requires a textbook response to inflation centered on rate 
hikes. 
Leaving base money in place would lead directly to inflation risk 
Under Scenario 2, however, Japanese companies that previously refused to borrow will 
start to take out loans again. The key issue then becomes how much lending capacity 
banks possess. 
Lending capacity is normally assessed by examining the amount of excess reserves 
banks hold relative to statutory reserve requirements, or the increase in base money 
supplied by the central bank. In Japan, both figures are enormous as a result of the 
“unprecedented easing” implemented by former BOJ Governor Kuroda. 
Base money in Japan (i.e., the monetary base) has increased 1,370% from the collapse 
of the asset bubble in 1990 to the present. Under the textbook assumption that a 10% 
increase in base money produces a 10% increase in both the money supply and bank 
lending, Japanese banks could expand lending by as much as 1,370%, from current 
levels. 
More precisely, Japanese bank lending has increased by 64% over the past 35 years. 
Subtracting 64% from 1,370% implies that sufficient funds remain in the system for 
banks to expand lending by just over 1,310%.  
However, if bank lending and the money supply increased by that amount, prices would 
likely rise by a similar multiple.

In practice, capital and liquidity regulations would prevent bank lending from expanding 
that rapidly. But even if the increase were only a fourth of the amount described above, 
inflation could still hit 300%. 
Eliminating this possibility would require the BOJ to use QT to withdraw the remaining 
¥390trn in excess reserves injected into the system under Mr. Kuroda’s QQE program. 
And as noted above, this would be extremely challenging. 
The policies required of the BOJ in Scenarios 1 and 2 are therefore very different. 
Scenario 2 is the outcome Japan has been seeking for 35 years. However, if it is finally 
reached and private companies begin borrowing aggressively, the central bank will be 
forced to make a massive policy U-turn. 
Why Warsh wants to quickly withdraw excess liquidity 
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh is confronting the need for this policy U-turn head-on. The US 
monetary base has grown 514%, far less than Japan’s 1,370%. However, Mr. Warsh 
subscribes to the idea associated with Milton Friedman that a 10% increase in base 
money provided by the central bank produces a corresponding increase in the money 
supply and credit. He therefore believes that the excess liquidity released through 
quantitative easing must be mopped up quickly as the US economy puts the balance 
sheet recession behind it and returns to normal. 
Mr. Warsh’s position is that QE is essential during emergencies like the global financial 
crisis that was triggered by the 2008 Lehman shock. Once the economy normalizes, 
however, he believes the Fed should shift rapidly to QT. Accordingly, he blames the 
Powell Fed for the inflation that was caused partly by delaying that change of course. 
Starting around 2011, when the financial crisis that began in 2008 was finally stabilizing, 
Mr. Warsh argued that the Federal Reserve should wind down QE. His disagreements 
with mainstream Fed policymakers, who were seeking a further expansion of QE under 
Ben Bernanke, are thought to be one reason he left the institution. 
I suspect similar debates also occurred at other central banks that implemented QE. In 
Japan, then-Policy Board member Takahide Kiuchi advocated an early exit from QE. 
However, because the continuation of QE during a balance sheet recession produced no 
evident harm, his proposal appears to have been overruled by the majority of Board 
members under the leadership of Mr. Kuroda, who wanted to demonstrate that the BOJ 
was taking strong action to stimulate the economy. 
Preparing for normalization of Japan’s economy after 35 years 
Viewed in this light, the global economy (ex China) appears to be moving toward 
synchronized QT as well as synchronized rate hikes. Interest rates are therefore likely to 
remain elevated.  
And if the entire world is moving in the same direction, interest rate differentials may not 
narrow despite Governor Ueda’s dedicated efforts to normalize monetary policy. This 
could keep the yen under pressure for some time, in which case imported inflation would 
also persist. 
Japan faces an additional challenge because the quantitative and qualitative monetary 
easing implemented by Governor Kuroda was exceptionally large. Unwinding it requires 
shock absorbers such as purchasing small amounts of JGBs, transitional measures, 
which are delaying the BOJ’s efforts to establish its reputation as an inflation fighter. 
Many observers in Japan and overseas focus only on the Bank’s continued JGB 
purchases and fail to recognize that QT has substantially reduced the monetary base. 
This means that Mr. Ueda’s “camouflage” is working extremely well. However, it also 
makes it difficult for the BOJ to build a reputation as an inflation hawk.

time in 35 years—as bank lending data suggest—the normalization of fiscal policy as

This change in Japanese corporate behavior is not yet evident in the flow-of-funds data. 
Nevertheless, the likelihood that Japan’s economy is finally returning to normal after 35 
long years continues to rise. Both the private sector and the authorities should begin 
preparing for this possibility.
