Executive summary 
 Global public debt has risen sharply. Higher interest rates and less central-bank support have 
made borrowing more expensive and markets more discerning. 
 Debt sustainability depends primarily on the relationship between interest rates and growth, 
the primary balance, and the direction of debt. Affordability and refinancing needs also 
matter greatly. 
 Credibility, political stability, maturities of existing debt, the investor base, and access to 
central-bank support determine how much financial stress a country can absorb. 
 France faces a large primary deficit, rising debt, and political gridlock. Despite its higher debt 
ratio, Italy benefits from a primary surplus. 
 An acute debt crisis is not our baseline, given long maturities and European backstops. A 
gradual deterioration driven by rising interest costs is more likely. 
Bond markets are asking questions again 
Governments worldwide are borrowing at the highest interest rates in 15 to 20 years. In early 
October, the US 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.34%, its highest level since 2002. Japan’s 10-
year yield moved above 3% for the first time since 1996, while the German equivalent reached its 
highest level since 2011. 
Within Europe, France now pays its highest risk premium since the euro crisis. The spread 
between French and German 10-year yields, the OAT-Bund spread, rose above 170 basis points 
on 22 October, compared with roughly 55 in February.

This raises several questions. Why has market unease returned now? What determines whether a 
country can carry its debt? Why is France under particular scrutiny, while countries with even 
higher debt ratios are still given the benefit of the doubt? And are we heading toward another 
debt crisis? This article offers a framework for answering those questions and discusses the most 
likely scenario according to RaboResearch. 
Debt: A staircase that seems to go only one way 
Public debt has risen in steps over the past 15 years. With each shock, the global financial crisis, 
the euro crisis, the pandemic, and the 2022 energy crisis, governments absorbed the blow 
through larger deficits. In better years, debt ratios barely declined. According to the IMF, global 
public debt stood at almost 94% of GDP at the end of 2025 and is projected to reach 100% 
around 2029, a level seen only in the aftermath of World War II. Spending pressures are also 
increasing, including aging, defense, and the energy transition. 
Since 2005, debt ratios have risen particularly sharply in China, the United Kingdom, the United 
States, Spain, and Japan.

Interest rates: From free money to a new reality 
For years, that debt caused little pain. Interest rates were exceptionally low, and central banks 
absorbed a significant share of new issuance through quantitative easing. That changed in 2022. 
The inflation surge forced central banks to raise policy rates sharply, and they are now gradually 
shrinking their bond portfolios1. Private investors must therefore absorb more government paper, 
and they will do so only at a more attractive yield. 
Several additional forces have emerged recently, especially this year 
 Energy and inflation. Since the war between Iran and the United States began in late February, 
shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has been severely disrupted. Energy prices and inflation 
expectations rose. The ECB increased its deposit rate to 2.5% in June and September, and bond 
investors expect further tightening. 
 Defense, infrastructure, and now AI. The investment boom in data centers, semiconductors, 
and power grids requires enormous amounts of capital. Technology companies are issuing 
bonds at a record pace and may be competing with governments for savings. Many analysts, 
including ourselves, expect this to keep the yield curve higher for longer. 
 Fiscal concerns. Investors require more compensation for long-term lending to countries with 
large deficits and no credible adjustment plan. The IMF recently urged governments to act 
before markets force a more painful correction.

 Rising uncertainty. In a rapidly changing world, geopolitics and a growing “might makes right” 
dynamic are adding uncertainty. Will central banks remain independent? Will protectionism and 
war keep inflation higher? Such uncertainty can translate into larger sovereign risk premiums 
and higher required returns. 
Box 1 – What is debt, anyway? 
A country’s debt ratio, public debt as a share of GDP, is widely used but conceptually awkward. 
It divides a stock, total debt, by a flow, one year of income or output. It is like comparing a 
mortgage with one year’s salary while ignoring the interest rate, maturity, and annual savings. 
Debt2, including sovereign debt, is not only a burden. Every dollar of debt is someone else’s 
asset, such as the government bonds held by pension funds, insurers, banks, or citizens. Where 
a government borrows mainly from residents and domestic institutions, debt largely 
redistributes resources across groups and generations rather than representing an external 
claim. Governments also own infrastructure, equity stakes, and reserves. Net debt can therefore 
tell a different story. 
A modern, complex, and highly integrated world economy also needs more money and larger 
balance sheets, both assets and liabilities, than a simple economy. Government bonds act as a 
liquid store of value, boosting demand for debt denominated in widely used currencies, 
particularly the dollar and euro. This allows some countries to issue more debt, as their bonds 
enjoy stronger demand and are seen as safe-haven assets. 
The debt ratio, in itself, is therefore a poor stand-alone predictor of crises. Japan has carried 
gross public debt above 200% of GDP for years without a crisis. Ireland and Spain entered the 
euro crisis with government debt below 40%, but suffered when losses on risky private credit 
migrated onto the sovereign balance sheet. Some emerging markets encounter trouble at debt 
ratios of only 50% to 60%.  
The framework: When is debt sustainable? 
More important than the debt level itself are affordability, interest costs relative to revenue, 
annual financing needs, and above all the direction of debt. Debt is sustainable when a country 
can continue meeting its obligations without implausibly large spending cuts, external assistance, 
or restructuring. At its core, the IMF applies two tests: the fiscal balance required to stabilize debt 
must be economically and politically feasible, and refinancing risk must remain low. Sustainability 
is therefore about both the capacity and the willingness to pay.

the debt ratio d, debt divided by GDP, can be approximated as:

Interest rates versus growth 
When nominal growth exceeds the interest rate, r < g, a government can usually run a primary 
deficit while its debt ratio still declines, provided the deficit is not too large. This was the position 
of many countries during the past decade and helps explain why high debt caused limited 
concern. A RaboResearch calculation shows that a higher but stable debt ratio can even create 
additional fiscal space in this setting. But that benefit is fragile: in the example, a 0.3 percentage 
point increase in interest rates eliminates it. 
When the differential reverses, r > g, the result is a snowball effect. Interest costs grow faster than 
the economy and the debt ratio rises unless the government runs a sufficiently large primary 
surplus. The higher the starting debt ratio, the more powerful the effect and the larger the 
offsetting surplus must be. 
Box 2: Interest rates and growth, a dynamic duo 
A complication is that growth and interest rates cannot be viewed independently. If rates rise 
because the growth outlook is improving, that is not necessarily a reason for concern. 
Under intertemporal consumption theory, a higher real interest rate makes saving more 
attractive relative to current consumption. Higher saving and investment may be associated 
with stronger future growth. Inflation also tends to coincide with higher required bond yields. 
In practice, long time series show a clear relationship between a country’s nominal interest rate 
and nominal GDP growth, as the figures below illustrate.

The debt-stabilizing balance and the fiscal gap 
The equation implies that the primary balance required to keep the debt ratio constant is 
approximately (r − g) × d. The difference between that balance and the actual primary balance is 
the fiscal gap: the structural adjustment needed to stabilize debt, or, when favorable, the 
additional room a government can use. A decade ago, the global primary balance was more than 
1% of GDP stronger than needed to stabilize debt, according to the IMF. That buffer has now 
almost disappeared. 
Consider France. This year’s deficit is about 5.4% of GDP, including 2.6% in interest costs, leaving a 
primary deficit of roughly 2.8%. Even if interest rates and growth were equal, the balance would 
need to improve by about 3% of GDP, more than €90 billion, to stabilize debt. Italy has much 
higher debt but already runs a small primary surplus, so its gap is far smaller.

Why an interest-rate shock takes time to feed through 
Higher market rates do not affect the budget immediately. Existing bonds retain their fixed 
coupons until maturity; only new and refinanced debt becomes more expensive. The longer the 
average maturity, the slower the shock, but also the longer its effects persist.  
In the Netherlands, almost €150 billion of government bonds with coupons below 1% will mature 
by 2032. RaboResearch estimates that this helps push interest costs from 0.7% of GDP today to 
almost 1.8% in 2035. The IMF warns that many advanced economies have shortened maturities to 
reduce current interest expense and term premiums being paid: nearly 20% of debt in the G7 and 
Spain now matures within one year, increasing their future sensitivity to rate swings. 
Stress factors and the risk perimeter 
The equation describes the mechanics of the debt ratio, but its inputs are uncertain. Around the 
equation sit two layers: stress factors that affect the variables directly, and risk factors that 
determine how well a country can absorb that stress. 
Stress factors: What can go wrong? 
 Higher interest rates. A permanent 1 percentage point increase in rates, all else equal, 
eventually raises annual interest costs by roughly 1% multiplied by the debt ratio. At a 120% 
debt ratio, that is 1.2% of GDP per year. 
 Lower growth. A recession shrinks GDP, reduces tax revenue, and increases spending. The debt 
ratio can rise by several percentage points in a single year. 
 Fiscal slippage. Spending may exceed plans or revenue may disappoint. France missed its 
deficit target in three of the four years from 2023 through 2026. 
 Hidden liabilities. Guarantees to other countries or emergency support for banks can suddenly 
migrate onto the government balance sheet. This is how Ireland’s banking crisis became a 
sovereign-debt crisis.

The risk perimeter: Who can absorb the stress? 
Two countries with identical debt and deficit figures can be assessed very differently because of 
factors outside the equation: 
 Political willingness. Will the government tighten policy when necessary, and how much is that 
ability constrained by political fragmentation? 
 Credibility and track record. Does a country deliver what it promises? In several southern 
European countries, budget outcomes have often been slightly weaker than projected, which 
can erode trust. Italy, by contrast, has run a primary surplus in almost every year for decades, 
supporting confidence despite high debt. 
 Currency and central bank. Countries borrowing in their own currency with their own central 
bank, including the United States, Japan, the United Kingdom, and Australia, can ultimately 
always pay in nominal terms – by printing money – though potentially at the cost of inflation. 
Eurozone countries borrow in a currency they cannot issue individually, making them more 
vulnerable to panic. 
 Financing structure. Maturity, the share of short-term or inflation-linked debt, and the investor 
base all matter. Domestic investors are generally more stable; foreign investors, including hedge 
funds, may leave more quickly. 
 Affordability and financing needs. The higher interest costs are relative to revenue, the weaker 
affordability becomes. Where the tax burden is already high, raising additional revenue is 
harder. A large gross financing need, the deficit plus redemptions, increases sensitivity to rates 
and liquidity risk. The European Commission uses 16% of GDP as a threshold for a high 
financing need. 
 Quality of growth. Productivity- and investment-led growth is more durable than inflation-led 
growth, especially when inflation results from a supply shock such as higher oil prices. Aging 
economies with weak productivity growth have less debt-carrying capacity. 
 Institutions and politics. Can parliament pass a budget? Are fiscal rules credible and is the fiscal 
watchdog independent? A strong central bank and emergency mechanisms such as the ECB and 
ESM can provide a backstop.

Fundamentals, markets, and politics: How a crisis 
develops 
Debt crises rarely arise from weak numbers alone. They emerge from the interaction of 
fundamentals, markets, and politics. Traditional sustainability analyses often treat the interest rate 
as given. In reality, rates respond to policy and sentiment, creating feedback loops. 
The interest-rate doom loop 
Doubts about fiscal policy raise the risk premium. Higher rates increase interest costs and the 
deficit. Closing that gap requires larger spending cuts or tax increases, which are politically 
difficult and can weaken growth. That fuels further doubt. Debt that is sustainable at a 3% interest 
rate may become unsustainable at 6%, allowing markets to make their own forecast come true. 
A second vicious loop runs through banks, which often hold large amounts of their own 
government’s bonds. Falling bond prices weaken bank balance sheets, hurting the economy and 
ultimately the sovereign. This was central to the euro crisis, though bank balance sheets have 
since been strengthened considerably. 
Politics as the trigger 
Almost every recent episode began with a political event rather than a sudden change in fiscal 
data: 
 Greece, 2009–2010: disclosure that fiscal figures had been manipulated. 
 Italy, 2018: a new government including Lega and the Five Star Movement openly challenged 
European fiscal rules. In 2022, markets were initially uncertain about the Meloni government, 
but it ultimately adopted a pro-EU course. 
 United Kingdom, 2022: the Liz Truss episode, in which unfunded tax cuts triggered a chain 
reaction through the government bond portfolios of British pension funds. 
 France, 2024–2026: snap elections produced a fragmented parliament, successive government 
collapses, and a reversal of pension reform. 
What the French yield curve is telling us 
France’s risk premium is concentrated at medium to longer-term maturities. On October 21, the 
two-year spread over Germany was about 60 basis points, versus roughly 160bp at 10 years.

Backstops: What can institutions do? 
Since the euro crisis, Europe has built a substantial set of backstops. They reduce the probability 
of a crisis but are deliberately conditional: they buy time, not sustainability. 
The ECB 
 The Transmission Protection Instrument, introduced in 2022, can in principle support unlimited 
bond purchases when a country’s yield or spread rises in an unwarranted and disorderly 
manner. Eligibility includes compliance with EU fiscal rules and sustainable debt. TPI has never 
been used. 
 Outright Monetary Transactions, introduced in 2012, permit unlimited purchases, but only if the 
country accepts an ESM support program. 
 Less dramatic tools include flexibility in reinvestments and the pace of balance-sheet reduction 
(relating to their sovereign bond portfolio). These would likely be used earlier, as could the 
policy-rate instrument provided price stability is not jeopardized. 
 Perhaps most important is that these tools are available if all else fails. The ECB is likely to 
emphasize their availability before actually deploying them. Mario Draghi could calm markets in 
2012 by promising to do “whatever it takes” to save the Eurozone. 
The ESM, IMF, and fiscal rules 
The European Stability Mechanism (ESM) can provide loans or a precautionary credit line, while 
the IMF performs a similar role globally. Both normally demand reforms and discipline, a 
politically costly step governments delay as long as possible. The ESM’s precautionary credit line 
does not itself impose new reform or austerity conditions, but is available only to countries that 
comply with fiscal rules and have sustainable debt. The EU fiscal framework, which since 2024 has 
centered on a multiyear expenditure path, is not a backstop in its own right. It is, however, a 
gateway: countries that do not comply are not eligible for TPI. 
The risk of fiscal dominance 
There is another, less visible escape route, and it is risky. Under fiscal dominance, the central bank 
is pressured to keep rates low to protect the government even when inflation does not justify 
doing so. A related strategy is financial repression: forcing banks and pension funds to hold 
government bonds while keeping interest rates below inflation. High postwar debt ratios were 
reduced in large part through these mechanisms, with savers paying through inflation. For 
investors, this may be the largest long-term risk: not outright default, but a quiet erosion of 
bonds’ real value. 
Warning signs include political pressure on central banks and a more “flexible” interpretation of 
the inflation target. For now, this appears more relevant in the United States than in Europe, but 
nothing is set in stone.

Box 3 – Will the ECB intervene in France? 
Probably not, particularly in the near term, for four reasons. 
First, the market concern is largely justified. TPI is intended to counter panic not explained by 
fundamentals. France’s spread reflects a large deficit, rising debt, and political uncertainty, 
exactly the risks markets are supposed to price. 
Second, it is unclear whether France meets TPI’s fiscal conditions5. The European Commission 
has not formally concluded that France violated the rules in 2026. Looking ahead, however, 
France will need a credible 2027 budget that is sufficiently stringent to show continued 
compliance with the EU-mandated adjustment path. 
Third, the market is functioning in an orderly manner. France can still borrow and demand 
remains adequate, though at a higher rate. Short-term yields are comparatively low and there is 
no broad contagion to other eurozone members. 
Fourth, intervention would create moral hazard. Acting before the budget is adopted would 
relieve pressure on Paris. Lagarde said last week that the solution lies primarily with France itself. 
The calculation would change only if spreads rose disorderly and contagion spread to other 
countries while France had adopted a credible budget. Even then, the likely sequence would be 
verbal reassurance first, a slower pace of balance-sheet reduction second, and targeted 
purchases only as a last resort. An OMT program, requiring an ESM package, is politically almost 
inconceivable before the April 2027 presidential election.

France. A textbook illustration of the framework. The debt level is not exceptional, but its direction 
is. With a deficit of about 5.4% and real growth near 0.5%, the debt ratio is rising quickly. The 
2027 budget includes €54 billion in measures intended to reduce the deficit to 5%. That would 
slow, but not stop, the increase in debt. A minority government must also steer the plan through 
a fragmented parliament. The fiscal watchdog considers the growth assumptions optimistic, and 
presidential elections are due in April 2027. France has the capacity to adjust; markets question 
the political willingness. 
Italy. Higher debt, but better dynamics: a primary surplus and an overall deficit around 3%, with a 
possible exit from the EU excessive-deficit procedure. A stable government and large domestic 
investor base help. Growth remains the weakness. If r − g turns unfavorable, the margin for error 
is small. Elections in 2027 add some uncertainty. 
Spain. Strong real growth of 2.8% in 2025 reduced the debt ratio to about 101% despite a deficit. 
The risk is institutional: Spain has gone years without a new budget and now faces snap elections. 
Growth gives Spain room that France lacks. 
Germany. Germany does not face a sustainability problem, but its fiscal regime is shifting. Reform 
of the debt brake, including a €500 billion infrastructure fund and largely exempting defense 
spending, is set to raise debt toward 80% of GDP. More Bund issuance increases the eurozone’s 
“risk-free” rate, raising funding costs even for healthy countries. Pension and health-care reforms 
will also be needed to manage rapid aging. 
Netherlands. At about 44% of GDP, the Netherlands has one of the lowest debt ratios in the 
OECD. Even so, refinancing cheap debt raises interest costs, and RaboResearch estimates that the 
increase in rates since the Iran war will add a cumulative €17 billion through 2035. 
Belgium. Belgium stands out for weak fundamentals: high debt combined with a structural deficit. 
Fiscal consolidation is often impeded by the federal system. However, debt is conservatively 
financed at long maturities and the private sector is wealthy. The European Commission sees 
comparatively high risks over both the medium and long term. Investors see Belgium as having a 
very low Euro exit risk. 
United States. The least comfortable numbers, but the greatest flexibility. The deficit exceeds 6%, 
debt is above $40 trillion, and annual interest expense is around $1 trillion. The IMF projects gross 
debt at 142% of GDP in 2031. The dollar’s reserve-currency role and the world’s deepest capital 
market provide structural demand. Key risks are political gridlock and pressure on Federal Reserve 
independence.

United Kingdom. Public debt and the budget deficit are both relatively high, compounded by a 
heavy reliance on foreign financing. On the other hand, the UK has a clear fiscal framework aimed 
at stabilizing public finances. This does require the government to stick to its plans: any doubts 
about its commitment can quickly unsettle markets, as happened under Liz Truss in 2022. High 
interest rates are the Achilles’ heel. Moreover, without additional policy measures, public debt is 
set to rise sharply again over the long term. 
Japan. The exception that proves the rule. Gross debt is around 200% of GDP, but is held largely 
by domestic investors and the central bank, while the government owns substantial assets. With 
inflation back, nominal growth exceeds the effective interest rate and the debt ratio is falling. Still, 
rates are normalizing, and Prime Minister Takaichi’s expansionary policies are making investors 
nervous. Volatility in Japanese rates affects markets worldwide. 
Brazil. A debt ratio that might be manageable for an advanced economy is more constraining 
here. The policy rate is around 14% to 15%, and much of the debt is linked to it, so interest costs 
adjust quickly. The IMF recommends adjustment of 3% of GDP over five years. Borrowing in 
domestic currency reduces the risk of a classic currency crisis, but the risk of fiscal dominance is 
real. 
Australia. Low debt, an AAA rating, and deficits near 1% of GDP6. Yet the 10-year yield rose above 
5%, reflecting the global rate shock and domestic central-bank tightening. Australia shows that 
the current rise in yields is not solely about debt sustainability: fiscally sound countries are paying 
more too. 
Conclusion: Are we heading for another debt crisis? 
Not imminently, but many countries have lost their fiscal room for maneuver. No major country 
has lost market access, debt maturities are generally long, and Europe has backstops that did not 
exist in 2010. A euro-crisis-style panic would require more than a high spread: it would take a 
political rupture, contagion to other countries, and a central bank that could not or would not 
intervene. 
A slow-moving crisis is more likely. Interest costs will absorb a growing share of budgets, 
crowding out investment, defense, and social spending. That creates political tension, and markets 
are increasingly pricing that tension. The main lessons are: 
 Do not focus on the debt ratio alone. Watch r − g, the primary balance, and the direction of 
debt. 
 Interest rates and growth are not fixed inputs; they respond to policy and sentiment. However, a 
country’s ability to influence these – especially in the short-term – is pretty limited. 
 Politics is the key trigger. Credible governments are given time; governments that lose 
credibility pay for it. 
Key developments to monitor include votes on the French budget, rating-agency decisions, 
Spain’s elections, the run-up to the French presidential election, and long-term U.S. and Japanese 
yields as gauges of the global term premium.
