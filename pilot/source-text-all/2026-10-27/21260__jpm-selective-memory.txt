We remain constructive on semiconductor fundamentals but more 
selective on credit. Strong operating performance has not translated into 
consistent spread outperformance, with issuer-specific risks, financing 
technicals and relative valuations increasingly driving our views.
•
AI demand remains the primary growth driver. Accelerating hyperscaler 
capex continues to support strong semiconductor demand, with benefits 
extending beyond GPUs to custom ASICs, networking, memory and advanced 
packaging. Growing inference workloads and efforts to improve compute 
efficiency should provide additional opportunities as AI infrastructure 
deployments expand.
•
Financing risks are becoming increasingly important. The proliferation of 
GPU-backed financing, supplier guarantees and other alternative funding 
structures is raising questions around contingent liabilities and the 
sustainability of AI investment. Recent weakness at CoreWeave has 
intensified scrutiny of these arrangements, particularly for NVIDIA and 
Broadcom, although we expect well-capitalized hyperscalers to remain 
supportive of underlying demand.
•
Non-AI end markets are recovering unevenly. Automotive and Industrial 
demand continues to improve, supported by inventory normalization and 
rising semiconductor content, while PC demand remains mixed. Mobile 
remains comparatively weak, with higher memory costs and supply constraints 
creating additional headwinds for consumer-facing suppliers.
•
Strong fundamentals have not translated into uniform credit 
outperformance. Semiconductor spreads have diverged YTD, with AI-
exposed issuers facing greater pressure despite accelerating earnings growth. 
We expect financing technicals, contingent liabilities and relative valuations to 
remain important drivers of spread performance, supporting a selective rather 
than sector-wide approach.
•
China remains an important source of both demand and uncertainty. 
Domestic semiconductor investment and efforts to develop a more self-
sufficient chip industry continue to support demand for equipment and 
components, while U.S. export controls and broader trade restrictions present 
risks for suppliers with meaningful China exposure. We expect geopolitical 
tensions, potential changes to export licensing requirements and competition 
from domestic Chinese manufacturers to remain important considerations, 
particularly for advanced computing and semiconductor equipment suppliers.
•
Domestic semiconductor manufacturing remains a strategic priority. Intel 
continues to advance its foundry strategy, with progress on its 18A process and 
development of 14A critical to securing external customers and improving 
manufacturing economics. More broadly, government incentives, geopolitical 
concerns and efforts to diversify supply chains are supporting investment in 
U.S. semiconductor capacity, creating opportunities for equipment suppliers 
and domestic manufacturers.

Spread Performance & Trade Ideas
Semiconductor credit performance has been mixed YTD, with widening dispersion 
despite generally constructive fundamentals. Weakness has been concentrated among 
larger AI infrastructure beneficiaries, as substantial investment requirements, financing 
needs and concerns around the sustainability of AI spending have pressured spreads. 
Broadcom and NVIDIA have widened particularly meaningfully amid scrutiny of AI 
financing structures and potential obligations for semiconductor suppliers. 
Performance elsewhere has been more resilient, with traditional equipment, analog 
and diversified chipmakers trading in a narrower range. This divergence suggests 
investors are increasingly focused on the financial implications of AI investment rather 
than simply rewarding exposure to compute demand.
Outside of AI, performance has been more differentiated, reflecting company-
specific fundamentals and AI financing exposure. Intel and Marvell have tightened 
YTD, while Dell has outperformed much of the AI-exposed universe. Qualcomm and 
NXP have widened modestly, while semiconductor equipment issuers have seen limited 
spread movement despite favorable leading-edge manufacturing trends. Overall, 
performance has not necessarily tracked earnings momentum, with technical factors, 
relative valuations and potential financing requirements increasingly influencing 
spreads.
We continue to favor selective opportunities offering sufficient spread compensation for company-specific risks or 
improving fundamentals. Our Overweight ratings on Intel, Marvell and Skyworks reflect this preference, while our Underweight 
positions on Broadcom, NVIDIA and AMD reflect valuation concerns and an expectation for sustained AI financing pressures. 
We also remain Underweight several mature semiconductor and distribution issuers with limited catalysts for meaningful 
tightening. We expect dispersion to persist, with incremental AI financing and greater scrutiny of capital commitments 
remaining important spread drivers despite healthy semiconductor demand and earnings growth.

AI Infrastructure Investment & Semiconductor Demand
Hyperscaler capex continues to accelerate as Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft expand their multi-GW AI data 
center footprints, driving compute, networking and power infrastructure demand. All four are developing custom ASICs 
optimized for their workloads, with Broadcom and Marvell emerging as key partners. Amazon’s Trainium accelerators, co-
designed with Marvell, support training and inference, while its Graviton processors use Arm-based CPUs. As of 2Q, Amazon’s 
chip business had reached a $25bn run rate.
Broadcom has expanded its Alphabet relationship through multi-generational custom TPU agreements, building on an 
established partnership. Google also maintains a relationship with Marvell that could generate $120bn of revenue through 
2033. Microsoft is developing Maia accelerators with Marvell, with Maia 300 expected to launch in the coming weeks. Meta 
continues expanding its Broadcom partnership, with MTIA 450 scheduled for early 2027 deployment and additional generations 
expected later that year. Despite growing custom ASIC adoption, hyperscalers remain significant buyers of NVIDIA and AMD 
GPUs as they diversify compute supply.
Increasingly complex AI workloads are driving greater emphasis on networking performance, infrastructure utilization 
and cost efficiency. Broadcom, Marvell and NVIDIA are benefiting from demand for high-speed connectivity, with Ethernet 
adoption and rising bandwidth requirements driving demand for silicon, optical interconnects and custom networking solutions. 
Meanwhile, inference is expanding demand beyond model training, increasing the importance of memory bandwidth, power 
efficiency and cost per token. We expect inference to become an increasingly important semiconductor demand driver, 
benefiting GPUs, custom ASICs and networking silicon, although increasingly specialized architectures could intensify 
competition.
AI infrastructure investment is also supporting strong demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM), conventional server 
DRAM and advanced packaging, extending capex benefits beyond accelerator suppliers. HBM remains a key growth driver 
for Micron, with rising memory content per accelerator and increasingly complex chip architectures supporting demand. AI server 
deployments are also tightening conventional DRAM supply and supporting higher pricing. Meanwhile, demand for leading-edge 
foundry capacity and advanced packaging is benefiting semiconductor equipment suppliers such as Applied Materials, Lam 
Research and KLA. However, capacity expansion and the prioritization of AI-related manufacturing could create additional 
supply constraints across other semiconductor end markets.

AI Infrastructure Financing & Credit Implications
AI financing structures are becoming increasingly complex, with greater reliance on strategic partnerships, credit 
backstops and residual value guarantees. NVIDIA has become particularly active, partnering with Apollo, BlackRock, 
Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR on a $500bn financing platform to expand access to third-party capital. While 
hyperscalers generally provide these backstops, NVIDIA has agreed to offer residual value guarantees of up to 25% on 
certain transactions, implying maximum potential exposure of $125bn. Recently, Broadcom has similarly leveraged its 
balance sheet to support a $35bn debt financing package, involving Alphabet and a large customer, supplying custom Google 
TPUs and guaranteeing customer payments for the silicon. A follow-on financing is reportedly being marketed, with an 
offering expected in the coming weeks to support additional TPU deployments under the same customer relationship.  The 
package will reportedly include ~$42bn of senior secured debt supported by a Broadcom residual value guarantee (RVG) and ~
$18bn of junior debt led by Blackstone.
Separately, Oracle and SpaceX are reportedly pursuing large-scale GPU financing arrangements to support NVIDIA chip 
purchases. Oracle is reportedly in discussions with Apollo and Goldman Sachs, while SpaceX is seeking ~$40bn of financing, 
including $30bn of IG debt and $10bn of bank loans. Broadcom is also reportedly arranging more than $50bn of financing for 
custom accelerators developed with OpenAI, separate from its existing Google TPU arrangement under the XPV platform. While 
final structures remain uncertain, we expect these transactions to broadly resemble the Apollo-backed model, with third-party 
investors funding hardware purchases and customers making payments over time. We view these arrangements as credit positive 
for Oracle, potentially reducing incremental unsecured borrowing, although SpaceX’s substantial funding needs across other 
businesses could limit the benefits.
The sharp selloff in CoreWeave debt and equity last week has heightened scrutiny of debt-funded AI infrastructure 
investment and potential implications for semiconductor suppliers. CoreWeave’s leverage, capital requirements and reliance 
on external financing raise concerns around the broader neocloud model, with potential GPU demand implications if financing 
constraints delay deployments. NVIDIA faces the greatest direct exposure as CoreWeave’s primary GPU supplier, equity 
investor and contracted capacity purchaser, raising questions around additional financial support and similar arrangements 
across the AI ecosystem. While restructuring is not our base case, a severe liquidity event could disrupt capacity deployments, 
pressure chip orders and increase scrutiny of supplier-backed financing. We remain constructive on semiconductor fundamentals, 
although tighter financing conditions among smaller, leveraged developers could increasingly constrain growth.
The pace of AI monetization remains an important consideration as infrastructure investment outpaces revenue and cash 
flow generation among frontier model developers. While adoption and commercial opportunities continue to expand, 
substantial training and deployment costs raise questions around the sustainability of current spending. More broadly, slower 
spending by major AI developers could pressure demand visibility across GPUs, networking silicon and memory, although 
continued hyperscaler investment should provide an important offset. While most chip suppliers remain insulated from these 
financing arrangements, we expect GPU-backed financing to accelerate in the coming months, with NVIDIA, Broadcom and 
potentially AMD participating in additional transactions as AI infrastructure funding needs expand.

improved production volumes to provide additional support. ADAS, software-defined vehicles, electrification and connectivity

suppliers anticipate further restocking as production recovers.

Explanation of Credit Research Valuation Methodology, Ratings and Risk to Ratings:
 J.P. Morgan uses a bond-level rating system that incorporates valuations (relative value) and our fundamental view on the security. Our 
fundamental credit view of an issuer is based on the company's underlying credit trends, overall creditworthiness and our opinion on whether the 
issuer will be able to service its debt obligations when they become due and payable. We analyze, among other things, the company's cash flow 
capacity and trends and standard credit ratios, such as gross and net leverage, interest coverage and liquidity ratios. We also analyze profitability, 
capitalization and asset quality, among other variables, when assessing financials. Analysts also rate the issuer, based on the rating of the 
benchmark or representative security. Unless we specify a different recommendation for the company’s individual securities, an issuer 
recommendation applies to all of the bonds at the same level of the issuer’s capital structure. We may also rate certain loans and preferred 
securities, as applicable. This report also sets out within it the material underlying assumptions used. We use the following ratings for bonds 
(issues), issuers, loans, and preferred securities: Overweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to 
outperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark); Neutral (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to perform 
in line with the relevant index, sector, or benchmark); and Underweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected 
to underperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark). For CDS, we use the following rating system: Long Risk (over the next three months, 
the credit return on the recommended position is expected to exceed the relevant index, sector or benchmark); Neutral (over the next three 
months, the credit return on the recommended position is expected to match the relevant index, sector or benchmark); and Short Risk (over the 
next three months, the credit return on the recommended position is expected to underperform the relevant index, sector or benchmark).
