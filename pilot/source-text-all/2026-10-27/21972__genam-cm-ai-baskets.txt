Artificial Intelligence (AI) has evolved into a broad investment cycle spanning infra-
structure, software and productivity-enhancing applications. This report is structured as 
follows. Section 1 assesses whether current demand trends, including token consump-
tion, enterprise adoption and data-centre expansion, justify the scale of AI-related in-
vestment currently underway. Section 2 reviews the main risks facing the AI invest-
ment cycle, including monetisation, financing, competition, regulation and infrastructure 
constraints. Section 3 examines how these opportunities and risks are reflected in cur-
rent market valuations, with a particular focus on hyperscalers and the broader US 
technology sector. Building on this analysis, Section 4 introduces our proprietary in-
vestment framework, which divides the AI ecosystem into four value-capture layers 
and forms the basis of our thematic AI baskets. Finally, in Appendix we extend the 
framework to China and Europe, highlighting regional opportunities, risks and diversi-
fication benefits. 
1. AI demand: so far, it justifies the capex 
AI is driving one of the largest investment cycles in modern technology history. Global 
AI-related infrastructure investment in 2026 is expected to be around $1 trillion, 
of which the largest part is in the US1. The key investment question is whether AI de-
mand can justify the scale of capital expenditure currently underway. So far, the evi-
dence is encouraging. 
1.1 Token consumption 
Token consumption has become the main unit of AI demand. Unlike traditional 
software, typically measured by seats or subscriptions, AI usage is increasingly meas-
ured in tokens: text, code, images and multimodal inputs processed by models. As AI 
expands into copilots, coding, search, analytics, customer support and, most of all, 
agents, token volumes grow much faster than the user base.

1.2 AI adoption 
According to the Stanford AI Index report, 88% of surveyed organisations reported 
using AI in 2025, while 70% used generative AI in at least one business function. Dif-
fusion has also been faster than for Personal Computers or the internet, reaching 53% 
adoption in the last three years, as also testified by S&P500 CEOs in the Q2 2026 
reporting season. 
AI adoption is broad but shallow: experimentation is already widespread, while 
scaled deployment, workflow redesign and measurable financial returns remain at an 
early stage. A survey of 396 enterprises by Mavvrik and Benchmarkit found that, de-
spite widespread cost tracking and formal budgets, only 11% could forecast AI spend-
ing within a ±10% margin of accuracy. Unexpected AI costs are increasingly affecting 
margins and business decisions. Furthermore, many firms are still in pilot mode, and 
AI agents are only starting to enter enterprise workflows, suggesting that demand is 
still early in its cycle. The first wave came from experimentation, coding, content and 
knowledge work. The next wave will see deeper process integration, vertical applica-
tions and agentic/autonomous workflows. KAIST – a Korean Institute - estimates that 
if daily agent requests matched Google search volumes, AI would add ~200 GW of 
power demand — over 40% of average US grid load. This is an illustrative upper bound 
rather than a forecast: it assumes static efficiency and full agent adoption.

1.3 Data centre expansion 
Data-centre expansion is the physical backbone of the AI opportunity. It also brings 
financing, cooling and operational challenges into focus. McKinsey estimates that 
global data-centre capacity demand could rise from approximately 82 GW in 2025 
to 219 GW by 2030. AI-related capacity is expected to increase from around 44 GW to 
156 GW, accounting for roughly 70% of total demand by the end of the decade. Brokers’ 
estimate of token utilisation rising from nearly 100 trillion in 2026 to almost 250 trillion 
in 2027 looks broadly consistent with the expected increase in installed GW capacity 
by 2030. The build-out is visible also in China2, which has around 24 GW of operational 
data-centre computing capacity and a further 50 GW under construction or announced, 
highlighting the increasingly global scale of the infrastructure race.  
2. AI risks: what could derail the cycle 
The AI investment cycle faces structural risks that could affect the pace and profit-
ability of deployment: there is the need to monitor financing, monetisation, competition 
and regulation, in addition to increasing infrastructure and environmental-political con-
straints. The momentum in analysts’ revisions to revenues, margins and operating prof-
its (currently still positive) is also fundamental. 
2.1 Financing risk  
As hyperscalers’ once-abundant free cash flow is increasingly absorbed by surging 
capex, AI investment is being funded more through debt. On average, rising issu-
ance has driven hyperscalers’ credit spreads from 55bps a year ago to 127bps today. 
The widening comes on top of already elevated Treasury yields, as large fiscal deficits 
and the AI capex supercycle reinforce upward pressure on long-term real rates – the 
fight for capital. The Bank for International Settlements has identified AI as a potential 
source of financial-system fragility, given the scale of investment, rising leverage and 
increasingly complex financing structures. According to the Wall Street Journal, the off-
balance sheet commitments by Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft amount to 
$2.4tn, four times the $604bn recorded on their balance sheets. Big US Tech debt also 
accounts for nearly 1% of benchmark indices for euro-denominated corporate bonds, 
with $40bn of bonds, and 10% of gross issuance in euros. 
Should returns disappoint, a repricing of risk could tighten financing conditions and 
slow infrastructure investment, reducing productivity gains. The key issue is therefore 
whether AI capex can generate durable revenues and acceptable returns on capital, 
and whether such risks are already sufficiently priced in by the market (see chapter 3 
on valuation).

2.2 Competition risk 
Competition is intensifying across the AI ecosystem. Lower-cost Chinese models, ad-
vances in semiconductor capabilities and the wider availability of open-source solutions 
are challenging incumbent US providers and encouraging companies to consider 
cheaper alternatives to proprietary systems.  This is accelerating commoditisation 
and driving prices down. Enterprises are therefore likely to optimise their model usage 
by deploying cheaper open-weight models for simpler tasks, and reserving frontier 
models for the most advanced or demanding applications. This shift is already visible 
in usage patterns: in August, open-weight models accounted for 56% of all tokens pro-
cessed through Vercel’s AI Gateway, up from just 7% in December3. 
Lower prices could accelerate adoption but also compress margins and weaken pric-
ing power among hyperscalers, model developers and semiconductor companies. The 
key risk is not weaker AI demand, but a redistribution of economic value across the 
value chain, including towards newer entrants, in ways that differ from current market 
expectations. 
2.3 Regulatory risk 
Rules governing AI safety, data privacy, intellectual property and competition may 
increase compliance costs, delay deployment, restrict data access or limit specific ap-
plications. Regulatory fragmentation across jurisdictions could further complicate im-
plementation and reduce the scalability of global business models. The US midterms 
election will matter, too. A Democratic sweep of Congress in November 2026 could 
increase regulatory scrutiny across the AI value chain, including on data-centre devel-
opment, permitting, power consumption and water usage. For now, President Trump 
would likely veto any regulatory tightening, but investors may be looking forward to the 
2028 elections and fear a tightening from 2029.  
2.4 Infrastructure and Environmental constraints 
Agentic AI mass adoption may push data-centre emissions to 1.2 Gt by 2030, accord-
ing to Morgan Stanley, above Japan's annual emissions. The sustainability case rests 
on whether AI-enabled GHG savings can reach up to 5.4 Gt, as recent research sug-
gests. Power, cooling and water availability are becoming critical constraints on AI ex-
pansion. Goldman Sachs Research expects data-centre power demand to rise by 
around 220% from 2023 to 2030. Data-centre supply may struggle to keep pace: there 
could be a potential 20%-30% US power shortage versus demand through 2028. 
According to the Stanford AI Index, AI data-centre capacity is comparable to New 
York State’s peak electricity demand, while the annual water consumption

3. US AI valuation: what is priced in? 
Large-scale capex commitments continue to support demand for semiconductors, 
data-centre infrastructure, cloud capacity, power equipment and other “pick-and-
shovel” segments of the AI value chain. These areas still look better supported than IT 
services and software. That said, after a period of sharp underperformance, software 
companies started to recover through the Q2 earnings season, also following signs that 
AI adoption is less of a threat than feared and could instead support revenue 
growth and margin expansion. Microsoft has provided the clearest evidence, with 
stronger backlogs, higher cloud and computing power revenues, improved free-cash 
flow and better guidance despite rising capex. Other hyperscalers also reported accel-
eration in cloud computing revenues and data centre leasing. Moreover, over the past 
three months, 2027–28 sales forecasts for hyperscalers and the 23 largest IT stocks 
have increased.  
However, the scale of spending raises the bar for execution and extends the payback 
period. Scrutiny is highest for the largest hyperscalers, whose sharp capex increases 
will weigh on near-term free cash flow. Funding needs are moving more visibly toward 
debt and equity markets, putting pressure on valuations – i.e. lower PE multiples – 
through wider credit spreads which add to structurally higher bond yields.

3.2 Is the capex-driven EPS boost overstated? At least partially priced-in 
Are we underestimating the EPS boost resulting from an unusually capital-inten-
sive period and the associated front-loaded earnings momentum? The capex 
wave will inevitably slow, and profit growth with it. AI infrastructure companies have 
benefited immediately from new orders, while their clients – mostly hyperscalers - will 
spread these costs over four to five years through depreciation, which can even under-
state costs given GPU obsolescence. This has created a front-loaded earnings boost. 
In addition, cross-participations and the Tech rally have generated capital gains that 
have further supported earnings.

4. US AI baskets: investing along the value chain 
The AI investment case is not a single trade but a progression along the value chain. 
Value may transition from the most visible earnings beneficiaries, the Magnificent 7 
(Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla), to infrastructure providers, 
then to companies monetising AI through products, and finally to broader productivity 
beneficiaries. A basket approach helps reduce concentration risk while maintaining ex-
posure to the full AI opportunity set. 
Our framework is based on the detection of four phases, which are to be intended 
as “value-capture layers” or “waves” rather than chronological phases: 
- 
Phase 1 – Precursors (Magnificent 7): validates AI as an earnings and capex 
theme, with Nvidia monetising computing scarcity and the broader Magnificent 7 
amplifying computing demand by building AI capacity and applications. 
- 
Phase 2 – AI infrastructure: industrialises AI through semiconductors to build 
data centres, providing power, cooling, networking, storage, electrical equipment 
and materials. 
- 
Phase 3 – New AI revenues: commercialises AI through software, cybersecu-
rity, data platforms, consulting, agents, copilots and usage-based revenue 
streams. 
- 
Phase 4 - Enhanced productivity: captures the broader diffusion of AI across 
the economy, where companies use AI internally to reduce costs, automate work-
flows and improve margins. 
The investment implication is that profit visibility declines, but breadth increases as 
the theme moves through the phases. Phase 1 has the clearest earnings signal; Phase 
2 has the strongest capex linkage; Phase 3 depends on proven monetisation and pric-
ing power; Phase 4 is broader, as productivity gains propagate. 
Our eligible universe comprises US liquid listed companies meeting a minimum mar-
ket-capitalisation of $1 billion. Companies are selected on a combination of metrics: 
exposure to AI-related revenues, infrastructure investment, products or potential oper-
ating-efficiency gains. Phase 1 groups the Magnificent 7 based on scale, first-mover 
advantage and market leadership, while subsequent phases classify companies ac-
cording to their dominant expected source of AI-related value creation. The baskets are 
equally weighted. Valuation indicators are based on median company-level multiples 
(i.e. PE, using 12-month forward consensus earnings).

driven. Nevertheless, their forward PE ratio has fallen materially from earlier peaks of

4.2 Phase 2 - AI infrastructure: the industrialisation of AI – 94 stocks 
Phase   captures the “picks-and-shovels” of AI  It includes 94 companies enabling

risk is that infrastructure capex runs out of steam if hyperscalers struggle to monetise 
the giant investments.

heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC), heat exchangers and thermal-man-
agement systems. Cooling is also a reminder that many AI beneficiaries sit outside 
traditional technology sectors. Industrial companies with expertise in climate control, 
heat transfer and energy management are increasingly becoming critical enablers of AI 
adoption. Our Phase 2b basket includes 10 companies most of which operate in the 
capital goods sector.  
4.3 Phase 3 - New revenues from AI: turning AI into products – 39 stocks 
Phase 3 shifts the focus from building AI infrastructure to monetising AI through prod-
ucts and services. Our basket includes 39 firms, mostly in the software space. They 
seek to embed AI into new software, cybersecurity, data platforms, consulting, agents 
and workflow tools, turning AI into recurring revenues. They offer tools enabling others 
to utilize AI, while demonstrating AI product packaging capability, recurring revenue 
uplift, platform expansion, proprietary data advantages and potential margin benefits. 
The key risk is disruption: software and IT services firms face competition from model 
providers and AI-native challengers. At present it is hard to distinguish companies that 
can charge for AI from those that must provide AI simply to defend existing revenues.

Valuation alone, however, is unlikely to be sufficient to identify the next winners of the 
AI cycle. As AI capabilities become more widely available and deployment costs con-
tinue to decline, economic value should progressively migrate from providers of scarce 
inputs to companies able to embed AI into products, workflows and decision-making 
processes. The investment opportunity is therefore becoming less about access to AI 
and more about the ability to generate tangible economic benefits from its adoption. 
Against this backdrop, our preferred allocation is Phase 4 – Enhanced Productivity. 
This preference is not based solely on valuation (13% discount on PE). Rather, it re-
flects the combination of broad exposure to AI diffusion, lower direct capital intensity 
and our expectations of a pathway to mid-term earnings growth through operational 
improvements, possibly surprising current analysts’ EPS forecasts. Companies in this 
segment do not necessarily need to become AI leaders; they need to use AI effectively 
to improve productivity, strengthen competitive positioning and enhance returns on 
capital. 
Phase 3 – New Revenues offers a different investment profile. While valuations appear 
attractive relative to history, the sustainability of AI-related revenue streams remains 
less certain, and competitive pressures within software and digital services are likely to 
intensify as AI capabilities become increasingly commoditised. As a result, we believe 
the valuation discount appropriately reflects a higher degree of execution and moneti-
sation risk. 
Within Phase 2 - Infrastructure, our preference is selective. Although the overall phase 
trades at elevated valuation levels, we continue to favour Cooling, where the invest-
ment case is supported by structural rather than cyclical drivers. Rising rack density, 
growing power consumption and the increasing complexity of AI workloads are making 
thermal management a critical bottleneck in data-centre expansion. This provides 
stronger demand visibility than in several other infrastructure segments and supports a 
favourable long-term outlook despite richer valuations. Cooling is the richest US basket 
relative to its history (+39%), but its PEG (0.7x) indicates that this premium is backed 
by superior eps growth visibility. 
In all, we believe investors should increasingly focus on the beneficiaries of AI adoption 
rather than solely on the providers of AI technology. The next phase of the investment 
cycle is likely to be characterised by a broadening of value creation across industries, 
with the greatest opportunities emerging where AI can be translated into productivity 
gains, stronger cash flows and sustainable earnings growth.

5. Conclusions: from building to adopting AI  
The AI investment cycle is entering a new phase. While the first wave of returns ac-
crued primarily to providers of compute, infrastructure and foundation models, the next 
wave is likely to be driven by adoption and value creation. As AI capabilities become 
more widely available, the key investment question is shifting from building to adopt-
ing AI. 
In the US, we maintain a preference for Phase 4 – Enhanced Productivity, where 
AI adoption has the potential to support earnings growth, margin expansion and higher 
returns on capital across a broad set of companies. We complement this exposure with 
selective investments in Cooling, a critical infrastructure bottleneck where structural 
demand remains supported by rising data-centre density and power requirements. 
As the technology diffuses across industries and geographies, leadership is likely to 
broaden from AI builders to AI adopters. In our view, the next phase of the AI investment 
cycle will be driven less by access to compute and more by the ability to convert AI 
capabilities into productivity gains, cash-flow growth and sustainable economic value.

Appendix A.1: China AI - exposure at a discount 
The AI ecosystem is global, highly fragmented and increasingly diversified across 
regions. Concentrating AI exposure solely in US equities risks overlooking relevant 
pieces of the global supply chain and sources of innovation. Geographical diversifica-
tion is also important because of the increasing concentration risk within US equity 
markets, with big tech firms representing nearly 40% of the S&P 500 index. Addition-
ally, parts of the non-US AI universe offer exposure to similar structural growth themes 
at substantially lower valuations.  
China is developing an AI ecosystem with different strengths, opportunities and risks. 
While the US remains the global leader in frontier models, cloud computing and AI 
monetisation, China has emerged as a major force in AI infrastructure, power equip-
ment and batteries, as well as industrial automation and selected AI applications.  
Following the same logic as our US AI framework, we identify four phases, whose 
relative importance differs however significantly. 
Phase 1 – AI Champions - Foundation Models and AI Platforms (9 stocks): value 
creation within China's AI ecosystem is more dispersed across model developers, cloud 
providers and infrastructure companies. Competition is intense, barriers to entry are 
lower, and the rapid proliferation of open-source models has further accelerated com-
moditisation. China's first AI wave therefore resembles less a winner-takes-all oppor-
tunity and more a diversified exposure to the country's evolving AI ecosystem. 
Phase 2 – AI Infrastructure - Industrialising AI (107 stocks): whereas the US in-
frastructure story is largely driven by spending decisions of a small group of hyperscal-
ers, China's infrastructure build-out is more fragmented, reflecting China's strengths in 
industrial production and engineering. Infrastructure occupies a larger role in the 
Chinese value chain than it does in the United States. 
Phase 2B – Cooling (10 stocks) follows the same logic as US Phase 2b. 
Phase 2C – Robotics and Physical AI - Bringing AI into the Real Economy (17 
stocks): China's AI ecosystem extends beyond data centres and software/IT services, 
to include robotics and intelligent automation. This segment focuses on applying AI in 
factories, warehouses, logistics networks and industrial processes. China's large man-
ufacturing base, extensive automation capabilities and leadership in industrial robot 
deployment provide a natural advantage in this area. The latest International Federa-
tion of Robotics data confirm that China’s lead continues to widen: it installed    ,    
industrial robots in 2025, accounting for 59% of global deployments and exceeding US 
installations by more than ninefold. 
Phase 3 – AI Monetisation - Turning AI Into Revenues (27 stocks): monetisation 
dynamics in China remain more challenging. Competition is fierce, open-source adop-
tion is widespread, and pricing power is generally weaker. The risk is that AI becomes 
a basic requirement rather than a paid service, compressing margins rather than ex-
panding them.

A.1.1 China AI baskets: A Significant growth-adjusted discount to US  
The aggregate Chinese AI basket currently trades on a 12-month forward price-to-
earnings ratio of 18.6x, below its own historical median of 25.5x since 2015 (history 
since 2015 only, due to more limited data availability and quality)  While China’s AI 
basket is slightly dearer than the US one (17.9x), it looks more attractive when we 
adjust its PE by the future EPS growth (PEG): 0.6x versus 0.9x for US AI. Furthermore, 
around 7% of Chinese AI stocks are expected to swing from negative in 2025 to positive 
earnings within the next three years, a significantly larger share than in the US AI basket 
(1%). 
Phase 1 (AI Champions) appears attractively valued, trading 13% below its historical 
median PE and at a steep 52% discount to the Magnificent 7. However, this valuation 
gap largely disappears once growth is taken into account: its PEG of 1.4x is well above 
the US peer group's 1.1x. The discount is also due to higher competition: without a 
NVIDIA-like champion capturing outsized AI profits, value creation in China's Phase 1 
is diffused across the chain rather than concentrated. 
Phase 2 stocks look fair valued relative to their US peers once expected earnings 
growth is considered: average PEG ratio of 0.4x, compared with 0.5x for the US.  
The largest valuation gap appears further down the value chain. The average 
PEG ratio for Chinese cooling, physical AI, monetisation and productivity baskets is 
approximately 0.7, compared with 1.1 for corresponding US stocks. This indicates that 
investors continue to demand a substantial risk premium for Chinese AI-related equi-
ties, despite comparable growth prospects and increasingly compelling growth-ad-
justed valuations.

A.1.2 Risks Specific to China AI Investments 
Although valuations appear relatively attractive, investors should remain mindful of 
risks that could pose headwinds to future valuations and profitability. First, geopolitical 
tensions and export restrictions continue to create uncertainty around China's ability 
to close the remaining technology gap in key segments of the AI stack. Second, mon-
etisation remains less proven than in the US owing to fiercer competition. Third, reg-
ulatory risk remains higher as Chinese authorities have demonstrated a willingness to 
intervene in strategic technologies. Fourth, there is a risk of overinvestment. China's 
strategic commitment to technological self-sufficiency may lead to excess capacity in 
certain areas of AI infrastructure. Finally, governance and transparency standards 
remain more heterogeneous than in developed markets.  
A.1.3 Investment Implications: infrastructure, physical AI and productivity 
A China AI basket should place greater emphasis on infrastructure, physical AI 
and productivity beneficiaries, while maintaining selective exposure to foundation-
model developers and the AI monetisation theme. Such an approach not only reflects 
China's comparative advantages but also provides exposure to one of the most attrac-
tive valuation opportunities within the global AI landscape.
