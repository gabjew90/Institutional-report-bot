 
Data Center Bloodbath Spreads Down Under: 
Nextdc Implodes 45%, Mulls Force Majeure As 
Angry Residents Pull The Plug 
 
BY TYLER DURDEN 
WEDNESDAY, OCT 21, 2026 - 12:45 PM 
And another one goes down. 
One day after we reported that Korea's biggest IPO of the year cratered 32% within seconds, 
and two weeks after Nvidia-backed Firmus slashed and then pulled its ASX float, another 
Australian data center operator has just smashed into the cold, hard wall of reality. This time the 
casualty is an ASX-listed heavyweight: according to Bloomberg, Nextdc (NXT AU) said it is 
considering force majeure options and cut earnings guidance by more than a third after 
community opposition delayed power to M3, one of its major projects in Melbourne. 
And it didn't stop there: the company is also reviewing its entire capex program and placing all 
new development projects and land acquisitions on hold. The stock, fresh out of a two-day 
trading halt, plunged as much as 45% in Sydney to A$5.07, dragging the rest of the local sector 
with it, and is now back where it traded in 2019. 
 

 
 
CEO Craig Scroggie didn't mince words on the investor call: "We are not going to continue any 
work on any sites that don't have an existing customer commitment." Which, in the middle of 
what was supposed to be the biggest data center buildout in Australian history, is quite the 
sentence. 
Residents 1, Data Center 0 
The story behind the collapse is almost comically low-tech. To power up M3, network operator 
Jemena Electricity Networks needed to run high-voltage transmission poles through the 
inner-city residential streets of Maribyrnong in Melbourne's west. The locals had other ideas: in 
September, residents blockaded the work trucks with their cars and filled in the excavation pits, 
and Jemena paused the works citing "safety concerns and lack of access to work sites due to 
community and stakeholder opposition to the work." It does not expect to restart before 
February 2027, and the next grid supply increase to M3 has now slipped into FY2028. 
Meanwhile, the local council is withholding consent pending "further community consultation," 
and the planning decision on M3 Stages 6 and 7 has been kicked to 2027, conveniently after 
Victoria's state government enters caretaker mode on Nov 3 ahead of the Nov 28 election. 
Because nothing says "approve my high-voltage power line" like a looming election.

The new guidance strips out roughly A$140 million of revenue and EBITDA from the affected 
M3 contracts, including capacity that is already billing, and the company warns that service-level 
credits owed to the customer could push EBITDA even lower. Hence the force majeure 
language: Nextdc said it is "considering whether relief is available to it under the force majeure 
and other relevant provisions of those contracts." The board is also weighing selling 
development sites "not required in the near term" which, translated from corporate, probably 
means we bought more land than we can plug in. 
Scroggie, for his part, framed it as an industry problem: "There is very little capacity available 
anywhere, not just in Melbourne or in Australia, anywhere in the world. And even finding five 
megawatts of data center capacity today is a huge challenge." Which is probably true, and also 
a fairly strange pitch for a company that just told the market it can't even energize the capacity it 
already sold. 
Funding Was Always The Handbrake 
The timing is a little awkward for the Street, which loved the Australian AI infrastructure story 
right up until this morning. Just eight days ago, Goldman's Chris Boulus and team launched a 
 

 
massive initiation on the region's digital infrastructure in "ANZ Data Centres – Initiating our 
Supply-Demand Model" (available to pro subs), projecting ANZ IT load demand scaling to 
5.2GW by 2030, leaving a 0.7GW supply shortfall and kicking off what the bank's desk called 
"an enormous A$50-65bn capex supercycle." 
 
Note the right-hand panel: most of the 2030 capacity Goldman models is still under 
construction, in planning or merely "announced," with the bank assigning delivery risk to every 
one of those buckets. As of today, at least one "under construction" project in Melbourne needs 
a bigger risk haircut. 
But here is the thing: Goldman was not blind to the risk, and it was precisely Nextdc that the 
bank singled out. While it initiated Goodman Group and Megaport at Buy, it started Nextdc at 
only Neutral, with a A$11 target, because, as Goldman's desk summarized it, "while the street 
loves the broad demand story, GIR Chris cautions that funding is the actual handbrake." Boulus 
modeled FY28/29 EBITDA 3-5% below consensus and flagged "a looming A$1bn+ funding 
shortfall," with the desk adding that "total focus is on the resolution of their funding 
pathway—will we see a de-risking capital joint venture, or a potentially dilutive equity raise?" 
The report itself warned that "capital access [is] emerging as a critical bottleneck," and the 
winners will be operators "with managed fund platforms or self-funding capabilities."

The stock now trades about 48% below Goldman's "Neutral" target... a target set before a third 
of FY27 EBITDA went up in smoke. Even after today's crash, Market Index estimates Nextdc 
trades at roughly 37x FY27 EV/EBITDA, vs 22x for the average global pure-play peer per

Others were quick to generalize the lesson. RBC called the M3 delay "evidence that community 
concerns around data center developments present a real risk to the timing of delivery of 
contracted capacity for data center owners," while Citi's head of Japan, North Asia and Australia 
banking, Mark Luet, told Bloomberg TV that investors will be "more discerning" going forward, 
before adding, "we don't for a moment doubt that this thematic of AI infrastructure will continue." 
Spoken like a true banker with a pipeline to fill. 
Force Majeure Is Now A Data Center Feature 
If the phrase "data center force majeure" sounds familiar, that's because we have heard it 
before, a month ago. On Sept 24, Oracle sent a force majeure notice over its massive Project 
Jupiter campus in New Mexico, citing permitting delays and an inability to power up the site, and 
two weeks later we learned it was trucking natural gas to its AI data centers just to keep the 
lights on. Back then, Barclays assured clients the notice was "neutral from a credit perspective." 
We disagreed, and this was our take at the time: 
Then came Finland, which ordered Google to halt work at two data centers in what we dubbed 
"Forest Majeure" (Oct 6). Now it's Melbourne. The common thread in each case is the same: the 
GPUs are there and the customers are there (for now), but the electrons (and the neighbors' 
permission) are not. 
Nor is this an Australian quirk. Bloomberg notes that in the US, at least 106 server-farm projects 
representing about $198 billion of investment were blocked or delayed amid local opposition in 
the first half of this year alone, per Data Center Watch. Morgan Stanley put the damage even 
earlier at "$156 billion of data center projects canceled or delayed in 2025, and $130 billion in 
1Q26," warning that "sustained data center pushback could ultimately extend the cycle and 
 

 
reduce future supply," as we noted when Wells Fargo flagged a fourfold jump in active 
moratoriums. Texas has since ordered a halt to new data centers outright. 
We have been warning about this since this time last year, when the local pushback against 
data centers was still a curiosity, and by November we were writing that Americans were starting 
to revolt. A year later the revolt has gone global, and as we put it last week, demand for 
compute keeps rising... 
Bring Your Own Power Plant (Or Don't Bother) 
Which brings us to the obvious fix, the one we have been pounding the table on for a year: if 
you want to plug a city's worth of load into an already-strained grid, bring your own power plant, 
and don't route high-voltage lines through someone's front yard. Australia's government more or 
less said the same thing in July, when it slapped mandatory national rules on AI data centers 
requiring operators to "fully fund their power needs and underwrite new electricity generation." 
Goldman has since come around too. In its 50-page Carbonomics report on behind-the-meter 
power, which we covered in detail last month, the bank's Michele Della Vigna raised his BTM 
forecast to 67GW by 2030, with on-site gas turbines, reciprocating engines and fuel cells 
supplying 25% of global data center power demand by 2030 vs "effectively 0%" in 2025. The 
reason: grid connection times have stretched from about two years historically to 7+ years 
today, per engine maker INNIO, while a data center itself takes just 2-3 years to build. 
 
 

 
M3 is a textbook case: the building is (mostly) there, the customer is there, and the missing 
piece is a 66kV augmentation that local residents managed to stop with their cars and some 
shovels. 
And there's a macro irony here too. Overnight, RBA Deputy Governor Andrew Hauser flagged 
"a boom in data centre construction here in Australia" as one of three upside risks to inflation, 
prompting Goldman's Australia economists to scrap their on-hold call and now expect a 25bp 
RBA hike to 4.85% in November. Translation: the data center boom is now pushing up the cost 
of capital for... data centers. For an industry where, as Goldman warned, funding is the 
handbrake, that is hardly ideal. 
The Casualty List Grows... Again 
Nextdc is the first already-listed operator to end up on our tally of data center wipeouts, which 
has now grown to eight entries in a month. And just as we were putting this post together, 
Bloomberg reported that DayOne, Firmus' Batam partner which was set to start marketing its 
up-to-$5 billion US IPO as soon as today, is now "weighing options." Usually that means the IPO 
is probably getting quietly shelved.

When The Neighbors Hold The Off Switch 
The bull case for AI data centers has always rested on one assumption: that demand for 
compute is so overwhelming that every megawatt built gets filled. Nextdc's CEO said as much 
this morning, and he is probably right about the demand. What today showed is that the binding 
constraint sits elsewhere: in permits, power lines, and the patience of the people who live next 
to them. Firmus and Elice showed the public market won't pay up for the buildout; Nextdc shows 
that even a funded, contracted, half-built project can lose a third of its earnings when residents 
decide they've had enough. 
Goldman flagged funding as Nextdc's handbrake eight days ago. As of this morning, the 
handbrake has a second lever, and it's held by the residents of Maribyrnong. The board will 
present the results of its capex review at the AGM on Nov 20, and we suspect the "potential 
sale of development sites" won't be the last asset to go on the block.
