# The Jobs Number Nobody Forecast

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The payroll print that broke the week's setup

Every desk built its September view on a weak August jobs report. Goldman estimated 40,000 against a consensus of 55,000 [c1564]. JPMorgan's Michael Feroli put it at 50,000 [c2248]. Deutsche Bank was the highest forecast on the street at 65,000 [c713]. Payrolls rose 162,000, with another 55,000 added to the prior two months in revisions [c733]. Private employers added 127,000 [c734]. The three and six month averages moved up to 75,000 and 106,000 [c735], and the share of industries adding jobs rose to 55.8, the highest since January 2024 [c736]. Unemployment ticked up to 4.14% only because more people came looking for work, with participation rising to 61.6% and the broader underemployment rate falling to 7.7% [c1939][c738][c737].

The number matters because of the frame the desks had already agreed on. JPMorgan spent the week arguing that good news would be bad news: a stronger report pushes bond yields higher and pulls equities down, with 30,000 to 70,000 the only range that leaves both alone [d7][c2398]. Its own scenario table gave a print above 95,000 a 10% chance and put the S&P 500 down between 50 basis points, one hundredth of a percent, and 1.25% on that outcome [c1709]. What arrived was nearly double the top of that table.

That splits the Tier-1 banks on the September 16 meeting. Deutsche Bank's read is that Chair Warsh lowered the bar enough that a hike is now the most likely outcome unless data surprise meaningfully to the downside [c744][d59]. TS Lombard reaches the same place by a different route, arguing the Fed has to hike this month or Warsh carries a credibility problem into the rest of his term [c1229][d64]. Against that, Goldman's economists still forecast the Fed on hold at 3.5% to 3.75% for the rest of the year [c2051], and JPMorgan's equity strategists argue central banks will deliver less tightening than markets price, because rising yields reflect stronger activity rather than inflation expectations coming unanchored [c1046][c1045][d5].

Friday's inflation report decides it. Deutsche Bank forecasts core CPI at 0.21% for the month and an implied core PCE reading of the same, and states that a 21 basis point core PCE leans toward a hike without automatically triggering one [c1943][c1388][c743][d59]. Above that line, the hold case is finished.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Energy still has its hand on the rates lever

Goldman's trading desk argues bond yields cannot fall until energy prices do, and trades it directly, holding a short position in heating oil against the US 10-year Treasury [c1551][c1554][d29]. The supporting numbers are about refined fuel, not crude. Diesel accounts for more than 40% of the $40 per barrel rise in global refined product wholesale prices since February, and global exports of refined products are down 25% year over year, with the Persian Gulf and Russia responsible for three quarters of that drop [c807][c808]. European gas closed at 73.60 euros per megawatt hour, its highest since January 2023, and the six month contract at 67.43 says traders expect it to persist [c666][c667]. Northern Europe cannot refill storage before the heating season [c669]. Goldman's commodities team is on the other side of its own desk on crude, forecasting Brent down to $80 by December [c291] from roughly $95 now [c924]. The thing to watch is whether fuel prices, not crude, break first.

### Broadcom's order book against the price of a token

Two numbers point opposite ways. Broadcom guided AI chip revenue to double to $115 billion in fiscal 2027 and double again to $230 billion after that, named Anthropic its largest 2027 customer, and said component supply is locked through 2028 [c168][c169][c170][c172]. Goldman raised its target to $540 and stays a buyer at 12 times management's own 2028 earnings guidance [c194][c173]. The stock fell 2.7% anyway [c211]. The reason sits in the other number. The price of a million AI tokens fell 29% in August to about $0.97, a record low and more than half off the May peak near $2.05 [c1535][c1536]. Volume is not covering the gap: traffic on one large model router rose about 47% in August while dollars spent rose about 7% [c2154][c1537]. The five largest cloud builders are on track for roughly $737 billion of capital spending this year, about 38% of revenue, and 78 of the 91 bonds they sold in 2026 traded below issue price by late August [c1542][c1544]. Goldman's Rich Privorotsky puts it plainly: demand can be infinite and the stocks can still fall if that demand arrives at a price that does not service the debt built to serve it [c1546][d43].

### Software took the money that left the chip names

Thursday split the technology trade cleanly. Goldman's AI software basket rose 5.36%, which the desk flagged as a two standard deviation move, while its AI semiconductor basket managed 0.25% [c956][c1575]. The software against semiconductors pair gained 2.57% [c955]. This is not one session. Software beat semiconductors by 10 percentage points across August [c1216][d30]. Snowflake is the clearest case, up 21% after product revenue grew 37% against a 30% guide, with full year guidance now implying growth accelerating to 36% [c385][c376][c388]. JPMorgan's desk has been telling clients to hold Magnificent Seven names and software ahead of AI, memory and semiconductors, on the view that the chip complex cannot hold a rally [c1195][c1196][d18]. Goldman's volatility desk would rather fund the chip side than abandon it, selling software index upside to pay for semiconductor index upside, with one month semiconductor upside volatility against software near its cheapest in more than five years [c315][c320].

### Everyone has sold and nobody is hedged

Goldman's flow strategists describe a market that already did its selling. Hedge funds are carrying less directional risk than at any point in the past year, with net leverage at 47.6% and the long to short ratio at 1.597, both first percentile [c1447][c1448]. More than 44% of surveyed retail investors are bearish, a four week average above 40 that has historically come alongside 7% to 10% declines [c1445][c1446]. Yet one month S&P 500 implied volatility, the price the options market puts on future swings, sits in the third percentile [c800], and Goldman's own panic gauge reads 0.73 out of ten [c797]. Goldman treats that as too many people waiting for a fall and wants cheap short dated upside, quoting September 780-strike S&P ETF calls at $2.29 [c803][c324]. Citadel Securities' Scott Rubner reads the identical setup and says use strength to cut exposure and add protection [c1315], pointing to buyback windows closing around September 12 [c1299], $6.2 trillion of options expiring September 18 [c1309], the VIX at 14.4 [c1302] and the cost of downside protection at its flattest in a year [c1300]. BofA sizes what happens if Rubner is right: systematic funds sell $163 billion in a falling market [c1280].

### The yen is doing the Bank of Japan's tightening for it

Japan's currency rose 1.86% against the dollar and 1.55% against the euro, so this is not a weak dollar story [c728][c729]. The 10 year Japanese government bond yield crossed 3% earlier in the week for the first time since 1996 before easing back to 2.97% [c677]. Board member Takata said a 25 basis point increase is not necessarily set in stone, which traders read as room for more [c675]. Pricing now sits just above 25 basis points for September and close to 100 basis points over the next year [c2218][c2219]. Goldman reads loose fiscal policy running against tight money, with Takaichi's 2027 budget requests at 143.1 trillion yen, and expects exporters to struggle while domestic banks trade better [c2220][c1613][c1013][d35]. JPMorgan's research calls the currency move overdone and does not expect dollar-yen to break below the 155 to 165 range, with the large outstanding bet against the yen as the risk to that view [c1684][c2313][d6]. The September 18 meeting settles the argument [c2123].

### Europe's bond market is pricing a different central bank

German 10 year yields reached 3.37%, the highest since 2011, and French 10 year yields 4.25%, the highest since 2008 [c1347][c1350]. The German inflation-adjusted yield also hit a post-2011 high at 0.99%, so this is not only about inflation expectations [c1349]. Markets fully price three more European Central Bank increases by June 2027 [c1346], and the ECB's Simkus said a single September hike will not be enough [c1331]. Gas is the transmission line, and Goldman's trading desk argues European stocks cannot sustainably outperform while gas prices stay sharply higher [c1412]. Goldman's strategists still like the region at 14.5 times earnings and a 5.3% free cash flow yield, meaning cash left after all spending as a share of market value, with 2026 earnings growth raised to 15% [c751][c752][c947]. They prefer Germany and stay cautious on France, where public debt approaches 120% of output and the CAC 40 has been the region's worst major market three years running [c1573][c1504][c1506]. JPMorgan disagrees outright and prefers the US and Asia [c1113].

### Gold bounced, and the reason it bounced is now in doubt

Gold closed at $4,436 an ounce, up 2.55%, after three straight declines took it near $4,305 [c1][c21]. JPMorgan says the bounce landed on the 100 day average price on speculation of fresh Japanese intervention, that whether this is the low depends on the September Fed decision, and that the desk still expects the uptrend to resume before year end [c1101][c1102][d45]. It took profit on the position and is looking to buy back in [c1120]. Goldman's commodities team carries an end-2026 fair value of $4,900 and a 12 month forecast of $5,275, built on continued central bank buying and a recovery in ETF inflows while the Fed stays on hold [c2][c4][c3]. That last condition is exactly what a 162,000 payroll print puts at risk. Goldman's volatility desk prefers to own the theme cheaply, quoting a December option that pays only if gold, bitcoin and the S&P 500 all rise at 0.95% against 2.1% for the cheapest single-asset version [c6].

### The midterms move sectors, not the index

S&P 500 options price a 0.82% move for the day after the November vote [c68]. The average day-after move in past midterms is 1.3%, and in only a third of cycles since 1975 has that day exceeded the prior month's average daily move [c69][c70]. BofA calls the pricing reasonable and argues that falling equity leverage and low correlation between individual stocks make this an election that shifts money between sectors rather than moving the index [c67][d21]. Its trades follow from that: a December S&P put that pays on declines between 5% and 20% and stops paying below that, at 0.55%, a VIX October put sold against a November 22-30 call spread for $0.50, and a November 280-strike small cap put at $6.13, because regional banks and small caps are the most underpriced against their 2024 election week moves [c46][c47][c274][c257][c260]. Goldman goes further into the rotation, long healthcare-subsidy beneficiaries, renewables and grid infrastructure, short drug makers and defense [c959][c960][c1578][c1579]. Since 1990 the S&P has gained 5.4% in the three months after a midterm and 14.7% over twelve, positive 78% and 100% of the time [c1076].
