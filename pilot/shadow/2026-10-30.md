# Warsh Holds, Bonds Steepen

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The pause nobody can price

The Fed left its policy rate at 3.75 to 4.00 percent on a 10-2 vote, with Governor Cook and Cleveland's Hammack voting to raise it 25 basis points, one basis point being a hundredth of a percent [c739][c752]. The statement upgraded its description of the economy to a strong pace from a solid one [c741] and added a line saying that gathering more information would help the Committee deliver a timely return of inflation to target [c740]. JPMorgan reads that new sentence as a deliberate hawkish bias meant to keep December alive, and still expects a hike then [c641][c642]. The press conference pulled the other way. Chair Warsh gave no clear economic rationale for the pause, and JPMorgan says the market came out of it less convinced the Committee has a December plan [c610].

Rates took the second reading. Two-year Treasury yields fell almost 10 basis points and five-year yields fell 5 [c552], while longer-dated yields barely moved, so the curve steepened again [c594]. Tightening priced through December 2027 came out by about 8 basis points [c622], and the dollar slipped [c1288].

The fight is over what December is actually worth. MUFG prices a 25 basis point December hike at 55 percent against the market's roughly 80 [c645], arguing that if stronger activity is better productivity rather than excess demand, the case for an aggressive cycle is weaker than the growth data alone implies [c644]. Handelsbanken goes further, calling the delivery unexpectedly dovish and close to the July meeting's, and flags the risk of a dovish policy mistake [c635][c636], while still forecasting a December hike [c608]. Scotiabank's read is mechanical: the Fed skipped mainly because a surprise hike would have violated its own dot plot, and pricing for December 9 was trimmed only 1 to 2 basis points and still leans to a hike [c755][c618].

Data settles this, not rhetoric. September core PCE, the inflation measure the Fed targets, was due the following day at 0.3 percent on the month [c1351], and Warsh said he is unwilling and unable to call financial conditions tight even after acknowledging they have tightened [c744]. A firm print with that line intact leaves the hawks their December. A soft one leaves the market pricing the first dovish mistake of this chair's tenure.

## 3. BRIEFS

### AI's bill moved to the credit market

Meta raised $97.5 billion, with $60 billion to be sold through an at-the-market program starting in January [c6], and the common stock piece was upsized to $25 billion and priced at $625 a share [c27]. Broadcom closed a $63 billion vehicle to finance Anthropic's chip purchases, and the roughly $43.5 billion first lien, 69 percent of the total, is effectively guaranteed by Broadcom [c37], which agreed to provide residual value support equal to that first lien [c34]. The lenders are holding the chip seller's credit rather than the buyer's risk [d7]. Goldman's Amanda Lynam puts off-balance-sheet leases and purchase commitments across five hyperscalers at $2.8 trillion, eight times the 2023 level [c886]. Junk-rated US company bonds pay 337 basis points over Treasuries, past the level Goldman's own credit team set as its bear case [c881], and the weakest tier of US borrowers has not been this expensive to fund in three years [c889]. Goldman's Privorotsky says sustained relief in AI credit spreads would be a powerful catalyst [c861].

### The capex test, not the beat

Microsoft and Alphabet report Wednesday and Amazon Thursday [c61], inside a week carrying 44.5 percent of S&P 500 market value [c1059]. Goldman's desk set the bar plainly: capex flat or up is fine, a cut would be disastrous for the equipment and infrastructure suppliers [c512]. Goldman sits above the street on the cloud lines, with Azure growth in constant currency of 46 to 47 percent against 45 [c81] and Google Cloud near 100 percent against consensus of 96 [c49], and below it on Search by a percentage point [c55]. Nasdaq futures length is still elevated into those prints, and Goldman warns a disappointment could bring meaningful futures selling [c56]. BofA's derivatives team argues the options market underprices the risk: across the largest 50 S&P names, realised earnings-day moves sit at the 100th percentile on a market-value weighted basis against implied at the 77th [c1207]. It wants to own US megacap earnings options [c1211] and sell or avoid European ones [c1218].

### A squeeze dressed as a recovery

Korea led the bounce. The KOSPI rose 6.63 percent on stronger than expected SK Hynix earnings [c336], Samsung rose about 8 percent [c357], and SK Hynix rose about 10 percent on a capital return of roughly $45 billion [c326] after beating on third-quarter revenue [c347]. Goldman's basket of companies that rent out AI computing power rose 585 basis points [c264] and its memory basket rose 368 [c267]. UBS reads the move as mechanical: software gained only 1.1 percent since Thursday [c171] and the S&P 500 0.8 percent [c1017] while the VIX fell 9.6 percent [c1221], which it calls an unwind of bets placed as hedges rather than a return to buying [c1167][d30]. Its trend-following model shows Nasdaq positioning roughly halved in two weeks with another $1.2 billion of selling projected [c1168], and its bias is to cut long positions instead of chasing the rebound [c1166]. Goldman's flow desk agrees on the mechanism, saying the bounce reflects selling pressure ending rather than fresh demand [c373], while JPMorgan's technology desk sees tentative re-risking in semis and optical names [c309].

### Refiners roll over at the top

The best year refiners have had in three decades broke on Tuesday. BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky notes the group was up 157 percent year to date as of October 26, nearly double its prior record year [c810], then fell 6.8 percent intraday, its worst day since the April 2025 selloff [c811]. Margins did it, not demand. The 3-2-1 crack spread, the profit on turning three barrels of crude into gasoline and diesel, has fallen from a September peak near $70 a barrel to about $51 [c813] while the shares kept climbing until roughly two weeks ago [d26]. JPMorgan measured the same session as a 2 standard deviation move and about $35 billion of lost market value across its refining index [c411]. Mizuho stepped aside on October 8, raising price targets about 40 percent on average as it moved to the sidelines [c150], with the group at roughly 8.5 times two-year forward EV/EBITDA, company value against operating profit before interest, taxes and depreciation, versus a 6.7 average [c823]. Krinsky's wider point is the one to watch: price and breadth in the S&P 500 have diverged on 33 percent of trading days this year, the most since at least 1996 [c1019].

### Something is breaking under housing

TS Lombard's case is that builder fundamentals have rotted while the chart still looks orderly. Operating margins sit below pre-Covid levels because median new-home prices are falling while construction, labor and financing costs rise [c425], with goods and services input costs running 10 to 11 percent a year [c426]. The NAHB buyer traffic index has fallen to 18, its weakest since the Covid recession [c428], mortgage rates are back near 7.3 percent [c429], and inventories are rising even as starts and permits weaken [c431]. The expression is relative rather than directional: TS Lombard is long the equal-weight S&P 500 against short homebuilders at a 1.75 to 1 ratio [c200]. The 10-year Treasury yield decides it, since homebuilders move inversely to it [c228], which makes a hawkish Fed pushing mortgage rates higher the firm's stated catalyst [c430].

### Oil's risk premium has a deadline

Brent traded above $117 a barrel this week after attacks in Saudi Arabia and around Gulf shipping [c786]. The Houthis claimed a ballistic missile strike on Ras Tanura, the kingdom's largest refinery [c801], which pushed Brent to $116 [c802], and Saudi Aramco has not confirmed it [c844]. Goldman's desk has put a date on the next leg: without tangible progress in Gulf diplomacy by Thursday morning, it expects the market to start climbing the escalation ladder again into the weekend [c789]. Reporting relayed to clients says Washington told several Gulf states to prepare for a short, intense military campaign after the midterms [c1085]. The range of outcomes is unusually wide. Goldman's base case has Brent sliding back toward $75 by late 2027 [c819] and its upside case puts it above $140 next spring if Gulf supply runs badly short [c792], which is why its commodity strategists recommend targeted exposure to refined fuels and European natural gas rather than flat crude [c793].

### The UK Budget's narrow bar

Chancellor Healey presents the Budget on Wednesday [c966]. Mizuho has set the test in numbers: fiscal headroom around £12 to 15 billion, a gilt remit rising only modestly toward £249 billion, and no added pressure on long-dated issuance [c955][c956][c957], with headroom materially below £10 billion reviving credibility worries [c935]. Gilts have stayed calm into it, 10-year yields holding a 5.31 to 5.35 percent range [c954]. UBS expects caution rather than a reset, with headroom down from £23.6 billion in March to about £10 billion and only a £15 billion buffer rebuilt through smaller tax rises [c947][c974]. The bank levy is the equity line. UBS models a rise from 3 to 6 percent, cutting NatWest and Lloyds earnings 3 to 4 percent and Barclays and HSBC 1 to 2 percent [c89].
