# Bonds Do The Tightening

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Thirty-Year Yields Take Over the Fed's Job

The bond market moved before the Fed could. The 10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.96%, its highest since October 2023. The 30-year reached 5.36%, a level last seen in 2007, and the 2-year, which tracks Fed expectations most closely, rose to 4.59%. The chain that got them there ran through energy. Brent crude closed 6.34% higher at $107.63 a barrel, the producer price categories that feed the Fed's preferred inflation gauge came in firmer, and futures moved September hike odds from 60% to 72%. The Treasury's attempt to lean against the move failed. It bought back $5.19bn of long-dated debt, short of the $6bn maximum it had announced, and yields rose across the curve anyway.

Two banks read the same levels in opposite directions. Deutsche Bank calls this normalisation rather than stress: US nominal GDP is running 6.6% year over year, comfortably above the 10-year yield, which makes 4.8% historically average if nominal growth settles at 5.5% and about 5.3% reasonable if it settles at 6% on AI and inflation. ING takes the other side and calls a break above 5% near inevitable, with higher inflation-adjusted yields and fiscal supply the dominant forces and the rest of 2026 a dangerous gauntlet. Long-dated swap pricing already implies two to three Fed hikes. JPMorgan sides with the higher number, carrying a long-term 10-year target of 5.5% on 3% expected inflation, built on an assumed 2.5% inflation-adjusted Treasury yield.

Today's August CPI decides which read survives the week. JPMorgan's Feroli forecasts core at 0.21% month over month and treats a rounded 0.2% as low enough for the Fed to hold and 0.3% as high enough to hike. Deutsche Bank sits at the same 0.21%. Goldman is higher at 0.23% against consensus of 0.2%. Above 0.30% core, JPMorgan's desk maps a one-day S&P 500 decline of 1.5% to 2.5%. Into that print, Goldman's desk likes buying September puts on the small-cap ETF $IWM as the hedge, calling those options cheap.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Crude Above $100 and the Diesel Problem

Brent closed 6.34% higher at $107.63 a barrel and WTI rose 6.69% to $102.48, with the six-month contract up to $88.85, which says traders expect the squeeze to last. The tightness is worse in fuels than in crude: US wholesale diesel trades within 1% of its 2022 peak, and Saudi output has fallen to its lowest level since 1990 after Houthi forces captured the Yemeni port of Mokha near the Bab el-Mandeb shipping lane. JPMorgan's Natasha Kaneva argues crude may not rise much further even in a forever-conflict case, given excess supply, production outside the Middle East and ample inventories, while RBC's high scenario puts Brent at $121.99 a barrel in the fourth quarter and names a full resumption of Saudi-Houthi war as the trigger. Societe Generale's base case, at 50% probability, has Brent back to $80 by November and December. The near-term decider is the reported meeting between Iran and Gulf states on a Hormuz deal.

### AI Borrowing Becomes a Rates Story

Goldman's macro desk argues AI has stopped being only an equity story and is now a rates and credit story. AI-related debt issuance approached $500 billion by early August, roughly one-fifth of US high-grade supply this year. Funding next year's hyperscaler capital spending means that group collectively sells more interest-rate exposure into the market than the US Treasury does, and buyers fund it by cutting Treasury and mortgage holdings, which pushes up credit spreads, inflation-adjusted yields and the extra compensation investors demand for owning long bonds. Mark Wilson's framing is that the Fed is more passenger than driver. JPMorgan puts the scale in its own numbers, with high-grade AI issuance at $266bn year to date against $139bn for all of 2025 and $2.1trn projected over the next five years, while Nomura's credit team models $250-275bn this year and $350-400bn in 2027. The trade the desks actually name is convertible bonds, where AI borrowers are 44% of $135 billion of issuance and Goldman calls the paper attractive for total return credit portfolios.

### The Machines Sold at the Open

Trend-following funds tripped their short-term triggers across the Nasdaq 100, S&P 500 and Russell 2000 on Thursday morning, after a European index broke earlier in the week. Goldman models the resulting selling at 23bn over one week and 28bn over one month, and those figures exclude the pre-market decline. That supply lands on top of $421 billion of systematic equity exposure across trend, volatility-control and risk-parity strategies, close to five-year highs. Dealers are still long options at current prices, which damps moves, but Goldman models that position flipping on a further 200 basis point decline, at which point dealers start selling into weakness. Goldman also notes the VIX usually sits near 25 when technicals deteriorate this way, against 17 now. The counterweight is that the money is already out: hedge fund net leverage sits in the 6th percentile over the past year, which is why Goldman's sales desk still says the pain trade is higher.

### Lagarde Calls It a No Brainer

The ECB raised its deposit rate 25 basis points to 2.50% and added a line saying inflation stays well above target for an extended period, with the 2028 core forecast lifted to 2.3%. Lagarde called the hike a no brainer, and money markets now fully price three more over the next year. European bonds repriced hard: the German 2-year rose to 3.23%, its biggest daily move since the March 2025 debt-brake reform, and the 10-year Bund closed at 3.50%, above its 2011 euro-crisis high and back to levels last seen in 2009, with France at 4.44% and the UK at 5.37%. Goldman moved its call to an extra December hike and a 2.75% peak, while Deutsche Bank's European economists hold at one more, in December, arguing further hikes are hard to justify without evidence that energy costs are feeding into wages and other prices. That evidence is the test, and JPMorgan already got the direction wrong, expecting a dovish reaction and getting a hawkish one.

### Supply Is Still the Binding Constraint

The company-level picture at Goldman's technology conference did not soften. TSMC is still adding 3-nanometer capacity and does not expect supply to be sufficient, a break from previous chip generations where demand faded two to three years in, and its advanced packaging is tight enough that it has started outsourcing selected steps to partners. Applied Materials reiterated roughly 40% revenue growth in 2026 and expects leading-edge logic, packaging and memory to take about 80% of equipment spending in both 2026 and 2027, while SanDisk says memory chip supply stays tight through 2027 and possibly 2028. Dell reported a $9.5bn backlog of AI servers and warned component availability could be worse in 2027 than in 2026. Goldman's desk conclusion is to stay long AI infrastructure into 2027 and to buy its AI hardware basket. What holds the stocks back is top-down hesitation over rates, geopolitics and midterm policy rather than anything in the company numbers.

### Breakout or Bubble in Earnings

Deutsche Bank's chart of the week shows S&P 500 earnings per share growing inside a stable channel of about 6.5% a year through depressions, wars and technology cycles, and now breaking out above it. Binky Chadha and Parag Thatte expect that to hold in coming quarters, with the caveat that the measure is per share, so buybacks flatter it, though Thatte reads the buyback effect as having peaked in the 2010s, before the breakout began. JPMorgan's counter is historical: four interwar private investment cycles were each equivalent to 2-4% of GDP and lasted 2-4 years, and none ended in a soft landing, against US AI capital spending now running at 5% of GDP and total US market value at 248% of GDP. The valuation tell is that forward price-to-earnings multiples on AI stocks have slumped this year even as forward earnings and margins rose. Spending by the top 1% of AI model customers, which drives most of the model companies' enterprise revenue, fell 9.7% last month, from $7,976 to $7,205.
