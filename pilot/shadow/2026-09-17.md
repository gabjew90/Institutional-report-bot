# The Fed Hikes, Dots Bite

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The dots did the damage, not the hike

The Fed raised its target range by 25 basis points (one hundredth of a percent) to 3.75-4.00%, unanimously, its first increase since 2023 [c538][c462]. The move itself was priced at 94% going in [c496]. What was not priced is the projection sheet that came with it [d23][d35]. Sixteen of eighteen officials now expect at least one more increase this year and four of them expect two [c517][c541], and nobody dissented, even though Goldman had told clients to expect Governor Waller to break ranks [c519][c507]. The median estimate of the rate that neither helps nor slows the economy rose to 3.25%, the highest since 2016 [c543][c521]. Chair Warsh called the hike the removal of "a dose of accommodation" and said he was hard-pressed to describe current policy as restrictive [c545][c544].

The bond market took the projections at face value. Two-year yields jumped to a two-year high of 4.74% and the 10-year set a post-2007 high of 5.02% [c363][c364]. Bank shares fell 2.30% and energy 2.97%, dragging the S&P 500 down 0.45% [c667][c665].

Goldman and JPMorgan read the same meeting differently [d36][d35]. Goldman reversed its call that September would be the only hike and moved to a second one in October, arguing that back-to-back moves fit the Committee's stated aim of getting inflation back to 2% sooner than an every-other-meeting pace would [c524][c525]. JPMorgan is staying with December alone, in line with the median dot, and reads the revised forecasts as a Committee that has stopped worrying about the oil shock and started worrying about demand-led overheating [c546][c547]. Both are looking at the same 3.25% neutral estimate and the same unemployment forecast of 4.1%, a tenth below the Fed's own estimate of the natural rate [c1076].

Pricing moved toward Goldman. October odds rose 8 points to 51% and December 9 points to 78% [c527][c528]. Deutsche Bank's economists are closer to Goldman than to JPMorgan, expecting another 50 basis points through December and March [c464].

The prints between now and the October 28 meeting [c609] settle whether the 51% priced for October is too high or too low [c527]. Goldman's own forecast for core PCE, the Fed's preferred inflation measure excluding food and energy, is 3.2% for this year against the Committee's 3.4% [c1099], so its October call rests on the Fed's stated urgency rather than on the data Goldman expects to arrive.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Saudi Arabia started putting the barrels back

Tuesday was the supply panic. Loadings at Yanbu were suspended, three Libyan fields went offline, Brent rose 2.90% to $108.75 and WTI 4.38% to $105.83 [c712][c716][c713][c714]. Wednesday was the walk-back: Saudi Arabia raised tanker loadings and signaled about half the East-West pipeline's capacity restored within days and full capability in roughly six weeks, Libya came back, and Brent fell 2.69% while WTI fell 3.21% to $102.43 [c768][c769][c770]. That pipeline had been carrying about 4 million barrels a day around the Strait of Hormuz since the conflict started [c762]. Raymond James says a lasting decline in Treasury yields needs oil to ease first [c378], and JPMorgan puts a number on it: WTI down to the mid-$80s would be a boost for stocks [c750]. [d22][d23][d21]

### Selling bonds to buy chips

Technology companies are funding the AI buildout with debt, which puts them in line at the same window as the Treasury [d20][d5]. JPMorgan forecasts US technology net bond issuance of roughly $335bn this year, up from $140bn [c891], inside investment-grade supply the bank sees rising from around $615bn last year [c894]. Measured against total global net bond issuance [c906], that adds only 10-15 basis points to global bond yields, and 5-10 once changed rate expectations are stripped out [c412][c413], which is why the firm says AI justifies higher yields but not an outright bet against bonds [c411]. The sharper signal sits elsewhere: insurance against default by AI-linked borrowers has repriced further than options on those same companies' shares [c896][c897]. JPMorgan's expression is three-to-six-month put spreads on the Nasdaq 100 [c105], plus owning capital-light AI winners over the companies carrying the heaviest spending [c640].

### Fewer and fewer stocks are holding the index up

Only 49% of S&P 500 members closed above their 200-day average price, the first reading below half since April 2000 while the index itself sat within 4% of a record [c655]. Sentiment does not match that. Put/call ratios came into the meeting near the lows of the year [c969], the mirror image of July, when fear was near yearly highs and more than 70% of names were above that average [c974][c697]. Financials, industrials, transports and hotels have all broken the same line [c76][c68], and 10-year real rates, meaning yields after expected inflation, closed at a fresh multi-year high of 2.68% [c381]. About 70% of NYSE volume was in falling stocks [c659]. BTIG's condition for calling the correction finished is specific: less complacency, genuinely oversold readings, and a decline where stocks fall together rather than one at a time [c658]. [d18]

### The AI trade split into two trades

Raymond James watched the S&P 500 Software Index rally 3.2% in the same session the Semiconductor Index fell 4.4% [c710]. JPMorgan's software-versus-semis pair posted the largest one-day move in its history, 12%, after Anthropic's Amodei called for slower development [c337]. The money is following that split. Software is the most net-bought technology subsector at Goldman's prime brokerage while semiconductors are the most net-sold [c793][c810], and Pictet notes chip earnings estimates are being cut after a long run of increases [c287]. UBS is unmoved, keeping its 2027 AI capex forecast at $1.2 trillion, a 33% rise from $900 billion this year, and pointing at chip stocks trading near 21 times forward earnings against 33 times in June [c230][c23]. JPMorgan would buy this dip and stays bullish on semiconductors and the Mag7 [c159][c158]. [d21][d29][d9][d16]

### Restaurants are where the oil price hits the consumer

JPMorgan's restaurant basket had its worst day since liberation day, with crude grinding higher and the 10-year holding above 5% doing the damage rather than anything company-specific [c265][c266]. Positioning is already empty. The long/short ratio sits at the 0th percentile both for the past year and going back to 2018, and shorts accelerated last week [c814][c815]. That has not put a floor under the group, because most of these names still trade at premium multiples well above their five-year lows, and JPMorgan trader Alexandra Murphy expects many of them to rerate lower as consumer multiples keep compressing [c267][c268]. The desk saw long-only selling in Wingstop and Darden with nobody stepping in [c53]. The unlock JPMorgan names is a double one: lower oil and lower yields together would lift airlines, autos, homebuilders and restaurants off low positioning [c807]. [d15]

### Security spending stopped being optional

The window between a vulnerability becoming public and somebody using it has gone negative. JPMorgan puts mean time to exploit at minus 7 days in 2025 against 63 days in 2018, while enterprises face 132 new disclosed vulnerabilities a day and fix 16% of their known ones per month [c200][c202]. Anthropic's Mythos model finds previously unknown flaws, writes working exploits and completes full network penetration tests without a human [c207]. JPMorgan sizes the incremental spend at more than $430bn across a three-year addressable market and expects a steeper adoption curve than cloud or mobile [c197][c198]. Second-quarter results scattered wildly, from CrowdStrike up 20% to Cellebrite down 29% the next day [c49][c38], with Palo Alto's Prisma AIRS crossing $100 million in recurring revenue within four quarters of launch [c100]. JPMorgan came out of the season incrementally positive on OKTA, PANW, CRWD, VRNS and ZS [c47]. [d11]

### Gold handed back August before the Fed even spoke

Gold has round-tripped its August rally, back at early-August prices and testing its 50-day average, with the main support level around $4,200 [c954][c955][c965]. It fell more than 2% to below $4,300 an ounce as the oil-driven inflation scare firmed up hike odds [c953]. Goldman's Tony Kim expects a choppy path from here and treats the hike, higher energy and rising yields as headwinds that are largely in the price already, with institutional and sovereign buyers having shown up around $4,000 [c957][c958][c932]. Gold's own volatility gauge is relaxed compared with equity and long-bond volatility [c1004]. Goldman's named trade is cheap upside rather than spot: call skew and risk reversals are not pricing much upside tail risk, and concentrated dealer positioning in GLD options could amplify a move [c1][c3]. [d1][d14]

### The midterms became an oil story

JPMorgan's base rates put the sitting president's party down about 27 House seats and 3 Senate seats across 23 cycles [c1027], which lines up with betting markets at 86% for a Democratic House and 48% for Republicans holding the Senate [c1029][c1028]. The variable that moved since the bank's last note is fuel. Energy went from $91.01 to $106, up 16.5%, gas prices sit 31% above pre-conflict levels, and Trump's economic approval fell with them [c751][c1020]. Joyce Chang expects the House to flip to a 220-230 seat majority, short of a wave, because redistricting shrank the number of competitive seats [c1063][c1065][c1064]. Democrats need four flips against a 53-47 Senate, contested in Alaska, Iowa, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio and Texas [c1035][c1036]. Dubravko's read on the outcome: divided government has coincided with 20% S&P 500 returns over a two-year Congress against 18% under single-party control [c638]. [d17]
