# Tech Reloaded Into Trouble

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The rebuild landed one day early

Hedge funds spent the last two weeks putting back on the trade they had spent the summer taking off. Goldman Sachs prime brokerage data shows funds bought US technology, media and telecom stocks in 10 of the last 11 sessions [c761], a two-week buying pace that is the fastest since June 2025 and sits in the 97th percentile of the past five years [c762]. Semiconductors and semiconductor equipment led it [c761]. Positioning in the group had fallen to the 25th percentile before the buying started [c769], which is why the desks read it as a repair rather than a chase. Then Dario Amodei spent the weekend arguing the industry should slow the pace of frontier-model development [c603], and Asia opened on it. The KOSPI fell 2.74% [c582], SK Hynix 6.60% [c583] and SoftBank 11.24% [c585], with Nasdaq 100 futures down 1.25% [c64].

One essay moves this much because the risk sits in individual names rather than the index [d4]. The 200-day volatility of Goldman's basket of technology winners against losers runs near 76 against roughly 13 for the S&P 500 [c1705], so single stocks have been moving about six times more violently than the market [c1706]. Broad-market fear had actually fallen going into the weekend, with Goldman's Panic Index dropping three points on Friday, its third-largest one-day fall in three years [c854]. Freshly rebuilt single-name positions met a headline aimed at exactly those names, with almost nothing priced at the index level to cushion it [d23].

The argument is over what the essay actually changes. Deutsche Bank's reading is that the safety debate shifts the composition of AI spending toward oversight and governance rather than shrinking it, because the race between companies and between countries makes voluntary restraint hard to imagine [c338][c336]. Goldman's desk is buying the dip in its AI hardware basket [c433], and J.P. Morgan would buy the pullback too [c462]. Morgan Stanley's objection runs through politics instead of safety, where roughly 500 local jurisdictions have already passed data-center bans or moratoriums [c646] and a slowdown is no longer a risk the bank calls negligible [c670].

What decides it is a level, not an argument. $SMH has broken the bottom of its chart formation near $544 [c214] and trades below its 100-day average price [c215], and BTIG expects semiconductors to eventually test their 200-day average, about 17% lower [c224]. If power, land and data-center capacity are still the binding constraint behind Jensen Huang's $3-4tn of AI infrastructure spending by 2030 [c395][c396], this is a positioning flush. If $SMH keeps sliding toward that 200-day line, the safety debate has turned into a spending debate.

## 3. BRIEFS

### A hike built on pricing, not on data

Goldman Sachs added a 25 basis point hike (one basis point is one hundredth of a percent) to its September forecast on Friday after August core CPI printed 0.29% [c932][c1193], while stating plainly that it sees no strong economic case for raising rates [c995]. The reason is market pricing near 90% [c933], high enough that holding costs more than hiking. Where the banks split is what follows. Goldman expects the rate projections to show a 10-8 majority for only one hike this year [c1008] and Governor Waller to dissent [c1001]. BofA expects 25bp at each of September, October and December [c887], SocGen adds March on top of that [c968] and sees the median projection showing two hikes for 2026 [c967], and Morgan Stanley still expects a hold [c1061]. Wednesday's projections settle the question, and October pricing already near 50% [c935] is what moves on them.

### The 30-year is doing the tightening

30-year Treasury yields closed last week at their highest level since 2007 [c299][c247]. The 10-year rose 18.5 basis points to 4.97%, its biggest weekly move since May [c264], the 2-year added 25.9bp to 4.63% in the largest weekly jump since April 2025 [c261][c262], and German 10-year yields reached a post-2009 high of 3.50% [c265]. Energy prices did most of the work [c260], and a Treasury buyback that took only $5.19bn against a $6bn maximum did not help [c277]. Goldman Sachs raised its year-end 10-year forecasts to 4.75% in the US and 3.25% in Germany [c275], while BofA forecasts the 10-year back at 4.50% against a 4.64% spot and the 30-year at 4.90% against 5.21% [c254][c255]. Buyers keep showing up anyway, with asset managers' Treasury futures length near cycle highs [c784] and $TLT taking its largest inflow since September 2024 [c227]. Mizuho is staying out of long-dated bonds into Wednesday [c297].

### Two chokepoints and one shut pipeline

Brent is back above $107 after Saudi Arabia shut its East-West pipeline following a drone attack [c1091][c1106], the route that carries 4 to 5 million barrels a day around the Strait of Hormuz [c1153]. Inventories at Yanbu may cover export commitments for only another five to seven days [c1154], and roughly 4% of world supply is at risk if the outage runs past that [c1155]. Houthi forces advancing on Bab el-Mandeb turn one chokepoint into two [c1107]. Diesel is where the pressure is visible: October futures rose more than 8% between September 8 and 11 [c1103], open interest grew $1.9bn as traders rebuilt long positions [c759], refining margins broke above their 2022 crisis highs [c1149], and distillates now price as though crude were near $140 [c1152]. J.P. Morgan's oil trading desk expects Hormuz to stay contested past the November midterms and calls a retest of roughly $120 likely [c1131][c1133]. The postponed Iran-Gulf meeting in Oman is the next thing that can change it [c1618].

### The permits, not the demand

Morgan Stanley's case is that the binding constraint on AI spending is becoming political rather than financial. A Gallup poll finds seven in ten Americans oppose data-center construction in their own communities [c641], with roughly two-thirds of Republicans, nearly 80% of independents and 83% of Democrats against [c642]. New York enacted a state-level pause in July, Texas ordered a review and froze new grid connections, about 500 jurisdictions have passed bans or moratoriums and 14 more states are considering them [c644][c645][c646][c647]. Roughly $156bn of projects were cancelled or delayed in 2025 and another $130bn was affected in the first quarter of this year [c648][c649]. The stake is large because AI investment contributed 0.8 percentage points of US growth in the first half of 2026 and accounts for 0.85 points of the 2.6% forecast for 2027 [c1589][c1590]. A slowdown is not the base case [c670], and because the constraint is regulatory the bank reads it as good for hyperscalers and bad for the companies buying AI [c539][c671].

### Supply arrived and nothing broke

Investment grade companies sold $73bn of bonds last week, the third busiest week of a record year and close to the $70bn Goldman's credit desk had estimated [c1299][c1300]. Composition mattered more than the total. There were 46 deals in four days, no jumbo trades, and only 7% of the supply came in maturities beyond 20 years against 20% over the prior three months [c1301][c1302]. That pushed longer-dated spreads tighter than shorter ones, with Goldman's 30-year basket 2bps tighter against roughly half a basis point for the 5-year and 10-year baskets [c1258]. The desk expects $50bn next week and argues credit is not the best way to express a short over the next four weeks [c1303][c1260], with a surprise Microsoft deal named as the main supply risk to that view [c16]. Bear Traps takes the other side, arguing investment grade credit has deteriorated ahead of every major US equity drawdown since 2021 [c1282].

### The yen trade that funds itself in euros

The yen has gained more than 4% against the dollar in eight sessions [c1321], and the Bank of Japan is 98% priced to raise rates on Friday [c1560], to 1.25% on SocGen's forecast with a 2% endpoint [c1567][c1568]. Goldman's desk wants a long yen position funded out of euros rather than dollars [c1322], because higher energy prices, a Fed hike and weaker risk appetite all argue for near-term dollar strength [c1323]. It quotes a six-month digital put struck at 140 in dollar-yen at 11.15% [c1326]. The forecasts are unusually far apart: BofA sees 153 by September and 149 by December [c1340] against Goldman carrying 163 at end-2026 [c1364]. The larger issue is that 10-year Japanese government bond yields near 3% make domestic bonds competitive again [c1573], which would take Japan out of the market as the marginal buyer of everyone else's long-dated debt [c282].

### Fifteen is a strange number here

The VIX sits at 15 with the one-week S&P straddle priced at 130 basis points [c1707], and there have been 27 consecutive sessions without an intraday range wider than 1% [c1711]. Goldman attributes that compression to same-day options dominating a market where cash volumes have fallen almost 40% from July's record [c1712][c819]. Protection is not as cheap as the headline suggests. The premium traders pay for downside puts over upside calls has steepened to its highest since July while at-the-money volatility has barely moved [c1696], and hedge funds shorted index and ETF products last week at the fastest pace since the April 2025 tariff selloff [c813]. Goldman's desk calls volatility dormant rather than dead and sees little room for complacency in the 15 weeks left this year [c1682]. Morgan Stanley's own testing found that adding a one-month straddle to its skew strategy raised the Sharpe ratio (return earned per unit of risk taken) by 19% and cut the worst drawdown by 38% [c1684][c1685].

### Adoption up, prices down

Morgan Stanley opened thematic coverage of digital assets with a base case it calls convergence, meaning selective institutional adoption across both public and permissioned blockchains through 2030 [c525]. The adoption evidence is concrete. Stablecoin supply is above $300bn [c1781], tokenized real-world assets exceed $30bn and are six times their level at the start of 2025 [c527], and about a quarter of the Bloomberg World Financials Index by count and 60% by market value already have live, piloted or announced digital-asset projects [c526]. Prices have gone the other way, with the crypto market 37% below its October 2025 peak of $4.3tn [c1757] and Bitcoin roughly 30% below where it traded a year ago [c1758]. The gap is that the marginal buyer is still retail, leverage and narrative rather than the institutions doing the adopting [d18]. Stablecoins make the point: supply is large, but actual payment volume runs near $63bn a month [c1762] against 0.25% of global money supply [c1781].
