# Bonds Priced Stress, Stocks Didn't

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Stress is in the bonds, not in the stocks

The 10-year Treasury yield finished last week at 5.27%, a fifth straight weekly rise [c244], and the S&P 500 still closed within 1% of its record [c589]. Last week the gap between what France and Germany pay to borrow for 10 years widened 32 basis points (one-hundredth of a percent), the biggest one-week move in the data back to 1990 [c343]. That put the spread at 141, and it touched 160 at one point on Friday [c666][c667]. Corporate borrowing costs barely registered any of it. Euro investment-grade spreads reached only 101 basis points, far below the 2011-12, 2020 and 2022 stress episodes, and the STOXX 600 lost 1.1% [c665][c839].

Price swings tell the same story. The MOVE index, which measures how much the bond market expects to move, has gone from 77 on September 10 to 113 [c654], practically back at the March panic highs [c287], while the VIX closed at 15.51 [c658]. Goldman's own panic gauge for US equity volatility sits below 2 out of 10 [c656] and the ratio of VIX to high-yield bond ETF volatility is at its lowest in more than three years, so credit is pricing far more stress than stocks are [c657]. Part of the gap is mechanical. With the S&P no longer reacting to macro news, traders have moved macro bets into bond ETF options, where volume has more than doubled in three weeks toward 2.2 to 2.4 million contracts on a 10-day average [c1][c2] [d2].

Friday's jobs report did the repricing. US payrolls rose 29,000 against 133,000 expected [c931]. The priced chance of a Fed hike in October fell from 64% to 23% over the week, and about 86 basis points of hikes remain priced over the next 12 months [c301][c315].

Deutsche Bank's call is that this resolves against stocks. Above-target inflation limits how dovish central banks can get [c288], and unless the stress fades quickly the way it did after SVB in March 2023, risk assets come under mounting pressure [c840]. Goldman works from the same flows and reaches the opposite place [c262]. Quarter-end selling is done, funds have already cut exposure, buybacks restart as companies report, and retail sat out September, which is why Pasquariello likes the setup into Q4 [c417][c418][c419][c420][c535] [d15].

BofA supplies the level that settles it. With the 10-year between 5% and 6% and rising, the S&P has still averaged 0.8% a month [c270], between 6% and 7% that average falls to roughly zero [c236], and above 7% it turns negative [c237]. The nearer test is real yields. Goldman's asset-manager clients sold another $5.5bn of S&P futures alongside a 25 basis point jump in 10-year real yields [c247][c260], and if real yields start falling, the biggest reason to cut equity exposure runs in reverse [c217].

## 3. BRIEFS

### France has a buyer problem

Deutsche Bank's rates team puts French government bonds 40 to 50 basis points cheap even against France's weak finances, and is still not willing to buy them outright [c661][c678]. The other side of the trade is who buys. Japanese investors are pulling money home out of French and other foreign government debt, and Sumitomo sold its entire French holding, which TS Lombard reads as a sign of the broader shift [c367][c368]. The France-Germany gap could break above 200 basis points before the ECB intervenes meaningfully, on that view, because the ECB cannot underwrite French policy while Paris refuses to adjust [c365][c366]. Pricing has already moved, with Deutsche Bank's economists now seeing the ECB's peak rate nearer 2.75% to 3% against 3.5% priced eight business days ago [c941]. For European stocks the arithmetic is Goldman's: every 10 basis point widening in French and Italian spreads to German bonds costs 0.9% of Europe's price-to-earnings multiple, what investors pay per dollar of profit [c255]. Goldman would buy dips in European banks only if French risk stays contained [c329].

### Nobody is positioned for up

Goldman's prime brokerage data has US long/short hedge fund net leverage down a third straight week to 46.4%, the lowest since April 2025 and the 2nd percentile of the past five years [c468]. Its clients' net US positioning sits at the 0th percentile [c410], and Russell 2000 exposure is at the 7th percentile with leveraged funds carrying record short positions as of September 22 [c412][c413]. Some of that has started to unwind, with $900mn of Russell 2000 futures open interest coming out since September 29 [c414]. Deutsche Bank supplies the warning attached to light positioning: volatility-targeting funds sit at the 98th percentile of equity allocation with almost no room to add, which makes the flow picture one-sided if volatility rises [c403][c455] [d17]. Goldman will not call the small-cap trade yet, saying sustained Russell 2000 outperformance needs durable relief in rates before investors rebuild [c503].

### Earnings arrive narrow

Q3 reporting starts the week of October 12, and about 70% of S&P 500 market value will have reported by the end of October [c622][c623]. Consensus has index earnings per share growing 27% from a year ago, down from 33% realized in Q2 [c625]. Goldman's breakdown of that number is the story: companies benefiting from AI infrastructure spending supply over half the growth, with hyperscaler capital spending up 116% [c539][c540], while the median company slows from 14% growth to 9% [c597]. The top 10 contributors account for 68% of index earnings growth against 48% in Q2, and Micron and Nvidia alone are 34% of it [c643][c124]. The index is 2% from its high while the median stock is 17% below its own, the narrowest participation since the dot-com years [c601]. Tariff refunds of $77bn in Q2 and $69bn in Q3, roughly 6% of US pre-tax profits, keep flattering the comparison [c339].

### Goldman turns on semis

Two months in which the semiconductor index fell 11% while the S&P rose 4% have moved Goldman's desk from cautious ahead of Q2 to constructive ahead of Q3 reports [c862][c773]. The preferred buys are Applied Materials, Seagate and Microchip, with Qualcomm, KLA and Western Digital as the weaker side [c17][c101]. Microchip is the favorite on the argument that analysts still under-model the recovery in analog chips [c111], and Goldman carries Applied Materials at Buy with a $670 target, 32 times a normalized $21.00 of earnings [c13]. Pulled-forward equipment spending and visibility into 2028 are what drive the estimate revisions [c774], and August shipments ran about 10% above the long-run demand trend [c778]. The derivatives desk prefers one-month call spreads on Applied Materials and Cadence, where selling the higher strike is unusually well paid and Applied Materials sits at the 83rd percentile of that pricing [c20].

### Brazil repriced in one night

Flavio Bolsonaro took 47.0% to Lula's 45.2% in the first round, which Goldman called substantially better for markets than expected [c336], and the runoff is October 25 [c324]. The Brazil ETF EWZ rose 13%, with Goldman's desk seeing hedge funds buying against long-only supply and an early chase for short-dated upside calls [c74][c75]. Positioning explains the size of the move. Morgan Stanley counts roughly $8bn of foreign money out of Brazilian stocks since mid-April and derivatives positioning about three times heavier than the 2022 election, so dealer hedging can amplify whatever the October 25 runoff prints [c492][c517][c518]. Deutsche Bank's Latin America strategists expect about 100 basis points of rally in local rates as election risk premium unwinds, and prefer bets that short-term Brazilian rates fall [c328]. Goldman's Brazil Rate Sensitive basket is the trade it has flagged into the vote, with up to 30% re-rating if cuts reach 200 basis points [c330][c331].

### The oil curve keeps being wrong

Brent crude rose 4.94% last week to $102.25, using the December contract to get past the roll [c705]. Deutsche Bank's point is that the futures curve has spent more than six months pricing a decline that never arrives, quoting $90 in six months and $83 in twelve while the front contract grinds to fresh highs [c709][c703]. On that reading the inflation risk from a supply shock this long is understated, with energy costs able to leak into core prices the way they did in 2021-22 [c940][c939]. Goldman is calmer about the physical side, noting Persian Gulf exports are back at their 2025 average [c718]. The risk itself has not gone anywhere, with the Pentagon sending a third carrier group and 10,000 troops to the Gulf and Tehran preparing a response to any renewed large-scale attack [c684][c685]. European diesel fell 7.31% last week after the G7 said it would release up to 100 million barrels of reserves [c711].

### The AI bill moves to the credit market

Goldman's credit strategists see hyperscaler bond issuance reaching roughly $250bn this year against less than $20bn in 2024, and $420bn in 2027 [c672], and argue borrowing on that scale is cutting investor appetite for long-dated bonds and raising the chance long yields overshoot what fundamentals justify [c258]. The spending itself is not slowing. US data center construction ran $85bn in August, up 73.2% from a year earlier, while total US construction fell 1.7% [c785]. SEB is watching the funding side rather than the valuation: hyperscalers' free cash flow has fallen sharply, some AI companies need banks and capital markets to cover negative cash flow, and investor attention is shifting from high multiples to leverage and financing need [c877][c676][c677]. A correction in AI assets would not stay inside the equity market, on SEB's read, because data centers now matter to the real economy [c879][c917].

### Banks report into a fair multiple

Goldman opens its large-cap bank previews with the group down 5% in Q3 purely on a lower multiple [c861], trading at 60% of the S&P's multiple, one point below its historical relationship, and at the 50th percentile of its own two-year-forward earnings multiple [c895][c894]. The revenue mix normalizes from here: trading comes back toward normal after a strong Q2, with soft spots in bond and currency trading given the move in rates, and a much slower investment banking quarter that is still up on the year [c891][c858][c892]. Interest income gets help from loan growth and the repricing of fixed-rate assets, offset by what banks have to pay depositors [c893]. Buyback capacity moderates after three straight years of higher capital requirements for the biggest banks [c860][c871]. Wells Fargo sits on Goldman's Conviction List with Bank of America and Citigroup behind it, and a re-rating of the group needs clarity on the shape of the rates curve [c212][c47][c908].

### Japan is the AI trade abroad

Japan is the standout region for Goldman's Asia desk, driven by a $140bn data center investment push and a Bank of Japan normalization path slow enough for stocks to absorb [c747][c918]. Flows have followed, favoring Japan AI exposure over emerging-market beneficiaries, and Goldman's Japan AI basket is pulling away from its broader Asia data center basket [c748][c749]. The performance is there, with TOPIX up 20.0% this year in local currency and the Nikkei 36.6% against about 11.8% for the S&P, though currency swings have kept it from feeling like a winning year [c754][c887]. Goldman's preferred way in is its Optical Networking basket, which it calls the key beneficiary of the next leg of AI infrastructure spending [c751]. Investors are still underweight Asia even with price targets on the region's largest companies implying about 90% upside [c753].
