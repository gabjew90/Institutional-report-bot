# Builders Punished, Owners Paid

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The Moratorium Splits The AI Trade

Two days after the midterm results, the AI trade stopped moving as one block. The 72 S&P 500 companies that sell equipment and services into the data center buildout fell 13.3% while the five hyperscalers that already own working compute rose 1.7%, with the rest of the index flat [c414]. State pauses on new approvals cut future capacity, which raises what the owners of existing capacity can charge and lowers what they have to spend to add more [d5]. Goldman puts roughly 45% of planned US capacity in states where that risk now sits [c154], and Georgia, Ohio and Michigan are lining up behind Texas [c155].

The selling went further than the earnings. Next-year profit estimates for the AI spending beneficiaries have risen more than 50% relative to the S&P 500 [c379], yet the group trades at 13.1 times forward earnings against 17.8 for the index [c415], the cheapest in both absolute and relative terms since late 2022 [c416].

Goldman's own desks sit on opposite sides. Its custom baskets desk wants the rebound and recommends a January 110/120 call spread on the hyperscaler basket costing 1.8% [c382], and Adam Bubes raised the firm's US data center construction spending forecasts to $80 billion for 2026 and $100 billion for 2027, from $67 billion and $87 billion [c103], adding $125 billion for 2028 [c104], arguing the restrictions push spending later rather than shrink the total [c92]. The credit side is positioned the other way. Amanda Lynam sees near-term credit risk tilted toward wider spreads [c551] after hyperscaler bond sales hit $296 billion so far this year, nearly $50 billion beyond Goldman's own assumption [c549], and the desk recommends selling $500 million of the 30-year investment grade basket against buying about 143 S&P futures contracts [c548]. On the day the S&P fell 4.4%, that spread widened only 2.2 basis points, one basis point being one hundredth of a percent [c546], against the roughly 8 the historical relationship implied [c561].

Morgan Stanley's analysts put the weight on the delay itself, seeing many Texas projects at risk of significant slippage given the incoming governor's regulatory reach [c166]. Texas alone is 26% of cumulative net capacity additions from 2026 to 2030 [c451].

Two dates decide it. A Texas state data center audit is due December 10 [c469], and Wells Fargo's Jerry Revich expects the pause to run about as long as the state's regular legislative session, January 12 to May 31, 2027 [c467]. Goldman's Michael Ng puts the test plainly: moratoriums are not bans or cancellations, and the risk is projects arriving late to service [c458]. If approvals restart on that timeline, the discount on the sellers closes. If the pause outlives the session, the delay starts charging developers higher financing costs, lost tax breaks and in some cases cancelled contracts [c552].

## 3. BRIEFS

### Leverage Already Sits At The Lows

Hedge funds spent the week cutting, not adding. US funds that run both long and short books reduced gross exposure by 8.7 points to 207.5%, the largest weekly drop since March 2020 [c271], and net exposure fell 3.7 points to 43.3%, the lowest reading in five years [c272]. Beta-adjusted net exposure is down 14.1 points this year to its weakest since May 2025 [c309]. Goldman's high-touch desk has sold a net $30 billion of technology since the decline began, more than the $21 billion it sold in July [c285][c286]. Mark Wilson reads net leverage at an 18-month low and nine straight days of risk reduction as the raw material a bottom is made from [c225]. The counterweight is mechanical, with Goldman modelling trend-following strategies holding $28 billion of S&P futures to sell over the next week and $45 billion over the next month [c410][c411].

### Thursday Was Structure, Not News

Thursday's Nasdaq 100 drop measured 4.9 standard deviations [c412], a size that should appear roughly once every 8,000 years if returns followed a normal distribution [c436], and the S&P 500's move was itself a 4 standard deviation day [c413]. Goldman's Wilson traces it to who owns the market rather than to any headline: more than half of stocks are held passively and a large share of daily volume comes from traders not looking at fundamentals [c185], so when leveraged, similarly modelled fast money hits its risk limits at once, nobody price-sensitive is left on the other side [c186][d1]. ETFs made up 32% of volume by Goldman's estimate [c298], and Nomura's McElligott sized levered ETF selling at $47 billion with another $61 billion from volatility-targeting funds [c311]. Fundamental technology funds lost 410 basis points in the session [c299], erasing a 2026 gain that had stood near 24% before the summer selloff [c301]. Wilson's warning is that none of that structure was repaired [c153].

### ETFs Did Most Of The Trading

US-listed ETFs traded $580 billion on November 5, the third largest day on record and 33% of all market volume against a 28% average [c318]. QQQ set its own record at $86 billion [c18], the levered and inverse semiconductor funds together printed more than $37 billion [c39], and the Magnificent Seven fund turned over $890 million [c36]. Money went the wrong way for the move, with levered funds taking in $3.3 billion while inverse funds lost $2.2 billion, the widest gap on record and a net $1.1 billion into the complex [c321][c320]. That is the normal pattern, since levered inflows have historically been strongest on the worst days [c322]. The complex is still shrinking, with assets just under $140 billion after a $55 billion fall over 20 days and gross exposure down $135 billion [c324][c325]. Volume stayed heavy into Friday at 35% of all trading [c330].

### The Hiding Place Got Expensive

US consumer staples just posted their best month since October 2000 [c181], and Goldman's staples basket is up 19.8% against the S&P 500 over the past 22 trading days, the highest reading since at least 2014 [c183]. Goldman's Miller recommends shorting that basket, which trades at its largest valuation premium to the S&P in more than a decade [c145], with a forward dividend yield about 2.05 points below the 10-year Treasury yield, the 1st percentile of its own history [c146]. In the nine previous months when staples rallied more than 8%, they went on to lag the S&P over the following three months in seven cases, by as much as 14.1 points [c147]. Goldman's structure pays roughly seven times on 14% of notional if XLP closes below 97.5% of spot and the 10-year swap rate falls more than 15 basis points below the forward by January 15, 2027 [c67]. The rate half is the weak leg, because October payrolls of 165K put a December Fed hike back in play [c505].

### Oil Gave Back The War Premium

January Brent touched $118 on October 30 as the market priced a post-midterm Gulf escalation [c346], then closed the week just under $98, the steepest weekly drop since April 2020 [c361] and the largest in dollar terms in data going back to 1988 [c362]. One session accounted for 8.7% of it, with $2.7 billion of open interest gone [c359]. Goldman's Robert Quinn counts $6.6 billion of Managed Money selling between October 27 and November 3 [c288], $4.9 billion of it liquidation of existing longs [c290], taking net length from about $28 billion in October to about $19 billion by November 4 [c291]. In the three prior episodes this size, selling ran on for another two weeks [c295]. UBS's Dominic Ellis argues fundamentals still floor the price, since a near-700 million barrel drawdown in global inventories will not refill because Congress changed hands [c344]. Call buyers are already back, with downside protection cheaper and calls repurchased [c343], and fresh strikes on Iranian air defenses at Qeshm Island followed Friday's peace call [c474].

### Payrolls Beat, And The Fed Is The Problem

October payrolls rose 165k against Morgan Stanley's 60k estimate and a 70k consensus, with prior months revised up a net 84k [c486][c487]. Private payrolls added 159k, lifting the three-month average to 126k from 82k over six months [c488], and professional and business services contributed 70k [c493]. The unemployment rate edged down to 4.14% from 4.18% [c489] while wage growth kept slowing, with average hourly earnings at 3.1% over the year against 3.9% twelve months earlier [c502]. Morgan Stanley reads the report as keeping the Fed on track for a December hike [c596], while flagging that jobless claims, job openings, hiring plans and ADP have all run softer [c500] and that production worker hours fell 0.2% on the month [c501]. Tuesday's inflation print is the counterweight, with Citi's economists looking for core CPI at 0.19% and headline at 0.16% on the month, both below consensus [c512][c513].

### Morgan Stanley Calls The Last Leg

Morgan Stanley argues the correction that began mid-year is in its final phase, with the cap-weighted S&P 500 catching down to breadth that already improved [c390]. The equal-weight index has beaten it by 6% since September [c392] and the share of S&P 500 members above their 200-day average price has risen about 20 points to 62% [c393]. The year-end base case stays 8000 [c391], but the firm wants either 7100 to 7300 on the index or a capitulation reading from its sentiment gauge, which has not arrived, before adding risk harder [c419][c310]. Industrials is its top pick among quality cyclicals [c117], helped by free cash flow surprises moving stocks more than earnings surprises this season [c205]. Goldman's Tony Pasquariello agrees on direction and not on duration, expecting one more push higher [c370] and a less rewarding 2027 [c407], with the largest increase in net equity supply since 2000 landing that year [c264].

### Power Names Carry The Policy Risk

Morgan Stanley calls the Texas governor's race by far the most important election result for data center development [c207], with ERCOT interconnection requests at roughly 474 GW, more than five times the grid's record peak demand, and data centers about 90% of new power requests [c208]. Gina Hinojosa's win raises policy risk to the pace of approvals and construction [c164], and her platform included a moratorium on new ones [c165], while Amy Acton's win in Ohio puts project delays there in play [c168], including a 10 gigawatt project involving NVIDIA and a major US AI lab [c7]. The firm still carries asset values above market: $129 a share on NRG against $96 [c37], $430 on TLN against $357 [c61], about $180 on VST against $140 [c63], a $378 target on CEG [c9], and $51.50 on CIFR, whose existing leases and mining assets alone it values at $18 [c22][c24]. It expects the first reaction to run the other way, with VST leading the knee-jerk underperformance, then NRG and CEG [c10]. WMB stays its high-conviction call on behind-the-meter data center power demand [c64].

### SpaceX Keeps Growing Inside The Index

SpaceX's public float has gone from 5% at the IPO to roughly a third of shares [c40], and Goldman expects about half to be freely traded by the middle of 2027 [c41]. Passive ETFs held around $30 billion of the stock at the end of October, about 1.5% of its market value, with roughly $20 billion of that in Nasdaq-100 trackers [c43][c44]. The index rules damp the mechanical effect, weighting a company at three times its float market value while float sits under 33% and at full market value above it [c429]. The next scheduled Russell 1000 Growth rebalance is December 11 [c433], and Goldman estimates a rebalance today would pull over $10 billion of additional passive demand [c51]. S&P 500 entry looks like a second-half-2027 question, once GAAP profitability arrives with Q2 2027 results [c47][c49]. For all the size, stripping SpaceX out would lower the Nasdaq-100's price-to-earnings multiple by less than half a turn [c58].
