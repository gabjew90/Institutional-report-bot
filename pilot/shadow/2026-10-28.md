# Funding Costs Break AI

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Capital, not demand, is what broke

Thursday delivered the demand confirmation the market had asked for all year, and the market sold anyway. Meta's beat confirmed AI advertising demand [c41], and the stock fell 6.98% while the cost of insuring its debt widened [c28]. Western Digital guided roughly 20% above on earnings and still reversed from up 4% to down 8% [c188]. The S&P 500 fell 1.2% and the Nasdaq 100 2.9% [c1036], even though 335 S&P names closed higher [c1031], and AI stocks had their worst day against the rest of the index since the DeepSeek selloff [c1030].

The mechanism is funding, not products [d1] [d23]. Goldman's Rich Privorotsky argues the selloff reprices the cost of the capital paying for the buildout, which leaves issuers three choices, cut spending, delay deployment, or raise equity, and none of them support share prices [c321]. The figures behind that: Meta's disclosed future spending commitments rose to $825 billion from about $700 billion in July [c40], Goldman tracks hyperscaler off-balance-sheet debt at $3.6 trillion and expects it above $4 trillion once the rest report [c322], and Goldman estimates CoreWeave needs roughly $61 billion more to power its backlog [c8].

Positioning turned that into forced selling. All three hedge fund strategies Goldman tracks fell more than 1% on the same day for the first time since Covid [c680], its basket of crowded longs against crowded shorts fell 4.2%, the worst move since 2011 and down 10.4% for the month [c681], and funds have cut exposure in 7 of the past 8 sessions [c655], leaving net leverage at 43.4%, the bottom of its five-year range [c569].

Goldman reads cleaner positioning and tells clients to buy the dip selectively in Power, Hyperscalers, Inference and Cyber [c234], paying 2.75% for a November call spread on its AI basket [c235] and funding it by shorting cyclicals [c329]. Barclays' Asia positioning team tells clients the opposite, to use any bounce to de-risk [c491], and BTIG expects the S&P to test its 200-day average near 7300 instead of rallying after the midterms [c1016].

Next week decides it. Privorotsky says the test is how the hyperscalers turn backlog into revenue, how fast they deploy, and what they say about funding [c406], with 44% of S&P market value reporting [c400] and options pricing a 1.80% index move through October 30 [c1050].

## 3. BRIEFS

### AI's financing bill shows up in credit

Junk bond spreads have widened through Goldman's own downside case [c863]. US high yield closed Thursday at 338 basis points (one basis point is a hundredth of a percent), past the 335 basis points Goldman's credit strategists had set as their downside level, and the widest of the past year [c866]. The bottom of the stack is worse, with CCC-rated paper at 1,111 basis points, 313 basis points wider in three months and the widest in three years [c867]. Goldman's Amanda Lynam expects spreads to overshoot those downside ranges and sees risk into year-end tilted toward more widening [c865] [c868]. Single names carry it: insuring CoreWeave's debt cost 66 basis points more in a day [c7], the stock fell 10.06% to its lowest since May 2025 [c6], and its bond yields are back above 15% [c9]. Barclays argues the fundamentals are better than that, judging neo-cloud supply and demand more resilient than the price action implies [c195] and the spillover to hyperscalers contained [c378].

### Bonds are working as the AI hedge

The 10-year Treasury yield fell about 15 basis points Thursday, its largest one-day move since August 2025, as the AI selloff forced investors to mark down growth [c1075]. That puts the yield back below 5%, where Goldman's next trend-following buy trigger sits at 4.85% [c1077], and its model has those funds buying roughly $18.7 billion of bond futures over the coming week in a flat market [c1078]. Goldman's Delta One desk calls the reflexivity striking, since the selloff creating the equity problem also eases the Treasury supply that was the rates problem [c1076]. Privorotsky is less comfortable, saying a bond rally that works only as an AI hedge is not the most supportive setup for equities [c1114]. UBS puts the move down to trend-following and program flows plus short covering rather than any change in inflation views [c1095]. Citi is fading it, selling the middle of the curve ahead of next week's 7-year auction [c1109] into $213 billion of supply [c1110].

### The war premium in crude is binary

Brent reached $117 a barrel, about $7 higher on the week, after Houthi attacks threatened Saudi energy infrastructure [c905]. Goldman's oil trader says more than $20 a barrel of war risk premium is already in that price [c903] and that a return to all-out war would probably push Brent above $120 [c904]. The same desk's scenarios run both ways: Gulf supply next year only 0.3 million barrels a day below February levels takes Brent to the mid-$70s by late 2027, below the forward curve [c66], while supply 5 million barrels a day short sends it to roughly $140 or higher by early 2027 [c64]. Privorotsky calls the outcome binary, with a fast targeted strike that spares infrastructure able to trigger an immediate unwind of the premium [c949]. Gas is the second channel, with northwest Europe starting winter on its lowest inventories in 14 years [c910] even as Goldman forecasts Dutch gas easing to 70 euros a megawatt hour from 87 now [c952].

### France has become a bond problem

The French 10-year yield is 5.08%, and its gap over Germany is 173 basis points, the widest since the euro was introduced [c1154] [c1151]. French 10-year yields now sit above US Treasuries for the first time since 2013 [c1150] and 50 basis points above Italy's, a reversal that did not happen even in the 2011 crisis [c1162]. Goldman's Chryssafis says cheaper energy is the clearest route to compressing that gap [c1139] and expects wider spreads and more pressure on the euro without it [c1156]. The repricing is spreading past France. Market odds of another ECB hike by December fell to 69% from 90% at Wednesday's close [c1238], European junk bonds reached their widest level since April 2025 [c844], and euro-area bank shares fell 4.17% to a four-month low [c277].

### A hold with three dissents

Rabobank expects the Fed to leave rates at 3.75% to 4.00% this week [c994], but not unanimously, with Hammack, Kashkari and Logan expected to repeat their July dissents in favor of a hike [c963]. September's projections carried four dots implying two more quarter-point hikes before year-end [c970], and Rabobank reads three of those as belonging to the July dissenters, who actually vote [c972]. Skipping October buys the committee more data and avoids raising rates a week before the midterms [c996] [c998], after which Rabobank expects a December hike, made likelier by oil feeding into consumer prices [c966] [c974]. Chair Warsh is expected to withhold forward guidance as he did at Jackson Hole, and may say inflation trends have not meaningfully improved without saying a hike is imminent [c964] [c965]. Futures carry about three more hikes from here against the dot plot's one [c975].

### The private marks come down

OpenAI cut the target for its round in progress to $1.25 trillion [c365], down from the $1.4 trillion Morgan Stanley and Dragoneer had pitched to private wealth clients [c354]. A person familiar with that process said there has been zero clamouring for it, unlike the frenzy around earlier OpenAI rounds [c355]. Commitments are due October 30 on a round of up to $30 billion [c366], now expected to be led by Coatue and United Arab Emirates funds [c441]. Anthropic's investors have discussed roughly $1.5 trillion for its November listing, $500 billion under the $2 trillion previously penciled in [c367], so the two labs have taken about $650 billion off their hoped-for values inside a week [c368]. OpenAI is on the hook for some $1.5 trillion of compute commitments against that [c372]. Rockefeller Global Family Office's Jimmy Chang put the stake plainly, saying the market needs a successful listing because these companies need the funding [c429].

### The bug wave turns into a spending case

On October 21 Wiz disclosed PHP2Shell, two flaws in PHP's core engine rated as severe as 9.2 that let an attacker run code without logging in [c361], and says every PHP release of the last decade carries at least one of them [c362]. Oracle issued an emergency alert for a 9.1-rated flaw in how Java checks certificate chains [c356], and LiteLLM, an AI gateway library with roughly 23 million weekly downloads, had a malicious release pushed to PyPI and Docker [c363]. Epoch AI's tracker has high-severity disclosures across 21 large tech organizations running near 2,900 a month, about ten times the 2022 to 2025 norm [c281], while cautioning that reporting practices vary enough that part of the jump is more bugs being found rather than more bugs existing [c395]. Privorotsky calls the pace somewhat alarming [c318] and says it is hard to imagine security spending not accelerating materially as AI cuts the cost of finding and exploiting flaws [c230]. JPMorgan's Matt Reiner says security software was the only real pocket of client buying [c777].
