# Five Percent Breaks Everything

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The 10-year cleared 5% and equities finally flinched

September's flash reading of US business activity came in at a five-year high of 58.4 against a 55.3 consensus, and the bond market treated it as proof the Fed is not finished. Yields rose across every maturity, with 2-year, 5-year, 10-year and 30-year Treasury yields up 12, 16, 14 and 9 basis points (one-hundredth of a percent) on the day. The 10-year closed at 5.11%, the highest since 2007, and posted its biggest one-day jump since the market turmoil of April 2025. The 5-year cleared 5% for the first time since 2007, helped by a weak auction where $70bn of new 5-year notes sold at 5.03%, 3.1 basis points above where the market was pricing them just before the sale. Futures now put a 71% chance on an October hike, up from 53% on Tuesday, with 37 basis points of hikes priced by year-end. Stocks gave way, the S&P 500 falling 0.75%, its worst day in a month, with every major sector except energy lower.

The argument between the houses is whether this yield level breaks stocks or gets bought. JPMorgan puts fair value on the 10-year near 5.5% and argues that past yield-driven equity dips have been buying opportunities, so it intends to buy this one through a broadening trade, while holding a cautious, neutral equity stance into the Fed because energy prices will not settle lower. Morgan Stanley takes the other side of the level. Its work names 4.50% on the 10-year as the point where stocks become meaningfully rate-sensitive, the 10-year closed at 5.11%, and the short-term relationship between stock prices and yields is already negative, which is what high rate sensitivity looks like. It still holds an 8,000 year-end S&P 500 target on earnings resilience, with 7000-7100 as the downside case over the next three to five weeks if oil climbs further.

What settles it is the yield itself. BTIG reads the 10-year as consolidating inside an uptrend and says that read only flips on a break below roughly 4.92%. JPMorgan's own trigger is symmetric: lower oil and lower yields turn it bullish, while a consumer that breaks or an AI capital-spending cut turns it bearish.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Meta's agent is two weeks old and already repricing the sector

Meta shares are up 21% since Muse launched two weeks ago, against 1% for the S&P 500 over the same stretch. Muse is the top free download in the US and Canada app stores, with roughly 1.8 million iOS downloads in its first 12 days versus about 1.3 million for ChatGPT over a comparable period. JPMorgan reiterates Overweight with a $820 December 2027 target, about 23x its 2028 GAAP earnings estimate of $35.44, and expects the money to come from commissions on business agents rather than subscriptions, which it does not see before 2027. UBS reads the same adoption curve as evidence consumer agents are approaching mainstream use and points investors at the CPU supply chain rather than the app. The fight over who owns the transaction is already open: Amazon blocked Muse from its marketplace while Shopify partnered with it for agent checkout.

### Banks sold hard, and JPMorgan's own specialist says the reason is wrong

Financials fell 1.98% in the S&P 500 on worries that AI erodes bank margins and fees, the same trade that knocked the financials sector ETF down 2% while semiconductors rallied. JPMorgan's financials specialist O'Dwyer does not accept the story, saying there is very little in the Muse product that disrupts the unit economics of banking. He points at which names are moving: the crowded holdings C, MS, GS and USB dominate the selling volume while regionals and LAZ and JEF hold up, which reads as investors unwinding crowded books rather than the market re-rating the business. He wants to buy the drop patiently, favoring MS, PNC and FITB. Hedge funds went the other way this week, selling banks outright and adding shorts in regionals. BTIG sees a warning instead: the BKX bank index sits more than 10% below its 30-day high while the S&P is within 1% of a 52-week high, a split last seen in January 2000.

### Oil swung on a Hormuz headline and swung straight back

Brent closed at $99.02, below $100 for the first time in over two weeks, after a Kyodo report said Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days if the US lifted its naval blockade. Doubts about any deal reversed it, and Brent rebounded 3.86% to $103.08, which added to the hawkish pressure on bonds. JPMorgan's commodity team models Brent averaging $87 in 2027 if the conflict runs on, against $64 in a peace baseline, and escalation that pulls the Bab al-Mandab strait in would put a further large block of oil and LNG supply at risk. Saudi Arabia is restarting its East-West pipeline and aims for a partial resumption of flows by Saturday. On the product side, Politico reported preparation of a 90-day diesel export ban, which US Energy Secretary Wright said is not under discussion in any blanket form.

### The index is fine, the stocks underneath it are not

BTIG's read is that internals have detached from headline strength. The semiconductor index sits about 14% below its June high, yet only three of its 30 members are closer to their own 52-week highs than the index is, and 10 of the 30 are more than 30% off their highs. The S&P 500 has now logged five consecutive sessions with more stocks making new 52-week lows than new highs, even while the index trades near a record, and it is up 1.2% over the past month while nine of eleven sectors are down. The 2000 comparison is the point: 13 of the 20 current semiconductor index members that traded back then kept setting new highs after the index topped in March 2000. BTIG's conclusion for anyone still bullish on tech is to own the names near their highs, not the laggards.

### France is the bond market's other problem

The gap between French and German 10-year borrowing costs widened to 110 basis points, the widest since the 2012 euro crisis, as the German 10-year yield hit a post-2009 high of 3.55% and the French 10-year rose 15.4 basis points. The fiscal arithmetic behind it has not improved: the 2026 deficit is now forecast at 5.4% of GDP against a 5% target, with public debt at 117% of GDP, and the government plans a record €310 billion of bond sales in 2026. JPMorgan's momentum work shows positioning in French government bond futures versus German ones at an extreme short reading of -2.5, consistent with investors continuing to unwind overweights inside the euro area. The bank runs an option hedge that is short France against long European domestic names on politics and dislocation. Rate pricing is moving with it, with October ECB hike odds up from 48% on Tuesday to 66% at the close after Bundesbank's Nagel raised the idea of moving policy into mildly restrictive territory.

### Forty days out, the political risk is aimed at AI

A Democratic House flip is treated as settled, with conference speakers putting it at 90% and forecasts pointing to a 220 to 230 seat majority, while the Senate stays a toss-up despite the GOP's 53-47 hold. Affordability is the reason: only 25% of Americans are satisfied with how things are going, against a 33% historical average, and when gasoline prices are elevated the incumbent party's average midterm House losses rise to about 32 seats. That matters for oil because Middle East tension has pushed Brent into a $100-110 band, above the $75-100 range that held since late May. The policy consequence lands on AI regardless of who wins, with speakers calling it "the new China" as a bipartisan target, cities in 32 states advancing data-center moratoriums, and the House already passing a bill making large power-hungry data centers pay the full cost of grid upgrades. After the vote, JPMorgan flags an approaching debt ceiling and a ratings reassessment of the fiscal outlook as further pressure on Treasuries.

### Companies are generating more cash than they can spend

The US all-corporate financing surplus, meaning cash flow left over after capital spending, reached close to 2% of GDP in the second quarter of 2026, near the highest outside crisis periods since the data began in 1952. Strip out financials and it stands near 1.5% of GDP, the highest since 1958. JPMorgan's explanation for why aggregate capital spending has not risen to match is that AI spending is crowding out everything else, holding total capex growth down. The effect on markets is that companies need less outside funding and can keep buying back their own shares, particularly outside tech. JPMorgan expects tech's share of buybacks to shrink over the next five to ten years as AI infrastructure spending eats into hyperscaler cash surpluses.

### Bitcoin climbed back over what it costs to mine

JPMorgan puts the implied average cost of producing one bitcoin at roughly $85,000. The price moved back above that line in recent days, before the latest correction, after 280 days beneath it, compared with about 224 days below cost during the 2018 down-cycle. JPMorgan reads the crossing as reducing the pressure on miners to sell coins into the market to cover costs. Supporting that, network hashrate and mining difficulty have fallen about 19% and 15% from their peak last October as miners redirect capacity toward AI work. The bank's momentum measure for bitcoin now sits at a somewhat elevated +1.0, and it attributes the past week's rally to an unwind of bearish positions rather than fresh demand.

### Flows say rotation, not exit

Last week's ETF numbers were distorted by index rebalancing around Friday's quarterly futures and options expiry, so the headline prints are unreliable. Adjusted for that, US equity inflows ran modestly below average, with money leaving Financials and Health Care for Tech, Communication Services, Discretionary and Industrials. In bonds, ETF investors and asset managers shortened maturities, moving out of long-dated government bonds into short-dated ones. Commodity positioning turned defensive in spots, with Managed Money cutting copper longs sharply and energy ETF money going mostly into funds that profit when oil falls. Systematic trend-followers stayed net long global equities and added to those longs while remaining short global rates outside China. Hedge funds are the split signal: gross exposure has been rising globally at more than two standard deviations, yet the US measure of positioning momentum fell another 0.5 standard deviations this week, near the 25th percentile and approaching July's lows.
