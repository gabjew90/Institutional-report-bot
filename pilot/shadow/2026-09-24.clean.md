# The Five Percent Problem

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The bond market repriced growth, not inflation

September's flash composite business survey printed 58.4 against a 55.3 estimate and 56.0 the month before, a five-year high, per JPMorgan. Bonds sold off hard. Ten-year Treasuries had their worst session since April 2025, the yield closed at 5.11%, a post-2007 high, and the five-year yield rose above 5% for the first time since 2007. The government sold $70bn of five-year notes at 5.03%, 3.1bps above where the market expected them to price, and JPMorgan's rates desk said no buyers showed up even at the highest yield since 2007.

Which part of the yield moved is the whole story. Of the roughly 97 basis points (one-hundredth of a percentage point) that the ten-year has added this year, only around 10bps comes from the inflation rate priced into the bond market, per Deutsche Bank, and that priced inflation rate has barely moved in more than three years. The rest is the return investors demand after inflation, now back at 2008 levels. The extra yield charged for lending long has been flat for 18 months, so fiscal fear is not driving this either. What is left is where the Fed ends up. October hike odds went from 53% Tuesday to 69% at Thursday's close and 71% this morning, and Goldman Sachs now expects a second 25bp hike in October, changed from its view that September would be the only one.

The banks disagree about what that does to stocks. JPMorgan puts fair value on the ten-year near 5.5% and argues past yield-driven equity dips were buying opportunities. Morgan Stanley's trigger sits far below here, flagging a move through 4.50% as the level where stocks turn meaningfully rate-sensitive, while arguing that a Fed acting early lowers inflation uncertainty, so a higher policy rate need not raise long-term borrowing costs. Deutsche Bank lands between them, citing Matt Raskin's view that the neutral rate is elevated but still inside a plausible range at its higher end, with yields now edging toward that upper bound.

Speed decides it. Goldman Sachs says equities start reacting once the ten-year makes a two-standard-deviation move, roughly 30bps in two weeks or 50bps in a month, and it is up about 25bps in two weeks and 35bps in a month. BTIG's read that yields are still consolidating inside an uptrend breaks on a move below 4.92%.

## 3. BRIEFS

### The index is hiding what the average stock is doing

The S&P 500 has now gone six straight sessions with more stocks hitting 52-week lows than highs while the index itself sits back near record territory, per BTIG. It is up 1.2% over the past month even though 9 of its 11 sectors are down over that stretch, and the last time the index was within 1% of a 52-week high on internals this weak was January 2000. Banks are the clearest hole: the KBW bank index is more than 10% below its 30-day high. Semiconductors are split too, with the SOX about 14% under its June highs and only three of its 30 members closer to their own highs than the index is to its. BTIG's read is that this can persist, since 13 of the 20 current SOX members that existed in 2000 made new 52-week highs after the index peaked on March 14 that year, which is why it tells bullish investors to own the names at new highs rather than the laggards. The combination of surging new lows and an index within 1% of a record has otherwise only shown up in December 1999 and July 1929.

### Muse is repricing banks, and one desk is fading it

Meta shares are up 21% in the two weeks since Muse launched against a 1% gain for the S&P 500, per JPMorgan. UBS counts about 1.8 million iOS downloads in the US and Canada over the app's first 12 days versus roughly 1.3 million for ChatGPT at a comparable point. The market's second-order trade is short whatever an agent might disintermediate, and financials wore it, with the sector ETF down 2% on the session JPMorgan identified as the theme's return. JPMorgan's financials specialist O'Dwyer says there is very little in the Muse offering that disrupts the unit economics of banking and reads the selloff as a positioning unwind rather than a re-rating. He wants to fade the move lower in banks and wealth management, sizing patiently, with MS, PNC and FITB his preferred names. Meta itself traded about 2% lower pre-market after a Connect keynote that leaned on hardware over AI utility, per Goldman Sachs. JPMorgan keeps Overweight with an $820 target, about 23x its 2028 GAAP earnings estimate of $35.44.

### Oil is a policy problem now, not just a price

Brent broke back above $100, rising 4.6% to $103.59 as Iran's UN speech hardened the tone, per JPMorgan. Iran claims it can reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days, but President Pezeshkian says the demands attached have not changed: a return to the memorandum, an end to hostilities, and certain funds unfrozen. Trump said separately that he does not expect a deal until after the midterms. JPMorgan's commodity team prices the two paths: Brent averaging $87 in 2027 if the conflict persists, against $64 in its peace baseline. Escalation that draws the Bab al-Mandab Strait in would put a further large block of oil and LNG supply at risk. The nastier part is refined fuel. Apollo notes the US Gulf Coast diesel crack spread cleared $100 a barrel in August against a normal $15 to $30, and because diesel is an input embedded in the delivered cost of nearly every physical good rather than a one-time tax on discretionary spending, it migrates with a lag into core goods and services. Diesel is averaging about $6.52 a gallon, up 230% from $2.83 a year ago, per JPMorgan.

### Pump prices are running the midterm math

With the election about six weeks out, JPMorgan's conference speakers put a Democratic House takeover at 90% and call it done, while betting markets give Republicans only a 36% chance of holding the Senate. Fuel is the transmission channel. Middle East tensions have lifted Brent into a $100 to $110 range, above the $75 to $100 band that held since late May, per JPMorgan. Its economists estimate gasoline sustained above roughly $4 a gallon could offset much of the household tax relief from the administration's tax law, and when pump prices are elevated the incumbent party's average midterm House seat loss rises to about 32 seats. Voters are already there: 71% disapprove of the president's handling of the cost of living against 22% who approve. The condition that decides this theme is whether oil stays above $100 into late October, because that is what turns a polling gap into a seat count.

### The buyback bid leaves just as the robots are set to sell

Systematic trend-follower flows are not symmetric here. A down move greater than two standard deviations would trigger about $184.4 billion of selling, against up-market scenarios that cap out near $88.7 billion to $89.9 billion of buying. That lands as corporate demand steps away. An estimated 64% of S&P 500 companies had entered their buyback blackout window as of September 21, with the count climbing from roughly 320 in late September toward about 490 by mid-October and near 85% by the end of this week. The reason it matters is that buyback demand is price-insensitive and trend-following demand is not, so removing the first while the second is positioned to sell is what accelerates an ordinary pullback. The falsifiable version: if the S&P takes a two-standard-deviation down day inside the blackout stretch, the follow-through should be larger than the news that caused it.

### Small-cap volatility is the quiet asset in a loud rates market

The Russell 2000 is down about 8% since mid-August and is now sitting on its long-term trend line and 200-day average at the same time. Money is leaving: IWM just posted its largest weekly outflows since March. The odd part is the pricing of risk. Bond volatility jumped on this week's ugly price action while Russell 2000 volatility has barely moved. That gap only holds while the rates move stays orderly, and if it turns disorderly small-cap volatility is unlikely to stay this quiet, which is what makes Russell volatility screen as a cheap hedge against the rates selloff spreading. Watch whether the ten-year adds another leg without small-cap volatility reacting. That divergence resolves one way or the other, and it is the cleanest thing to monitor in the index nobody is hedging.

### Bitcoin cleared the miners' cost line

JPMorgan puts the implied average cost of producing one bitcoin near $85,000, and spot moved above that line in recent days after 280 days beneath it. The comparison that frames it: in the 2018 down-cycle the price spent roughly 224 days below production cost, so this stretch was the longer one. The mechanical read is less forced selling, because miners under water sell coin to fund operations and miners above water do not have to. Supply-side behavior has already shifted, with network hashrate and mining difficulty down roughly 19% and 15% from October's peak as miners pivot hardware toward AI work. Price tagged $87,000 overnight before easing back. The level to watch is the $85,000 cost line itself, since a decisive break back below it restores the forced-selling pressure this rally removed.

### Half the index no longer moves with the index

Goldman Sachs counts 45% of S&P 500 stocks with a negative beta to the index, by a wide margin an all-time high. Its explanation is index concentration produced by AI capital spending and tech dominance, though it says that is not the only one. There is a benign side, since the index itself now diversifies a risk-off unwind better than it used to. The cost is factor risk: a high share of negative-beta names raises the odds of a momentum unwind. Goldman's own hedges against that reversion are explicit. Brian Garrett's recommendation is puts on the firm's momentum index, and Christian Mueller-Glissmann prefers owning options on the equal-weight S&P 500 outperforming the cap-weighted index. If momentum reverts, those two positions are where the pain shows up as profit, and nothing else in the pack is priced for it.
