# Bonds Do The Tightening

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Thirty-Year Yields Take Over the Fed's Job

The bond market moved before the Fed could. The 10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.96%, its highest since October 2023 [c1206]. The 30-year reached 5.36%, a level last seen in 2007 [c1207], and the 2-year, which tracks Fed expectations most closely, rose to 4.59% [c1205]. The chain that got them there ran through energy. Brent crude closed 6.34% higher at $107.63 a barrel [c750], the producer price categories that feed the Fed's preferred inflation gauge came in firmer [c999], and futures moved September hike odds from 60% to 72% [c996]. The Treasury's attempt to lean against the move failed. It bought back $5.19bn of long-dated debt, short of the $6bn maximum it had announced, and yields rose across the curve anyway [c1208] [d23].

Two banks read the same levels in opposite directions. Deutsche Bank calls this normalisation rather than stress: US nominal GDP is running 6.6% year over year, comfortably above the 10-year yield [c1271], which makes 4.8% historically average if nominal growth settles at 5.5% [c1272] and about 5.3% reasonable if it settles at 6% on AI and inflation [c1161]. ING takes the other side and calls a break above 5% near inevitable [c1238], with higher inflation-adjusted yields and fiscal supply the dominant forces [c1239] and the rest of 2026 a dangerous gauntlet [c1251]. Long-dated swap pricing already implies two to three Fed hikes [c1031]. JPMorgan sides with the higher number, carrying a long-term 10-year target of 5.5% on 3% expected inflation [c1262], built on an assumed 2.5% inflation-adjusted Treasury yield [c1261].

Today's August CPI decides which read survives the week. JPMorgan's Feroli forecasts core at 0.21% month over month [c1587] and treats a rounded 0.2% as low enough for the Fed to hold and 0.3% as high enough to hike [c1053]. Deutsche Bank sits at the same 0.21% [c1570]. Goldman is higher at 0.23% against consensus of 0.2% [c1567]. Above 0.30% core, JPMorgan's desk maps a one-day S&P 500 decline of 1.5% to 2.5% [c946]. Into that print, Goldman's desk likes buying September puts on the small-cap ETF $IWM as the hedge, calling those options cheap [c2].

## 3. BRIEFS

### Crude Above $100 and the Diesel Problem

Brent closed 6.34% higher at $107.63 a barrel and WTI rose 6.69% to $102.48 [c750] [c751], with the six-month contract up to $88.85, which says traders expect the squeeze to last [c752]. The tightness is worse in fuels than in crude: US wholesale diesel trades within 1% of its 2022 peak [c754], and Saudi output has fallen to its lowest level since 1990 [c755] after Houthi forces captured the Yemeni port of Mokha near the Bab el-Mandeb shipping lane [c1397]. JPMorgan's Natasha Kaneva argues crude may not rise much further even in a forever-conflict case, given excess supply, production outside the Middle East and ample inventories [c819] [c820], while RBC's high scenario puts Brent at $121.99 a barrel in the fourth quarter [c792] and names a full resumption of Saudi-Houthi war as the trigger [c793]. Societe Generale's base case, at 50% probability, has Brent back to $80 by November and December [c822] [c824]. The near-term decider is the reported meeting between Iran and Gulf states on a Hormuz deal [c821].

### AI Borrowing Becomes a Rates Story

Goldman's macro desk argues AI has stopped being only an equity story and is now a rates and credit story [c1315]. AI-related debt issuance approached $500 billion by early August, roughly one-fifth of US high-grade supply this year [c1318]. Funding next year's hyperscaler capital spending means that group collectively sells more interest-rate exposure into the market than the US Treasury does [c1324], and buyers fund it by cutting Treasury and mortgage holdings, which pushes up credit spreads, inflation-adjusted yields and the extra compensation investors demand for owning long bonds [c1222] [c1226]. Mark Wilson's framing is that the Fed is more passenger than driver [c1045]. JPMorgan puts the scale in its own numbers, with high-grade AI issuance at $266bn year to date against $139bn for all of 2025 [c1349] and $2.1trn projected over the next five years [c1350], while Nomura's credit team models $250-275bn this year and $350-400bn in 2027 [c1334]. The trade the desks actually name is convertible bonds, where AI borrowers are 44% of $135 billion of issuance and Goldman calls the paper attractive for total return credit portfolios [c1340] [c1339] [c1304].

### The Machines Sold at the Open

Trend-following funds tripped their short-term triggers across the Nasdaq 100, S&P 500 and Russell 2000 on Thursday morning [c1088], after a European index broke earlier in the week [c1089]. Goldman models the resulting selling at 23bn over one week and 28bn over one month, and those figures exclude the pre-market decline [c1090] [c1091]. That supply lands on top of $421 billion of systematic equity exposure across trend, volatility-control and risk-parity strategies, close to five-year highs [c1092]. Dealers are still long options at current prices, which damps moves, but Goldman models that position flipping on a further 200 basis point decline, at which point dealers start selling into weakness [c1436] [c1461]. Goldman also notes the VIX usually sits near 25 when technicals deteriorate this way, against 17 now [c1435]. The counterweight is that the money is already out: hedge fund net leverage sits in the 6th percentile over the past year [c1068], which is why Goldman's sales desk still says the pain trade is higher [c860].

### Lagarde Calls It a No Brainer

The ECB raised its deposit rate 25 basis points to 2.50% and added a line saying inflation stays well above target for an extended period [c1645] [c1646], with the 2028 core forecast lifted to 2.3% [c1649]. Lagarde called the hike a no brainer [c1647], and money markets now fully price three more over the next year [c1651]. European bonds repriced hard: the German 2-year rose to 3.23%, its biggest daily move since the March 2025 debt-brake reform [c1201], and the 10-year Bund closed at 3.50%, above its 2011 euro-crisis high and back to levels last seen in 2009 [c1202], with France at 4.44% and the UK at 5.37% [c1203] [c1204]. Goldman moved its call to an extra December hike and a 2.75% peak [c1655] [c1656], while Deutsche Bank's European economists hold at one more, in December, arguing further hikes are hard to justify without evidence that energy costs are feeding into wages and other prices [c1670]. That evidence is the test, and JPMorgan already got the direction wrong, expecting a dovish reaction and getting a hawkish one [c1684] [c1688].

### Supply Is Still the Binding Constraint

The company-level picture at Goldman's technology conference did not soften. TSMC is still adding 3-nanometer capacity and does not expect supply to be sufficient, a break from previous chip generations where demand faded two to three years in [c53], and its advanced packaging is tight enough that it has started outsourcing selected steps to partners [c55]. Applied Materials reiterated roughly 40% revenue growth in 2026 and expects leading-edge logic, packaging and memory to take about 80% of equipment spending in both 2026 and 2027 [c92] [c89], while SanDisk says memory chip supply stays tight through 2027 and possibly 2028 [c287]. Dell reported a $9.5bn backlog of AI servers [c177] and warned component availability could be worse in 2027 than in 2026 [c173]. Goldman's desk conclusion is to stay long AI infrastructure into 2027 [c402] and to buy its AI hardware basket [c445]. What holds the stocks back is top-down hesitation over rates, geopolitics and midterm policy rather than anything in the company numbers [c1094].

### Breakout or Bubble in Earnings

Deutsche Bank's chart of the week shows S&P 500 earnings per share growing inside a stable channel of about 6.5% a year through depressions, wars and technology cycles [c955], and now breaking out above it [c845]. Binky Chadha and Parag Thatte expect that to hold in coming quarters [c846], with the caveat that the measure is per share, so buybacks flatter it [c958], though Thatte reads the buyback effect as having peaked in the 2010s, before the breakout began [c962]. JPMorgan's counter is historical: four interwar private investment cycles were each equivalent to 2-4% of GDP and lasted 2-4 years [c716], and none ended in a soft landing [c623], against US AI capital spending now running at 5% of GDP [c714] and total US market value at 248% of GDP [c949]. The valuation tell is that forward price-to-earnings multiples on AI stocks have slumped this year even as forward earnings and margins rose [c649]. Spending by the top 1% of AI model customers, which drives most of the model companies' enterprise revenue, fell 9.7% last month, from $7,976 to $7,205 [c630].
