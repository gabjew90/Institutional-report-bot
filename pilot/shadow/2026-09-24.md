# Five Percent Breaks Everything

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The 10-year cleared 5% and equities finally flinched

September's flash reading of US business activity came in at a five-year high of 58.4 against a 55.3 consensus [c426], and the bond market treated it as proof the Fed is not finished. Yields rose across every maturity, with 2-year, 5-year, 10-year and 30-year Treasury yields up 12, 16, 14 and 9 basis points (one-hundredth of a percent) on the day [c224]. The 10-year closed at 5.11%, the highest since 2007, and posted its biggest one-day jump since the market turmoil of April 2025 [c220] [c219]. The 5-year cleared 5% for the first time since 2007 [c221], helped by a weak auction where $70bn of new 5-year notes sold at 5.03%, 3.1 basis points above where the market was pricing them just before the sale [c222]. Futures now put a 71% chance on an October hike, up from 53% on Tuesday [c247] [c248], with 37 basis points of hikes priced by year-end [c249]. Stocks gave way, the S&P 500 falling 0.75%, its worst day in a month, with every major sector except energy lower [c198] [d10].

The argument between the houses is whether this yield level breaks stocks or gets bought. JPMorgan puts fair value on the 10-year near 5.5% and argues that past yield-driven equity dips have been buying opportunities, so it intends to buy this one through a broadening trade [c228], while holding a cautious, neutral equity stance into the Fed because energy prices will not settle lower [c211] [d7]. Morgan Stanley takes the other side of the level. Its work names 4.50% on the 10-year as the point where stocks become meaningfully rate-sensitive [c234], the 10-year closed at 5.11% [c220], and the short-term relationship between stock prices and yields is already negative, which is what high rate sensitivity looks like [c235]. It still holds an 8,000 year-end S&P 500 target on earnings resilience [c194], with 7000-7100 as the downside case over the next three to five weeks if oil climbs further [c205] [d13].

What settles it is the yield itself. BTIG reads the 10-year as consolidating inside an uptrend and says that read only flips on a break below roughly 4.92% [c215]. JPMorgan's own trigger is symmetric: lower oil and lower yields turn it bullish [c185], while a consumer that breaks or an AI capital-spending cut turns it bearish [c200].

## 3. BRIEFS

### Meta's agent is two weeks old and already repricing the sector

Meta shares are up 21% since Muse launched two weeks ago, against 1% for the S&P 500 over the same stretch [c15]. Muse is the top free download in the US and Canada app stores [c16], with roughly 1.8 million iOS downloads in its first 12 days versus about 1.3 million for ChatGPT over a comparable period [c33]. JPMorgan reiterates Overweight with a $820 December 2027 target, about 23x its 2028 GAAP earnings estimate of $35.44 [c14] [c25], and expects the money to come from commissions on business agents rather than subscriptions, which it does not see before 2027 [c21] [c39]. UBS reads the same adoption curve as evidence consumer agents are approaching mainstream use and points investors at the CPU supply chain rather than the app [c128] [c130] [d5]. The fight over who owns the transaction is already open: Amazon blocked Muse from its marketplace [c6] while Shopify partnered with it for agent checkout [c35].

### Banks sold hard, and JPMorgan's own specialist says the reason is wrong

Financials fell 1.98% in the S&P 500 on worries that AI erodes bank margins and fees [c139], the same trade that knocked the financials sector ETF down 2% while semiconductors rallied [c79]. JPMorgan's financials specialist O'Dwyer does not accept the story, saying there is very little in the Muse product that disrupts the unit economics of banking [c106]. He points at which names are moving: the crowded holdings C, MS, GS and USB dominate the selling volume while regionals and LAZ and JEF hold up [c52] [c61], which reads as investors unwinding crowded books rather than the market re-rating the business [c375] [d4]. He wants to buy the drop patiently, favoring MS, PNC and FITB [c107] [c57]. Hedge funds went the other way this week, selling banks outright and adding shorts in regionals [c394]. BTIG sees a warning instead: the BKX bank index sits more than 10% below its 30-day high while the S&P is within 1% of a 52-week high, a split last seen in January 2000 [c136].

### Oil swung on a Hormuz headline and swung straight back

Brent closed at $99.02, below $100 for the first time in over two weeks [c295], after a Kyodo report said Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days if the US lifted its naval blockade [c293] [d9]. Doubts about any deal reversed it, and Brent rebounded 3.86% to $103.08 [c271], which added to the hawkish pressure on bonds [d10]. JPMorgan's commodity team models Brent averaging $87 in 2027 if the conflict runs on, against $64 in a peace baseline [c278], and escalation that pulls the Bab al-Mandab strait in would put a further large block of oil and LNG supply at risk [c276]. Saudi Arabia is restarting its East-West pipeline and aims for a partial resumption of flows by Saturday [c297]. On the product side, Politico reported preparation of a 90-day diesel export ban [c273], which US Energy Secretary Wright said is not under discussion in any blanket form [c306].

### The index is fine, the stocks underneath it are not

BTIG's read is that internals have detached from headline strength [d8]. The semiconductor index sits about 14% below its June high, yet only three of its 30 members are closer to their own 52-week highs than the index is, and 10 of the 30 are more than 30% off their highs [c134] [c135]. The S&P 500 has now logged five consecutive sessions with more stocks making new 52-week lows than new highs, even while the index trades near a record [c196], and it is up 1.2% over the past month while nine of eleven sectors are down [c197]. The 2000 comparison is the point: 13 of the 20 current semiconductor index members that traded back then kept setting new highs after the index topped in March 2000 [c166]. BTIG's conclusion for anyone still bullish on tech is to own the names near their highs, not the laggards [c80].

### France is the bond market's other problem

The gap between French and German 10-year borrowing costs widened to 110 basis points, the widest since the 2012 euro crisis [c140], as the German 10-year yield hit a post-2009 high of 3.55% [c141] and the French 10-year rose 15.4 basis points [c142]. The fiscal arithmetic behind it has not improved: the 2026 deficit is now forecast at 5.4% of GDP against a 5% target, with public debt at 117% of GDP [c322] [c323], and the government plans a record €310 billion of bond sales in 2026 [c324]. JPMorgan's momentum work shows positioning in French government bond futures versus German ones at an extreme short reading of -2.5, consistent with investors continuing to unwind overweights inside the euro area [c233]. The bank runs an option hedge that is short France against long European domestic names on politics and dislocation [c151]. Rate pricing is moving with it, with October ECB hike odds up from 48% on Tuesday to 66% at the close [c434] after Bundesbank's Nagel raised the idea of moving policy into mildly restrictive territory [c435].

### Forty days out, the political risk is aimed at AI

A Democratic House flip is treated as settled, with conference speakers putting it at 90% and forecasts pointing to a 220 to 230 seat majority, while the Senate stays a toss-up despite the GOP's 53-47 hold [c330] [c331] [c332]. Affordability is the reason: only 25% of Americans are satisfied with how things are going, against a 33% historical average [c334], and when gasoline prices are elevated the incumbent party's average midterm House losses rise to about 32 seats [c338]. That matters for oil because Middle East tension has pushed Brent into a $100-110 band, above the $75-100 range that held since late May [c274]. The policy consequence lands on AI regardless of who wins, with speakers calling it "the new China" as a bipartisan target [c144], cities in 32 states advancing data-center moratoriums [c147], and the House already passing a bill making large power-hungry data centers pay the full cost of grid upgrades [c145] [d11]. After the vote, JPMorgan flags an approaching debt ceiling and a ratings reassessment of the fiscal outlook as further pressure on Treasuries [c223].

### Companies are generating more cash than they can spend

The US all-corporate financing surplus, meaning cash flow left over after capital spending, reached close to 2% of GDP in the second quarter of 2026, near the highest outside crisis periods since the data began in 1952 [c179]. Strip out financials and it stands near 1.5% of GDP, the highest since 1958 [c180]. JPMorgan's explanation for why aggregate capital spending has not risen to match is that AI spending is crowding out everything else, holding total capex growth down [c181] [d12]. The effect on markets is that companies need less outside funding and can keep buying back their own shares, particularly outside tech [c105]. JPMorgan expects tech's share of buybacks to shrink over the next five to ten years as AI infrastructure spending eats into hyperscaler cash surpluses [c156].

### Bitcoin climbed back over what it costs to mine

JPMorgan puts the implied average cost of producing one bitcoin at roughly $85,000 [c416]. The price moved back above that line in recent days, before the latest correction, after 280 days beneath it [c417], compared with about 224 days below cost during the 2018 down-cycle [c422]. JPMorgan reads the crossing as reducing the pressure on miners to sell coins into the market to cover costs [d12]. Supporting that, network hashrate and mining difficulty have fallen about 19% and 15% from their peak last October as miners redirect capacity toward AI work [c421]. The bank's momentum measure for bitcoin now sits at a somewhat elevated +1.0, and it attributes the past week's rally to an unwind of bearish positions rather than fresh demand [c419] [c418].

### Flows say rotation, not exit

Last week's ETF numbers were distorted by index rebalancing around Friday's quarterly futures and options expiry, so the headline prints are unreliable [c396]. Adjusted for that, US equity inflows ran modestly below average, with money leaving Financials and Health Care for Tech, Communication Services, Discretionary and Industrials [c397] [c398] [d6]. In bonds, ETF investors and asset managers shortened maturities, moving out of long-dated government bonds into short-dated ones [c380] [c385]. Commodity positioning turned defensive in spots, with Managed Money cutting copper longs sharply and energy ETF money going mostly into funds that profit when oil falls [c381] [c382]. Systematic trend-followers stayed net long global equities and added to those longs while remaining short global rates outside China [c368]. Hedge funds are the split signal: gross exposure has been rising globally at more than two standard deviations [c372], yet the US measure of positioning momentum fell another 0.5 standard deviations this week, near the 25th percentile and approaching July's lows [c391].
