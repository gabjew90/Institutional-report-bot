# Hot CPI Stalls The Squeeze

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The volatility crush ran out of road

Monday's rally was machinery, not conviction. Dealers came into the week short puts after Thursday produced the biggest single day of put buying in Cboe history [c1508], and as prices rose and the cost of protection collapsed, those positions lost value fast enough that dealers had to buy back the futures they had sold as a hedge [c819]. The VIX fell from 28 to 20 in roughly two and a half sessions [c1507], 424 of 503 S&P 500 members closed higher [c583], and the Dow, S&P 500 and Russell 2000 erased their post-election losses [c584]. Nomura's McElligott reads the broad advance as the sideshow and the volatility collapse as the actual story [c750][c818].

That engine stalled on Tuesday. October CPI printed 0.4% on the month with core at 0.3% against 0.2% expected, a combination only one of 70 analysts had forecast [c1552], and the volatility meltdown stopped, taking the rally with it [c1509]. A December hike is now fully priced, with 14% odds on a half point [c1241], after Waller said on Monday that a hot core reading would put 50 basis points, each one a hundredth of a percent, on the table [c1220].

The argument is about whether the selling is finished. UBS says it is, with Risk Control exposure at 55%, the 1st percentile against the past year [c850], trend-followers net short at -5% of global equity exposure [c851], and an expectation that the S&P 500 sits more than 10% higher in three months [c610]. JPMorgan's Lakos-Bujas calls the momentum capitulation far along [c797] and keeps his 8,000 year-end target [c569]. BTIG's Krinsky takes the other side with history. Across eight prior episodes of a 10% collapse and a 5% rebound, seven lost about 10% over the following 30 days and none marked the bottom [c639][c640]. He wants the index nearer 7,000 to 7,100 before buying [c645] and puts 73% odds on another day where 80% of NYSE volume sits in falling stocks [c643]. Morgan Stanley's Wilson wants a marginal new low into 7,100 to 7,300 to finish the retest [c675].

The level decides it. Goldman's long-term trend-following pivot is 7,084 [c743], with nearer thresholds at 7,685 and 7,550 [c1106], and its desk models those funds as sellers in every scenario this week, about $31 billion in a flat market [c945]. Against that, company buybacks are running near $5 billion a day with 90% of corporates out of blackout [c210], which Goldman says outbids the supply over a month [c788].

## 3. BRIEFS

### Chip risk passed hyperscaler risk

The quiet part of last week was credit. A basket of Broadcom, AMD, Nvidia and Intel protection traded around 80 basis points in early August, some 20 inside the hyperscaler basket [c56], and has roughly doubled to about 160, crossing above that basket for the first time in three months [c18]. Volume followed, with AI default-swap activity at $8-10 billion every week, around seven times the run rate of a year ago [c1398]. The index barely registered it. Goldman's CDX investment-grade measure widened 3.5 basis points on Thursday while the Nasdaq fell 6.4% and the S&P 4.4% [c1422]. Buyers absorbed $76 billion of investment-grade supply in a week, nearly double expectations [c1428], including five hyperscaler deals worth $67 billion since October 29 that took year-to-date issuance to $296 billion, past Goldman's $250 billion full-year forecast [c1392]. Concessions on AI deals ran above 18 basis points in November [c1394]. Goldman's forecast is investment-grade spreads above 93 basis points into early December, overshooting March's wides [c1408].

### Permits are the new bottleneck

Goldman's economists see short-term moratoria potentially touching about 45% of planned US data center capacity [c372]. JPMorgan counts 42 of 45 local ballot measures to restrict data centers as passed or leaning that way [c374], with newly elected governors in Georgia and New Mexico running on project pauses [c377] and Ohio and Nevada on full utility-cost coverage [c378]. JPMorgan still holds 2027 hyperscaler capex at $1.7 trillion against $1.3 trillion consensus, with a floor near $1.5 trillion [c78], and since every $100 billion of capex is worth roughly $9 of S&P 500 earnings per share [c564], even that floor adds 4-5% to 2027 index earnings [c565]. UBS puts about 30% of 2028 capacity additions at risk from state pauses [c476], and its analyst Keirstead argues the fight is over permitting rather than projects already under construction, which pushes the damage to 2028 and beyond [c308]. Financing already felt it when banks shelved the junk bond sale for Anthropic's $15 billion Texas site on October 29 [c1421]. Next read: the ERCOT interconnection-queue audit due in December [c493].

### Chip multiples fell, estimates did not

The de-rating happened without an earnings cut behind it. JPMorgan's 61 upstream AI names, worth $12.5 trillion, went from 24.3x to 12.0x forward earnings in under six months [c380], with memory collapsing from 8.0x to 3.2x [c385] and optical networking from 49.6x to 16.4x [c383]. Syz measures the same gap from the other side. Since June 30 the forward multiple on memory makers fell 50% [c414] while their expected earnings for the coming year climbed 32% [c249]. UBS's Arcuri calculates that current semiconductor prices already imply roughly 30% and 35% cuts to consensus 2027 and 2028 earnings [c453], and about 55 gigawatts of 2028 AI chip shipments against an original expectation near 70 [c454]. The classic warning signs of a cycle peak, rising inventories and shortening lead times, are still absent [c277]. Morgan Stanley's Wilson is not buying the bounce, and his silver-stock analog says the first rally in the group should be sold [c155].

### Nvidia's governance problem has a date on it

On November 8 Nvidia's board announced an independent investigation after CEO Jensen Huang was reported to be the target of a foreign intelligence effort [c65]. Huang admitted an extramarital affair in a release issued the same day [c66], and the board stated confidence in him and the management team [c68]. The stock fell 5.5% on Monday [c46]. UBS expects no effect on the fiscal third-quarter results due November 17 [c70][c69], citing AI compute demand that still outstrips supply [c71], and says investors are not backing away from a January-quarter revenue figure near $135 billion [c11]. Its longer-dated worries are key-person risk with no clear internal successor and a very flat organization [c42][c43], and better odds that China chip restrictions persist rather than ease [c1653]. Position size makes this everyone's problem: Nvidia is about 7% of S&P 500 weight and around 25% of its variance [c49], and options price roughly a 1% index move on the print [c74].

### Hedge funds are out of the way

Morgan Stanley's prime book shows US long/short net leverage falling 5% on Thursday alone to 42%, the fifth largest single-day decline since 2010, leaving nets 8% lower on the week and at the lowest level all year, though still above April 2025's roughly 35% [c1122][c1123]. Gross leverage ended at 202%, pushing toward 12-month troughs [c1124]. Exposure to AI themes has fallen to about 32% of total US net exposure [c1128], down from a 47% June peak, leaving funds roughly 2% underweight those names against market-cap weight [c1129]. Goldman's book tells the same story with net exposure at five-year lows [c914]. Morgan Stanley's read is that positioning is now clean enough that funds are no longer the driver of sharp reversals, while remaining sensitive enough to accelerate a move either way [c1127][c1136]. The crowded trade that is left is the short in small caps, with Russell 2000 short interest above the 90th percentile [c1004].

### The war premium came out of crude

Brent fell below $90 a barrel for the first time since August 31 [c1284], about 23% off the $118 six-month high set on October 30 [c1285]. The trigger was Trump telling reporters he would hold off on new strikes against Iran after regional appeals [c1651]. Supply helped, with OPEC output rising in October while high prices dampened demand [c1286]. Nothing has been settled. Iran's security chief dismissed US reports of negotiations and demanded acceptance of Tehran's seven conditions [c1658], leaving no clear near-term path to restoring normal shipping through Hormuz [c1287]. TS Lombard's central case is periodic escalation inside a contained conflict with oil retreating toward $80 a barrel [c1282]. BofA's Hartnett is explicit about what he needs before adding risk, which is the 10-year Treasury yield under 4.5% and oil under $80 [c1322]. The week closed with the 10-year near 4.85% and crude near $90 [c1323].

### Goldman takes its gold target down again

Goldman cut its end-2026 gold forecast to $4,150 an ounce from $4,650 [c1686] and its end-2027 forecast to $4,800 from $5,400 [c1687], against spot near $4,130 on the day of the note [c1694], so the near-term call is for almost no further move. The reasoning is the Fed. A hawkish shift contains hedging demand expressed through call options, and net GLD call open interest near 1.9 million contracts is assumed to normalize toward roughly 1.0 million [c168]. Central bank buying assumed at 60 tonnes a month, below the current 85-tonne pace [c1695], still drives most of the 15% upside Goldman forecasts to end-2027 [c1671]. Three hikes instead of two puts the end-2027 figure at $4,660 [c1688]. Goldman's own desk leans the other way, with Gillard noting the firm's trend-following model is close to maximum short and the pain trade is higher [c1675].

### China's cheap power is the margin threat

Nvidia's share of Chinese data-center AI accelerator shipments fell to 34% in the first half of 2026 while Huawei climbed to 28% [c21]. Power is why that gap can keep closing. China added roughly ten times as much generating capacity as the US in 2025 [c253], and data-center hubs in Inner Mongolia pay around 4.7 cents per kilowatt hour, about half the US industrial average [c255]. Models are becoming interchangeable, with open-weight versions now 72% of tokens served on OpenRouter against 27% of spending [c506] and Chinese labs supplying 69% of those tokens [c257]. Against Nvidia gross margins near 75%, that leaves a very large pool for a cheaper competitor to attack [c23]. TS Lombard's argument is that the next big disruption comes from China becoming the low-cost supplier of compute [c418], and that the risk to Western technology is structurally lower margins rather than weaker demand [c421]. Its expression is low-delta upside calls in SMHC, already halved from its summer highs [c187][c188].
