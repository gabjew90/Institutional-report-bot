# Oil Now Sets Rates

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Brent At $97 Decides The Fed

Brent crude rose 7.80% last week to $96.28 a barrel [c96] and added another 1.05% this morning to $97.29 [c95]. European natural gas gained 7.42% to 72.00 euros per megawatt hour, a fourth straight weekly rise [c97]. Government bond yields went with it. The 10-year German Bund yield rose 6.1 basis points (one hundredth of a percent) last week to 3.34% and touched a post-2011 high of 3.37% [c329]. The US 10-year rose 6.3 basis points to 4.78% [c330] after closing Tuesday at 4.80%, its highest since October 2023 [c331]. This is an energy shock arriving through the inflation channel rather than a fiscal scare, and coupon income is still holding total returns up even as the pressure for higher yields persists [d7].

Then the jobs number landed on top of it. August payrolls rose 162k against expectations of 55k [c81], with 55k of upward revisions to the prior two months [c82]. Futures ended Friday pricing a 62% chance of a September hike [c87]. The meeting itself is priced at 14.5bps [c587].

The committee's split is out in the open. Warsh's Jackson Hole speech was unambiguously hawkish and lowered the inflation number that would justify a September hike [c188][c189]. Waller pushed the other way and will vote to hold unless the print runs hot [c425]. That leaves Friday's CPI as the whole decision. Headline is forecast at 0.38% month-on-month by Deutsche Bank [c88], with core at 0.21% month-on-month [c551]. A 0.2bp monthly core print should be enough for a hold, though Goldman calls it a close call [c616].

Where the two houses actually part ways is what follows the first move. Goldman is growing more confident that any hiking cycle is shallow [c427], and its Bingham argues for a hike even on a 20bp core print because a hold read as a policy error costs more than tightening into above-target inflation [c158]. Deutsche Bank's objection is historical: pricing for a shallow cycle is inconsistent with how past cycles ran [c345], this is the fourth year in five that markets have underestimated Fed hawkishness [c346], and inflation at 4-5% has previously meant more than 200bps of hikes in the first year [c352]. The August prices-paid reading in ISM services hit a four-year high, a level that last time went with inflation above 5% [c106].

Friday settles it. The market has reduced the decision to whether core CPI prints 0.2% or 0.3% month-over-month [c742]. Bond markets have become a speed limit for stocks, and a sharper yield move from energy, a more hawkish Fed or investors demanding more compensation to hold long bonds would hurt equities more than this gradual grind has [c513][c514].

## 3. BRIEFS

### The Five Million Barrels Nobody Can See

Visible tanker data show Persian Gulf flows of only about 10 million barrels a day on a seven-day average [c571]. Count the ships running near the Omani coast with their tracking transponders switched off and the real number is 15-16 million barrels a day, roughly two-thirds of pre-war levels [c125][c126]. That revision came in over two weeks [c127] and cuts the estimated net hit to Gulf flows to 7.9 million barrels a day [c370]. It also means the visible inventory data understate stocks by 39 million barrels [c132], against draws running at 2.1 million barrels a day over the last 30 days [c373]. Crude upside is capped by those hidden transits and by price-sensitive Chinese buying, absent large damage to oil infrastructure [c3]. Refined fuel is the part that is not capped. The national average diesel price hit $5.783 a gallon on Wednesday, above the previous wartime peak set in April [c301]. From here the sharp move is skewed lower on a positive headline against a slow grind higher on no news [c420].

### Watch The Gas, Not The Oil

Goldman's Dart puts winter Dutch gas at 45 euros per megawatt hour if flows through Hormuz normalize by early in the fourth quarter [c384] and about 105 euros if they do not [c146]. That is the entire European rate path in one spread. Stehn maps a move to 100 euros into 0.6 percentage points of extra headline euro area inflation [c151] and 0.3 points of core [c152]. December 2026 gas could rally toward 100 euros from about 71 now if exports only normalize gradually through 2027 [c218], which is why the instruction is to watch European gas rather than oil into winter [c215]. An ECB hike next week is locked in [c193], and the bar for another in December is low if energy does not ease [c481]. British front-end rates are now tracking gas rather than crude [c196], fourth-quarter gas prices already sit above the Bank of England's own adverse scenario [c197], and current levels imply another 12% rise in the household energy cap in the first quarter of 2027 [c199]. Goldman's base case is still that the Bank holds [c438].

### Astra Restarts The Spending Clock

SoftBank rose more than 10% overnight as the cleanest listed proxy for OpenAI [c70] and Oracle gained about 5.5% [c15], but the price moves are the least interesting part. The argument from Goldman's Privorotsky is that a model stepping meaningfully ahead of the pack makes companies find new uses rather than buy the same amount of AI more cheaply [c239], and forces every rival lab to chase, which keeps the spending cycle from going stale [c240]. His earlier caution still stands: more demand plus lower prices is not automatically bullish if pricing falls faster than consumption grows [c659]. The counterweight is that peak AI multiples were probably set in the second quarter, and the market now needs proof that spending durability can outrun a shrinking multiple [c512]. Goldman research still sees compute demand outstripping supply well into 2028 [c221]. Oracle reports Thursday and is the key corporate release of the week for AI capital spending [c18].

### Shorts Covered, Protection Gone

Non-dealer buying of Nasdaq futures ran every week from August 4 to September 1, a record $51.3bn in notional terms [c120]. Hedge funds supplied $37.5bn of that [c66]. Almost all of it was closing existing shorts rather than fresh risk, $38.6bn worth [c66]. That leaves net futures positioning at the 92nd percentile of the last two years as of September 1 [c61], and still the 84th percentile looking at asset managers alone [c62]. The hedges left with the shorts: Nasdaq put exposure has collapsed [c369], S&P skew has collapsed with investors paying considerably less for downside protection [c499], and put positioning is light enough that hedges look attractive [c500]. The cash book tells a different story, with hedge fund net leverage at the 4th percentile of the past year [c135]. Light cash exposure plus crowded futures plus no protection is why Coppersmith prefers owning upside through options into the catalysts [c225].

### The Long End Has No Buyer

The biggest structural theme in markets is long-end weakness across every curve as the market hunts a clearing level for supply against shrinking demand for long-dated bonds [c434]. Deficits stay large with no sign of consolidation [c428], and the US 30-year yield has crossed 5.3%, the highest since 2007 [c523]. The Treasury's expanded buyback of longer-dated bonds starts Wednesday [c93], but Goldman treats that as a signalling tool rather than a fix [c430] and calls the increase noise once AI-related investment grade supply is counted [c431]. The scale supports them: AI borrowers have raised $356bn in US credit markets this year against $174bn in all of 2025 [c527], about half the Treasury's $681bn of net coupon supply [c528]. The 10-year swap spread at -44bps is unchanged since January [c529], so this is not crowding out, it is the whole market repricing. European long-end rates stay Goldman's short [c479].

### Japan Is The Live Experiment

Overnight index swaps are more than fully pricing a 25 basis point BOJ hike for September [c299], and Goldman's Japan economists have moved their baseline to the same meeting [c160]. The yen just posted its second-biggest two-day gain since August 2024 [c298] and 30-year JGBs rallied roughly 20bps in 48 hours [c163]. The domestic bid is the problem underneath. Regional banks are still liquidating 20-year bonds bought at 1% yields that now yield 3.7% [c398], and Cole now expects the 10-year JGB at 3% by year-end [c395]. Allianz looks for another 75bp of BOJ tightening to a 1.75% terminal rate by end-2027 [c313], with Japanese investors holding around $1.1 trillion of US Treasuries that could come home [c541]. Japan is the best case to watch for whether hawkish central bank pricing and a stronger currency can calm long-end yields anywhere [c617].

### Gridlock Is The Trade

The trading edge in November sits in the Senate, not the House, per JPMorgan's Tyler [c695]. Betting markets already give Democrats an 85% chance of taking the House [c709], while Kalshi prices a Republican Senate hold at 53% [c698]. Six races decide the chamber and Democrats need four flips [c699]. JPMorgan's own probabilities favor Democrats in exactly four: North Carolina at 91% [c701], Maine at 69% [c702], Alaska at 65% [c703] and Ohio at 54% [c705], against Iowa at 62% Republican [c706] and Texas as a toss-up [c707]. The split-Congress basket keeps managed care long on the argument that the health premium credits become a must-pass item [c277] and keeps the big defense contractors long because baseline spending survives gridlock [c278]. Divided government has coincided with roughly 20% two-year S&P 500 returns since 1950 against 18% under single-party control [c38].
