# Credit Breaks The AI Trade

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### AI credit is sorting the stack

The sell-off that mattered this week was in bonds, not stocks. Spreads on AI and hyperscaler-linked corporate debt reached their widest levels of the year, and the last time they were nearly this wide the underlying hyperscaler shares were trading 15% lower. CoreWeave's debt yields hit 15% and the company became the market's reference point for the whole trade. Oracle's long bonds widened about 43 basis points (one-hundredth of a percent) to 320 over Treasuries while Meta's widened about 14, and the cost of insuring Oracle's debt against default rose 30 to 40 basis points. The broad investment-grade index barely moved, about 2 basis points wider on the week, which hides violent dispersion underneath it.

What is happening is a sorting, not a retreat. Capital still wants credit risk, but it is now pricing an issuer's ability to finance its own growth against dependence on financing staying open. Morgan Stanley puts the top of the stack outside that squeeze: hyperscalers rated AA or higher are mostly insensitive to higher rates, and their profits cover interest payments more than 30 times over, against about 11 times for the median investment-grade borrower. Neoclouds, funded with asset-level debt and convertible bonds, are the most exposed to a higher cost of debt. Below investment grade the damage is already visible. CCC yields reached about 15.4% and spreads breached 1,045 basis points, and a sample of high-yield data-center project bonds has fallen from $101.5 in June to $94.7 in October.

Academy Securities raised its credit alert from yellow to orange, expects the orderly widening to turn fast and furious, and sizes the move at 10 to 20 basis points on investment grade and 3 to 5 points on high yield, a far bigger risk than any move tighter. Its desk already sees dealers pulling back from two-week-old new issues. Goldman reads the same tape as something to trade around: its desk likes buying the S&P 500 ex-AI basket, which has outperformed the index by 586 basis points since October 6, and it expects the biggest AI spenders to start evidencing return on investment as third-quarter results arrive. CoreWeave is the scoreboard. It is testing its July lows and a break lower is what to watch next week, though distressed and equity buyers were already appearing in its debt late in the week.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Memory went from the trade to the unwind

A basket of memory names ran from around 600 to a June peak of 2,675 and has given back almost 30%, sitting near 1,900. Samsung and SK Hynix fell roughly 22% and 26% in five sessions, and Goldman puts KOSPI's drop at more than 20% over eight trading days, a magnitude previously seen only in the Asian and global financial crises and the Covid outbreak. The trigger was CXMT's claimed memory breakthrough, which started the Korea sell-off and spread through the global tech supply chain. Physical pricing has not followed the equities. DDR5 spot prices keep rising, and Samsung and SK Hynix trade at about 1.2 and 0.9 times book value, which implies investors are already discounting a deep downturn by 2028. Goldman thinks the market overestimated CXMT, which still has to qualify customers and reach high yields without access to EUV machines, and with foreign ownership of Korean semiconductors 2.5 standard deviations below its long-run average it calls a tactical bounce due.

### The volatility move was bigger than the equity move

Information technology saw its largest percentage net selling since records began in 2016 and was the most aggressively sold global sector of the week, mostly long liquidation with extra pressure from short sales. Hedge funds running both long and short books cut directional exposure 3.2 percentage points in a week to 43.7%, the lowest in five years, and a separate prime desk logged a three standard deviation selling event that took US long/short net exposure to 49%, near its lows for the year. Then the volatility market broke. Asset managers went into Friday with their largest recorded net short position in VIX futures, the VIX jumped to its highest level since July, and VVIX moved 17 points, one of its biggest days in four years. The S&P 500 is barely off its highs and about 250 points above its range lows, so the mismatch looks like forced deleveraging rather than ordinary risk-off. Three-month implied volatility on the Magnificent 7 is still near multi-year lows, so protection on the largest names has not got expensive.

### Hot inflation data, lower yields

September core consumer prices rose 0.33% on the month, and producer prices rose 1.3%, the biggest monthly gain in four and a half years and far above the 0.5% consensus. The 10-year Treasury yield briefly pushed above 5.30% on the inflation data and then finished the week about 15 basis points lower at 5.07%, as growth worries and the equity slide pulled money into government bonds. Academy Securities ties the week's 17 basis point drop to fading expectations for compute-related debt issuance rather than the data, the Fed or the deficit. Goldman upgraded bonds to overweight for the next 12 months on Monday, and its economists still look for an October pause followed by a December hike. UBS argues the market's pricing of nearly 90 basis points of tightening over 12 months is too hawkish and forecasts 50, in December and March, then a hold through late 2027. RBC BlueBay stays constructive on short-dated bonds and cautious on long ones, favoring a steeper curve.

### The index is hiding the damage

About 74% of S&P 500 members are more than 10% below their 52-week highs while the cap-weighted index sits just 0.5% under its own record, per Raymond James. Goldman's ex-AI basket has outperformed the S&P 500 by 586 basis points since October 6 and closed higher on the day AI worries peaked, and its desk likes buying that basket as the way out of AI concentration. Morgan Stanley is the reason that rotation is uncomfortable: it has AI names driving 78% of 2027 earnings growth while the non-AI part of the index grows earnings per share only 4%. TS Lombard argues the profit broadening needed to stay risk-on is already underway and wants the rotation into the rest of the economy. Raymond James gives the test to watch: small caps and the equal-weight S&P 500 holding their 200-day moving averages, with a break below both signalling breadth deterioration.

### Where the $1.6 trillion actually lands

Morgan Stanley re-cut the AI spending number and traced where it goes. Adding about $195 billion of lease-financed spending takes its 2027 hyperscaler and neocloud capex estimate to $1.58 trillion, against disclosed commitments of more than $1.1 trillion for leases that have not yet started. At a 65% absorption rate, that spending is 5% of 2027 S&P 500 revenue and about 25% of its revenue growth, and of the $1.0 trillion reaching the index nearly $700 billion goes to Nvidia. Morgan Stanley reinstated Nvidia as a top pick and calls it the best risk-reward in semiconductors at 15 times fiscal 2028 earnings. The growth rate moderates from about 100% in 2026 to 60% in 2027 and 14% in 2028. The constraint it flags is electricity, a 32 gigawatt US shortfall through 2028, which it treats as chips arriving before capacity is energized rather than demand disappearing.

### Brent at $100 is now a house forecast

UBS raised its year-end Brent forecast to $100 a barrel after Ukraine's strike set fire to the Ryazan refinery, nearly 5% of Russia's refining throughput, US crude stocks fell 8.6 million barrels against expectations for a 288,000-barrel decline, and a hurricane shut in as much as 71.5% of Gulf of Mexico oil output. Zelenskyy has said Kyiv is done supporting the ceasefire on energy targets, which UBS reads as more supply risk in an already tight physical market. The relief headline was smaller than it looked. Academy Securities puts the EU and IEA diesel release at about 20 million barrels moved forward, not the 50 million headlined, and only if every government meets its quota. It stays long energy producers and expects any Middle East resolution to bring only a brief sell-off. TS Lombard takes the other side, expecting Trump to escalate with Iran to force accommodation and settle oil below triple digits.

### The midterms are becoming a market input

TS Lombard's base case is Democrats taking at least one and probably both chambers in November, with prediction markets giving better than 60% odds of a sweep and about 90% on the House. Its evidence is Trump's net approval 11.6 points underwater even on immigration, his best issue, Democrats trusted more than Republicans on the economy for the first time since 2010, and diesel above $6 a gallon putting reliably red farm states in play. It maps three post-election risk waves, with invocation of the Insurrection Act around Election Day as the highest-volatility one for markets, and a government shutdown if Congress misses the 11 December funding deadline. Morgan Stanley adds the market-specific angle: data-center construction has become a midterm wedge issue, and sentiment on the buildout is weakest under a Democratic sweep. TS Lombard's stated hedge is tactical long gold and long VIX futures.

### Earnings week is a positioning story

A third of industrials and materials names report this week, and the desk notes are mostly about who owns what. Goldman has Financials net sold at 1.2 standard deviations on a one-year basis, short sales outpacing long buys 4.4 to one, led by capital markets and insurance. Into Blackstone, bulls point to its funds' leverage to the data-center theme while bears want the pace of management fee growth, a BREIT recovery update and BREP fundraising, and cite the AI slowdown now showing in credit. Hedge funds are least inclined to stay short CBRE after a month of bearish feedback about a slower fourth-quarter guide, and MSCI is a liked long even with subscription expectations low. In industrials, Goldman's desk calls raised 2028 guidance the upside at GE against an options market pricing a 5% move, reads Northrop negatively on free cash flow and its FAXX contract loss, and flags rising short interest in CSX against a fading merger-premium debate.

### Retail bought the chip selloff with leverage

The semiconductor index had its worst week of the year, down 11%, second only to the Liberation Day selloff, and SMH fell for nine straight sessions, the longest run in its 15-year history. Buyers went the other way. Nearly $10 billion flowed into levered ETFs last week, the largest weekly inflow in Goldman's data, with SOXL taking a record $7 billion, 74% of the total, and TQQQ $1.3 billion. Goldman says this fits the historical pattern, where levered ETF flows are strongest on the market's worst days and weakest on its best. Academy Securities reads that same exposure as a hazard, pointing to $34 billion in TQQQ against $2.2 billion in SQQQ as aggressively bullish leveraged positioning in the Nasdaq 100, plus $22 billion in SOXL. Goldman is watching whether better conditions turn those added units into a multiplier on gross levered exposure, now $152 billion of assets and $386 billion gross.
