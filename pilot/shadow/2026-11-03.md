# The AI Trade Cracks

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Hyperscaler earnings settled nothing

The S&P 500 closed Friday down 182 basis points (one-hundredth of a percent) at 7580 and the Nasdaq 100 down 346 basis points at 28384 [c841][c842]. Semiconductors fell about 8% on the day, their third largest day of underperformance in more than 15 years [c462], and the Nasdaq 100 matched its biggest monthly decline since the AI cycle began, down roughly 7% in October [c461]. Monday opened firmer, with S&P futures up 40 basis points and Nasdaq futures up 80 [c826].

What broke is the thing that was supposed to fix sentiment. Hyperscaler results left the market focused on the AI funding and returns argument rather than resolving it [c515][d35]. Alphabet's cloud revenue grew 103% with backlog up 256% to $561 billion [c5], and it still burned $9.1 billion of free cash flow against $3.5 billion expected, bought back no stock, sold $12.4 billion of new shares and issued $28.7 billion of bonds [c16][c17][c18]. Amazon's AWS grew 39% and missed [c71] on a backlog of $578 billion [c68]. Growth was never the question. Who pays for it was [d4].

The banks are now openly split. Deutsche Bank upgraded Tech back to overweight, arguing positioning has fallen to the 40th percentile, a level consistent with about 10% earnings growth against the 47% Tech actually delivered in the third quarter [c731][c1420][c1206], and that the last five rotations of this kind saw the sector gain 32% [c733]. RBC will not follow, holding Tech at market weight and saying not enough has improved to leave the sidelines [c540][c626]. JPMorgan's objection is the harder one: the de-risking is not capitulatory, with hedge fund gross leverage at the 89th percentile over three years and quant gross leverage at the 98th percentile over one year [c1355][c1356][c1357].

The level decides it. The S&P broke its medium-term trend-following threshold of 7544.2 on Friday [c838], and a further break flips Goldman's systematic momentum signal negative, which stops projected trend-follower buying [c460]. JPMorgan's Jason Hunter puts support at 7616 to 7643, with the next shelf in the 7200s [c870]. UBS's Cheong estimates volatility-targeting funds need to sell $75-100 billion on Monday and says heavier Tuesday selling arrives only if the S&P falls more than 2% [c1395][c1397]. Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson wants 7100 to 7300, or clearer sentiment confirmation, before adding risk, and keeps 8000 as the year-end base case [c880][c794].

## 3. BRIEFS

### The obligations nobody puts on a balance sheet

A rebuild from primary filings puts disclosed commitments across the hyperscalers, Nvidia, Broadcom and SpaceX at $4.11 trillion, up $992 billion in roughly 90 days [c1519]. The five companies that filed third-quarter reports added 23% in a single quarter, from $2.48 trillion to $3.05 trillion [c1523]. Alphabet alone passed $1 trillion, at $1,059 billion, with purchase commitments up $95 billion and leases signed but not yet started rising from $85 billion to $131 billion [c23][c26]. Reported debt and lease liabilities across the eight companies total only about $1.0 trillion, so more than four dollars of obligation sits outside the balance sheet for every reported dollar [c1526]. Goldman's Amanda Lynam counts $2.8 trillion across five hyperscalers, an eightfold rise since 2023, and flags that S&P adjusts leverage for those leases while Moody's and Fitch do not [c1513][c1514]. Nvidia reports in late November and Oracle and Broadcom in December, so the figure is a floor [c50].

### Funds got hurt, retail bought

Goldman's Prime desk had global fundamental long/short managers down 0.85% Friday, 3.3% for October and up 9.3% on the year, with technology-focused funds down 3.1% on the day and 11.9% for the month [c1313][c1314]. Systematic managers slipped 0.7% to end October down 3.7%, their first negative month of 2026, still up 16% year to date [c1318]. UBS's deleveraging alert triggered for US equity long/short after gross exposure cuts of 2.3 and 2.1 standard deviations on Wednesday and Thursday, concentrated in hyperscaler names [c1385][c1386][c1390]. What set the alert off is that Friday's cut, at 0.4 standard deviations, was far smaller than the day's price action implied [c1389]. Retail went the other way, with late-day flow reversing to $72 billion of buying from 2:45pm, concentrated in technology ETFs and memory stocks [c1262][c1273].

### Everything that is not AI had a great month

Goldman's S&P ex-AI index was headed for its best month since 2022 while the megacaps fell [c759], and UBS measured the equal-weighted S&P beating the cap-weighted index by 6% in October [c907]. The Nasdaq 100 posted its worst monthly underperformance against the equal-weighted S&P since 2002 [c901]. RBC now prefers small caps to large caps, pointing to Russell 2000 valuations that fell below their Iran-war trough and short positions that are closing [c419][c423][c1250]. The warning attached to this trade is that the longer-term downtrend in the Russell-versus-Nasdaq ratio is still intact, and chasing small-cap outperformance here carries as much risk as chasing tech did at the highs [c903][c905]. Goldman also notes the Russell setup stays constrained by rate sensitivity and refinancing costs [c1288]. Falling yields built this rotation, so rising ones unwind it [d41].

### The Fed is not done and said so politely

The FOMC held at 3.45%-4% in a 10-2 vote, with both dissenters wanting a 25 basis point hike [c1155][c1183]. Chair Warsh called financial conditions tighter but stopped short of tight, which Morgan Stanley reads as a high bar for any dovish turn [c1174][c1173]. Goldman's baseline is one more hike in December with a risk of a third in March if inflation surprises higher [c1168]. Generali Asset Management is more hawkish, forecasting two more hikes, December and the first quarter of 2027, on core PCE (the Fed's preferred inflation measure, stripping out food and energy) ending 2026 near 3% [c1157][c1695]. The market is well short of either, closing last week pricing 18 basis points of hikes through year-end and about 60 by the middle of next year [c1187], and UBS notes no full hike is priced until January 2027 [c1205].

### Wednesday's refunding is the long end's test

UBS expects the Treasury to hold its quarterly package at $125 billion, split $58 billion of 3-year, $42 billion of 10-year and $25 billion of 30-year debt for November through January [c722]. Goldman also expects 10-to-30-year auction sizes steady and the buyback envelope maintained or modestly larger [c699]. The disagreement is about what comes next. Citi expects cuts to 20-year and 30-year sales starting this month [c648], while JPMorgan argues cutting long-bond sales would be imprudent because demand for Treasury bills is already showing early signs of stalling [c687]. Dealers expect the Treasury to drop its "at least the next several quarters" language, which would clear the way for later cuts [c676]. Natixis, which carried the Street's highest 10-year forecast at 4.60%, has turned neutral on the long end and now buys dips in a 4.90% to 5.25% range [c691][c662]. The 10-year slid below 4.9% Friday, a six-week low [c677].

### Oil built a risk premium and started giving it back

Brent touched $118 on Friday [c1122] and then printed its biggest downside candle in a long time, falling back to the trend line from the July lows [c1135], with UBS marking Brent down 2.8% to about $110 on Monday as Washington gave Iran diplomacy more time [c1136]. Goldman's Daan Struyven raised his December Brent forecast from $90 to $105 on supply-disruption risk [c1101], and Goldman separately estimates about $27 of Brent's price was geopolitical premium as of Friday, against fundamental fair value near $90 [c1141][c1123]. Deutsche Bank puts WTI 65% above its medium-term fair value [c1138]. The positioning is the fragile part: trend-followers entered this selloff heavily long, and their downside convexity raises the risk of a self-reinforcing liquidation [c1343][c1344]. European gas is the other leg, through €100 per megawatt hour with northwest storage at a 14-year low, 64% full against a pre-2022 average of 88% [c1117][c1118].

### Gold stopped working as a hedge

Speculative investors sold $19.5 billion of gold over five consecutive weeks from September 22, with $8 billion of that in the October 20 to 27 week alone, $5.2 billion from longs closing and $2.8 billion from new shorts, per Goldman's Quinn [c1561][c1560][c1562]. Gold broke below $4,000 on Friday [c1570] and lost another 4.3% between October 27 and 30 [c1575]. The tell is what it ignored. US 10-year real yields fell 11 basis points over the same stretch, which normally supports the price, and Goldman reads the divergence as a positioning-driven market [c1564]. Other gross longs remain above their two-year median despite five weeks of liquidation, so further technical damage could set off another wave regardless of yields [c1565]. Three-month implied volatility and put skew have risen, with 25-delta put-call skew at the 85th percentile of its one-year range [c1568].

### Memory priced the top, then argued about the next one

Kioxia's December guidance showed NAND price gains slowing from 70% to the mid-20s [c444], and Sandisk fell more than 11% on Friday despite beating and announcing an 8-for-1 split [c171]. That sets up the bull case nobody owns. Bernstein's Mark Newman rates Sandisk Outperform with a $3,000 target against a $1,150 close, arguing stacked flash memory could ease the bottleneck between how fast chips compute and how fast they can read data [c167]. A competing estimate in the same debate puts the market for it at $40 billion to $150 billion by 2030 and cuts inference costs roughly 30% through better chip utilisation [c266][c267]. Both sides flag the same unresolved condition: Nvidia has not endorsed the technology, and its announced Rubin, Rubin Ultra and Feynman chips do not include it [c47]. Semiconductors closed right on their 200-day average with the SOX forward price-to-earnings multiple back at 16x [c632][c633].

### The election is priced, the policy is not

Kalshi has a Democratic sweep at 68% [c1062], and JPMorgan assigns 66% to that outcome with a modest index move either way [c1048][c933]. Goldman's favourite long for it is the ACA Beneficiaries basket, on the view that reauthorised premium subsidies lift hospital and insurer earnings [c968][c969]. The basket has fallen about 7% from its early-October peak relative to the S&P ex-AI even as sweep odds rose [c1004], because House control is not the binding constraint. Citi expects any shutdown deal to delay spending cuts rather than add subsidies [c1023]. RBC is the outlier on the celebration, rating a Democratic sweep neutral-to-negative and noting that under a Republican president, a Democratic Congress has produced the weakest average S&P return of the three outcomes at 5% [c1018]. Goldman's options desk priced Wednesday's straddle at 1.51%, smaller than Friday's actual 1.82% move [c1026].

### Where the money went instead

Healthcare took the rotation. UBS measures XLV up 23% in the second half, with about half of that gain, 11%, coming in October alone [c177][c179]. RBC upgraded the sector to overweight on October 14 and downgraded Tech the same morning, and since that swap Health Care has been the top S&P 500 sector while Tech has been the second worst [c411][c540][c412]. Goldman's Healthcare AI Beneficiaries basket is up about 38% year to date against roughly 17% for its broad AI basket and 20% for the sector [c379], and Louis Miller argues those names carry low correlation to AI, rates and oil [c380]. The credit version of the same trade comes from Goldman's Amanda Lynam, who names healthcare and pharma among the investment-grade sectors least correlated to AI credit spreads [c1496]. Goldman's own caution applies: a weaker growth outlook would hit the ex-AI pockets that rallied mainly on short covering [c868].
