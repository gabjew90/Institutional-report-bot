# Power Caps The Buildout

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Electricity, not orders, sets the ceiling

Electricity decides the AI buildout between now and 2028, not chip orders. Morgan Stanley sizes the US data center power gap at 57 GW gross over 2026 to 2028, or 32 GW once the faster ways of getting connected are counted, and has developers short 32-42 GW through 2028, 34-43% of the power they are asking for. When power is the scarce input, the buyers who get energized are the ones utilities will sign with, which is why Morgan Stanley has the largest cloud providers and the AI-only cloud companies taking 60-95% of global data center capacity coming online, with access concentrating among a handful of large, credit-worthy players.

Where that bites is not where the headlines are. Nvidia and Broadcom together hold about 90% of the market for AI accelerator chips, and Morgan Stanley stays convicted that both meet the combined $800 billion of AI revenue their twelve-month outlooks carry, naming Nvidia its preference on the widest geographic and customer footprint. The disruption, if power bites, lands on memory, optics and other small-dollar components, and custom accelerator chips risk losing share to general-purpose GPUs. The same work cut Morgan Stanley's estimate of the US share of global compute to 55% from 60%, and overseas expansion does not solve the domestic shortage.

Goldman's technology desk reads the same memory tightness in the opposite direction. On a site visit, Dell's management pushed back on the idea that AI infrastructure is being commoditized and described supply, memory above all, as the gating factor on growth, expecting memory markets to stay constrained into next year, with Dell's FY2027 revenue estimates up nearly 100% over the past year. For Goldman the durable bull case is compute demand outstripping compute supply, which is what supports 2027 and later capital spending. One desk has tight memory as the first thing to crack under a power ceiling. The other has it as evidence of demand the industry cannot yet serve.

TSM settles part of this Thursday, October 15, before the open, with sentiment upbeat into the print, after the stock fell about 7% over four days last week, its biggest such drop since late July. Goldman's research team models TSM capital spending at $85 billion in 2027 and $98 billion in 2028. Guidance that walks those numbers back is the power-constrained read winning. Guidance that confirms them says the binding limit is still equipment supply, not electricity.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Generation moves onto the site

The fix Morgan Stanley expects is power generated at the data center itself, bypassing the grid. Its thematics team has 160 GW of new US data center power demand from 2026 to 2029, of which 22 GW is already under construction with a grid connection, leaving a 77 GW shortfall to be covered by onsite generation once available grid capacity and already-purchased equipment are counted. That is a market of roughly 120 GW and $240 billion through 2029, split 77 GW data center and 40 GW commercial and industrial. Ranked on a cost measure that adds the price of idle chips waiting for power, Bloom Energy's fuel cells come first, BorgWarner's turbine generator second and FTAI's aircraft-engine-derived unit third, and the top equipment ideas are Bloom, Baker Hughes, BorgWarner, Cummins, ERock, FTAI, INNIO and Rolls Royce. The queue is already full, with INNIO and Caterpillar booked through 2028 at locked pricing and shipment levels. Training campuses of 1 GW and up still belong to the grid.

### Permitting is the new bottleneck

Local politics is now doing what a supply chain used to. Third parties count roughly $156 billion of US data center projects cancelled or delayed during 2025, and an estimated $130 billion more in the first quarter of 2026. Pennsylvania's governor ended the state's fast-track permitting program and now requires local official approval first. Texas is auditing every project moving through ERCOT's interconnection process against more than 474 GW of pending requests, about 90% of it data-center related, and Virginia's July 31 Rider T1 order moves Dominion toward making the party that causes a direct-connection cost pay for it. Morgan Stanley's reading is that resistance does not reverse the buildout, it redirects it toward onsite power and smaller sites closer to users. Pennsylvania's order is the template worth watching, because the 22 GW counted as already connected is the part permitting can still take back.

### Home batteries ran as a power plant

September gave the clearest proof yet that distributed storage is dispatchable. Sunrun and Tesla coordinated more than 140,000 residential batteries to deliver over 580 MW of peak power to the California grid during a heat wave, of which 517 MW came from roughly 110,000 Tesla Powerwalls and 63 MW from other manufacturers' batteries. Sunrun owned or operated 55% of the participating Powerwalls and dispatched more than 30,000 additional customer batteries on top. The two, with Renew Home, signed a non-binding framework in June to aggregate more than 16 GW of flexible capacity from home batteries, solar and EVs, and have already identified more than 300 MW of it in Virginia, a figure that could reach at least 500 MW by 2030. Morgan Stanley has the US storage market growing from 57 GWh in 2025 to 280 GWh by 2030, a 38% annual growth rate to 279 GWh of yearly deployments.

### The Fed is not behind, on this math

Morgan Stanley's estimate of the real neutral policy rate, the setting that neither speeds up nor slows the economy, has risen from just under 100bp to about 150bp since 1Q25, and a basis point is one-hundredth of a percent. That makes the current fed funds target of 3.75-4.00%, reached with September's 25bp increase, neutral to modestly restrictive rather than loose, even though Fed Chair Warsh called that move the removal of "a dose of accommodation". Two financial conditions measures agree. One reads about 100bp tighter than at the end of February, the other sits 0.78 standard deviations above its historical average, and the move since the September meeting is worth about a 33bp hike on its own. Because policy is not accommodative, Morgan Stanley sees no catching up to do and keeps its call for two more 25bp hikes, in December and March, to 4.25-4.5%. The September minutes had only 4-5 participants calling policy not, or only mildly, restrictive.

### This week's prints

September core CPI lands October 14. Morgan Stanley forecasts 0.24% on the month and 2.5% over the year, five basis points below August's 0.29% increase, with headline CPI at 0.62% on the month and 3.69% over the year on another sizable gasoline increase. Core PCE, the inflation gauge the Fed targets, is penciled at 0.23% for September, with the firm looking for 3.0% at the end of 2026 and 2.6% at the end of 2027, measured fourth quarter over fourth quarter. September retail sales follow on October 15, seen up 0.3% on the month, 0.5% excluding autos, and 0.2% for the control group. Jobless claims are forecast to fall to 190k in the week ahead. Core CPI near forecast, with gasoline carrying the headline, leaves Morgan Stanley's December hike call standing. A hotter core is what changes the argument.

### Growth holds while import costs climb

Morgan Stanley has real GDP growth at 2.4% for 2026 and 2.3% for 2027, measured fourth quarter over fourth quarter, with AI-related investment contributing around 60bps of it in each year. Its own third-quarter tracking estimate sat at 2.8% in the week of October 6, while the Atlanta Fed's slipped to 3.6% from 3.7%. Consumption through the third quarter is running at a 2.6% annual rate, half a point faster than last year, and in the firm's stronger scenario, built on elevated household wealth and business spending, 2027 growth reaches 3.0%. Pulling the other way, the effective US tariff rate averaged 6.7% from June to August and Morgan Stanley expects it near 10% by year-end, with August trade data putting it at about 6.9%. USTR's final forced-labor action, effective July 24, set a 10% duty on economies that have adopted or committed to a forced-labor import ban and 12.5% on most others.

### Seventh month of the oil shock

The energy price shock is in its seventh month as of early October. Crude kept rising into the month and diesel margins widened further, with WTI at Cushing at $96.13 a barrel and Brent at $125.51 as of October 5. Part of the split between diesel and crude is refineries around the Strait of Hormuz buying less crude because disruption limits their ability to ship finished fuel out of the region. The cushion is thinning. Strategic Petroleum Reserve stocks fell to about 283.0 million barrels as of October 2, the lowest since April 1984, and if drawdowns continue at roughly 30 million barrels every four weeks, inventories approach the operational minimum of about 70 million barrels by March 2027. Morgan Stanley's baseline has oil near $100/bbl through the fourth and first quarters, weighing on near-term growth, and its 10% probability recession scenario puts prices at $140-160/bbl with shortages and demand destruction.

### A calm index hiding a split market

The Nasdaq 100 closed higher for a fourth straight week and six of the last seven, while the Russell 2000 fell for a fifth straight week. Underneath the index, software beat semis by approximately 800 basis points over the week, and Goldman's cyclicals-versus-defensives pair had its worst week since mid-August. Analog chipmakers are now meaningfully underperforming their historical relationship with ISM manufacturing data. Consumer sentiment in the University of Michigan survey is back to its lowest in more than 20 years, and Goldman's sentiment indicator shows light equity positioning. What is holding up is the group investors treat as levered to AI deployment rather than AI capital spending, with SNOW, CRWD, PANW, NET and PLTR trading at or near all-time highs. The question Goldman puts to that group is whether earnings growth will be enough to support the multiples.
