# Oil Now Sets Rates

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Fuel prices are pricing a Fed hike

Crude and fuel, not the economy, are setting the bond market. Brent rose 4.60% to $94.65 a barrel at Tuesday's close and added another 0.67% to $95.28 early this morning, European natural gas hit a three-year high of €72.22 per megawatt hour, and diesel, gasoil and heating oil broke out alongside it. Government bond yields followed to multi-year highs. The 10-year US Treasury yield rose 4.8 basis points (one basis point is one hundredth of a percent) to 4.80%, the highest since 2023, the German 10-year reached 3.34%, the UK 10-year 5.22%, and the Japanese 10-year crossed 3% for the first time in 30 years. The S&P 500 fell 0.71% to 7631. Inside it, the stocks that need growth lost 212 basis points against defensive ones while energy shares gained 154 basis points and utilities 85.

What matters is which maturities took the pressure. Fuel inflation is no longer only a long-dated problem. It is pulling short-maturity yields up too, because traders are pricing a real chance the Fed answers it, and the 2-year Treasury yield rose 5.8 basis points to 4.40%, its highest since July 2024. Odds of a September hike are 69%, up from 27% on August 17, with Governor Barr saying the Fed should move if inflation is not moderating sufficiently. Goldman Sachs calls this the bad version of an energy move: a supply shock hurts growth at the same moment it pushes the Fed toward tightening, which is a poor setting for what investors will pay per dollar of earnings. Their desk puts the link bluntly, saying heating oil and the US 10-year have been trading as if they were one asset, so rate relief has to come from energy first.

J.P. Morgan's global strategy team reads the same yields the other way. Most of the rise reflects stronger activity rather than inflation expectations breaking loose, wage growth is soft, and they expect central banks to deliver less tightening than markets price.

Goldman set the test itself. If crude, gas and product prices come down, yields can stabilize and risk assets recover, provided Friday's payrolls and the September 11 CPI cooperate. Its own escalation history points that way, since acute periods usually give way to aggressive de-escalation and shipping flows may already be normalizing. If energy keeps climbing, sustained equity upside gets much harder.

## 3. BRIEFS

### The September meeting nobody is sure about

Markets have mostly called the September hike, at 69% odds against 27% on August 17, with Goldman marking it at 66%. TS Lombard's point is that two weeks before a meeting the market is normally about 95% certain, so this is unusually open, and its case is that no hike is needed: the standard rate rule produces anything from 4.47% to 6.83% depending on which inflation measure goes in, and using the Dallas Fed's six-month trimmed average, which never really topped 2.5% and is back to 2.35%, the current rate is already about right. Kevin Warsh's speech changed nothing either way, and TS Lombard expects him to find room in the data to hold for another month or two. The tightening impulse is not only American, with the ECB's Simkus saying one September hike will not be enough and the RBNZ moving to 2.75%. August CPI on September 11 decides it.

### Friday's payrolls now cut the wrong way

A stronger jobs number is the bad outcome here, because it lifts yields and weighs on stocks. Michael Feroli at J.P. Morgan looks for 50,000 jobs against the street's 55,000, after a prior print of -23,000, with unemployment steady at 4.1% and hourly earnings up 0.25% on the month and 3.0% on the year. His desk maps the outcomes: a 25% chance of 5,000 to 35,000, which buys the S&P 25 to 75 basis points, a 30% chance of 35,000 to 65,000 for anything between -25 and +50, and a 10% chance of a print above 95,000 that costs 50 basis points to 1.25%. Options expiring Friday imply a 1.1% move. The same desk says the September 11 inflation print matters more than this one.

### Broadcom reports into a crowded long

Broadcom is down 53 basis points before the open and reports after the close, carrying a J.P. Morgan positioning score of +3 on a scale that runs to +5. Consensus wants revenue up 33% on the quarter and 85% on the year, and the buyside survey wants 36% and 89%, underpinned by AI revenue growing more than 150% year over year and a 2.2% revenue beat, a wider bar than recent quarters. Most survey respondents expect Hock Tan to raise the FY27 AI revenue guide toward $120 billion, and Harlan Sur's estimate of a fiscal 2027 AI backlog above $150 billion leaves room to wrong-foot the bears, probably not this quarter. The worry is share loss at Alphabet to work co-developed with MediaTek, against a shipment inflection from the TPU v8i ramp and a Tomahawk 6 line that is essentially sold out. Goldman expects the print to reinforce the compute demand message and offset cycle and rate fears, while J.P. Morgan's desk warns it may be spectacular and still fail to lift AI, memory and semiconductor names.

### AI hardware delivered and the chip stocks fell anyway

Dell earned $7.04 a share, ahead of Goldman, consensus and its own guidance, with infrastructure revenue up 89% against guidance for 75%. The outlook moved more than the print, with the F2027 AI server revenue target raised to $74 billion from $60 billion and total revenue to $192 billion from $165-169 billion, built on $60.9 billion of AI orders booked in the quarter against $24 billion three months earlier and a $95 billion backlog. Goldman raised its F2027 to F2029 earnings estimates by 38% on average and keeps Buy with a $425 target. Palo Alto Networks raised guidance across every key metric and repeated its $20 billion FY30 target for next-generation security revenue. None of that held the chip group up, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index leading US declines at 2.14% and memory-chip funds posting their first weekly outflow since launch at $0.9 billion. J.P. Morgan is split on what to own, its strategy team ranking chipmakers over the big cloud builders over AI-exposed losers while its US desk prefers Mag7 because the chip and memory names cannot hold a bid.

### The selling that only happens on down days

The funds that trade on price signals rather than views are now positioned so that direction decides the size of the flow. BofA estimates they sell $163 billion in a down market, $1 billion in a flat one, and still sell $9 billion when markets rise. Deutsche Bank puts the funds that size their equity exposure by how calm markets are at the 100th percentile, meaning no room left to buy. Goldman's one-month projections say the same thing from the other side, modest extra buying if markets rise and a violent unwind in its down-big case, both globally and in the S&P, with its estimate of systematic length at $146.5 billion, up about $2 billion on the week. Company share buybacks are near their peak just as the pre-earnings quiet period widens through September, removing the steadiest bid. Against all of that, the VIX sits at 16.34, up only 1.42 points, and J.P. Morgan finds the muted move in the cost of downside protection genuinely surprising.

### Momentum has already broken, and J.P. Morgan disagrees with itself

J.P. Morgan's momentum indices are down more than 21% and nearly 34% from their June highs, which is a factor unwind, not a market correction. Its US desk has gone tactically cautious and neutral, wants net long exposure cut and a dollar-neutral stance, and sees no clean read from positioning with a choppy three to four weeks ahead. Its international desk holds options hedges on momentum while still buying dips, and hedge fund returns have slipped back to plus 0.8% for the month from over 2% earlier in the week. September screens as the S&P's worst month, ahead of the strongest quarter. The house strategy view has not moved with the desks, still bullish into year end, holding equities at 65% against a 60% benchmark, and calling overall positioning light enough that the pain trade is higher.

### Europe is cheap and its gas bill is the problem

European gas above €70 per megawatt hour is more expensive than in 2022, which turns the region's valuation case into a cost question. Goldman is trading it directly, long a 16-stock basket of utilities that benefit from higher power prices and short a 51-stock basket of companies that burn the gas. It stays constructive on the region at 14.5 times earnings, where companies generate cash equal to 5.3% of market value a year against under 2% for the ten largest US stocks, and it upgraded Deutsche Bank to Buy at €43.75 on roughly 300 basis points of improvement in returns on its tangible equity over three years. Its own equity sales desk is more cautious, arguing Europe cannot sustainably outperform while gas stays sharply higher. J.P. Morgan's desk would buy cheap index hedges on Europe now and prefers the US and Asia, while its strategy team overweights the Eurozone at 11.0% against an 8.7% index weight with a December 2026 MSCI Eurozone target of 420.

### Asia takes the yield shock hardest

The Kospi fell 3.79% and led Asian losses, the Nikkei is quoted 2.85% lower, and behind both is a Japanese 10-year yield above 3% for the first time in 30 years. J.P. Morgan expects the Bank of Japan to hike roughly once a quarter from here, which keeps the yen in a 155 to 165 range against the dollar absent a shift in Fed expectations, and Treasury Secretary Bessent has said he would rather see BoJ hikes than repeated currency intervention. The positioning reset is the reason its Asia team separates the next few weeks from the medium term: aggregate investor positioning has fallen from the 75th percentile in late June to the 40th, after big second-quarter cuts to tech in Korea and Taiwan, leaving it cautious near term with an upward path beyond. Emerging market inflows have resumed at $19 billion since July and Korean corporate buybacks are a new source of demand. The trades named are tactical longs in China internet through KWEB, Taiwan, and Latin America through ILF as a hedge against AI-theme weakness.

### Gold gave back the whole geopolitical premium

Gold has fallen 20-25% since the Iran conflict began, having been the consensus long in January, which is the opposite of how a war trade is supposed to work. It sits at $4,309, down 46 basis points, right on the $4,300 level J.P. Morgan's desk flags as support. That desk took profit and is looking for a re-entry, and it expects the trade built on a debasing dollar to keep struggling until the September 16 Fed meeting. J.P. Morgan's strategy team treats the selloff as an opportunity to add, expecting that trade to resume into year end, on the view that a dollar which has rallied as a safe haven is no longer cheap and can start falling again. For now the dollar index is up 0.44% at 99.79, so the near-term condition is the Fed, not the conflict.

### The credit calendar is the next pressure point

J.P. Morgan's desk expects the wave of new bond sales in the two weeks after Labor Day to widen credit spreads, which can then pressure equities. The default data gives it no support yet, with year-to-date rates at 2.63% for high-yield bonds and 2.33% for leveraged loans against 25-year averages of 3.2% and 2.9%, and Nelson Jantzen forecasting 2.25% for both at the end of 2026 before a rise to 2.75% and 4.5% at the end of 2027. Bond investors have already shortened up, rotating out of medium and longer-maturity government bond funds at 3.8 standard deviations into short-maturity funds at 6.3, alongside $37.5 billion of net buying in rate futures concentrated in shorter maturities. Bank shares are being sold too, with financials funds seeing a third straight week of outflows at $1.6 billion. The desk's named hedge into the September 16 meeting is shorting credit funds and buying VIX products.
