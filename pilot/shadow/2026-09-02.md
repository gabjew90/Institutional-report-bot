# The Downside Is Preloaded

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Cautious into a one-sided market

Asian stocks and US and European futures are lower, and the drivers are bond yields and crude oil rather than anything company specific [c66]. S&P futures are down 0.3%, Nasdaq futures 0.6% and Russell 0.2% before the open [c87], with the 10-year Treasury yield at 4.812% and the VIX at 16.76 [c120]. JPMorgan's US Market Intelligence desk moved to a tactically cautious and neutral stance on equities for the next two to three weeks [c109], and its international desk did the same on global equities [c105]. Both want smaller net long positions and a book closer to market neutral [c111].

The reason is a calendar problem rather than a growth problem [d1]. Warsh's post-Jackson-Hole line that the US is at full employment with inflation still too high makes the September 16 Fed meeting live, which is why the desk treats the September 11 inflation print as more important than Friday's jobs number [c112]. That inverts Friday. A strong payroll number pushes yields up and equities down [c80]. Positioning gives no clean directional read and history points to three or four choppy weeks [c124], September screens as the worst month of the year for the S&P before the strongest quarter [c86], and the wave of new corporate bond supply after Labor Day should widen credit spreads in a way that leaks into stocks [c94].

JPMorgan's case is that this is chop and not a drawdown. A recession is unlikely over the next few quarters [c61], earnings should print very strongly for at least two more quarters [c60], and even two quarter-point hikes would not end the bull market [c45]. The flow models describe a different shape of decline [d3]. BofA has systematic strategies selling $163bn if the market falls, and still selling $9bn if it rises [c62]. Deutsche Bank has funds that size stock exposure to a volatility target already at the 100th percentile of their equity allocation, so there is nobody left on that bid [c63]. Goldman's one-month projections show modest additional buying if markets rise against a violent unwind in a large down move [c64], with its estimate of systematic length at $146.5bn, roughly $2bn higher than the prior week [c65]. Company buying is fading at the same time, with buyback activity near its peak just as the September blackout window expands and removes it [c96].

The condition that decides it is mechanical: the desk expects equities to keep struggling until crude and rates stabilize [c88]. Until then JPMorgan is paying for protection rather than selling stock, shorting credit ETFs and buying VIX products into September 16 [c83].

## 3. BRIEFS

### Friday's jobs number now cuts the wrong way

Michael Feroli forecasts 50k jobs added against a consensus of 55k, after a prior month that lost 23k [c113], with the unemployment rate unchanged at 4.1% [c114]. A stronger print lifts yields and weighs on equities [c80]. The desk puts a 25% probability on a print between 5k and 35k, which it thinks adds 25 to 75 basis points to the S&P, a basis point being one hundredth of a percent [c46]. Anything above 95k gets a 10% probability and costs the index between 50 basis points and 1.25% [c81]. Options on the S&P expiring September 4 are priced for a 1.1% move [c116]. The larger event is still the September 11 inflation print, which the desk ranks above payrolls with the Fed meeting on September 16 behind it [c112].

### Broadcom reports into a crowded position

Broadcom reports tonight and the desk warns the quarter may be spectacular and still fail to lift AI, memory and semiconductor stocks [c13]. Consensus has revenue growing 33% on the quarter and 85% on the year, while JPMorgan's buyside survey sits higher at 36% and 89% [c4], backed by AI revenue growing more than 150% year over year [c5]. Most survey respondents expect Hock Tan to raise the fiscal 2027 AI revenue guide, with $120b the consensus landing zone [c6], and Harlan Sur's estimate of a fiscal 2027 AI backlog above $150B leaves room to wrong-foot the bears, probably not this quarter [c7]. The second half of 2026 rests on the TPU v8i shipment ramp, and Tomahawk 6 is essentially sold out [c8]. Working the other way, buyside expectations sit 5% below consensus for fiscal 2028 AI revenue [c10], the stock is a crowded net long [c12], and the main worry is losing Alphabet volume to projects co-developed with MediaTek [c11]. The stock is down 53 basis points before the open, with the print after the close [c9].

### The momentum trade already broke

JPMorgan's momentum factor index is down more than 21% from its June record and the classic version down nearly 34% [c85], well outside the 10% to 15% these unwinds normally run. That is what the caution is actually about [d1]. The crowded side of the market has been liquidated while the index has barely moved, which is why the international desk expects the rotation between factors to run several more weeks and stays hedged with options on momentum while buying dips [c106]. Inside technology the preference is the Mag7 over AI, memory and semiconductor names, on the view that the second group cannot sustain buying interest [c47]. The trade list opens with long Nasdaq 100 against short Russell 2000 [c119], with small and mid caps expected to lag [c82].

### Asia took the hit, but the positioning is already cleaner

Korea's KOSPI fell 4.0% overnight and Japan's Nikkei 2.9% [c95]. The driver is a hawkish repricing of central bank paths, the Bank of Japan's above all [c67], where JPMorgan now expects a hike roughly once a quarter [c72] and sees the dollar holding between 155 and 165 yen unless Fed expectations change materially [c104]. The damage to positioning is mostly done [d2]. JPMorgan's aggregate measure of Asian investor positioning has fallen from the 75th percentile in late June to the 40th today [c99], and the largest second-quarter change among Asian long-only funds was a substantial cut to technology in both Korea and Taiwan [c69]. The Asia strategy team is cautious for the next few weeks while keeping the medium-term path upward [c70], and expresses the constructive side with a tactical long in China internet through $KWEB [c19] and a long in Taiwan as less volatile AI exposure than Korea [c41].

### Bond money ran to the short maturities

Fixed income investors spent last week shortening maturities and cutting how much their bond holdings move when yields shift [c89]. ETF flows left multi-year government bonds at 3.8 standard deviations below normal and went into short-dated government bonds at 6.3 standard deviations above [c17]. In futures, US rates took $37.5Bn of net buying at 1.9 standard deviations, led by 2-year and shorter [c51]. Asset managers cut long positions in 30-year Treasury bonds by 2.1 standard deviations while leveraged funds covered Nasdaq shorts by 1.9 [c91]. Equity ETFs took $35Bn [c52], crypto ETFs a second consecutive week of inflows at $1.8Bn [c53], and semiconductor funds led technology with $3.3Bn across $SMH, $SOXX and $SOXL [c20]. The group being sold is financials, a third straight week of outflows at $1.6Bn [c90].

### The credit risk is supply, not defaults

New bond issuance after Labor Day should widen credit spreads over the two weeks that follow, and wider spreads pressure equities [c94]. The default picture behind that is benign [d1]. Year to date defaults run 2.63% for high yield bonds and 2.33% for leveraged loans [c121], against 25-year averages of 3.2% and 2.9% [c122]. Nelson Jantzen forecasts both finishing 2026 at 2.25% [c59], then splitting in 2027, with high yield at 2.75% and leveraged loans at 4.5% [c93]. The desk is not playing this by owning credit. Its hedge into the September 16 Fed meeting is short credit ETFs and long VIX products [c83].

### Gold gave back a little, crude did the damage

Gold is down 46 basis points at $4,309 and silver down 67 at $63.65 [c15], with $4,300 the support level the desk is watching [c16]. The international desk took profit on the position and wants back in, which is a timing call rather than a change of view [c22]. Crude is the other half of the pressure on equities, with WTI up 28 basis points at $90.47 and natural gas up 148 to $2.95 [c21], and JPMorgan has raised its Brent and European gas price assumptions [c31]. In equities the desk reads that as room to add rather than a crowded trade, with metals and mining positioning not stretched despite the past month's rally [c108] and European miners screening attractive [c36]. Consumer discretionary is the one it wants if energy prices come back down [c48].

### The consumer accelerated in August

Spending through August 20 ran 5.4% above last year against 4.4% in July [c55]. The gain came from the optional half of the basket, with discretionary spending at 5.8% and non-discretionary flat at 4.5% [c56]. It was not confined to the top either. Upper income spending rose 6.9% against 5.7% in July and middle income 7.3% against 6.2% [c57], with lower income at 4.8% against 4.3% [c58]. That is the evidence behind treating this as a positioning event rather than an economic one, with recession seen as highly unlikely over the next few quarters even with September live [c61], and manufacturing coming in weaker than expected while still showing expansion [c123].

### Europe is a hedged long, with named shorts against it

The regional preference is long the US and Asia including Japan, South Korea and Taiwan against Europe [c32], even though the desk has been tactically bullish European stocks since early May on cheap positioning, underperformance and the regime change from the Strait of Hormuz reopening [c33]. It now wants cheap index hedges on that position [c73]. The shorts are specific rather than thematic: high conviction against European chemicals and Asian consumer electronics [c75], plus AI-vulnerable names excluding software, European insurers, autos, MedTech and the MDAX [c74]. Two relative hedges stay on, FTSE 250 underperforming the FTSE 100 through options until UK spending policy clears [c76], and short France against European domestic names on politics [c77]. The backdrop is mixed, with euro area headline inflation up to 3.3% on fuel [c102] while core inflation eased to 2.4% [c103], and three consecutive months of improving business surveys [c29].

### Nobody is bearish past September

The caution has an expiry date. The international desk expects an attractive Q4 setup [c23], supported by JPM Economics' forecast of 3% annualised global industrial growth in the second half of 2026 off the manufacturing upturn [c24]. The global equity strategy team stays bullish into year end on positive earnings revisions in every region [c28], and Mislav's September chartbook holds the same line on revisions, business surveys and aggregate positioning [c27]. Emerging markets are the funding for that view, with $19bn of inflows since July [c25] and Korean corporate buybacks emerging as a major new source of demand [c26]. The timing argument is flow based: 20-day gross flows in the US have bottomed and are getting less negative [c42], and every year since 2018 they have turned positive by mid-September [c43]. Past that sits the midterm pattern, where since 1990 the S&P has added 5.4% three months after election day and 14.7% after twelve, hitting 78% and 100% of the time [c50].
