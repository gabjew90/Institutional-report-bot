# The Fed Isn't Done

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The market wants four hikes, the desks say two

The Fed raised its policy rate by 25 basis points (one basis point is a hundredth of a percent) to 3.75-4.00% with no dissents, and the press conference went out of its way to say that was not the end of anything. Warsh described the move as having removed a dose of accommodation and said policy is still not restrictive, and he called inflation risks skewed to the upside. The projections did the same work quietly: the committee's estimate of the neutral rate went up to 3.25% from 3.06%, which means any given rate slows the economy less than the Fed assumed in June.

The bond market's first reaction was to break a two-decade ceiling. The 10-year Treasury yield closed above 5% for the first time since 2007, after touching 5.04% and finishing the week at 5.00%. Then the squeeze released. Longer-dated yields settled, the inflation rate priced into bonds came down, option prices on stocks fell, and the most rate-sensitive stocks outperformed, an easing Morgan Stanley scores as the equivalent of an 8 basis point cut. The dollar took the hawkish read instead, gaining 1.11% for its best week since June.

That leaves a clean split on how much more tightening is real. Markets price four hikes in total, three beyond September. Morgan Stanley now calls for two more, December and March, to a 4.25-4.50% ceiling, and Societe Generale lands in the same place at 75 basis points of total tightening. Goldman disagrees with the priced path outright: its house view is that the Fed underdelivers versus the four priced and gets closer to two, and even after adding an October hike its economists hold the endpoint at 3.25-3.50% because they expect cuts in late 2027 to offset it. HSBC calls two-to-three the sensible ceiling for managing upside inflation risk, since 100 basis points of tightening would already put the funds rate well above the Fed's own neutral estimate.

Next Friday's payrolls report settles more of this than any speech, and it is the last major release before the October meeting. Morgan Stanley expects market-implied odds of an October move to stay high but flags the other branch: a faster de-escalation in the Middle East or quicker disinflation would make December the last hike instead of the first of several.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Oil is now the inflation forecast

Morgan Stanley moved its whole US baseline off Middle East de-escalation and onto a sustained oil risk premium, at 45% probability. Its marks on September 15 were $107.02 for WTI and $130.80 for Brent, with the Strategic Petroleum Reserve down to roughly 285 million barrels, the lowest since April 1984. Deutsche Bank's own end-of-week mark is far lower, Brent closing at $103.87 after slipping to $101.65 overnight, so which print you anchor on decides how bad the arithmetic looks. On the higher path, core PCE (the inflation measure the Fed actually targets) ends 2026 at 3.2% and 2027 at 2.7%, with 2027 growth cut to 2.3%. Goldman prices the escalation directly: every sustained 10% cut to Middle East exports adds about $20 a barrel to next year's fair value. Hormuz has been closed for nearly 200 days, and that clock is the whole trade.

### The index is fine, the market underneath it is not

BTIG's read is that the S&P 500 is hiding the damage. Selling volume on the NYSE has climbed to 54% of all volume on a 20-day average while the index sits only about 3% below its high and small caps are down 7%. That combination has shown up 12 times since 1996, including March 2000, November 2007 and November 2021, and small caps averaged and median -2.1% over the following 40 trading days. Credit is the tell underneath it: the riskiest high-yield bonds now yield more than 9% above Treasuries, the widest since 2023, and more than 6 points above B-rated paper, with another 36 basis points added this week to 922. Breadth has not been this bad in almost 100 years, with 65 new highs against 370 new lows. Goldman's pushback is valuation, not breadth: second-quarter earnings grew 51% while the forward price-to-earnings multiple fell from 23x to 19x, which is how it keeps an 8,700 target.

### Nobody is paying for protection

The list of what is unresolved is long. A global hiking cycle, crude closing above the $100 handle every day last week, the 10-year Treasury yield approaching 5%, and a midterm election less than two months away. VIX still closed Friday at 14, per Goldman's weekend read. The contracts that pay off most in a large move are near their cheapest ever, index versions in the sub-10th percentile of their range and a few single names at the very bottom, with Nvidia's one-month implied volatility (what the option market charges for expected movement) under 30 for the first time since before Covid and small-cap option pricing at five-year lows. Goldman's desk would rather own the move than sell it: long QQQ against short small caps on a view that breadth does not widen, and options that guarantee selling the highest close between now and year-end for 60 to 100 basis points over a standard put.

### Two prime desks, two opposite weeks

Goldman's prime brokerage saw the largest net buying of US stocks in five weeks, with tech the most-bought sector for a third straight week, alongside $79.3bn into equity funds including $63.8bn into US stocks. Morgan Stanley's book says the opposite for the same week: hedge funds net sold across every major region, with new shorts doing the selling, rotating out of semiconductors and broad AI into hyperscalers, cybersecurity and financials, even as net leverage rose to 53% and gross to 209%, the 60th and 93rd percentiles since 2010. Sentiment is not the same thing as flow. Goldman's own sentiment gauge fell to -0.9 and AAII bears jumped to 53.3% while its long/short client estimate lost 1.02% against a 0.35% gain for world equities. Next week's flow data resolves which book was early.

### Permitting, not chips, is the datacenter constraint

RBC's tracker now counts about 66 GW of datacenter capacity actively under development, up 21 GW since March, plus over 304 GW planned, with the Americas alone going from 26.8 GW to 45.8 GW under construction. The brake is political. New York's Executive Order 62 froze permitting for anything above 50MW while 9,682MW sat in the interconnection queue, and it cites grid-upgrade costs on the order of $1 million per megawatt. Projects already permitted or under construction are protected, but anything still at the discretionary stage faces a one-year floor with no stated ceiling. A Gallup survey in May found 70% of Americans opposed to datacenters being built locally, and Virginia has already lost the multi-gigawatt Prince William Digital Gateway to a zoning ruling. RBC's point is that the mispriced risk is copying, since the order's sponsors pitch it as a national template.

### The CPU bill nobody was modeling

Bank of America raised its 2030 server CPU market estimate to $210bn or more from roughly $170bn, on the argument that agentic AI changes the hardware mix rather than the total. Training used about one CPU for every four accelerators, inference closer to one in two, and agent workloads approach one to one, which lifts CPUs to roughly 10% of a $2.2 trillion datacenter systems market. AMD is the pick on that thesis with a $620 objective, Nvidia stays the top overall name at $350, and Intel's share of server CPU value is modeled down to about 22% by 2030 from about 34%. Morgan Stanley's version of the same spending sits further upstream in materials, where printed circuit board demand tied to AI grows more than sixfold by 2030 and the addressable market goes from roughly $58 billion to $135 billion. That group is up 91% in twelve months and already 36% off its high.

### The consumer is an equity position now

The spending that matters belongs to households that own stocks. The top fifth hold $49.8 trillion of equity wealth against $8.3 trillion of wages, and the top tenth own 87% of all corporate equities. Their portfolios are concentrated in one theme: JPMorgan finds retail turnover in the AI, datacenter and electrification basket up 56.54% this year, while Goldman's consumer inertia index has dropped from 100.56 in January to 85.39 and its discretionary basket has fallen to 86.6 against AI stocks from above 105. The uncomfortable part is the labor side of the same cohort. Occupations with high AI exposure carry median pay of $81,680 against $48,680 for low-exposure work, so the jobs most exposed to automation are the high-paying ones held by the people whose spending depends on their portfolios. Michigan sentiment at 47.8 is already close to its record low.

### France is the bond market's other problem

French 10-year yields closed more than 100 basis points above German equivalents on Friday, the first time since 2012, with the French 10-year hitting a post-2008 high of 4.56%. The cause is budget arithmetic rather than a surprise: the deficit is heading to 5.4% of output against a target near 5%, on weaker growth and higher interest costs. Ratings pressure followed, with a Scope downgrade and DBRS putting France on negative watch, and Japanese life insurers have been selling French bonds into it. One published estimate has the France-Germany spread widening to 120 basis points. The knock-on matters for US rates, because the flight into German and US government bonds pulled G7 yields down and worked against the Fed's hawkish message. Societe Generale has already cut government bonds to 12% of its multi-asset portfolio from 15%.

### Gold's bull case survives the hikes

Goldman kept its end-2027 gold target at $5,400 through both the September hike and the newly added October one, while trimming near-term fair value to $4,650 from $4,900 as higher rates weigh on ETF demand. The stress case is explicit: three hikes beyond current expectations would take gold to roughly $4,070. What holds the floor is official buying, running at more than five times the pre-2022 pace. Societe Generale ties that directly to the freezing of Russian central bank assets in February 2022 and keeps commodities and gold at 20% of its portfolio. Goldman's own desk preference is to add around the $4,000 level, where sovereign and institutional demand has shown up on pullbacks. That level, not the Fed path, is the line that decides whether this is a pause or a top.

### Bitcoin held the line that mattered

Bitcoin broke below 75,000 last week and reversed straight back toward recent highs, which reversed BTIG's earlier expectation of a slide to about 69,000. Its call now is conditional and stated as such: as long as 75,000 holds, the path runs through 82,000 toward roughly 90,000. Washington cut both ways this week. The Clarity Act market-structure bill failed a cloture vote 49-50 against the 60 needed, which dropped its 2026 odds to a record-low 8%, while the Bitcoin Act, which would authorize $80 billion of federal purchases over five years, cleared committee. One argument for owning bitcoin over gold is relative: it can outperform if ETF hedging eases, with bitcoin ETFs eventually holding triple what gold ETFs do. The 75,000 level is the invalidation, and it is the only number in this theme with a desk behind it.

### The balance sheet is the quiet second lever

Morgan Stanley's base case is roughly $1.5 trillion of balance-sheet reduction over about two years, starting as early as the first quarter of 2027, inside a plausible $0.6 trillion to $2.5 trillion range. The mechanism is passive, not active selling: bonds are allowed to mature at roughly $1 trillion a year, against about $950bn of Treasuries rolling off over the next twelve months. Mortgage bond sales are given only a 15% chance and capped at $5bn a month on top of paydowns, which would widen spreads on lower-coupon mortgage bonds by 10 to 20 basis points if it happened. Warsh's stated view is that quantitative easing outlived its purpose and distorted pricing, while Governor Barr has argued that draining reserves too far risks bank resilience and money-market function. If this lever does the tightening, it takes pressure off the rate path.

### The yen is where the hike actually landed

The dollar's cleanest expression of a hawkish Fed was against the yen, which fell 2.08% last week for its worst week since October 2025 after breaking the 155 level and closing at 156.9, per Academy Securities. Morgan Stanley is long dollar-yen from 156.23 with a target of 163.00 and a stop of 150.00, and its Japan economist pulled the Bank of Japan path forward to 1.50% by December and 1.75% by March while still putting fair value near 171, so faster Japanese hikes are not the argument against the trade. The opposite side is structural. HSBC expects dollar depreciation to continue even while the Fed hikes, and Goldman's favored expression is short dollars against a basket of emerging markets including Turkey, Egypt, Hungary, Brazil, Mexico and Taiwan. The stop at 150.00 is where that argument wins.
