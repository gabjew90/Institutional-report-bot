# Oil Now Sets The Fed

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### The Hike Was About Crude, And So Is The Next One

The 10-year Treasury yield closed above 5% last week for the first time since 2007, peaking at 5.04% on Tuesday before ending the week at 5.00%, up 2.9 basis points (one basis point is one hundredth of a percent) [c276][c275]. The path there says more than the level. An early-week jump in energy prices drove yields through 5% on fears of slower growth alongside firmer inflation, and it was falling oil, not the Fed's decision, that unwound most of the move [d11]. The dollar index rose 1.11% on the week, its biggest gain since June [c508], and the yen fell 2.08% against it, the worst week since October 2025 [c514].

The Fed lifted its target range by 25 basis points to 3.75% to 4% with no dissenting votes [c410]. In the same projections it raised its estimate of the neutral policy rate, the level that neither speeds up nor slows the economy, to 3.25% from 3.06% [c317]. Warsh described the move as removing a dose of accommodation [c322] and signalled he does not view current policy as restrictive [c335].

The mechanism running underneath all of it is crude. Morgan Stanley switched its US baseline from Middle East de-escalation to a sustained oil risk premium and gives that path a 45% probability [c432][d20]. On that baseline Morgan Stanley raised core PCE inflation, the Fed's preferred price gauge, to 3.2% for 2026 and 2.7% for 2027 [c629], cut 2027 real GDP growth to 2.3% from 2.6% [c687], and trimmed payroll growth to 50,000 a month with unemployment reaching 4.3% [c548].

That is where the banks split. Morgan Stanley now expects two more 25 basis point hikes, in December and March, for a 4.25% to 4.50% terminal range [c314], and Societe Generale calls the same two meetings [c337]. The market is pricing three more beyond September, four including it [c313]. Goldman's house view is the opposite: the Fed underdelivers against the roughly four hikes priced, more likely delivering two [c306]. HSBC treats two to three total as the sensible ceiling, since 100 basis points of tightening would already leave the funds rate about 150 basis points above neutral [c380][c360].

What decides it is not the dots. Morgan Stanley says a faster de-escalation in the Middle East or quicker disinflation would leave only one more hike, in December [c363]. Next Friday's payrolls report is the most important release before the October meeting [c551].

## 3. BRIEFS

### The Index Is Fine, The Market Under It Is Not

Fewer than 30% of S&P 500 stocks trade above their 50-day average price [c580]. NYSE downside volume on a 20-day average has climbed to 54% of all volume, near the highs for the year, with the index only about 3% below its peak and the Russell 2000 down 7% [c573][c589][c574]. BTIG counts 12 instances of that combination since 1996 [c575], including March 2000, November 2007 and November 2021 [c577], after which small caps returned an average and median of -2.1% over the following 40 trading days [c576]. Friday's NYSE tally was 65 new highs against 370 new lows [c586]. Goldman's rebuttal is earnings, not breadth: S&P 500 profits grew 51% year over year in the second quarter while the forward price-to-earnings multiple fell from about 23x to 19x [c565][c594], which is why it holds a 12-month target of 8,700 [c566].

### The Junk Market Is Already Voting

Triple-C rated high-yield spreads widened another 36 basis points this week to 922, the top of their 52-week range [c614]. That puts them above 9%, the widest since 2023, more than 6 percentage points over single-B paper, and wider than at the worst of the 2022 bear market [c598][c599][c600]. BTIG ties the small-cap underperformance directly to this, with the Russell 2000 to S&P 500 ratio down more than 8% since late June [c601]. The supply side is just as loud: $135 billion of convertible debt has already set a full-year record with four months to go, and 44% of it came from AI-related names [c610][c611]. Goldman's 30-year hyperscaler credit basket now trades about 60 basis points wide of comparably rated investment-grade credit, up from roughly 20 when the theme started [c603]. Will Wolcott's favourite trade is a directional short in cash investment-grade credit outside the AI capex complex [c604].

### Nobody Is Paying For Protection

The VIX closed Friday at a 14 handle [c665] against oil that closed above $100 every day last week [c426] and midterm elections under two months away [c540]. Index convexity sits in the sub-10th percentile and a handful of single stocks sit at the 0th [c666][c667]. Nvidia's one-month implied volatility is below 30 for the first time since before Covid [c25], and small-cap implied volatility has fallen to five-year lows heading into a tightening cycle [c73]. The 10-day average put-to-call ratio is near its lows for the year [c664]. Goldman's desk is using that to buy structures rather than sell them: three-month SPX 95% lookback puts cost 1.8% against 1.25% for the plain version [c38], and the lookback guarantees selling the high of any session between now and year end for an extra 60 to 100 basis points [c669]. The desk also likes long QQQ against short IWM on the view that breadth will not widen [c74].

### Two Sets Of Books On Positioning

EPFR recorded $79.3 billion into equities last week, $63.8 billion of it US, the fastest American buying in three months [c468]. Goldman's prime brokerage saw its largest net buying in five weeks, with client net exposure up 2.7 points month to date off one-year lows [c469][c473], though the full book still sits only in the 34th percentile of the past year [c478]. Morgan Stanley's prime data says the opposite for the same week: funds ended net sellers across every major region, driven by short additions [c497]. JPMorgan puts long/short net leverage near the 19th percentile over 12 months [c495], and Goldman Prime has fundamental long/short net leverage at the 7th [c484]. Sentiment agrees with the bears, with AAII bearishness up 14 points to 53.3%, the highest since May 2025 [c490]. Goldman's own cash desk reports long-only clients as better sellers in sectors its hedge fund book was buying [c493].

### The Permit Is Now The Bottleneck

RBC tracks roughly 66 gigawatts of active datacenter development globally, up 21 gigawatts since March [c146], with Americas capacity under construction jumping from 26.8 to 45.8 gigawatts [c151]. The constraint is no longer chips or capital. New York's Executive Order 62, signed July 22, froze permitting for anything above 50 megawatts while 9,682 megawatts of load sat in the interconnection queue [c204], and it cites roughly $1 million per megawatt as the emerging floor for grid-upgrade costs [c205]. Projects already permitted or under construction are shielded, but anything still at the discretionary stage faces a one-year minimum with no stated ceiling [c206]. A Gallup survey in May found 70% of Americans opposed to local datacenter construction [c202]. RBC's call is that the mispriced risk is contagion, because the order's sponsors are pitching it as a national template [c208].

### Refining, Not Crude, Is The Tight Part

The Strait of Hormuz has been shut to global trade for nearly 200 days [c436]. Goldman's Daan Struyven estimates every sustained 10% cut to Middle East exports adds about $20 a barrel to next year's fair value of crude [c421], and he prefers hedging that risk through deferred refined products rather than crude itself [c420]. The reason is capacity. A veteran commodity fund manager puts the Atlantic Basin short 5 million barrels a day of refining, meaning more crude through Hormuz would not relieve fuel markets [c437]. Diesel has already broken the chart level flagged a week earlier [c419], and Academy Securities argues US export restrictions cannot fix it, because the domestic price tracks the global price less transport and storage [c456]. The strategic reserve is down to about 285 million barrels, the lowest since April 1984 [c430]. Goldman's stated hedge is a December Brent $125 call expiring 27 October at $1.10 a barrel [c55].

### France Is The Loose Board In Europe

French 10-year government bonds closed more than 100 basis points above German bunds on Friday, the widest since 2012 [c521], with the French yield hitting a post-2008 high of 4.56% [c522]. The trigger was fiscal: the budget gap is now set to reach 5.4% of output against a target of roughly 5%, on weaker growth and higher interest costs [c530]. Scope downgraded France after hours and DBRS put it on negative watch [c535]. Japanese life insurers have been actively selling French paper, which is part of why the spread moved as fast as it did [c534]. Societe Generale's research points to 120 basis points as the next stop [c533]. A London-based chief investment officer expects the ECB to find or build a new tool to support French bonds if the pressure continues [c645]. The spread itself is the tell here.

### The Only Consumer Left Owns Stocks

The top 20% of US households hold $49.8 trillion of equity wealth against $8.3 trillion of labor income, while the bottom 40% hold $1.8 trillion of labor income against $1.7 trillion of equities [c227][c228]. The top 10% own 87% of all corporate equities and mutual funds [c229]. So spending for the cohort that does most of the spending tracks the stock market rather than the paycheck, and the stock market gains are concentrated in AI. Goldman's Consumer Inertia Index has fallen from 100.56 in January to 85.39 [c170], and its US Discretionary basket has slid to 86.6 against AI names from above 105 earlier this year [c168]. Morgan Stanley puts median pay in high-AI-exposure occupations at $81,680 against $48,680 in low-exposure ones [c231][c232], meaning the jobs most exposed to AI are the high-paying ones held by exactly that cohort [c175]. Michigan consumer sentiment fell about 4 points to 47.8 [c193].

### The Capex Story Moved Off The GPU

Bank of America raised its CY30 server CPU market estimate to more than $210 billion from roughly $170 billion [c101], on the view that agentic workloads push the CPU-to-GPU ratio from about 1:4 in training toward 1:1 [c102], lifting processors to about 10% of a $2.2 trillion data center systems market [c103]. AMD is its top CPU pick with a $620 objective [c43][c44], and Nvidia stays its top overall semiconductor pick at $350 [c19][c20]. Morgan Stanley makes the same argument through materials rather than silicon, seeing AI-linked circuit board demand growing more than 6x by 2030 [c135] and the global market compounding 18% a year from roughly $58 billion to $135 billion [c137]. Societe Generale is the warning voice: it now sees hyperscaler capex peaking later and higher, in mid-2027 rather than end-2026 [c159], with free cash flow after distributions already negative [c210].

### Gold Gets Slowed, Not Stopped

Goldman kept its $5,400 an ounce target for the end of 2027 [c653] while cutting its year-end fair value to $4,650 from $4,900, because higher rates weigh on exchange-traded fund demand in the near term [c660]. If the Fed delivers three more hikes beyond what is currently expected, Goldman sees gold closer to $4,070 [c661]. The structural bid is official rather than financial: central banks are buying at more than five times their pre-2022 pace [c654], which Societe Generale traces directly to the freezing of Russia's central bank reserves in February 2022 [c658]. Societe Generale holds commodities and gold at 20% of its multi-asset portfolio [c655]. Goldman's Tony Kim says gold is still his favourite trade, with roughly $4,000 the level where sovereign and institutional buying supports adding on pullbacks [c651].
