# No Backstop, No Bottom

## 2. THE MAIN EVENT

### Panic without a policy blink

The S&P 500 fell for seven straight sessions and closed Friday at 6,910, down 8.6% on the week and holding a 0.9% gain for 2026. The VIX jumped more than 16 points, the fifth-largest weekly spike in its history, and Goldman's US Vol Panic Index finished at a record 9.8 out of 10. Readings that extreme have been buy signals. After the four prior comparable VIX spikes the index rose 3.3% on average over the following week and was higher every time, and 26.5% on average a year later, with the worst case still up 19.7%. Every one of those four panics ended with a policy rescue. This one has none in it. The Fed is still hiking, and Goldman expects a second 25 basis point increase in December, each basis point a hundredth of a percentage point, plus a third in March.

That gap is what the week was about. Goldman's derivatives desk measures the drawdown against the fourth quarter of 2018: the S&P's 8.2% fall from the October 6 peak is 42% of its 19.5% 2018 decline. The market for corporate debt has barely moved, with insurance against investment-grade defaults only 15% as wide as it got in 2018 and high-yield spreads 28% of the way. Brian Garrett calls credit spreads the single largest difference between now and then. Run the rest of the 2018 path and the S&P lands near 6,294, another 12% lower, with the low arriving in the first full week of January. Until credit or the Fed changes, his stance is to treat rallies as chances to sell.

Morgan Stanley is reading the same decline as a bottoming. The correction has carried the index into the 7,100 to 7,300 range where its valuation, trendline and retracement supports coincide, and Mike Wilson raised his 12-month target to 8,700, 19 times 2028 earnings of $458 a share. Christopher Metli supplies the detail that decides which read is right: in 2018 it was the market pricing the Powell pivot, not the pivot itself, that stopped the slide on December 24. Real two-year yields fell about 8 basis points Friday, which Metli reads as at least a start.

So the condition is explicit. Rallies are rentals while corporate debt stays calm and the Fed keeps hiking. Two-year and shorter Treasury yields starting to discount the end of the hiking cycle, or credit finally cracking, is what turns it.

## 3. BRIEFS

### Hedge funds have already sold

Goldman's prime brokerage book shows fundamental long/short net leverage at 42.3%, the 1st percentile of the past year, and 48.2% once adjusted for how much those holdings move with the market, down more than 22 points since the start of October to a five-year low. Momentum exposure has gone from a record +70% in June to negative, and last week brought the heaviest single-stock shorting since June 2022, roughly 1.5% of gross market value. Morgan Stanley counts US long/short net leverage at 41%, the 6th percentile since 2010, and expects Friday's move to push it under 40%. Trend-following funds are net short $19 billion of US stocks after selling $57 billion in a month, and Goldman's model has them selling again next week whether stocks rise, fall or go sideways. Deutsche Bank's own gauge sits at just the 11th percentile since 2010, which is why it argues maximum fear may still be ahead.

### Credit has not cracked

The investment-grade index closed 10 basis points wider last week, tied for its worst week of the year, widening on all five sessions at twice its average daily move for 2026. High-yield spreads closed 51 basis points wider, also the worst week of the year. Goldman's credit desk reads the cash move as exaggerated by thin holiday liquidity: it was net lifted on 1.3 billion of risk Friday, credit funds traded an orderly 0 to 1 basis point to net asset value, and default insurance widened 6 basis points against an implied 8.6, which the desk takes as credit investors being positioned correctly. The $44 billion SpaceX chip financing led by Goldman Sachs and Apollo priced Thursday and closed 15 basis points tighter than issue despite Friday's selloff. Hyperscaler debt now tracks the index at a 0.93 correlation against a 0.74 average, which the desk puts down to broad risk reduction rather than selling aimed at AI borrowers. It leans tactically bullish after the widening but would rather sell volatility than own the bonds.

### Nvidia beat and fell anyway

Revenue came in about $7 billion above expectations with guidance about $5 billion ahead, a 17th consecutive beat, fourth-quarter revenue guided to $130 billion against $125 billion expected, and fiscal 2028 growth raised to 75% from 70%. The stock fell 22.7% on the week to 8.3 times forward earnings, its lowest since the financial crisis and a record 50% discount to the S&P 500. Two things did the damage. The SEC opened a civil probe into disclosures with accounting a focus, and the shares dropped 13.8% on Friday as that was reported. CFO Kress also said the company will be more selective on customer financing, with guarantees kicking in only once projects have land, power and buildings. Goldman's TMT desk reads that as positive for Nvidia's own credit spreads and negative for neoclouds that depend on residual guarantees or offtake support. Goldman's 2027 credit outlook counts $985 billion of AI debt coming, including $545 billion of project finance, of which $320 billion needs a guarantor that has just stepped back.

### The biggest IPO ever drained the market

Anthropic priced above its range at $83, raising a record $107.3 billion including a $26 billion placement with its own chip and cloud suppliers, at a fully diluted $1.5 trillion, roughly 12 to 13 times 2027 sales. It opened at $111.50 and the broad market was sold at that moment. The S&P shed about $1.3 trillion of value that day, close to what Anthropic was worth at its IPO price, which is the funding-drain mechanism rather than a demand signal. The stock closed its debut up 17%, then fell to $83.10 by Friday's lows, more than 25% below Thursday's $115.10 high in under a day, after underwriters exercised the greenshoe in full, 147 million extra shares at $83, removing the stabilizing short. The book was six to seven times covered and index inclusion on December 21 brings about $19 billion of passive buying. JPMorgan's Ronald Adler frames the question this way: a clearing event might happen if everyone wants one, but will not if everyone needs one.

### The AI spending level rises, the growth rate stalls

Nobody in the pack disputes the dollars. Morgan Stanley keeps hyperscaler and neocloud capital spending at about $1.6 trillion in 2027 and $1.8 trillion in 2028, adding 0.9 percentage points to 2027 GDP growth. Citadel Securities puts 2027 at about $1.5 trillion. The disagreement is in the rate of change: Morgan Stanley has growth decelerating from 98% in 2026 to 73% in 2027 and 7% in 2028, while Citadel has it going from 106% to 57% next year, leaving the contribution to nominal growth flat at 0.55 to 0.56 percentage points. Supply is the other constraint. Newly elected governors could push about 5GW of Texas and Ohio capacity from 2028 into 2029, MetLife expects new governance rules to slow investment growth modestly with sharper-decline risk elevated, and Bernstein judges only 199GW of the 586GW pipeline credible. On demand, 44% of S&P 500 companies now cite a measurable AI benefit against 32% a year ago, but 48% of chief information officers who can measure the return on their token spend say it is below that spend.

### Copper and gold washed out together

High-grade copper futures fell more than 9% on the week, one of the biggest weekly drops in a decade. The setup was crowding, not a demand shock: producers, merchants, processors and users held gross longs near 20% of all outstanding contracts on November 10, roughly double the norm. The drop pushed prices through a momentum threshold and set off heavy selling by trend followers, which the physical buying community had no room left to absorb. Goldman's futures desk pins the move on tighter financial conditions and continued data center weakness, and Stan Druckenmiller has said he is short copper. Goldman's Global Copper Basket is down close to 35% from its August peak against a 22% fall in LME copper. Precious metals went with it, gold down 9.5% and silver 16.4% on the week, gold back below $3,600 for the first time since September 2025, and the cost of protecting against a further gold decline moved 4 to 5 standard deviations on November 20. Camille Cerisey's read is that copper is washing out rather than breaking down.

### Only Treasuries worked

Two-year Treasury yields fell 20 basis points to 4.65% and 10-year yields 19 basis points to 4.95% on the week, ending the longest stretch above 5% since 2007. The rally came against hawkish policy news, not with it: October's FOMC minutes showed almost all participants judged further rate increases likely appropriate, and Governor Waller pushed for 50 basis points in December. The week's data and the equity decline took that 50 off the table and dragged January hike odds below 60%, leaving about 62 basis points priced over the next ten months. Citadel Securities' Frank Flight puts his cross-asset growth gauge at -1.85 standard deviations against a 10-year yield at +1.75, a gap of more than 3.5 sigma he calls historically large, and expects it to resolve through a flatter curve first. In 2018 and 2022 stocks caught down to those internals rather than the reverse. Goldman's financial conditions index has tightened about 100 basis points this year, all of it since early September, worth a 0.5 percentage point drag on 2027 growth if it sticks, which Goldman does not expect.
